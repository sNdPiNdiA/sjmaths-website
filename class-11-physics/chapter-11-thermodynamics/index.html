11.1

Thermodynamics and heat as energy

Thermodynamics describes bulk energy transfers and transformations.

  • Thermodynamics studies heat, temperature, work and their interconversion. It is macroscopic: a system is described by measurable bulk variables rather than tracking each molecule.
  • Mechanical motion of a body as a whole is distinct from its internal energy. When a moving bullet stops in wood, organized kinetic energy becomes disordered internal energy of the bullet and nearby wood.
  • The old caloric-fluid model treated heat as a material fluid. Rumford’s cannon-boring observation showed heat production depended on work done, supporting heat as energy transfer and conversion.
  • Heat flows spontaneously from higher to lower temperature. Rubbing hands converts work into internal energy; a steam engine converts some transferred heat into useful work.
11.2–11.3

Thermal equilibrium and the Zeroth Law

Thermal equilibrium is the basis for defining temperature.

  • A system is in thermodynamic equilibrium when its macroscopic variables remain constant in time. Mechanical equilibrium (zero net force/torque) is a different idea.
  • An adiabatic wall prevents heat transfer; a diathermic wall permits it. Systems across a diathermic wall exchange energy until no net heat flows; they are then in thermal equilibrium.
  • Zeroth Law: if A and B are each in thermal equilibrium with C, then A and B are in thermal equilibrium with each other. It makes temperature a well-defined common property.
  • At thermal equilibrium, temperatures are equal. A thermometer is a third system that comes to equilibrium with the body; reproducible thermometric properties enable a temperature scale.
11.4

Heat, internal energy and work

Q and W describe energy transfer; U describes the system’s state.

  • Internal energy U is the sum of molecular kinetic and potential energies in the centre-of-mass frame. It includes random translational, rotational and vibrational energy, not kinetic energy of the system moving as a whole.
  • U is a state variable: its change depends only on initial and final states, not the path. Pressure, volume and temperature are also state variables for a gas.
  • Heat Q is energy transferred because of a temperature difference; work W is energy transferred by other macroscopic means (such as a piston). Heat and work are not properties “contained” in a state.
  • Sign convention throughout: Q>0 when heat enters the system; W>0 when the system does work on surroundings. For constant external pressure, W=PΔV; in a quasi-static process, W=∫P dV.
11.5

First Law of Thermodynamics

Energy supplied becomes internal energy and/or work.

  • For the stated sign convention, the First Law is Q=ΔU+W, or ΔU=Q−W. It is conservation of energy applied to a thermodynamic system.
  • ΔU is path-independent, while Q and W individually depend on the path. Their difference Q−W is fixed by the change of state.
  • If ΔU=0 (for an ideal gas in an isothermal process), then Q=W: supplied heat is converted into work. During a complete cycle, ΔU=0, so net heat equals net work.
  • For constant pressure, W=PΔV. Example from the chapter: vaporising 1 g water at 1 atm absorbs 2256 J; ΔV=(1671−1) cm³=1.670×10⁻³ m³, so W≈1.013×10⁵×1.670×10⁻³=169.2 J and ΔU≈2256−169.2=2086.8 J.
WORKED

Chapter calculation

1 g of water becomes steam at atmospheric pressure. Q=2256 J, Vᵢ=1 cm³, V𝒇=1671 cm³. Therefore W=PΔV≈169.2 J and ΔU=Q−W≈2086.8 J; most supplied energy raises internal energy.

11.6

Specific heat capacity

Heat capacity depends on amount, substance, temperature and process.

  • Heat capacity S=ΔQ/ΔT; specific heat s=S/m=ΔQ/(mΔT), in J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹. Molar heat capacity C=ΔQ/(μΔT), in J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹.
  • Specific and molar heat capacities depend on the material and temperature. For gases they also depend on the constraint: Cᵥ at constant volume and Cₚ at constant pressure.
  • For a monatomic solid modelled as atoms vibrating in three dimensions, mean energy per atom is 3kᵦT. Per mole U=3RT. Since solid expansion work PΔV is small, C≈ΔU/ΔT=3R (Dulong–Petit law); agreement fails at low T and for some materials such as carbon.
  • A calorie was precisely defined using water from 14.5°C to 15.5°C; SI uses joules. Water’s specific heat near room temperature is about 4186 J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹.
  • For an ideal gas, Cₚ−Cᵥ=R. At constant volume Cᵥ=(ΔU/ΔT)₍ᵥ₎. At constant pressure Cₚ=ΔU/ΔT+PΔV/ΔT; for one mole PV=RT gives PΔV/ΔT=R. Subtracting gives Mayer’s relation.
PROOF

Proof

For one mole at fixed volume, Qᵥ=ΔU, hence Cᵥ=ΔU/ΔT. At fixed pressure, Qₚ=ΔU+PΔV, hence Cₚ=ΔU/ΔT+PΔV/ΔT. Since PV=RT, at fixed P, PΔV/ΔT=R. Therefore Cₚ=Cᵥ+R.

11.7

Thermodynamic state variables and equation of state

State variables describe equilibrium states; an equation of state links them.

  • An equilibrium gas state is specified by macroscopic variables such as P,V,T and amount/composition. During rapid free expansion or an explosive reaction, pressure or temperature may be non-uniform, so no single equilibrium state describes the system.
  • An equation of state relates state variables. For an ideal gas PV=μRT; for fixed μ, only two of P,V,T are independent. A fixed-temperature P–V curve is an isotherm.
  • Thermodynamic state variables also include entropy S and enthalpy H. Entropy is a state function related to energy dispersal and accessible microscopic arrangements; enthalpy is defined H=U+PV and is useful for constant-pressure changes.
  • Extensive quantities scale with system size and halve when an equilibrium system is divided equally: U,V,total mass and entropy. Intensive quantities remain unchanged: P,T and density.
  • Dimensional and extensive/intensive checks help test equations. In Q=ΔU+PΔV each term is extensive because intensive P multiplies extensive ΔV.
11.8

Quasi-static and special thermodynamic processes

The path determines work and heat, even between the same states.

  • A quasi-static process is an ideal limit carried out infinitely slowly so the system remains arbitrarily close to equilibrium at every stage. The system and surroundings differ only infinitesimally in pressure or temperature. Real slow processes can approximate it.
  • Isothermal: T constant. For an ideal gas, PV=constant and P=μRT/V; integrating W=∫P dV from V₁ to V₂ gives W=μRT ln(V₂/V₁). Since ideal-gas U depends only on T, ΔU=0 and Q=W. Expansion absorbs heat; compression releases it.
  • Adiabatic: thermally insulated, Q=0. For an ideal gas, PVᵞ=constant (γ=Cₚ/Cᵥ). With P=C/Vᵞ, integrate W=∫P dV to obtain W=(P₂V₂−P₁V₁)/(1−γ)=μR(T₁−T₂)/(γ−1). Expansion work lowers U and T; compression raises them.
  • Isochoric: V constant, W=0, so Q=ΔU. Isobaric: P constant, W=P(V₂−V₁)=μR(T₂−T₁); heat changes both U and volume. Cyclic: returns to initial state, ΔU=0 and net Q=W.
  • On a P–V graph, quasi-static work is signed area under the path. Isotherms are less steep than adiabats through the same state; adiabatic compression heats more than isothermal compression.
WORKED

Worked comparison

For an isothermal ideal-gas expansion from V₁ to 2V₁, W=μRT ln 2 and Q=W. In an adiabatic expansion between the same initial state and a larger volume, Q=0 and the gas cools; its pressure falls more steeply than on the isotherm.

11.9

Second Law of Thermodynamics

The First Law allows energy bookkeeping; the Second Law constrains direction.

  • Energy conservation alone permits imagined processes never observed, such as a book spontaneously rising while its internal energy converts entirely into mechanical energy. The Second Law rules out such directions.
  • Kelvin–Planck statement: no cyclic process can have as its sole result absorption of heat from one reservoir and complete conversion of that heat into work. Thus a heat engine cannot be 100% efficient.
  • Clausius statement: no process can have as its sole result transfer of heat from a colder body to a hotter body. A refrigerator therefore requires work; its coefficient of performance cannot be infinite.
  • The statements are equivalent. If free cold→hot transfer were possible, it could return a real engine’s rejected heat to the hot source at no work cost, leaving the sole net effect to convert heat from one reservoir wholly into work (Kelvin–Planck violation). If a perfect engine existed, its work could drive a refrigerator; the combined device would move heat cold→hot with no other net effect (Clausius violation).
11.10

Reversible and irreversible processes

Reversibility requires restoring both system and surroundings without other effects.

  • A process is reversible only if it can be reversed so that system and surroundings both return to their original states and nothing else changes. This is an ideal limit.
  • A reversible process must be quasi-static and free of dissipative effects. A very slow isothermal expansion with a frictionless piston is an ideal example.
  • Natural processes are generally irreversible: heat flows down a temperature gradient, gases freely expand/diffuse, combustion occurs, and friction or viscosity dissipates mechanical energy.
  • Irreversibility arises when a system passes through nonequilibrium states and from dissipation (friction, viscosity, turbulence). These effects can be reduced but not fully eliminated in practice.
  • Reversible engines set the maximum efficiency between two reservoirs; any irreversible engine operating between them is less efficient.
11.11

Carnot engine and Carnot’s theorem

A reversible cycle sets the greatest possible engine efficiency between two temperatures.

  • A Carnot engine is reversible and operates between hot source T₁ and cold sink T₂ (T in kelvin). Its cycle consists of: isothermal expansion at T₁ absorbing Q₁; adiabatic expansion cooling to T₂; isothermal compression at T₂ rejecting Q₂; adiabatic compression returning to T₁.
  • For an ideal gas, isothermal expansion work and heat absorbed are Q₁=μRT₁ ln(V₂/V₁). Isothermal compression rejects Q₂=μRT₂ ln(V₃/V₄) in magnitude. The adiabatic legs obey TVᵞ⁻¹=constant, which gives V₂/V₁=V₃/V₄.
  • Efficiency η=W/Q₁=(Q₁−Q₂)/Q₁. Using the volume-ratio equality gives η=1−T₂/T₁. The efficiency depends only on reservoir temperatures, not the working substance.
  • Carnot’s theorem: no engine between the same two reservoirs can be more efficient than a reversible engine; all reversible engines between them have the same efficiency. Otherwise coupling a better engine to a reversible refrigerator would violate Kelvin–Planck.
  • Proof: suppose engine I were more efficient than reversible engine R. Let I run forward and R backward, adjusted to return to the hot source exactly the heat I removed. Since I produces more work than R consumes, the coupled device’s only net effect is to draw heat from the cold sink and convert it fully to work, contradicting Kelvin–Planck. Interchanging the working substances of two reversible engines gives the same contradiction if their efficiencies differ; hence all reversible engines between fixed reservoirs share the Carnot efficiency.
  • Reversing a Carnot cycle makes a refrigerator: work is supplied to remove heat from the cold reservoir and deliver it to the hot one. In a Carnot cycle Q₂/Q₁=T₂/T₁, a universal temperature relation.
PROOF

Proof of Carnot efficiency

Isothermal legs give Q₁=μRT₁ ln(V₂/V₁) and Q₂=μRT₂ ln(V₃/V₄). Adiabatic legs obey TVᵞ⁻¹=constant, so V₂/V₁=V₃/V₄. Thus Q₂/Q₁=T₂/T₁ and η=(Q₁−Q₂)/Q₁=1−T₂/T₁.

Ex 11.1NCERT

11.1A geyser heats water flowing at the rate of 3.0 litres per minute from 27 °C to 77 °C. If the geyser operates on a gas burner, what is the rate of consumption of the fuel if its heat of combustion is 4.0 × 10⁴ J/g?

Related concept: Review the matching notes

  1. Step 1. Assume water density 1 kg/L: mass flow =3.0/60=0.050 kg/s.
  2. Step 2. Heat rate =ṁsΔT=0.050×4186×50=1.0465×10⁴ J/s.
  3. Step 3. Fuel rate = heat rate/(4.0×10⁴ J/g)=0.262 g/s.

Answer: 0.26 g/s (about 15.7 g/min).

Ex 11.2NCERT

11.2What amount of heat must be supplied to 2.0 × 10⁻² kg of nitrogen (at room temperature) to raise its temperature by 45 °C at constant pressure? (Molecular mass of N₂ = 28; R = 8.3 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹.)

Related concept: Review the matching notes

  1. Step 1. Amount n=0.020/0.028=0.714 mol. At room temperature N₂ is diatomic, so Cₚ≈7R/2.
  2. Step 2. Q=nCₚΔT=0.714×(7/2×8.3)×45.

Answer: ≈9.33×10² J (about 0.93 kJ).

Ex 11.3NCERT

11.3Explain why (a) two bodies at different temperatures T₁ and T₂ if brought in thermal contact do not necessarily settle at the mean temperature (T₁ + T₂)/2; (b) the coolant in a chemical or a nuclear plant (i.e., the liquid used to prevent the different parts of a plant from getting too hot) should have high specific heat; (c) air pressure in a car tyre increases during driving; (d) the climate of a harbour town is more temperate than that of a town in a desert at the same latitude.

Related concept: Review the matching notes

  1. Step 1. (a) Final equilibrium follows energy balance, not a simple temperature average; it depends on masses and specific heats (and possible losses/phase changes).
  2. Step 2. (b) High specific heat lets coolant absorb much energy with a smaller temperature rise.
  3. Step 3. (c) Driving heats the tyre and enclosed air; at nearly fixed volume, ideal-gas pressure rises with absolute temperature.
  4. Step 4. (d) Water’s high specific heat moderates coastal temperature changes by absorbing/releasing substantial heat with small temperature changes.

Answer: Each explanation follows energy conservation and the relevant heat-capacity/ideal-gas relation.

Ex 11.5NCERT

11.5In changing the state of a gas adiabatically from an equilibrium state A to another equilibrium state B, an amount of work equal to 22.3 J is done on the system.
