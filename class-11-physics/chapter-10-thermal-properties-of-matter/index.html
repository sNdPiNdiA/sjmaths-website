10.1–10.2

Heat, temperature and equilibrium

Temperature describes thermal state; heat describes energy in transit.

  • Temperature is an objective measure of hotness. Touch is subjective and limited; a thermometer uses a measurable thermometric property that varies with temperature.
  • Heat is energy transferred between systems solely because of a temperature difference. It flows spontaneously from higher to lower temperature; its SI unit is joule (J). Temperature is measured in kelvin (K).
  • When system and surroundings reach the same temperature, net heat transfer ceases. Heat is not a substance stored inside a body; internal energy is a state property.
10.3

Measuring temperature and temperature scales

A scale assigns values using reproducible fixed points and equal intervals.

  • Liquid-in-glass thermometers use volume expansion; gas thermometers use pressure at fixed volume. Different thermometric properties may disagree between calibration points.
  • At standard pressure, ice and steam points are 0 °C and 100 °C, or 32 °F and 212 °F. Linear interpolation gives (tF−32)/180 = tC/100.
  • Thus tF=(9/5)tC+32 and tC=(5/9)(tF−32). Celsius and Fahrenheit differ in both zero and interval size.
10.4

Ideal-gas equation and absolute temperature

Low-density gases provide a common route to an absolute scale.

  • For a fixed amount of gas, Boyle’s law gives PV=constant at fixed T; Charles’ law gives V/T=constant at fixed P. Combining them gives PV/T=constant.
  • For μ moles, PV=μRT, where R=8.31 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹. At constant volume P∝T; gas-thermometer lines extrapolate toward zero pressure at absolute zero.
  • T=tC+273.15 K. Kelvin and Celsius have equal interval sizes, but different origins. Real gases depart from ideal behaviour, especially at low temperature; use dilute gas and extrapolation.
10.5

Linear, area and volume expansion

For small temperature intervals, expansion is proportional to original size and ΔT.

  • Linear expansivity α is defined by ΔL/L=αΔT, so L=L₀(1+αΔT). Area expansivity β=ΔA/(AΔT); for an isotropic solid β≈2α.
  • Proof: ΔA=aΔb+bΔa+ΔaΔb; put Δa=αaΔT and Δb=αbΔT. The product term is second order and negligible, leaving ΔA/A≈2αΔT.
  • Volume expansivity γ=ΔV/(VΔT). For a cube, ΔV=(L+ΔL)³−L³≈3L²ΔL, hence γ≈3α. These relations assume small expansion and isotropic solids.
NCERT Example 10.1 · Proof

Area expansivity of a rectangular sheet

Show that the area-expansion coefficient of a solid sheet is twice its linear expansivity.

Step 1. Let its sides be a and b. A rise ΔT gives Δa=αaΔT and Δb=αbΔT.
Step 2. The area change is ΔA=aΔb+bΔa+ΔaΔb=αabΔT(2+αΔT).
Step 3. Since αΔT≪1, neglect the second-order term: ΔA/A≈2αΔT.

Result: β=2α for small, isotropic expansion.

NCERT Example 10.2 · Solved

Heating an iron ring to fit a wheel

At 27 °C, an iron ring is 5.231 m across and a wheel rim is 5.243 m. Find the ring temperature required; use α=1.20×10⁻⁵ K⁻¹.

Step 1. Use D₂=D₁[1+α(T₂−T₁)] because every length of the free ring expands by the same fractional amount.
Step 2. T₂=27+[5.243/5.231−1]/(1.20×10⁻⁵)=218.4 °C.
Step 3. A small expansion is enough because the diameter deficit is only 0.012 m.

Result: About 218 °C, as in the NCERT rounded result.

10.5

Liquids, gases and constrained expansion

Expansion depends on state, temperature and mechanical constraints.

  • Liquids have volume expansivity γ. A liquid in a vessel appears to expand by γapp=γliquid−γvessel; the vessel expands too.
  • Water is anomalous between 0 °C and 4 °C: it contracts when warmed, has maximum density at 4 °C, and expands on cooling below 4 °C. Lakes freeze at the surface, helping aquatic life survive below.
  • For an ideal gas at constant pressure, V∝T, so γ=1/T. If a rod is fully constrained, total strain is zero; mechanical strain cancels αΔT and |σ|=Yα|ΔT|. Heating gives compression and cooling gives tension; expansion joints prevent damaging stress.
Worked check · Thermal stress

Fully restrained thermal strain

A steel rail cannot expand. Find the compressive thermal stress for α=1.2×10⁻⁵ K⁻¹, Y=2×10¹¹ Pa and a 10 K rise.

Step 1. The free thermal strain would be αΔT=1.2×10⁻⁴.
Step 2. Rigid supports impose an equal-and-opposite elastic strain.
Step 3. The stress magnitude is YαΔT=(2×10¹¹)(1.2×10⁻⁴).

Result: 2.4×10⁷ Pa compression. (This is the chapter’s rail illustration, solved explicitly.)

10.6

Heat capacity and specific heat

Temperature change depends on mass, material and the amount of heat transferred.

  • Heat capacity S=ΔQ/ΔT is for the whole body (J K⁻¹). Specific heat s=ΔQ/(mΔT) is per unit mass (J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹); hence ΔQ=msΔT when no phase change occurs.
  • Molar heat capacity C=ΔQ/(μΔT), in J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹. For a gas, specify constant pressure Cp or constant volume Cv; generally Cp>Cv.
  • Specific heats depend on material and temperature. Water’s large s≈4186 J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹ makes it useful as a coolant and moderator of day–night and land–sea temperature changes.
10.7

Calorimetry: heat balance

An isolated mixture conserves energy: heat lost equals heat gained.

  • A calorimeter limits heat exchange with surroundings. In the ideal isolated model, ΣQ=0; hot components lose energy and colder components gain it until one common final temperature is reached.
  • For sensible heating/cooling use Q=ms(Tf−Ti) with sign. Include the calorimeter’s own heat capacity or water equivalent.
  • For a phase change include latent heat too. Check that the predicted final state is physically possible before solving; account for environmental losses if the apparatus is not well insulated.
NCERT Example 10.3 · Solved

Specific heat of aluminium by calorimetry

A 0.047 kg aluminium sphere at 100 °C is placed in 0.25 kg water and a 0.14 kg copper calorimeter, both at 20 °C. Final temperature: 23 °C. Find sAl.

Step 1. Heat lost by Al = 0.047sAl(100−23).
Step 2. Heat gained = [0.25(4180)+0.14(386)](23−20)=3,297 J.
Step 3. Set lost equal to gained: sAl=3297/[0.047×77]=911 J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹.

Result: sAl≈0.911 kJ kg⁻¹ K⁻¹, consistent with the NCERT worked result.

10.8

Change of state and latent heat

During a phase transition, supplied energy changes arrangement rather than temperature.

  • Fusion is solid→liquid and freezing is liquid→solid. Vaporisation is liquid→gas and condensation is gas→liquid. During an equilibrium transition, both phases coexist and temperature remains constant at fixed pressure.
  • Latent heat per unit mass is L=Q/m (J kg⁻¹); Q=mL. Use Lf for fusion and Lv for vaporisation. Heating a sample across several stages requires summing sensible-heat and latent-heat intervals.
  • Evaporation occurs at the surface below boiling and cools the remaining liquid; boiling is bulk vapour formation at the pressure-dependent boiling point. The triple point is where solid, liquid and vapour coexist; a phase diagram shows the coexistence curves.
NCERT Example 10.4 · Solved

Finding latent heat of fusion

Mix 0.15 kg ice at 0 °C with 0.30 kg water at 50 °C; final temperature is 6.7 °C. Find Lf; sw=4186 J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹.

Step 1. Hot-water heat loss: 0.30×4186×(50−6.7)=54,376 J.
Step 2. Ice first melts, then the melt warms: 0.15Lf+0.15×4186×6.7.
Step 3. Equate gain and loss: Lf=(54,376−4,207)/0.15.

Result: Lf≈3.34×10⁵ J kg⁻¹.

NCERT Example 10.5 · Solved

Ice at −12 °C to steam at 100 °C

Find the heat to turn 3 kg ice at −12 °C into steam at 100 °C. Use sice=2100, sw=4186 J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹, Lf=3.35×10⁵ and Lv=2.256×10⁶ J kg⁻¹.

Step 1. Warm ice to 0 °C: Q₁=3×2100×12=75,600 J.
Step 2. Melt it: Q₂=3×3.35×10⁵=1,005,000 J; warm water to 100 °C: Q₃=3×4186×100=1,255,800 J.
Step 3. Vaporise at 100 °C: Q₄=3×2.256×10⁶=6,768,000 J. Add all four stages.

Result: Q=9.1044×10⁶ J≈9.1 MJ.

10.8

Pressure, regelation and phase diagrams

Phase boundaries shift with pressure; water is a notable exception.

  • Melting and boiling temperatures depend on pressure. Increased pressure lowers ice’s melting point because liquid water is denser than ice; a weighted wire can melt ice beneath it and water refreezes above it (regelation).
  • A P–T phase diagram separates solid, liquid and vapour regions. Sublimation, fusion and vaporisation curves mark coexistence of two phases; their intersection is the triple point.
  • Water’s triple point is 273.16 K at about 611 Pa. Its fusion line has negative slope; the fusion line of CO₂ and most substances slopes positively. At ordinary atmospheric pressure CO₂ sublimes instead of forming a liquid.
10.9

Heat transfer: conduction and convection

Heat can move by neighbouring interactions, bulk flow or electromagnetic radiation.

  • Conduction transfers energy between neighbouring parts without bulk matter flow. For a uniform insulated bar at steady state, H=K A(Thot−Tcold)/L; K is thermal conductivity (W m⁻¹ K⁻¹).
  • For layers in series the heat current is common and thermal resistances add: Rth=L/(KA), H=ΔT/ΣRth. Metals conduct well; trapped air and foams insulate.
  • Convection requires moving fluid. Natural convection uses buoyancy (warm, less-dense fluid rises); forced convection uses a pump or fan. Land/sea breezes reverse from day to night; Earth’s rotation shapes large-scale convection into trade-wind circulation.
NCERT Example 10.6 · Solved

Steel–copper junction temperature

Steel (L=0.15 m, K=50.2) and copper (L=0.10 m, K=385 W m⁻¹ K⁻¹) are joined in series. The steel area is 2A and copper area A; ends are at 300 °C and 0 °C. Find junction temperature.

Step 1. At steady state, heat currents are equal: 2(50.2)(300−T)/0.15 = 385T/0.10.
Step 2. Cancel the common area and solve 669.3(300−T)=3850T.
Step 3. The same current passes every cross-section; side losses are neglected.

Result: T≈44.4 °C, consistent with the NCERT worked result.

NCERT Example 10.7 · Solved

Two rods in series: junction and equivalent K

Equal 0.10 m rods of iron (K₁=79) and brass (K₂=109 W m⁻¹ K⁻¹), each area 0.02 m², have ends at 373 K and 273 K. Find junction temperature, equivalent conductivity and heat current.

Step 1. Equal steady heat currents give K₁(T₁−T₀)=K₂(T₀−T₂), so T₀=(K₁T₁+K₂T₂)/(K₁+K₂).
Step 2. T₀=(79×373+109×273)/188=315 K; K′=2K₁K₂/(K₁+K₂)=91.6 W m⁻¹ K⁻¹.
Step 3. H=K′A(T₁−T₂)/(2L)=91.6×0.02×100/0.20.

Result: T₀=315 K; K′=91.6 W m⁻¹ K⁻¹; H≈916 W.

10.9

Radiation and blackbody laws

Thermal radiation crosses a vacuum; its spectrum and intensity reveal temperature.

  • Thermal radiation is electromagnetic energy emitted because of temperature; unlike conduction or convection it needs no material medium. Dark, matte surfaces absorb and emit more effectively than shiny reflective ones.
  • A blackbody absorbs all incident radiation and is an ideal emitter. Its continuous spectrum is universal at a given temperature. Wien’s displacement law gives λmaxT=2.9×10⁻³ m K: hotter blackbodies peak at shorter wavelength.
  • Stefan–Boltzmann law: P=eσAT⁴ (σ=5.67×10⁻⁸ W m⁻² K⁻⁴; e=1 for a blackbody). In surroundings at Ts, net radiative loss is eσA(T⁴−Ts⁴). A thermos uses silvered walls and a vacuum gap to reduce radiation and conduction/convection.
10.10

Newton’s law of cooling

For small excess temperatures, heat-loss rate is proportional to the body’s excess temperature.

  • For body temperature T and surroundings Ts, −dQ/dt=k(T−Ts) for a small temperature difference. k depends on exposed area and surface; multiple transfer modes may contribute.
  • If the body has mass m and specific heat s, dQ=ms dT. Thus d(T−Ts)/dt=−K(T−Ts), where K=k/(ms).
  • Integrating gives ln(T−Ts)=−Kt+C, or T=Ts+(T₀−Ts)e⁻ᴷᵗ. It is an approximation for small excess temperature; a plot of ln(T−Ts) against time is straight.
NCERT Example 10.8 · Solved

Cooling time from Newton’s law

A pan cools 94→86 °C in 2 min in a 20 °C room. Estimate the time for 71→69 °C.

Step 1. For the first interval, mean excess temperature is 90−20=70 °C and cooling rate is 8/2=4 °C min⁻¹.
Step 2. For the second, mean excess is 70−20=50 °C; rate scales to 4×50/70=2.86 °C min⁻¹.
Step 3. Time for a 2 °C drop is 2/2.86=0.70 min.

Result: About 0.70 min = 42 s (small-excess, mean-temperature approximation).

CHECK

Points to keep in mind

  • Kelvin’s zero and its unit interval are defined by the scale; 273.16 K is water’s triple point, while 0 °C at 1 atm is 273.15 K.
  • Coexisting phases have the same equilibrium temperature and pressure, but different molar volumes and densities.
  • Heat transfer requires a temperature difference; energy transfer without that cause is not heat.
  • A hot bar under running water loses heat to the water by conduction at the interface; convection is the subsequent bulk motion within the fluid.

Ex 10.15NCERT

10.15Given below are observations on molar specific heats at room temperature of some common gases: H₂ 4.87, N₂ 4.97, O₂ 5.02, NO 4.99, CO 5.01, Cl₂ 6.17 cal mol⁻¹ K⁻¹. Related concept: Review the matching notes

Step 1. Diatomic molecules store thermal energy in rotational as well as translational degrees of freedom; additional vibrational modes can contribute.

Ex 10.19NCERT

10.19Explain why: (a) a body with large reflectivity is a poor emitter; (b) a brass tumbler feels much colder than a wooden tray on a chilly day; (c) an optical pyrometer (for measuring high temperatures) calibrated for an ideal black body radiation gives too low a value for the temperature of a red hot iron piece in the open, but gives a correct value for the temperature when the same piece is in the furnace; (d) the earth without its atmosphere would be inhospitably cold; (e) heating systems based on circulation of steam are more efficient in warming a building than those based on circulation of hot water.

Related concept: Review the matching notes

Step 1. (a) At thermal equilibrium, strong reflection means weak absorption and hence weak emission.
Step 2. (b) Brass conducts heat away from skin much faster than wood, so the skin cools faster.
Step 3. (c) Open iron has emissivity below 1, so a blackbody-calibrated pyrometer under-reads; inside a hot furnace reflected radiation adds and the cavity approaches blackbody conditions.
Step 4. (d) The atmosphere absorbs and re-emits outgoing infrared radiation, warming the surface.
Step 5. (e) Steam releases latent heat when it condenses, in addition to sensible heat.

Answer: Each effect follows from emissivity/absorptivity, conductivity, cavity radiation, atmospheric infrared trapping or latent heat.

Ex 10.20NCERT

10.20A body cools from 80 °C to 50 °C in 5 minutes. Calculate the time it takes to cool from 60 °C to 30 °C. The temperature of the surroundings is 20 °C.

Related concept: Review the matching notes

Step 1. For the first interval the mean body temperature is 65 °C, so mean excess is 45 °C; rate≈30/5=6 °C min⁻¹.
Step 2. For the second interval mean body temperature is 45 °C, so mean excess is 25 °C. Newton’s small-interval approximation gives rate≈6×25/45=3.33 °C min⁻¹.
Step 3. Time≈30/3.33.

Answer: About 9 min by the textbook mean-excess approximation. Exact exponential integration gives 10 min; Newton’s law is approximate over these finite intervals.

REVISION

Revision

Review the key ideas, formulas and conditions.

10.1–10.2 · Heat, temperature and equilibrium

Review this concept
  • Temperature measures thermal state; heat is energy crossing a boundary because temperatures differ.
  • Heat flows hot → cold until thermal equilibrium; SI units are J and K.
  • A thermometer reads a thermometric property; touch alone is unreliable.

10.3 · Measuring temperature and temperature scales

Review this concept
  • Use tF=(9/5)tC+32 and tC=(5/9)(tF−32).
  • A scale needs a thermometric property plus fixed reference points.
  • The Celsius and Fahrenheit ice/steam points are (0,100) °C and (32,212) °F.

10.4 · Ideal-gas equation and absolute temperature

Review this concept
  • PV=μRT; Boyle: PV const at fixed T; Charles: V/T const at fixed P.
  • Kelvin temperature is T=tC+273.15; never put Celsius into the ideal-gas law.
  • Absolute zero is inferred by dilute-gas extrapolation; real gases do not remain ideal there.

10.5 · Linear, area and volume expansion

Review this concept
  • ΔL/L=αΔT; ΔA/A≈2αΔT; ΔV/V≈3αΔT for small isotropic solid expansion.
  • Area proof drops the second-order ΔaΔb term; cube proof drops (ΔL)² and (ΔL)³.
  • Always state the initial dimension and temperature interval used.

10.5 · Liquids, gases and constrained expansion

Review this concept
  • Liquid apparent expansion: γapp=γliquid−γcontainer.
  • Water has maximum density at 4 °C; below 4 °C it expands as it cools.
  • At fixed pressure ideal-gas γ=1/T; fully restrained rod stress magnitude is Yα|ΔT|.

10.6 · Heat capacity and specific heat

Review this concept
  • S=ΔQ/ΔT; s=ΔQ/(mΔT); C=ΔQ/(μΔT).
  • Use Q=msΔT only while the substance remains in one phase.
  • For gases name the condition: Cp or Cv; water has unusually high specific heat.

10.7 · Calorimetry: heat balance

Review this concept
  • Isolated system: heat lost + heat gained = 0.
  • Include water, calorimeter and every object; a water equivalent is its heat capacity divided by sw.
  • Add latent-heat terms at phase transitions and check the assumed final phase.

10.8 · Change of state and latent heat

Review this concept
  • At a phase plateau, Q=mL while T is constant and both phases coexist.
  • Evaporation is surface-only below boiling; boiling occurs throughout at a pressure-dependent temperature.
  • Triple point: solid + liquid + vapour coexist. Regelation is pressure melting followed by refreezing.

10.8 · Pressure, regelation and phase diagrams

Review this concept
  • Phase boundaries depend on pressure; ice melts under increased pressure because liquid water is denser.
  • The three phase-boundary curves meet at the triple point (water: 273.16 K, about 611 Pa).
  • Solid CO₂ sublimes at 1 atm; water’s fusion curve slopes negatively.

10.9 · Heat transfer: conduction and convection

Review this concept
  • Conduction: H=KAΔT/L in steady state; no bulk material flow.
  • Series layers: thermal resistances L/(KA) add.
  • Convection transports heat with fluid motion; natural flow uses buoyancy, forced flow uses a pump/fan.

10.9 · Radiation and blackbody laws

Review this concept
  • Radiation is electromagnetic energy and needs no medium; only this mode works across vacuum.
  • Wien: λmaxT=2.9×10⁻³ m K. Stefan–Boltzmann: P=eσAT⁴; net P=eσA(T⁴−Ts⁴).
  • Black/dull surfaces are strong absorbers and emitters; shiny surfaces reflect radiation.

10.10 · Newton’s law of cooling

Review this concept
  • Cooling rate ∝ (T−Ts) only for a small excess temperature.
  • Integrate to T−Ts=(T0−Ts)e⁻ᴷᵗ; the ln(excess)–time slope is −K.
  • Compare mean excess temperatures for textbook finite-interval estimates.
