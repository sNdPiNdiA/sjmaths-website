8.1

Elasticity, plasticity and rigid-body limits

  • A rigid body is an idealisation: real solids can stretch, compress or bend under a sufficient force. A solid has a definite shape and size, but is not perfectly rigid.
  • Elasticity: tendency to recover the original shape and size after the deforming force is removed. A gently stretched spring exhibits elastic deformation.
  • Plasticity: permanent deformation after unloading. Putty and mud are close to ideal plastics.
  • Elastic properties guide buildings, bridges, automobiles, ropeways, aircraft and artificial limbs: stiffness, strength and weight must be balanced.
8.2

Stress and three types of strain

  • In static equilibrium, an applied deforming load is balanced by internal restoring forces. Stress is restoring force per unit area: $\sigma=F/A$. Its SI unit is pascal ($1\,\mathrm{Pa}=1\,\mathrm{N\,m^{-2}}$); dimensions $[ML^{-1}T^{-2}]$.
  • Longitudinal: forces normal to the cross-section produce tensile or compressive stress. Longitudinal strain $\varepsilon=\Delta L/L$ (positive for extension, negative for compression).
  • Shear: tangential forces produce relative face displacement $\Delta x$. Shearing strain $\gamma=\Delta x/L=\tan\theta\approx\theta$ for small angles in radians; at $10^\circ$, the approximation differs by about 1%. Balance both total force and torque in a static shear arrangement.
  • Hydraulic: equal pressure normal to every surface compresses volume without changing geometrical shape. Hydraulic stress equals pressure; volume strain $\varepsilon_V=\Delta V/V$.
  • All strains are ratios: they have no units and are dimensionless. Stress is not an ordinary force vector; its components depend on the chosen internal surface.
Do not double the tension: two opposite end forces each of magnitude $F$ give internal tension $F$, so stress is $F/A$, not $2F/A$.
8.3

Hooke’s law and its domain

  • For small deformations in the proportional region, $\text{stress}=k\,\text{strain}$; $k$ is the appropriate modulus of elasticity.
  • Hooke’s law is empirical, established experimentally; it is not a universal theorem to be proved. Some elastic materials have a nonlinear response.
  • The proportional limit and elastic limit need not coincide: elasticity means recovery, while Hooke’s law means a linear stress–strain relationship.
  • A modulus has stress units (Pa) because strain has no units. It characterises the material under the stated conditions, not the size of the specimen.
8.4

Stress–strain curve, strength and elastomers

  • Experimental method: increase a wire/cylinder’s tensile load in steps, measure extension, calculate stress and strain, then plot stress vertically against strain horizontally. Compression and shear curves are analogous.
  • OA: straight line; Hooke’s law holds and unloading restores the original dimensions. Its slope gives Young’s modulus.
  • AB: nonlinear but still recoverable. B is the textbook’s yield point/elastic limit; the corresponding stress is yield strength.
  • Beyond B: large strain develops for small stress changes. Unloading from C leaves a permanent set; this is plastic deformation.
  • D: ultimate tensile strength (largest engineering stress). Beyond D, further strain can occur with a smaller force; E is fracture.
  • D and E close: brittle material. Far apart: ductile material. Stiffness (initial slope), yield strength and ultimate strength are different properties.
  • Rubber and aorta tissue are elastomers: large recoverable strain, a nonlinear curve over most of the elastic region, and no well-defined plastic region.
  • An elastic material need not obey Hooke’s law; “stretches easily” is not the physics definition of “more elastic”.
8.5.1

Young’s modulus: axial extension and compression

$$Y=\frac{\sigma}{\varepsilon}=\frac{F/A}{\Delta L/L}=\frac{FL}{A\Delta L},\qquad |\Delta L|=\frac{FL}{AY}$$
  • Derivation: divide longitudinal stress by longitudinal strain. Use magnitudes when calculating a compression; its signed strain is negative.
  • Within the linear range, the same magnitude of tensile or compressive stress produces the same magnitude of longitudinal strain for a given material.
  • Larger Y means smaller strain for the same stress: steel is stiffer and, in this sense, more elastic than copper, brass or aluminium. A larger Y does not by itself imply a larger breaking stress.
  • For a wire, $\Delta L\propto F L/A$; with $A=\pi r^2$, extension varies as $1/r^2$. A wire acts like a spring with axial stiffness $k_{\rm wire}=YA/L$.
  • Reference comparison: to produce $0.1\%$ strain in a $0.1\,\mathrm{cm^2}$ wire, the stated forces are steel 2000 N, aluminium 690 N, brass 900 N and copper 1100 N. Values are approximate and depend on composition.

NCERT Example 8.1

Stress, elongation and strain

A structural steel rod has a radius of 10 mm and a length of 1.0 m. A 100 kN force stretches it along its length. Calculate (a) stress, (b) elongation, and (c) strain on the rod. Young’s modulus, of structural steel is $2.0\times10^{11}\,\mathrm{N\,m^{-2}}$.

Step 1. Convert: $r=0.010\,\mathrm m$, $F=1.00\times10^5\,\mathrm N$; $A=\pi r^2=3.14\times10^{-4}\,\mathrm{m^2}$.
Step 2. Stress: $\sigma=F/A=3.18\times10^8\,\mathrm{Pa}$.
Step 3. Extension: $\Delta L=\sigma L/Y=1.59\times10^{-3}\,\mathrm m=1.59\,\mathrm{mm}$.
Step 4. Strain: $\varepsilon=\Delta L/L=1.59\times10^{-3}\approx0.16\%$.
$$\Delta L_{\rm series}=F\sum_i\frac{L_i}{A_iY_i}$$
  • Wires connected end to end carry the same tension (neglect their weights); total extension is the sum of the individual extensions.
  • Parallel wires attached to a rigid level bar undergo the same extension if their original lengths are equal; their tensions divide in proportion to AY/L.
NCERT Example 8.2

Two wires in series

A copper wire of length 2.2 m and a steel wire of length 1.6 m, both of diameter 3.0 mm, are connected end to end. When stretched by a load, the net elongation is found to be 0.70 mm. Obtain the load applied.

Step 1. The same tension $W$ acts in both wires; $A=\pi(1.5\times10^{-3})^2=7.07\times10^{-6}\,\mathrm{m^2}$. Use $Y_c=1.1\times10^{11}$ and $Y_s=2.0\times10^{11}\,\mathrm{Pa}$.
Step 2. $\Delta L_c/\Delta L_s=(L_c/Y_c)/(L_s/Y_s)=2.5$. With $\Delta L_c+\Delta L_s=7.0\times10^{-4}\,\mathrm m$, obtain $\Delta L_c=5.0\times10^{-4}\,\mathrm m$ and $\Delta L_s=2.0\times10^{-4}\,\mathrm m$.
Step 3. $W=AY_c\Delta L_c/L_c=1.77\times10^2\,\mathrm N\approx1.8\times10^2\,\mathrm N$.

Compression of thighbones

8.5.2

Shear modulus (modulus of rigidity)

$$G=\frac{F/A}{\Delta x/L}=\frac{FL}{A\Delta x}\approx\frac{F}{A\theta},\qquad \tau\approx G\theta$$
  • Derivation: divide tangential stress by shear strain; replace tan θ by θ only at small angles. The area is the face parallel to which the shear force acts; L is the separation of the sliding faces.
  • Units: Pa. Only solids can support a sustained static shear stress; fluids flow under it. A helical coil stretches chiefly because its wire twists, so coil extension depends on shear modulus.
  • Typically $G$ is smaller than $Y$. The textbook estimate $G\approx Y/3$ is approximate, not an exact universal identity.

NCERT Example 8.4

Shear displacement of lead

A square lead slab of side 50 cm and thickness 10 cm is subject to a shearing force (on its narrow face) of $9.0\times10^4\,\mathrm N$. The lower edge is riveted to the floor. How much will the upper edge be displaced?

Step 1. The sheared narrow face has $A=0.50(0.10)=0.050\,\mathrm{m^2}$; the separation of top and bottom is $L=0.50\,\mathrm m$.
Step 2. $\tau=F/A=9.0\times10^4/0.050=1.80\times10^6\,\mathrm{Pa}$; use $G_{\rm lead}=5.6\times10^9\,\mathrm{Pa}$.
Step 3. $\Delta x=\tau L/G=1.61\times10^{-4}\,\mathrm m\approx0.16\,\mathrm{mm}$.

8.5.3

Bulk modulus and compressibility

$$B=-\frac{\Delta p}{\Delta V/V},\qquad \frac{\Delta V}{V}=-\frac{\Delta p}{B},\qquad \kappa=\frac1B=-\frac{1}{\Delta p}\frac{\Delta V}{V}$$
  • An increase in pressure produces a decrease in volume: Δp > 0 and signed ΔV < 0. The minus sign makes B positive. Fractional compression is the positive magnitude |ΔV|/V.
  • B applies to solids, liquids and gases under uniform hydraulic stress. Compressibility κ has unit Pa⁻¹; smaller κ means harder to compress.
  • Neighbouring atoms are tightly coupled in solids, less strongly coupled in liquids and poorly coupled in gases. Typical solids are least compressible, liquids intermediate and gases most compressible.
  • Gas compressibility depends strongly on pressure and temperature; typical gases can be about a million times more compressible than solids.

NCERT Example 8.5

Compression of ocean water

The average depth of Indian Ocean is about 3000 m. Calculate the fractional compression, $\Delta V/V$, of water at the bottom of the ocean, given that the bulk modulus of water is $2.2\times10^9\,\mathrm{N\,m^{-2}}$. (Take $g=10\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}$)

Step 1. Pressure increase relative to the surface: $\Delta p=\rho gh=1000(10)(3000)=3.0\times10^7\,\mathrm{Pa}$.
Step 2. Signed volume strain: $\Delta V/V=-\Delta p/B=-1.36\times10^{-2}$.
Step 3. Fractional compression magnitude is $|\Delta V|/V=1.36\times10^{-2}=1.36\%$. The negative signed strain means volume decreases.

8.5.4

Poisson’s ratio and lateral contraction

$$\nu=\frac{\Delta d/d}{\Delta L/L}=\frac{\Delta d}{\Delta L}\frac{L}{d}$$
  • Here Δd is the positive magnitude of diameter contraction and ΔL is extension. Lateral contraction accompanies longitudinal stretching.
  • With signed strains, the equivalent definition is $\nu=-\varepsilon_{\perp}/\varepsilon_{\parallel}$. Do not omit this minus sign if the lateral strain is negative.
  • Poisson’s ratio is a pure number, independent of specimen dimensions in the elastic regime. Typical values: steel 0.28–0.30; aluminium alloys about 0.33.
  • A force in one direction can produce strains in other directions, so one elastic constant cannot describe every deformation.
8.5.5

Proof: elastic potential energy in a wire

  • Work is done against interatomic restoring forces and stored as elastic potential energy. Stretch slowly within the linear elastic region. The force grows from zero with the instantaneous extension x; the final force must not be used as a constant throughout the work integral.
Step 1. From Young’s modulus, $F(x)=YAx/L$.
Step 2. Integrate: $U=W=\int_0^{\Delta L}F(x)\,dx=\frac{YA}{L}\int_0^{\Delta L}x\,dx=\frac{YA(\Delta L)^2}{2L}=\frac12F\Delta L$.
Step 3. Since wire volume is $AL$ and $\varepsilon=\Delta L/L$, $U=\frac12Y\varepsilon^2(AL)=\frac12\sigma\varepsilon(AL)$.
$$u=\frac{U}{AL}=\frac12\sigma\varepsilon=\frac12Y\varepsilon^2=\frac{\sigma^2}{2Y}$$
Energy density u has unit J m⁻³, numerically equivalent to Pa. It is the area under the linear stress–strain graph. For nonlinear elastic loading, integrate the actual curve rather than assuming a triangular area.
8.6

Applications: crane ropes and safe loads

  • Use yield strength to avoid permanent deformation; use ultimate strength only when considering breaking. Structural design also accounts for traffic, wind, self-weight, reliability, service life and cost.
Step 1. For a load $W=Mg$, require $W/A\le\sigma_y$, hence $A\ge Mg/\sigma_y$.
Step 2. For a 10-tonne load and mild steel with $\sigma_y=300\times10^6\,\mathrm{Pa}$, $A_{\min}=10^4(9.8)/(300\times10^6)=3.27\times10^{-4}\,\mathrm{m^2}$.
Step 3. Circular radius $r_{\min}=\sqrt{A/\pi}\approx1.02\,\mathrm{cm}$. A load safety factor 10 multiplies area by 10 and radius by $\sqrt{10}$: $r\approx3.2\,\mathrm{cm}$ (about 3 cm).
  • A thick single wire behaves like a rigid rod. Braided thin wires provide practical flexibility, ease of manufacture and strength.
8.6

Proof: beam bending and I-section design

$$\delta=\frac{Wl^3}{4Ybd^3}$$
  • For a rectangular beam: span l, breadth b, vertical depth d; simply supported near its ends and carrying a central load W. The formula assumes small elastic bending, a uniform beam and negligible shear deflection.
  • Increase Y or depth d; reduce span l. Doubling depth reduces sag by 8, doubling breadth reduces it by 2, and doubling span increases it by 8.
Step 1. For a rectangular section, the second moment of area about the neutral axis is $I=\int y^2\,dA=b\int_{-d/2}^{d/2}y^2\,dy=bd^3/12$.
Curvature relation. A fibre at distance y from the neutral axis has bending strain $\varepsilon=-y/R$ and stress $\sigma=-Yy/R$. The bending-moment magnitude is $M=\left|\int\sigma y\,dA\right|=(Y/R)\int y^2\,dA=YI/R$. For small slopes, $1/R\approx|v''(x)|$, so $|M|=YI|v''|$.
Step 2. Each support reaction is $W/2$. For $0\le x\le l/2$, bending moment $M(x)=Wx/2$. In the small-slope limit, the curvature magnitude follows $YI\,v''(x)=Wx/2$ (choose a consistent deflection sign).
Step 3. Integrate: $v'(x)=Wx^2/(4YI)+C_1$. Symmetry gives $v'(l/2)=0$, hence $C_1=-Wl^2/(16YI)$.
Step 4. Integrate again and use $v(0)=0$: $v(x)=Wx^3/(12YI)-Wl^2x/(16YI)$. Therefore $\delta=|v(l/2)|=Wl^3/(48YI)=Wl^3/(4Ybd^3)$.
  • A very thin deep beam can buckle sideways, especially under an off-centre moving load. An I-section combines useful depth and broad flanges with lower weight and cost.
  • Columns with well-distributed end support carry larger stable loads than comparable rounded/pinned-end arrangements; end restraint and load alignment affect buckling.
  • Pyramidal hills/heaps and broad structural bases spread loads. A heap of sand also depends on granular friction; it is not a uniform elastic beam.

8.6

Proof: maximum mountain height

  • The sides of a mountain are free: the base is not under uniform hydraulic pressure. Its self-weight produces a shear component of roughly the same order as the vertical stress.
Step 1. For a column of rock of height h and base area A: mass $m=\rho Ah$, so weight per unit area is $mg/A=\rho gh$.
Step 2. Require the characteristic shear stress to stay below the critical rock strength: $\rho gh\lesssim\tau_{\rm critical}$. Thus $h_{\max}\sim\tau_{\rm critical}/(\rho g)$.
Step 3. With $\tau_{\rm critical}=30\times10^7\,\mathrm{Pa}$, $\rho=3\times10^3\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}$ and $g\approx10\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}$, $h\sim10^4\,\mathrm m=10\,\mathrm{km}$.
This is an order-of-magnitude estimate, not an exact maximum for every rock or planet; it explains the terrestrial mountain-height scale.

Ex 8.1young

A steel wire of length 4.7 m and cross-sectional area $3.0\times10^{-5}\,\mathrm{m^2}$ stretches by the same amount as a copper wire of length 3.5 m and cross-sectional area of $4.0\times10^{-5}\,\mathrm{m^2}$ under a given load. What is the ratio of the Young’s modulus of steel to that of copper?

Prerequisite:Review the matching concept
Step 1. Same force and extension: $FL_s/(A_sY_s)=FL_c/(A_cY_c)$.
Step 2. $Y_s/Y_c=L_sA_c/(L_cA_s)=4.7(4.0)/(3.5(3.0))=1.79$.
Ex 8.2curve

Figure 8.9 shows the strain-stress curve for a given material. What are (a) Young’s modulus and (b) approximate yield strength for this material?

Prerequisite:Review the matching concept
Step 1. In the linear region, $(\varepsilon,\sigma)=(0.002,150\times10^6\,\mathrm{Pa})$. Thus $Y=\sigma/\varepsilon=7.5\times10^{10}\,\mathrm{Pa}$.
Step 2. The departure from the linear region is near $\sigma\approx300\times10^6\,\mathrm{Pa}$; approximate yield strength $\approx3\times10^8\,\mathrm{Pa}$. Read this as a graph estimate, not an exact material constant.
Ex 8.3curve

The stress-strain graphs for materials A and B are shown in Fig. 8.10. The graphs are drawn to the same scale.
(a) Which of the materials has the greater Young’s modulus?
(b) Which of the two is the stronger material?

Prerequisite:Review the matching concept
Step 1. (a) A has the steeper initial straight-line slope, so A has the larger Young’s modulus.
Step 2. (b) B reaches the greater maximum stress on the same scale, so B is the stronger material. Do not compare slopes to decide ultimate strength.
Ex 8.4curve · shear

Read the following two statements below carefully and state, with reasons, if it is true or false.
(a) The Young’s modulus of rubber is greater than that of steel;
(b) The stretching of a coil is determined by its shear modulus.

Prerequisite:Review the matching concept
Step 1. (a) False. Steel has a much larger Young’s modulus; rubber stretches more for the same stress.

Step 2. Use $Y_c=1.1\times10^{11}\,\mathrm{Pa}$. $\varepsilon=F/(AY)=44500/[2.9032\times10^{-4}(1.1\times10^{11})]=1.39\times10^{-3}$.

A steel cable with a radius of 1.5 cm supports a chairlift at a ski area. If the maximum stress is not to exceed $10^8\,\mathrm{N\,m^{-2}}$, what is the maximum load the cable can support ?

Prerequisite:Review the matching concept
Step 1. $A=\pi(0.015)^2=7.07\times10^{-4}\,\mathrm{m^2}$.
Step 2. Maximum load (force) $W_{\max}=\sigma_{\max}A=7.07\times10^4\,\mathrm N$. Corresponding supported mass $M=W/g\approx7.21\times10^3\,\mathrm{kg}$.
Ex 8.10young

A rigid bar of mass 15 kg is supported symmetrically by three wires each 2.0 m long. Those at each end are of copper and the middle one is of iron. Determine the ratios of their diameters if each is to have the same tension.

Prerequisite:Review the matching concept
Step 1. Symmetry and rigidity keep the bar level: equal original lengths therefore have equal extension. Equal tensions require $A_cY_c=A_iY_i$, hence $d_c^2Y_c=d_i^2Y_i$.
Step 2. Using Table 8.1, $Y_i=190\,\mathrm{GPa}$ and $Y_c=110\,\mathrm{GPa}$, $d_c/d_i=\sqrt{190/110}=1.31$. The diameter ratio is copper : iron : copper $=1.31:1:1.31$.
Step 3. Each tension is $Mg/3=49\,\mathrm N$, but the mass cancels from the diameter ratio.
Ex 8.11young

A 14.5 kg mass, fastened to the end of a steel wire of unstretched length 1.0 m, is whirled in a vertical circle with an angular velocity of 2 rev/s at the bottom of the circle. The cross-sectional area of the wire is $0.065\,\mathrm{cm^2}$. Calculate the elongation of the wire when the mass is at the lowest point of its path.

Prerequisite:Review the matching concept
Step 1. At the bottom, $T-mg=m\omega^2R$. Convert $\omega=4\pi\,\mathrm{rad\,s^{-1}}$, $A=6.5\times10^{-6}\,\mathrm{m^2}$; for a small extension use $R\approx L=1.0\,\mathrm m$.
Step 2. $T=m(g+\omega^2L)=14.5[9.8+(4\pi)^2]=2.432\times10^3\,\mathrm N$.
Step 3. $\Delta L=TL/(AY)=1.87\times10^{-3}\,\mathrm m=1.87\,\mathrm{mm}$ with $Y=2.0\times10^{11}\,\mathrm{Pa}$.
Step 4. If the radius increase is included, substitute $R=L+\Delta L$: $\Delta L=\frac{mL(g+\omega^2L)}{AY-m\omega^2L}\approx1.874\,\mathrm{mm}$. The school approximation is consistent.
Ex 8.12bulk

Compute the bulk modulus of water from the following data: Initial volume = 100.0 litre, Pressure increase = 100.0 atm (1 atm = $1.013\times10^5\,\mathrm{Pa}$), Final volume = 100.5 litre. Compare the bulk modulus of water with that of air (at constant temperature). Explain in simple terms why the ratio is so large.

Prerequisite:Review the matching concept
The question is preserved as supplied. “Final volume = 100.5 litre” is inconsistent with a pressure increase: it would give a negative bulk modulus. For the intended compression calculation, use a volume decrease of 0.5 litre (final volume 99.5 litre).
Step 1. $\Delta p=100(1.013\times10^5)=1.013\times10^7\,\mathrm{Pa}$; $\Delta V/V=-0.5/100=-0.005$.
Step 2. $B=-\Delta p/(\Delta V/V)=2.026\times10^9\,\mathrm{Pa}$.
Step 3. For air at constant temperature, $pV=\text{constant}$ gives $dp/dV=-p/V$ and $B_{\rm air}=-V(dp/dV)=p$. At 1 atm, $B_{\rm air}=1.013\times10^5\,\mathrm{Pa}$. Thus $B_{\rm water}/B_{\rm air}=2.0\times10^4$.
Step 4. Liquid molecules are close together and more strongly coupled; gases contain much more empty space, so their volume decreases far more readily.
Ex 8.13bulk

What is the density of water at a depth where pressure is 80.0 atm, given that its density at the surface is $1.03\times10^3\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}$?

Prerequisite:Review the matching concept
Step 1. Use water’s $B=2.2\times10^9\,\mathrm{Pa}$ from Table 8.3 and a constant mass. Since $V=V_0(1-\Delta p/B)$, $\rho=\rho_0/(1-\Delta p/B)$.
Step 2. If the surface is at 1 atm, $\Delta p=(80-1)(1.013\times10^5)=8.0027\times10^6\,\mathrm{Pa}$. Then $\rho=1030/(1-8.0027\times10^6/2.2\times10^9)\approx1.034\times10^3\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}$.
Step 3. Taking 80 atm as the pressure increase instead also gives $1.034\times10^3\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}$ at the stated precision. This small-strain calculation treats B as constant.
Ex 8.14bulk

Compute the fractional change in volume of a glass slab, when subjected to a hydraulic pressure of 10 atm.

Prerequisite:Review the matching concept
Step 1. $B_{\rm glass}=37\times10^9\,\mathrm{Pa}$ and $\Delta p=10(1.013\times10^5)=1.013\times10^6\,\mathrm{Pa}$.
Step 2. $\Delta V/V=-\Delta p/B=-2.74\times10^{-5}$. The fractional contraction magnitude is $2.74\times10^{-5}$.
Ex 8.15bulk

Determine the volume contraction of a solid copper cube, 10 cm on an edge, when subjected to a hydraulic pressure of $7.0\times10^6\,\mathrm{Pa}$.

Prerequisite:Review the matching concept
Step 1. $V=(0.10)^3=1.0\times10^{-3}\,\mathrm{m^3}$; $B_c=140\times10^9\,\mathrm{Pa}$.
Step 2. $|\Delta V|=V\Delta p/B=5.0\times10^{-8}\,\mathrm{m^3}=0.050\,\mathrm{cm^3}$. Signed $\Delta V$ is negative.
Ex 8.16bulk

How much should the pressure on a litre of water be changed to compress it by 0.10%?

Prerequisite:Review the matching concept
Step 1. $|\Delta V|/V=0.10/100=1.0\times10^{-3}$. Use $B=2.2\times10^9\,\mathrm{Pa}$.
Step 2. Increase pressure by $\Delta p=B|\Delta V|/V=2.2\times10^6\,\mathrm{Pa}\approx21.7\,\mathrm{atm}$. One litre decreases by 1 mL.
04 / Rapid Reference

One-page formula and concept recap

All chapter themes are retained as short revision points; return to Learn for the proofs and worked examples.

8.1 · Elasticity, plasticity and rigid-body limits

Review concept and reasoning
  • Real solids deform. Elastic: recovery; plastic: permanent set. Applications balance stiffness, strength and weight.

8.2 · Stress and three types of strain

Review concept and reasoning
  • $\sigma=F/A$, $\varepsilon=\Delta L/L$, $\gamma=\Delta x/L\approx\theta$, $\varepsilon_V=\Delta V/V$. Stress: Pa; strain: no unit. Tension is F, not 2F. Stress is not a simple force vector.

8.3 · Hooke’s law and its domain

Review concept and reasoning
  • Stress ∝ strain only in the proportional region. Hooke’s law is empirical; elastic does not always mean linear.

8.4 · Stress–strain curve, strength and elastomers

Review concept and reasoning
  • OA: Hookean. AB: nonlinear elastic. B: yield/elastic limit. Beyond B: plastic set. D: ultimate strength. E: fracture. Nearby D/E: brittle; separated: ductile.
  • Elastomers: large recoverable nonlinear strain. Slope measures stiffness; maximum stress measures strength.

8.5.1 · Young’s modulus: axial extension and compression

Review concept and reasoning
  • $Y=FL/(A\Delta L)$; $\Delta L=FL/(AY)$; $k_{\rm wire}=YA/L$. Larger Y gives less strain at equal stress. Series wires: same tension, extensions add. Equal-length parallel wires: same extension.

8.5.2 · Shear modulus (modulus of rigidity)

Review concept and reasoning
  • $G=FL/(A\Delta x)\approx F/(A\theta)$. Use the correct sheared face and separation. Solids resist static shear. Helical coil wires twist. $G\approx Y/3$ is only an estimate.

8.5.3 · Bulk modulus and compressibility

Review concept and reasoning
  • $B=-\Delta p/(\Delta V/V)$; $\kappa=1/B$. Compression has negative signed volume strain. B applies to all states. Typical compressibility: gas > liquid > solid; gas values depend on p and T.

8.5.4 · Poisson’s ratio and lateral contraction

Review concept and reasoning
  • $\nu=-\varepsilon_\perp/\varepsilon_\parallel$ with signed strains; equivalently use positive contraction magnitude. Dimensionless; steel 0.28–0.30, aluminium alloys ≈0.33. Axial loading also changes lateral dimensions.

8.5.5 · Proof: elastic potential energy in a wire

Review concept and reasoning
  • $U=\frac12F\Delta L=YA(\Delta L)^2/(2L)$. $u=\frac12\sigma\varepsilon=\frac12Y\varepsilon^2=\sigma^2/(2Y)$. Integrate the growing force; do not use FΔL.

8.6 · Applications: crane ropes and safe loads

Review concept and reasoning
  • $A\ge Mg/\sigma_y$. Safety factor n multiplies area by n and radius by $\sqrt n$. Braided thin wires give flexibility. Yield strength prevents permanent deformation.

8.6 · Proof: beam bending and I-section design

Review concept and reasoning
  • $I=bd^3/12$ and $\delta=Wl^3/(48YI)=Wl^3/(4Ybd^3)$. Depth has a cubic effect. Deep narrow beams can buckle; I-sections and distributed column ends improve structural efficiency.

8.6 · Proof: maximum mountain height

Review concept and reasoning
  • Stress scale $\rho gh$; $h_{\max}\sim\tau_{\rm critical}/(\rho g)\sim10\,\mathrm{km}$ for typical terrestrial rock. Mountain loading is not uniform hydraulic compression.
Common traps: radius is half the diameter; cm² → m² requires ×10⁻⁴; GPa → Pa requires ×10⁹; strain is a fraction, not a percentage; signs and magnitude of compression differ. Exercise 8.12’s inconsistent volume is explicitly explained in its solution.
