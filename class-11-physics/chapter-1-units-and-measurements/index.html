Concept 01

Measurement & physical quantities

Measurement is comparison with an accepted reference standard called a unit. A result is a numerical measure accompanied by a unit.

number × unit
Exam cueBase quantities use base units; derived quantities use combinations of base units.
Concept 02

CGS, FPS and MKS systems

Earlier systems included CGS, FPS and MKS. The chapter follows SI for international measurement.

CGS: cm, g, s | FPS: ft, lb, s | MKS: m, kg, s
Exam cueChanging a unit changes the numerical value, not the physical quantity.

NCERT SECTION

The International System of Units

1. SI and seven base units2. Radian & steradian3. Modern SI definitions4. Derived units, special names & prefixes
Concept 03

SI and seven base units

SI is the internationally accepted system. It has seven base units.

m, kg, s, A, K, mol, cd
Exam cueRemember the seven quantity–unit pairs: length–metre, mass–kilogram, time–second, current–ampere, temperature–kelvin, amount–mole, luminous intensity–candela.
Concept 04

Radian & steradian

Plane angle is arc length divided by radius; solid angle is intercepted spherical area divided by radius squared.

θ = ds/r | Ω = dA/r²
Exam cueBoth radian and steradian are dimensionless.
Concept 05

Modern SI definitions

The chapter describes SI base units through fixed numerical values of constants and notes that these defining values need not be memorised for tests.

c = 299792458 m s⁻¹; h = 6.62607015×10⁻³⁴ J s; N_A = 6.02214076×10²³ mol⁻¹
Exam cueFocus on the principle of fixed constants and correct unit symbols.
Concept 06

Derived units, special names & prefixes

Derived units are combinations of base units. Some have special names; SI prefixes represent multiples and submultiples.

N = kg m s⁻² | J = kg m² s⁻²
Exam cueTreat units algebraically during calculations and cancel identical units.

Significant Figures

1. Significant figures: meaning2. Rules for significant figures3. Scientific notation & order of magnitude4. Exact numbers5. Arithmetic operations with significant figures6. Rounding off uncertain digits7. Uncertainty and error in arithmetic results
Concept 07

Significant figures: meaning

Significant figures are the reliable digits plus the first uncertain digit. They indicate measurement precision.

1.62 s → 3 significant figures
Exam cueChanging units does not change the number of significant figures.
Concept 08

Rules for significant figures

Non-zero digits are significant; zeros between non-zero digits are significant; leading zeros are not; trailing zeros without a decimal are not; trailing decimal zeros are significant.

2.308 → 4 s.f. | 0.02308 → 4 s.f. | 3.500 → 4 s.f.
Exam cueScientific notation removes ambiguity about trailing zeros.
Concept 09

Scientific notation & order of magnitude

Write a×10ᵇ with 1≤a&lt;10. The exponent gives the order of magnitude when an estimate is required.

1.28×10⁷ m → order 10⁷ m
Exam cueThe power of ten does not alter the significant-figure count.
Concept 10

Exact numbers

Exact factors such as 2 in 2πr and counting numbers are treated as having unlimited significant figures.

2, n → exact
Exam cueExact numbers do not limit the final significant figures.
Concept 11

Arithmetic operations with significant figures

For multiplication/division use the least number of significant figures. For addition/subtraction use the least number of decimal places.

4.237/2.51 = 1.69 | 436.32+227.2+0.301 = 663.8
Exam cue×/÷ → s.f.; +/− → decimal places.
Concept 12

Rounding off uncertain digits

The chapter gives rounding rules, including the even/odd convention when the dropped digit is exactly 5.

2.746→2.75; 1.743→1.74; 2.745→2.74; 2.735→2.74
Exam cueIn complex calculations retain one extra digit in intermediate steps and round at the end.
Concept 13

Uncertainty and error in arithmetic results

The chapter relates measurement precision to least count and illustrates absolute error, relative error and combination of errors.

l = 16.2±0.1 cm → ±0.6%
Exam cueMore reported digits do not create more accuracy.

Concept 14

Dimensions of physical quantities

Dimensions describe the nature of a physical quantity using powers of base quantities. In mechanics [M], [L], [T] are sufficient.

Volume [L³]; force [MLT⁻²]; density [ML⁻³]
Exam cueDimensions describe type/nature, not magnitude.

NCERT SECTION

Dimensional Formulae and Dimensional Equations

1. Dimensional formulae & dimensional equations2. Principle of homogeneity3. Dimensionless quantities & functions
Concept 15

Dimensional formulae & dimensional equations

A dimensional formula shows how base quantities combine. A dimensional equation represents a physical quantity in dimensional form.

[V]=[M⁰L³T⁰]; [v]=[M⁰LT⁻¹]; [F]=[MLT⁻²]
Exam cueKeep dimensional formula and dimensional equation conceptually distinct.
Concept 16

Principle of homogeneity

Only quantities having the same dimensions can be added or subtracted. Every term of a physical equation must have the same dimensions.

x=x₀+v₀t+½at² → every term [L]
Exam cueA dimensional mismatch proves an equation wrong.
Concept 17

Dimensionless quantities & functions

Arguments of trigonometric, logarithmic and exponential functions must be dimensionless. Ratios of like quantities are dimensionless.

angle = length/length; refractive index = c/v
Exam cueA pure number has no dimensions.

Concept 18

Applications of dimensional analysis

The chapter applies dimensions to checking equations, converting units and deducing relations between physical quantities.

Check → convert → derive
Exam cuePassing the dimensional test does not prove an equation exactly correct.
Concept 19

Deducing a relation: simple pendulum

Assuming T depends on l, m and g as a product, dimensional comparison gives the powers of the variables.

T = k lˣ gʸ mᶻ → x=½, y=−½, z=0
Exam cueDimensional analysis gives T=k√(l/g), but cannot give the dimensionless constant k=2π.

A dimensional check of a proposed physics equation can definitely show that the equation is:

If the dimensions on the two sides of a physical equation differ, the equation cannot be correct. If dimensions agree, the equation has passed a necessary test, but a dimensionless numerical factor or other functional detail can still be wrong.

Q18Pendulum
UNANSWERED

Suppose T = k lˣ gʸ mᶻ for a simple pendulum. Dimensional analysis gives which set of powers?

Pendulum by dimensional analysis

Assume T=k lˣgʸmᶻ. Dimensions are [T]=[L]ˣ[LT⁻²]ʸ[M]ᶻ = [M]ᶻ[L]ˣ⁺ʸ[T]⁻²ʸ. Equating powers gives z=0, −2y=1 and x+y=0.

Q19Pendulum
UNANSWERED

Why can dimensional analysis not determine the numerical constant 2π in the pendulum formula?

Limitation of dimensional analysis

The factor 2π carries no dimensions. Dimensional comparison can determine the powers of dimensional variables, but it cannot determine a pure numerical constant such as 2π.

KEY IDEAThe actual pendulum relation is T=2π√(l/g), where 2π must come from the dynamics, not dimensional analysis.

UNANSWERED

A dimensionally inconsistent equation is definitely wrong. A dimensionally consistent equation may still have the wrong numerical coefficient, sign, or functional form.

KEY IDEATherefore dimensional consistency is a necessary condition for a physically meaningful equation, not a complete proof of correctness.

1.8MEASUREMENT

Answer the following:

(a)
You are given a thread and a metre scale. How will you estimate the diameter of the thread?
(b)
A screw gauge has a pitch of 1.0 mm and 200 divisions on the circular scale. Do you think it is possible to increase the accuracy of the screw gauge arbitrarily by increasing the number of divisions on the circular scale?
(c)
The mean diameter of a thin brass rod is to be measured by vernier callipers. Why is a set of 100 measurements expected to yield a more reliable estimate than a set of 5 measurements only?
Prerequisite concept↗ Uncertainty and error in arithmetic results
Solution
(a) Diameter of a thread

Wind the thread tightly in adjacent turns over a measured length L. If N turns occupy that length, the average diameter is

d ≈ L/N

A large number of turns reduces the effect of reading error.

(b) Can accuracy be increased arbitrarily?

No. Increasing the number of circular-scale divisions reduces the scale least count, but systematic errors, backlash, zero error and other instrumental limitations remain. Therefore accuracy cannot be increased without limit.

(c) Why 100 observations are more reliable than 5

Random errors fluctuate from reading to reading. Taking many observations and averaging them makes positive and negative random deviations tend to cancel, giving a more reliable mean value.

1.15ATOMIC SCALE

One mole of an ideal gas at standard temperature and pressure occupies 22.4 L (molar volume). What is the ratio of molar volume to the atomic volume of a mole of hydrogen? (Take the size of hydrogen molecule to be about 1 Å). Why is this ratio so large?

Prerequisite concept↗ Derived units, special names & prefixes
Solution
Given
Molar volume of ideal gas = 22.4 L = 2.24×10⁻² m³
Size of H₂ molecule ≈ 1 Å = 10⁻¹⁰ m
Atomic/molecular volume scale
For a characteristic size 1 Å, volume ≈ 4/3π(0.5×10⁻¹⁰)³ m³ per molecule
Multiplying by NA gives the molecular volume of one mole.
Step 3 • Ratio of molar volume to molecular volume
Vmol ≈ 3.93×10⁻⁸ m³ for the atomic-size estimate
Ratio = (2.24×10⁻²)/(3.93×10⁻⁸) ≈ 5.7×10⁵
Why is the ratio so large?

The gas molecules are extremely small compared with the macroscopic volume occupied by the gas. Most of the volume of an ideal gas is empty space between molecules.

1.16RELATIVE MOTION

Explain this common observation clearly: If you look out of the window of a fast moving train, nearby trees, houses etc. seem to move rapidly in a direction opposite to the train’s motion, but distant objects (hill tops, the Moon, the stars etc.) seem to be stationary.

Prerequisite concept↗ Measurement & physical quantities
Explanation

The observation is a consequence of relative motion and parallax. During the same displacement of the train, the line of sight to a nearby object changes by a much larger angle than the line of sight to a distant object.

Nearby object → large apparent angular shift → appears to move rapidly backward
Distant object → very small angular shift → appears almost stationary
Key ideaApparent motion depends on the observer's frame and the object's distance.
1.17DENSITY

The Sun is a hot plasma (ionized matter) with its inner core at a temperature exceeding 10⁷ K, and its outer surface at about 6000 K. In what range do you expect the mass density of the Sun, in the range of densities of solids and liquids or gases? Check using mass = 2.0×10³⁰ kg and radius = 7.0×10⁸ m.

Prerequisite concept↗ Derived units, special names & prefixes
Solution
Given
M = 2.0×10³⁰ kg,   R = 7.0×10⁸ m
Volume of the Sun
V = 4/3 πR³
= 4/3π(7.0×10⁸)³ m³
Average density
ρ = M/V = 3M/(4πR³)
≈ 1.4×10³ kg m⁻³
ConclusionThe average density is of the order of 10³ kg m⁻³, comparable to the density range of ordinary liquids rather than gases.

Significant-figure traps

  • Leading zeros are not significant.
  • Zeros between non-zero digits are significant.
  • Trailing zeros after a decimal are significant.
  • ×/÷ → least significant figures.
  • +/− → least decimal places.
  • Round at the end of a multi-step calculation.

Dimensional traps

  • Only same dimensions can be added/subtracted.
  • Arguments of sin, cos, log and exponential functions must be dimensionless.
  • Dimensionally correct ≠ necessarily physically correct.
  • Dimensions cannot determine pure numerical constants such as 2π.
