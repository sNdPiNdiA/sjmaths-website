13.1–13.2

Periodic and oscillatory motion

A motion can repeat without moving to and fro; oscillation is a narrower idea.

  • Periodic motion repeats after a least interval T; the state repeats at t+nT for integer n. Frequency ν=1/T is cycles per second (hertz), and angular frequency ω=2πν=2π/T.
  • Oscillatory motion is to-and-fro about an equilibrium position. Every oscillatory motion is periodic in the ideal case, but circular motion can be periodic without being oscillatory. Damping makes free oscillations die away; external periodic forcing can sustain them.
  • “Oscillation” and “vibration” are commonly used for lower- and higher-frequency to-and-fro motion; the distinction is conventional. A medium’s coupled oscillators transmit disturbances as waves.
  • Displacement may be position, angle, voltage, pressure or another variable that changes periodically. A periodic signal need not be a displacement in space.
NCERT Example 13.1

Heartbeat frequency

Question. A healthy human heart beats 75 times in a minute. What are its frequency and period?

  1. Step 1. Frequency is beats per second: ν=75/60=1.25 Hz.
  2. Step 2. The period is the reciprocal: T=1/ν.

Answer: ν=1.25 Hz and T=0.80 s.

13.2–13.3

Simple harmonic motion and phase

SHM is the special periodic motion with sinusoidal displacement or a linear restoring force.

  • Definition: x(t)=A cos(ωt+φ), where A≥0 is amplitude, ω is angular frequency and φ is the phase constant. The phase at time t is ωt+φ.
  • T=2π/ω and ν=ω/(2π). The phase sets the starting state; given ω, position and velocity at one instant determine A and φ.
  • Equivalent forms are x=A cos ωt+B sin ωt, x=A cos(ωt+α), or x=B sin(ωt+β). For A cosωt+B sinωt, amplitude is √(A²+B²); phase follows by matching coefficients.
  • A periodic function may be expanded as a sum of harmonics (Fourier series); the sum of two arbitrary SHMs is not necessarily periodic. Commensurate frequencies (their ratio rational) give a common repeat period.
  • Example 13.2: sinωt+cosωt=√2 sin(ωt+π/4) has period 2π/ω; sinωt+cos2ωt+sin4ωt repeats after 2π/ω. e^(−ωt) and log(ωt) are not periodic for t>0.
  • Example 13.3: sinωt−cosωt=√2 sin(ωt−π/4), so SHM with period 2π/ω. sin²ωt=(1−cos2ωt)/2 is periodic with period π/ω, but is not SHM about zero (its mean is 1/2).
NCERT Example 13.2

Testing periodic functions

Question. Which are periodic: (a) sinωt+cosωt, (b) sinωt+cos2ωt+sin4ωt, (c) e^(−ωt), (d) log(ωt)?

  1. Step 1. (a) Both terms have angular frequency ω, so their sum repeats after 2π/ω.
  2. Step 2. (b) The component periods are 2π/ω, π/ω and π/(2ω); the least common period is 2π/ω.
  3. Step 3. (c) The exponential decays and does not repeat. (d) log(ωt) is undefined for t≤0 and is monotonic for t>0.

Answer: (a), (b) are periodic with T=2π/ω; (c), (d) are not.

NCERT Example 13.3

SHM or periodic motion?

Question. Classify (a) sinωt−cosωt and (b) sin²ωt; give each period.

  1. Step 1. (a) Rewrite as √2 sin(ωt−π/4), a single sinusoid about zero.
  2. Step 2. (b) Use sin²ωt=(1−cos2ωt)/2; it oscillates about 1/2, not zero.

Answer: (a) SHM, T=2π/ω. (b) Periodic but not SHM about zero, T=π/ω.

13.4

SHM as a projection of uniform circular motion

The shadow of a uniformly rotating point on a diameter follows SHM.

  • Let a point P rotate on a circle of radius A with angular speed ω and initial angle φ. Its coordinates are x=A cos(ωt+φ), y=A sin(ωt+φ); each projection is SHM, with the two projections π/2 out of phase.
  • This is a kinematic correspondence: the projected particle’s restoring force is not the centripetal force acting on P. In two dimensions the restoring law F=−mω²r does produce circular motion when the perpendicular components have equal amplitude and differ in phase by π/2.
  • Example 13.4(a): T=4 s and initial angle π/4 anticlockwise gives x=A cos(πt/2+π/4). (b) T=30 s from the top clockwise gives x=B sin(πt/15)=B cos(πt/15−π/2).
NCERT Example 13.4

Projection of circular motion

Question. Find x(t) for (a) radius A, T=4 s, initial angle 45° anticlockwise; (b) radius B, T=30 s, initial angle 90° clockwise.

  1. Step 1. Use x=R cos(φ+ωt), with ω=2π/T and signed angular advance.
  2. Step 2. (a) ω=π/2 and φ=π/4. (b) angle=π/2−πt/15; apply cos(π/2−u)=sin u.

Answer: (a) x=A cos(πt/2+π/4). (b) x=B sin(πt/15)=B cos(πt/15−π/2).

13.5

Velocity and acceleration in SHM

Differentiate displacement to obtain the instantaneous velocity and acceleration.

  • For x=A cos(ωt+φ), v=dx/dt=−ωA sin(ωt+φ), so vmax=ωA. Speed is greatest at x=0; velocity is zero at x=±A.
  • a=dv/dt=−ω²A cos(ωt+φ)=−ω²x, so amax=ω²A. Acceleration always points toward equilibrium.
  • x, v and a have the same period T. Velocity is shifted by π/2 relative to displacement; acceleration is opposite in phase (π).
  • Example 13.5: x=5 cos(2πt+π/4) m at t=1.5 s gives x=−3.535 m; v=−10π sin(13π/4)=+22.2 m s⁻¹; a=−4π²x≈+140 m s⁻².
NCERT Example 13.5

Position, speed and acceleration

Question. For x=5 cos(2πt+π/4) m, find displacement, velocity and acceleration at t=1.5 s.

  1. Step 1. The phase is 2π(1.5)+π/4=13π/4; cos(13π/4)=−√2/2.
  2. Step 2. v=−10π sin(13π/4)=10π/√2.
  3. Step 3. a=−(2π)²x.

Answer: x=−3.54 m; v≈+22.2 m s⁻¹ (speed 22.2 m s⁻¹); a≈+140 m s⁻².

13.6

Restoring force and spring oscillators

A force proportional to displacement and directed toward equilibrium produces SHM.

  • Newton’s second law gives F=ma=−mω²x. Writing k=mω² gives Hooke’s restoring law F=−kx; therefore ω=√(k/m) and T=2π√(m/k). This is a linear oscillator.
  • For two identical springs k attached on opposite sides of a block, each exerts −kx after displacement x; net force is −2kx. Thus ω=√(2k/m) and T=2π√(m/(2k)).
  • Example 13.6: the two-spring arrangement has combined restoring force −2kx; identify the effective spring constant 2k and apply the standard period formula.
  • Forces need not be exactly linear far from equilibrium. Expanding a restoring force may include higher powers (x², x³, …); if the displacement is small, the linear term often dominates.
NCERT Example 13.6

Block between two springs

Question. Two identical springs, each of force constant k, are fixed on opposite sides of a block of mass m. Find the period.

  1. Step 1. A displacement x makes both spring forces point toward equilibrium; their sum is F=−kx−kx=−2kx.
  2. Step 2. Thus k_eff=2k and mω²=2k.

Answer: T=2π√(m/(2k)).

13.7

Energy in SHM

Without friction, energy shifts between kinetic and potential forms while total mechanical energy stays fixed.

  • With x=A cos(ωt+φ), v=−ωA sin(ωt+φ), and k=mω²: U=½kx²=½kA²cos²(ωt+φ); K=½mv²=½kA²sin²(ωt+φ).
  • Adding and using sin²θ+cos²θ=1 proves E=K+U=½kA², independent of time. This is conservation of mechanical energy. K and U are non-negative and each repeats after T/2.
  • At equilibrium x=0, U=0 and K=E. At x=±A, v=0, K=0 and U=E. At any intermediate position, K=½k(A²−x²).
  • Example 13.7: m=1 kg, k=50 N m⁻¹, A=0.10 m and x=0.05 m. E=½kA²=0.25 J; U=½kx²=0.0625 J; K=E−U=0.1875 J.
NCERT Example 13.7

Kinetic and potential energy

Question. A 1 kg oscillator has k=50 N m⁻¹ and amplitude 0.10 m. Find K and U at x=0.05 m.

  1. Step 1. Total energy E=½kA²=½(50)(0.10)²=0.25 J.
  2. Step 2. Potential energy U=½kx²=½(50)(0.05)²=0.0625 J.
  3. Step 3. K=E−U.

Answer: K=0.1875 J≈0.19 J; U=0.0625 J; E=0.25 J.

13.8

The simple pendulum

A simple pendulum is approximately an angular harmonic oscillator only for small swings.

  • For a bob of mass m on a light inextensible string of length L, gravity’s tangential component gives restoring torque τ=−mgL sinθ. The radial tension/gravity components produce no torque about the pivot.
  • Rotational Newton’s law Iα=τ gives α=−(mgL/I)sinθ. For small θ in radians, sinθ≈θ, so α=−(mgL/I)θ: this is angular SHM with ω=√(mgL/I).
  • For a point bob, I=mL²; hence ω=√(g/L), T=2π√(L/g). The period is independent of bob mass and (approximately) amplitude. The approximation is good for small angles; at large amplitudes the motion is not exactly SHM.
  • Example 13.8: a seconds-ticking pendulum has T=2 s. L=gT²/(4π²)=9.8×4/(4π²)≈1.0 m.
NCERT Example 13.8

Length of a seconds pendulum

Question. What is the length of a simple pendulum that ticks seconds?

  1. Step 1. “Ticks seconds” means a complete oscillation takes T=2 s.
  2. Step 2. From T=2π√(L/g), L=gT²/(4π²).
  3. Step 3. With g=9.8 m s⁻², L=9.8×4/(4π²).

Answer: L≈0.993 m, about 1.0 m.

13

Points to ponder

  1. The least repeating time is T; motion repeats after integer multiples nT.
  2. Not every periodic motion is SHM: SHM requires restoring force proportional to displacement and opposite in direction.
  3. A two-dimensional force −mω²r produces uniform circular motion for equal perpendicular amplitudes and a π/2 phase difference.
  4. For a fixed ω, two initial conditions determine SHM: x and v, or amplitude and phase, or energy and phase.
  5. Given amplitude/energy, the initial position and velocity determine the phase.
  6. Two SHMs with arbitrary amplitudes and phases need not sum to periodic motion; a common period exists when frequency ratios are rational. A periodic function can be represented as a Fourier sum.
  7. The ideal SHM period does not depend on amplitude, energy or phase.
  8. A simple pendulum is SHM only at small angular displacement.
  9. x=A cosωt+B sinωt, x=A cos(ωt+α), and x=B sin(ωt+β) are equivalent forms. Damped motion is only approximately SHM for intervals much shorter than 2m/b.

13.1Which of the following examples represent periodic motion? (a) A swimmer completing one (return) trip from one bank of a river to the other and back. (b) A freely suspended bar magnet displaced from its N-S direction and released. (c) A hydrogen molecule rotating about its centre of mass. (d) An arrow released from a bow.

Ex 13.2NCERT

13.2Which examples represent (nearly) simple harmonic motion and which represent periodic but not simple harmonic motion? (a) rotation of Earth about its axis; (b) an oscillating mercury column in a U-tube; (c) a ball bearing in a smooth curved bowl released slightly above its lowest point; (d) general vibrations of a polyatomic molecule about equilibrium.

Related concept: Review the matching notes

  4. Step 4. General polyatomic vibrations can combine modes and need not be a single SHM; near equilibrium, normal modes are harmonic in the small-amplitude approximation.

Answer: (a) periodic, not SHM; (b), (c) nearly SHM; (d) generally a combination of normal-mode oscillations (periodic only for commensurate mode frequencies).

  1. Step 1. Let y be the upward displacement of one side from equilibrium; the other side moves down by y, so the level difference is 2y.
  2. Step 2. The pressure imbalance gives restoring force F=−2ρAg y. If total moving liquid length is L, its mass is ρAL.
  3. Step 3. Thus y¨=−(2g/L)y, the SHM equation.

Answer: The motion is SHM, with ω=√(2g/L) and T=2π√(L/(2g)); L is the total length of the moving mercury column.

POINT-WISE SUMMARY

Revision

Definitions, equations, assumptions and key results by concept.

13.1–13.2 · Periodic and oscillatory motion

Review notes
  • Periodic means repeats after least T; ν=1/T and ω=2π/T.
  • Oscillatory means to-and-fro about equilibrium; periodic does not always mean oscillatory.
  • Damping reduces free-motion amplitude; forcing can sustain oscillation.

13.2–13.3 · Simple harmonic motion and phase

Review notes
  • SHM: x=A cos(ωt+φ), phase=ωt+φ, T=2π/ω.
  • Equivalent sine/cosine forms encode the same motion; A=√(C²+D²) for C cosωt+D sinωt.
  • Initial position plus velocity determine phase and amplitude. SHM period does not depend on amplitude or phase.
  • A sum of periodic harmonics has a common period when the frequency ratios are rational.

13.4 · SHM as a projection of uniform circular motion

Review notes
  • x=A cos(ωt+φ) is the x-projection of a uniform rotating radius.
  • The perpendicular projections have equal amplitude and phase difference π/2.
  • Linear SHM and circular motion are related kinematically; their forces differ.

13.5 · Velocity and acceleration in SHM

Review notes
  • v=−ωA sin(ωt+φ); vmax=ωA.
  • a=−ω²x; amax=ω²A and acceleration points to equilibrium.
  • At the centre: |v| maximum and a=0. At an extreme: v=0 and |a| maximum.

13.6 · Restoring force and spring oscillators

Review notes
  • SHM requires a=−ω²x (equivalently F=−kx).
  • Spring: ω=√(k/m), T=2π√(m/k); two identical springs in parallel: k_eff=2k.
  • Nonlinear corrections can matter at large amplitudes.

13.7 · Energy in SHM

Review notes
  • U=½kx²; K=½mv²=½k(A²−x²).
  • E=K+U=½kA² is constant without friction.
  • K and U trade energy and have period T/2.

13.8 · The simple pendulum

Review notes
  • τ=−mgL sinθ; with sinθ≈θ, α=−(g/L)θ.
  • Point bob: T=2π√(L/g), independent of mass.
  • Small-angle assumption uses radians. A seconds pendulum has period 2 s and length about 1 m.
