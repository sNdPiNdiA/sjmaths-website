1. Foundations of Probability & Axioms

Probability measures the likelihood of occurrence of an event. In competitive teacher examinations, both classical (theoretical) and experimental (empirical) approaches are evaluated.

A. Definitions

  • Random Experiment: An operation whose outcomes cannot be predicted with certainty in advance, but all possible outcomes are known (e.g., tossing a coin, rolling a die).
  • Sample Space ($S$): The set of all possible outcomes. Its cardinality is denoted by $n(S)$.
  • Event ($E$): Any subset of the sample space: $E \subseteq S$.
  • Classical Probability Formula: $$P(E) = \frac{n(E)}{n(S)} = \frac{\text{Number of favorable outcomes}}{\text{Total number of equally likely outcomes}}$$

B. Fundamental Axioms & Properties

  • Range Bound: For any arbitrary event $E$, $$0 \le P(E) \le 1$$
  • Impossible Event ($\phi$): An event that can never occur: $P(\phi) = 0$.
  • Sure / Certain Event ($S$): An event that is guaranteed to occur: $P(S) = 1$.
  • Complementary Event ($\bar{E}$ or $E'$): The event "not $E$": $$P(\bar{E}) = 1 - P(E) \iff P(E) + P(\bar{E}) = 1$$
  • Sum of Elementary Probabilities: The sum of probabilities of all elementary events in a sample space is strictly $1$: $$\sum_{i=1}^k P(e_i) = 1$$
  • Addition Theorem (General): $$P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \cap B)$$
  • Mutually Exclusive Events: If $A$ and $B$ cannot happen together ($A \cap B = \phi$): $$P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B)$$

2. Coin Tossing & Two-Dice Sum Speed Hacks

Two-Dice Sum Distribution Pyramid (Total 36 Outcomes)
Symmetric Peak Theorem: Sum 7 has the highest probability $\frac{6}{36} = \frac{1}{6}$.
Shortcut: Ways for sum $s \le 7$ is $(s - 1)$, while for $s \ge 8$ is $(13 - s)$.

A. Coin Tossing Archetypes

  • $1$ coin: $\{H, T\} \implies n(S) = 2$
  • $2$ coins: $\{HH, HT, TH, TT\} \implies n(S) = 4$
  • $3$ coins: $n(S) = 2^3 = 8$
    $S = \{HHH, HHT, HTH, THH, HTT, THT, TTH, TTT\}$
  • At least vs At most:
    • "At least 1 Head" in 3 tosses: $1 - P(\text{no heads}) = 1 - P(TTT) = 1 - \frac{1}{8} = \frac{7}{8}$.
    • "At most 2 Heads" in 3 tosses: $1 - P(HHH) = 1 - \frac{1}{8} = \frac{7}{8}$.

3. Playing Cards (52 Deck) & Calendar Probability

A. Anatomy of a Standard 52-Card Deck

Category Suits / Composition Total Cards
Red Cards Hearts (13) + Diamonds (13) 26 cards
Black Cards Spades (13) + Clubs (13) 26 cards
Face (Court) Cards 4 Kings, 4 Queens, 4 Jacks 12 cards (6 Red, 6 Black)
Honor Cards Aces (4) + Kings (4) + Queens (4) + Jacks (4) 16 cards
Numbered Cards Numbers 2 through 10 in all 4 suits $9 \times 4 =$ 36 cards
Crucial Card Distinction:
An Ace is NOT a face card! There are strictly 12 face cards in a pack of 52 playing cards. Counting Aces as face cards is the most frequent blunder in Super TET and UP recruitment tests.

B. Calendar & 53 Sundays Problems

  • Ordinary (Non-Leap) Year: 365 days $= 52 \text{ weeks} + 1 \text{ odd day}$. $$\text{Probability of 53 Sundays} = \frac{1}{7}$$
  • Leap Year: 366 days $= 52 \text{ weeks} + 2 \text{ odd days}$.
    The 2 consecutive odd days can be: $$(Sun, Mon), (Mon, Tue), (Tue, Wed), (Wed, Thu), (Thu, Fri), (Fri, Sat), (Sat, Sun)$$ Total pairs $= 7$. Pairs containing Sunday $= (Sun, Mon)$ and $(Sat, Sun) \implies 2$ pairs. $$\text{Probability of 53 Sundays in a leap year} = \frac{2}{7}$$
  • Probability of 53 Sundays OR 53 Mondays in a Leap Year: Favorable pairs $= (Sat, Sun), (Sun, Mon), (Mon, Tue) \implies 3$ pairs. $$P = \frac{3}{7}$$

4. Compound Bar Charts & Contingency Probability

In modern exams, probability questions are increasingly linked with Compound (Grouped / Double) Bar Graphs and two-way tables.

  • Joint Probability: Reading both category dimensions simultaneously from the bar heights.
  • Marginal Probability: Summing across rows or columns to find the overall category probability.
  • Odds in Favor vs Odds Against: If the odds in favor of an event $E$ are $a : b$, then: $$P(E) = \frac{a}{a+b}, \quad P(\bar{E}) = \frac{b}{a+b}$$ If the odds against $E$ are $x : y$, then $P(E) = \frac{y}{x+y}$.

Top-Rank Examination Archetype MCQs

Ten exhaustive questions covering every archetype tested in UP Upper Primary Teacher (Classes 6–8) recruitment examinations, equipped with dual solutions and examiner trap warnings.

Type 1 • Axiomatic Bounds & Complement Foundational / High Frequency
Q1. If the probability of winning a game is $0.07$, what is the probability of losing the game?
Standard Method:

Winning and losing are complementary events.
$$P(\text{Losing}) = 1 - P(\text{Winning})$$ $$P(\text{Losing}) = 1 - 0.07 = 0.93$$

Speed Hack:
$100 - 7 = 93 \implies 0.93$. Done in 1 second!
Examiner Trap:
Do not confuse $0.07$ with $0.7$. If $P = 0.7$, complement is $0.3$. For $0.07$, complement is $0.93$!
Type 2 • Three Coins Toss Combinatorics Core Archetype
Q2. Three unbiased coins are tossed together. What is the probability of getting at least two heads?
Standard Method:

Total outcomes $n(S) = 2^3 = 8$.
Sample space $S = \{HHH, HHT, HTH, THH, HTT, THT, TTH, TTT\}$.
"At least two heads" means either 2 heads or 3 heads:
Favorable outcomes $= \{HHT, HTH, THH, HHH\} \implies n(E) = 4$.
$$P(E) = \frac{n(E)}{n(S)} = \frac{4}{8} = \frac{1}{2}$$

Speed Hack (Symmetry):
By symmetry, exactly half the outcomes have more heads than tails ($2$ or $3$ heads), and half have more tails ($2$ or $3$ tails).
Therefore $P = \frac{1}{2}$ instantly!
Examiner Trap:
"At least two heads" includes 3 heads! If you only count exactly 2 heads, you get $\frac{3}{8}$ (Option A), which is incorrect.
Type 3 • Two Dice Prime Sum Hack Exam Classic
Q3. Two fair dice are thrown simultaneously. What is the probability that the sum of the numbers appearing on top is a prime number?
Standard Method:

Total outcomes $n(S) = 36$.
Prime numbers between 2 and 12 are: $\{2, 3, 5, 7, 11\}$.
Count favorable pairs for each prime sum:
• $\text{Sum } 2: (1,1) \implies 1 \text{ way}$
• $\text{Sum } 3: (1,2), (2,1) \implies 2 \text{ ways}$
• $\text{Sum } 5: (1,4), (2,3), (3,2), (4,1) \implies 4 \text{ ways}$
• $\text{Sum } 7: (1,6), (2,5), (3,4), (4,3), (5,2), (6,1) \implies 6 \text{ ways}$
• $\text{Sum } 11: (5,6), (6,5) \implies 2 \text{ ways}$
Total favorable ways $= 1 + 2 + 4 + 6 + 2 = 15$.
$$P(\text{Prime Sum}) = \frac{15}{36} = \frac{5}{12}$$

Speed Hack (Formula Sum):
Use our mirror formula:
Sum 2 $\implies 2-1 = 1$
Sum 3 $\implies 3-1 = 2$
Sum 5 $\implies 5-1 = 4$
Sum 7 $\implies 7-1 = 6$
Sum 11 $\implies 13-11 = 2$
Total $= 1+2+4+6+2 = 15 \implies \frac{15}{36} = \frac{5}{12}$. Solved without writing pairs!
Examiner Trap:
Remember: 1 is NOT a prime number, and 9 is NOT prime ($9 = 3 \times 3$). The only primes in range $2-12$ are $2, 3, 5, 7, 11$.
Type 4 • Playing Cards "Neither Nor" Standard Archetype
Q4. One card is drawn at random from a well-shuffled pack of 52 playing cards. What is the probability that the card drawn is neither a Red card nor a Queen?
Standard Method:

Total cards $n(S) = 52$.
Let $A =$ event of drawing a Red card $\implies n(A) = 26$.
Let $B =$ event of drawing a Queen $\implies n(B) = 4$.
Red Queens $= A \cap B \implies n(A \cap B) = 2$ (Queen of Hearts and Queen of Diamonds).
By the addition rule: $$n(A \cup B) = n(A) + n(B) - n(A \cap B) = 26 + 4 - 2 = 28$$ We require "neither Red nor Queen" $= (A \cup B)'$: $$n(\text{Favorable}) = 52 - 28 = 24$$ $$P = \frac{24}{52} = \frac{6}{13}$$

Speed Hack (Direct Black Non-Queens):
"Neither Red nor Queen" means the card MUST be Black and NOT a Queen.
Total black cards $= 26$.
Black Queens $= 2$ (Spades and Clubs).
Remaining black cards $= 26 - 2 = 24$.
$P = \frac{24}{52} = \frac{6}{13}$ directly in 3 seconds!
Examiner Trap:
If you do $52 - (26 + 4) = 22$, you subtracted the 2 red queens TWICE! Always remember $n(A \cup B) = 26 + 4 - 2 = 28$.
Type 5 • Leap Year Probability Recruitment Favorite
Q5. What is the probability that a leap year selected at random contains 53 Sundays?
Standard Method:

A leap year has $366$ days.
$366 \div 7 = 52$ complete weeks $+ 2$ extra days.
52 complete weeks guarantee exactly 52 Sundays.
The 53rd Sunday depends on the 2 extra consecutive days.
Possible pairs of extra days (Sample Space):
$S = \{(Sun, Mon), (Mon, Tue), (Tue, Wed), (Wed, Thu), (Thu, Fri), (Fri, Sat), (Sat, Sun)\}$.
Total possible pairs $n(S) = 7$.
Pairs containing Sunday $= \{(Sat, Sun), (Sun, Mon)\} \implies n(E) = 2$.
$$P(53 \text{ Sundays}) = \frac{2}{7}$$

Speed Hack:
Formula: $P(53 \text{ of any day}) = \frac{\text{Number of odd days}}{7}$.
For leap year, odd days $= 2 \implies \frac{2}{7}$.
For normal year, odd days $= 1 \implies \frac{1}{7}$.
Examiner Trap:
Do not select $\frac{53}{366}$! Days are grouped into 7-day cyclical weeks, so the denominator is strictly 7, not 366.
Type 6 • Range Divisibility & Union Moderate
Q6. A card is drawn at random from a box containing cards numbered 1 to 100. What is the probability that the number on the card is divisible by 4 or 6?
Standard Method:

Sample space $n(S) = 100$.
Multiples of 4 up to 100: $\lfloor \frac{100}{4} \rfloor = 25$.
Multiples of 6 up to 100: $\lfloor \frac{100}{6} \rfloor = 16$.
Common multiples are multiples of $\text{LCM}(4, 6) = 12$:
$\lfloor \frac{100}{12} \rfloor = 8$.
By Principle of Inclusion-Exclusion: $$n(\text{Divisible by 4 or 6}) = 25 + 16 - 8 = 33$$ $$P = \frac{33}{100}$$

Speed Hack:
$25 + 16 - 8 = 33 \implies \frac{33}{100}$. Solved in under 6 seconds!
Examiner Trap:
Never multiply $4 \times 6 = 24$ to find common multiples! The common multiples are governed by the $\text{LCM}(4,6) = 12$, NOT the product 24.
Type 7 • Complementary Logic in Urn Selection Moderate
Q7. A bag contains 4 white, 5 red, and 6 green balls. Three balls are drawn at random one after another without replacement. What is the probability that at least one green ball is drawn?
Standard Method:

Total balls $= 4 + 5 + 6 = 15$.
Non-green balls $= 4 + 5 = 9$.
Using complement rule: $$P(\text{At least 1 Green}) = 1 - P(\text{No Green ball in 3 draws})$$ $$P(\text{No Green}) = \frac{9}{15} \times \frac{8}{14} \times \frac{7}{13}$$ Simplifying: $$\frac{3}{5} \times \frac{4}{7} \times \frac{7}{13} = \frac{3 \times 4}{5 \times 13} = \frac{12}{65}$$ Therefore: $$P(\text{At least 1 Green}) = 1 - \frac{12}{65} = \frac{65 - 12}{65} = \frac{53}{65}$$

Speed Hack:
"At least one" ALWAYS triggers $1 - P(\text{None})$.
$P(\text{None}) = \frac{9 \times 8 \times 7}{15 \times 14 \times 13} = \frac{12}{65} \implies \text{Ans} = 1 - \frac{12}{65} = \frac{53}{65}$.
Examiner Trap:
Calculating cases: (1 Green, 2 Others) + (2 Green, 1 Other) + (3 Green) takes 4 times longer and invites arithmetic errors. Always use the complement!
Type 8 • Addition Theorem with Given Probabilities Standard Archetype
Q8. For two events $A$ and $B$, if $P(A) = 0.6$, $P(B) = 0.5$, and $P(A \cap B) = 0.3$, then the value of $P(\bar{A} \cap \bar{B})$ is:
Standard Method:

First, calculate $P(A \cup B)$ using the addition theorem: $$P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \cap B)$$ $$P(A \cup B) = 0.6 + 0.5 - 0.3 = 0.8$$ By De Morgan's Law: $$\bar{A} \cap \bar{B} = \overline{A \cup B}$$ $$P(\bar{A} \cap \bar{B}) = 1 - P(A \cup B) = 1 - 0.8 = 0.20$$

Speed Hack:
Union $= 0.6 + 0.5 - 0.3 = 0.8$.
Neither $= 1 - 0.8 = 0.20$. Done in 4 seconds!
Examiner Trap:
Do not multiply $P(\bar{A}) \times P(\bar{B}) = (0.4)(0.5) = 0.20$ unless independence is given! Here it happens to coincide numerically, but the correct mathematical justification is De Morgan's Law $1 - P(A \cup B)$.
Type 9 • Compound Bar Graph Probability Data Interpretation
Q9. In a school, a compound bar graph displays the transport choices of 500 students:

If a student is chosen at random, what is the probability that the student is a girl who walks to school?
Standard Method:

Total students $n(S) = 500$.
Number of girls who walk to school $n(E) = 55$.
$$P(E) = \frac{n(E)}{n(S)} = \frac{55}{500} = \frac{11}{100} = 0.11$$

Speed Hack:
$\frac{55}{500} = \frac{55 \times 2}{1000} = \frac{110}{1000} = \frac{11}{100}$. Solved mentally in 2 seconds!

Notice the wording: "chosen from all students", so denominator is $500$. If the question said "chosen from girls who walk", the denominator would be different.
Type 10 • Odds in Favor and Odds Against Advanced Concept
Q10. If the odds in favor of candidate X clearing the teacher recruitment examination are $3 : 7$, what is the probability that candidate X will FAIL the examination?
Standard Method:

By definition, if odds in favor of event $E$ are $a : b$:
• Number of favorable outcomes $= a = 3$
• Number of unfavorable outcomes $= b = 7$
• Total outcomes $= a + b = 3 + 7 = 10$
$$P(\text{Clearing}) = \frac{a}{a+b} = \frac{3}{10}$$ Failing is the unfavorable event:
$$P(\text{Failing}) = 1 - \frac{3}{10} = \frac{7}{10}$$

Speed Hack:
Odds in favor $= 3 : 7 \implies$ Fails $7$ times out of every $10$ attempts.
$P(\text{Failing}) = \frac{7}{3+7} = \frac{7}{10}$. Direct 2-second answer!
Examiner Trap:
Many examinees write probability as $\frac{3}{7}$ or $\frac{7}{3}$. Odds are ratio of favorable to unfavorable ($a:b$), whereas probability is ratio of favorable to TOTAL ($a : a+b$).
