1. Data Classification & Frequency Distribution

In statistical analysis, raw observations must be organized into systematic tables to compute central tendencies efficiently. Understanding the distinction between discrete and continuous distributions is essential for competitive examinations.

A. Types of Statistical Series

  • Individual Series: Raw observations listed individually: $x_1, x_2, \dots, x_n$ (frequencies are each 1).
  • Discrete Frequency Distribution: Distinct numerical values associated with frequencies: $(x_i, f_i)$.
  • Continuous (Grouped) Frequency Distribution: Data categorized into class intervals (e.g., $10-20, 20-30$).

B. Inclusive vs. Exclusive Class Intervals

Parameter Exclusive Series (Continuous) Inclusive Series (Discontinuous)
Structure $10-20, 20-30, 30-40$ $10-19, 20-29, 30-39$
Boundary Inclusion Upper limit is excluded; belongs to the next class Both upper and lower limits are included
Adjustment Factor None needed (already continuous) $\text{Correction Factor} = \frac{20 - 19}{2} = 0.5$. Subtract $0.5$ from lower limit, add $0.5$ to upper limit: $9.5-19.5, 19.5-29.5$.
Critical Golden Rule for Grouped Median & Mode: Never apply median ($M$) or mode ($M_o$) continuous formulas to an inclusive distribution directly! You must first convert it into an exclusive series by subtracting $0.5$ from the lower limits and adding $0.5$ to the upper limits.

C. Essential Terminology

  • Class Mark / Mid-Point ($x_i$): $x_i = \frac{\text{Upper Limit} + \text{Lower Limit}}{2}$
  • Class Size / Class Width ($h$): $h = \text{Upper Limit} - \text{Lower Limit}$ (in continuous series).
  • Cumulative Frequency ($cf$):
    • Less than type: Cumulative sum of frequencies corresponding to upper class limits.
    • More than type: Cumulative sum of frequencies corresponding to lower class limits.

2. Arithmetic Mean ($\bar{x}$)

The Arithmetic Mean represents the mathematical average and is the most widely utilized measure of central tendency.

A. Computation Formulas

  • Ungrouped Raw Data: $$\bar{x} = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^n x_i}{n} = \frac{x_1 + x_2 + \dots + x_n}{n}$$
  • Discrete & Grouped Data (Direct Method): $$\bar{x} = \frac{\sum f_i x_i}{\sum f_i} = \frac{\sum f_i x_i}{N} \quad \text{where } N = \sum f_i$$
  • Assumed Mean (Short-cut) Method: $$\bar{x} = A + \frac{\sum f_i d_i}{N}, \quad \text{where } d_i = x_i - A \text{ and } A = \text{assumed mean}$$
  • Step-Deviation Method (for uniform class width $h$): $$\bar{x} = A + \left(\frac{\sum f_i u_i}{N}\right) \times h, \quad \text{where } u_i = \frac{x_i - A}{h}$$

B. Fundamental Invariant Properties of Mean

  • Zero Algebraic Deviation: The algebraic sum of deviations of all observations from their arithmetic mean is always zero: $$\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - \bar{x}) = 0$$
  • Minimal Sum of Squared Deviations: The sum of squares of deviations from the mean is smaller than from any other arbitrary point: $$\sum (x_i - \bar{x})^2 < \sum (x_i - A)^2 \quad (\forall A \ne \bar{x})$$
  • Linear Transformation Effect: If each observation $x_i$ is multiplied by constant $a$ and then increased by constant $b$ ($y_i = a x_i + b$), the new mean becomes: $$\bar{y} = a \bar{x} + b$$
  • Combined Mean of Two Groups: If group 1 has $n_1$ items with mean $\bar{x}_1$ and group 2 has $n_2$ items with mean $\bar{x}_2$: $$\bar{x}_{12} = \frac{n_1 \bar{x}_1 + n_2 \bar{x}_2}{n_1 + n_2}$$

3. Median ($M$) & Graphical Ogives

Graphical Determination: Ogives Intersection Gives Median ($M$)
The x-coordinate (abscissa) of the intersection point of "Less than" and "More than" ogives is strictly the Median.

The Median is the positional average dividing the ordered statistical distribution into two equal halves. It is insensitive to extreme outlier values.

A. Median for Ungrouped Data

First, sort all $n$ observations in strictly ascending or descending order:

  • If $n$ is Odd: $$M = \left(\frac{n+1}{2}\right)\text{-th observation}$$
  • If $n$ is Even: $$M = \frac{1}{2} \left[ \left(\frac{n}{2}\right)\text{-th} + \left(\frac{n}{2} + 1\right)\text{-th} \right] \text{ observation}$$

B. Median for Continuous Grouped Data

  1. Compute the total frequency $N = \sum f_i$ and the cumulative frequency ($cf$) column.
  2. Determine the Median Class: the class interval whose cumulative frequency just equals or exceeds $\frac{N}{2}$.
  3. Apply the continuous interpolation formula: $$M = l + \left(\frac{\frac{N}{2} - cf}{f}\right) \times h$$
    where: $l =$ lower limit of median class, $N = \sum f_i$, $cf =$ cumulative frequency of the class preceding the median class, $f =$ frequency of the median class itself, and $h =$ width of the median class.
Graphical Determination of Median (Ogives):
The median can be found graphically by plotting both the "Less than" ogive and the "More than" ogive on the same axes. The abscissa (x-coordinate) of their point of intersection gives the exact numerical Median.

4. Mode ($M_o$) & Karl Pearson's Empirical Formula

The Mode is the value that occurs with the highest frequency in a statistical series. A distribution may be unimodal, bimodal, multimodal, or ill-defined.

A. Mode for Continuous Grouped Data

Identify the Modal Class (the class interval having the highest frequency $f_1$):

$$M_o = l + \left(\frac{f_1 - f_0}{2f_1 - f_0 - f_2}\right) \times h$$
where: $l =$ lower limit of modal class, $f_1 =$ frequency of modal class, $f_0 =$ frequency of preceding class, $f_2 =$ frequency of succeeding class, and $h =$ class width.
Graphical Determination of Mode (Histogram):
Mode is determined graphically from a Histogram by drawing diagonal lines from the top corners of the highest bar to the opposite top corners of adjacent bars. The x-coordinate of their point of intersection is the Mode.

B. Karl Pearson's Empirical Relationship

For moderately skewed (asymmetrical) distributions, the relationship connecting Mean, Median, and Mode is:

$$\text{Mode} = 3 \times \text{Median} - 2 \times \text{Mean}$$

Alternative forms frequently tested in competitive teaching recruitment exams:

  • $\text{Mean} - \text{Mode} = 3(\text{Mean} - \text{Median})$
  • $\text{Median} = \text{Mode} + \frac{2}{3}(\text{Mean} - \text{Mode}) = \frac{1}{3}(\text{Mode} + 2\text{Mean})$

5. Measures of Dispersion (Range, Variance & SD)

Dispersion quantifies the extent of scattering or variation of observations around central tendency values.

Measure Mathematical Formula Key Examination Properties
Range $\text{Range} = X_{\text{max}} - X_{\text{min}}$ $\text{Coefficient of Range} = \frac{X_{\text{max}} - X_{\text{min}}}{X_{\text{max}} + X_{\text{min}}}$. Sensitive to extreme values.
Variance ($\sigma^2$) $\sigma^2 = \frac{\sum (x_i - \bar{x})^2}{n} = \frac{\sum x_i^2}{n} - (\bar{x})^2$ Always non-negative ($\sigma^2 \ge 0$). Invariant under addition/subtraction of a constant ($c$): $\text{Var}(X \pm c) = \text{Var}(X)$. Scales quadratically under multiplication: $\text{Var}(kX) = k^2 \text{Var}(X)$.
Standard Deviation ($\sigma$) $\sigma = \sqrt{\text{Variance}} = \sqrt{\frac{\sum (x_i - \bar{x})^2}{n}}$ $\text{SD}(X \pm c) = \text{SD}(X)$, while $\text{SD}(kX) = |k| \times \text{SD}(X)$.
Coefficient of Variation ($CV$) $CV = \frac{\sigma}{\bar{x}} \times 100\%$ Unit-less relative measure of dispersion. The series with lower $CV$ is more consistent, stable, and uniform.

Top-Rank Examination Archetype MCQs

Ten exhaustive questions covering every archetype tested in UP Upper Primary Teacher (Classes 6–8) recruitment examinations, equipped with dual solutions and examiner trap warnings.

Type 1 • Natural Numbers & Zero Sum Property Moderate
Q1. If the mean of the first $n$ natural numbers is $\frac{5n}{9}$, then the numerical value of $n$ is:
Standard Method:

The sum of the first $n$ natural numbers is given by: $$S_n = \frac{n(n+1)}{2}$$ Therefore, their arithmetic mean is: $$\bar{x} = \frac{S_n}{n} = \frac{n(n+1)}{2n} = \frac{n+1}{2}$$ Equating this to the given value $\frac{5n}{9}$: $$\frac{n+1}{2} = \frac{5n}{9} \implies 9(n+1) = 10n \implies 9n + 9 = 10n \implies n = 9$$

Speed Hack:
Cross-multiply immediately: $9(n+1) = 10n \implies n = 9$. Takes under 6 seconds!
Examiner Trap:
Do not confuse the sum formula $\frac{n(n+1)}{2}$ with the mean formula $\frac{n+1}{2}$. Forgetting to divide by $n$ causes quadratic equations and wasted examination minutes.
Type 2 • Misread Observation & Correction Exam Favourite
Q2. The mean marks of 50 students in an examination was calculated as 40. Later, it was discovered that one score of 73 was wrongly entered as 23. The corrected true mean is:
Standard Method:

Original incorrect sum of marks $= 50 \times 40 = 2000$.
True sum $= \text{Original Sum} - \text{Wrong Value} + \text{Correct Value}$ $$\text{True Sum} = 2000 - 23 + 73 = 2050$$ $$\text{True Mean} = \frac{2050}{50} = 41.0$$

Speed Hack (Direct Mean Shift):
Net change in total $= \text{Correct} - \text{Wrong} = 73 - 23 = +50$.
Change per student $= \frac{+50}{50} = +1$.
True Mean $= 40 + 1 = 41.0$. (No multiplication of 2000 needed!)
Examiner Trap:
Watch the signs! Students frequently compute $\text{Wrong} - \text{Correct} = 23 - 73 = -50$ and subtract 1 to choose 39.0. Always verify: the true score (73) was higher than the entered score (23), so the true mean MUST be higher!
Type 3 • Combined Mean & Weighted Average Moderate
Q3. The average weight of 60 boys in a class is 50 kg and that of 40 girls is 45 kg. What is the combined average weight of all 100 students in the class?
Standard Method:

Combined Mean formula: $$\bar{x} = \frac{n_1 \bar{x}_1 + n_2 \bar{x}_2}{n_1 + n_2}$$ $$\bar{x} = \frac{60 \times 50 + 40 \times 45}{60 + 40} = \frac{3000 + 1800}{100} = \frac{4800}{100} = 48.0\text{ kg}$$

Speed Hack (Weight Ratio Method):
Ratio of boys to girls $= 60 : 40 = 3 : 2$.
Take baseline $= 45$ kg.
Extra weight from boys $= 3 \times (50 - 45) = 15$.
Distributed over total ratio units $(3 + 2 = 5)$: $\frac{15}{5} = +3$ kg.
Combined mean $= 45 + 3 = 48$ kg!
Examiner Trap:
Never take the simple average $\frac{50 + 45}{2} = 47.5$ kg unless the number of boys and girls are identical.
Type 4 • Median of Arrayed Data with Variable Core Archetype
Q4. The following numbers are arranged in ascending order: $11, 12, 14, 18, x+2, x+4, 30, 32, 35, 41$. If the median of the data is 24, then the value of $x$ is:
Standard Method:

Total number of observations $n = 10$ (which is even).
The two middle observations are the $\left(\frac{10}{2}\right) = 5^{\text{th}}$ and $\left(\frac{10}{2}+1\right) = 6^{\text{th}}$ observations.
$5^{\text{th}}$ item $= x + 2$
$6^{\text{th}}$ item $= x + 4$
$$\text{Median} = \frac{(x + 2) + (x + 4)}{2} = \frac{2x + 6}{2} = x + 3$$ Given that Median $= 24$: $$x + 3 = 24 \implies x = 21$$

Speed Hack:
The midpoint between $(x+2)$ and $(x+4)$ is obviously $(x+3)$.
$x + 3 = 24 \implies x = 21$ in 3 seconds!
Examiner Trap:
Ensure the series is already sorted. Here it is given in ascending order. If $x=21$, the terms are $23$ and $25$, which fit perfectly between $18$ and $30$.
Type 5 • Continuous Grouped Data Median Upper Primary Core
Q5. Consider the following grouped frequency distribution:
Class Interval0–1010–2020–3030–4040–50
Frequency ($f$)5812105
The median of this distribution is:
Standard Method:

Construct Cumulative Frequencies ($cf$):
• $0-10: cf = 5$
• $10-20: cf = 5 + 8 = 13$
• $20-30: cf = 13 + 12 = 25$
• $30-40: cf = 25 + 10 = 35$
• $40-50: cf = 35 + 5 = 40 = N$
Here $N = 40 \implies \frac{N}{2} = 20$.
The cumulative frequency just greater than or equal to 20 is 25, which corresponds to the class interval 20–30 (Median Class).
Parameters: $l = 20$, $\frac{N}{2} = 20$, $cf = 13$ (preceding class), $f = 12$ (median class), $h = 10$.
Applying the formula: $$M = l + \left(\frac{\frac{N}{2} - cf}{f}\right) \times h = 20 + \left(\frac{20 - 13}{12}\right) \times 10 = 20 + \frac{7}{12} \times 10 = 20 + \frac{70}{12} = 20 + 5.83 = 25.83$$

Speed Hack:
Need $\frac{7}{12}$ of class width 10.
$\frac{7}{12} \approx 0.5833 \times 10 = 5.83$.
Add directly to base $20$: $20 + 5.83 = 25.83$.
Examiner Trap:
The most common mistake is taking $cf = 25$ (the cumulative frequency of the median class itself) instead of 13 (the cumulative frequency of the preceding class). $cf$ always denotes the cumulative sum BEFORE entering the median class!
Type 6 • Continuous Grouped Data Mode Standard Archetype
Q6. Find the mode of the following continuous frequency distribution:
Class10–2020–3030–4040–5050–60
Frequency71420158
Standard Method:

Identify the modal class (highest frequency):
Maximum frequency is $20$, so the Modal Class is 30–40.
Parameters:
• Lower limit $l = 30$
• Modal frequency $f_1 = 20$
• Preceding frequency $f_0 = 14$
• Succeeding frequency $f_2 = 15$
• Class size $h = 10$
Formula: $$M_o = l + \left(\frac{f_1 - f_0}{2f_1 - f_0 - f_2}\right) \times h$$ $$M_o = 30 + \left(\frac{20 - 14}{2(20) - 14 - 15}\right) \times 10 = 30 + \left(\frac{6}{40 - 29}\right) \times 10 = 30 + \frac{60}{11} \approx 30 + 5.45 = 35.45$$

Speed Hack:
Denominator $= (f_1 - f_0) + (f_1 - f_2) = (20-14) + (20-15) = 6 + 5 = 11$.
Fraction to add $= \frac{6}{11} \times 10 = \frac{60}{11} = 5.4545...$
Mode $= 30 + 5.45 = 35.45$.
Examiner Trap:
Remember: the mode MUST lie inside the modal class $(30-40)$. Any option outside this interval can be eliminated immediately without calculation!
Type 7 • Karl Pearson's Empirical Law High Frequency
Q7. In a moderately asymmetrical frequency distribution, the difference between the Mode and the Mean is 18. What is the difference between the Mode and the Median ($\text{Mode} - \text{Median}$)?
Standard Method:

Karl Pearson's empirical relation: $$\text{Mode} = 3\text{Median} - 2\text{Mean}$$ Subtracting $\text{Median}$ from both sides: $$\text{Mode} - \text{Median} = 2\text{Median} - 2\text{Mean} = 2(\text{Median} - \text{Mean}) \quad \text{--- (Eq. 1)}$$ Now subtract $\text{Mean}$ from both sides of Pearson's formula: $$\text{Mode} - \text{Mean} = 3\text{Median} - 3\text{Mean} = 3(\text{Median} - \text{Mean})$$ Given that $\text{Mode} - \text{Mean} = 18$: $$3(\text{Median} - \text{Mean}) = 18 \implies \text{Median} - \text{Mean} = 6$$ Substituting this into Eq. 1: $$\text{Mode} - \text{Median} = 2(6) = 12$$

Speed Hack (2/3 Ratio Property):
Always memorize this ratio for asymmetric data:
$$\frac{\text{Mode} - \text{Median}}{\text{Mode} - \text{Mean}} = \frac{2}{3}$$ Therefore: $\text{Mode} - \text{Median} = \frac{2}{3} \times 18 = 12$. Done in 4 seconds!
Examiner Trap:
Don't confuse $\text{Mean} - \text{Median}$ with $\text{Mode} - \text{Median}$. The difference with the mean is $\frac{1}{3} \times 18 = 6$, while with the mode it is $\frac{2}{3} \times 18 = 12$.
Type 8 • Linear Transformation on SD & Variance Tricky Concept
Q8. The variance of 20 observations is 5. If each observation is multiplied by 3 and then increased by 7, the new variance of the resulting data is:
Standard Method:

Let the original observations be $x_i$, with $\text{Var}(X) = 5$.
The new observations are $y_i = 3 x_i + 7$.
By the mathematical properties of variance: $$\text{Var}(aX + b) = a^2 \text{Var}(X)$$ Adding a constant ($+7$) shifts the origin and does not change the spread around the mean.
Multiplying by 3 scales the variance by $3^2 = 9$.
$$\text{New Variance} = 3^2 \times \text{Var}(X) = 9 \times 5 = 45$$

Speed Hack:
Ignore $+7$ completely! Dispersion is independent of origin shift.
New Variance $= (\text{multiplier})^2 \times \text{old variance} = 3^2 \times 5 = 45$.
Examiner Trap:
Candidates often apply the mean rule $3(5) + 7 = 22$. Remember: Mean changes by $a\bar{x} + b$, but Variance changes by $a^2 \text{Var}(X)$, and Standard Deviation changes by $|a|\sigma$.
Type 9 • Graphical Representation of Averages Conceptual / Teaching Aptitude
Q9. In statistical data presentation, which of the following pairs of average and graphical determination tool is correctly matched?
Standard Method:

Let us review how each central tendency is determined graphically:

  • Median: Determined by cumulative frequency curves (Ogives). The abscissa of the intersection of "less than" and "more than" ogives gives the median. (Option B is correct).
  • Mode: Determined graphically using a Histogram by joining the top vertices of the tallest rectangle with adjacent bars.
  • Arithmetic Mean: Cannot be determined directly from simple graphic curves without algebraic computation.

Speed Hack (Memory Key):
• Ogive = Median (O-M: Ogive gives Median)
• Histogram = Mode (H-M: Histogram gives Mode)
Examiner Trap:
Examiners often list "Histogram gives Mean" or "Polygon gives Mode". Remember: ONLY Mode comes from Histogram, and ONLY Median comes from Ogives.
Type 10 • Coefficient of Variation & Consistency Advanced Archetype
Q10. Two cricket batsmen, A and B, scored runs in 10 test innings. Batsman A has a mean score of 50 with standard deviation 10. Batsman B has a mean score of 40 with standard deviation 6. Which batsman is more consistent in performance?
Standard Method:

Consistency is determined by the Coefficient of Variation ($CV$): $$CV = \frac{\sigma}{\bar{x}} \times 100\%$$ A smaller $CV$ indicates greater consistency and lower variability.
For Batsman A: $$CV_A = \frac{10}{50} \times 100\% = 20\%$$ For Batsman B: $$CV_B = \frac{6}{40} \times 100\% = 15\%$$ Since $CV_B < CV_A$ ($15\% < 20\%$), Batsman B is more consistent.

Speed Hack:
Compare fractions directly: $\frac{10}{50} = 0.20$ vs $\frac{6}{40} = 0.15$.
Lower fraction $= 0.15 \implies$ Batsman B is more consistent. Takes 5 seconds.
Examiner Trap:
Do not confuse "better / higher scorer" with "more consistent". Batsman A is a better run-getter (higher mean), but Batsman B is more consistent (lower CV).
