History of the English Language: Origins and Development

A pointwise guide to English origins, major historical stages, language influences, standardisation and the literary periods represented in the syllabus.

Pointwise study notes

Language family: English is a Germanic language in the Indo-European family. Its closest historical base is West Germanic; it is not a Romance language, although it has borrowed heavily from French and Latin.
Settlement and Old English: Germanic-speaking groups, commonly identified as Angles, Saxons and Jutes, settled in Britain from around the fifth century. Their varieties formed the foundation of Old English.
Old English: Old English had grammatical endings and vocabulary that can look unfamiliar to modern readers. Beowulf is its best-known surviving epic; Latin learning and Christianity added vocabulary and written traditions.
Old Norse contact: Scandinavian settlement and contact contributed words and influenced northern and eastern dialects. Everyday vocabulary such as they, them and their is commonly traced to Old Norse.
Norman Conquest: After 1066, Norman French became influential in administration, law, court life and elite culture. English remained in use and gradually absorbed many French words; the conquest did not create English.
Middle English: The language lost many Old English inflectional endings and relied more on word order and function words. Geoffrey Chaucer’s Canterbury Tales is a major Middle English work.
Printing and standardisation: William Caxton’s Westminster press (1476) helped circulate written English. London and the East Midlands variety gained influence in print, while spelling remained variable.
Early Modern English: Renaissance scholarship brought many Latin and Greek loanwords. Shakespeare is a major writer of this period; the Great Vowel Shift describes broad long-vowel changes over several generations, not a single event.
Modern English: Grammar books and dictionaries helped codify written conventions, but pronunciation and vocabulary continued changing. English developed many regional and world varieties.
How to describe change: Separate a language’s ancestry from later borrowing. French vocabulary does not make English a Romance language; a borrowed word changes vocabulary without replacing the language family.

Exam tips and quick recall

Sequence cue — O-M-E-M: Old → Middle → Early Modern → Modern. Attach one event and one writer to each stage.
1066 trap: The Norman Conquest marks a major French influence and political change. English was already spoken and written before 1066.
Borrowing trap: French and Latin influenced English vocabulary; English remains Germanic by linguistic ancestry.
Writer-period matches: Chaucer—Middle English; Shakespeare—Early Modern / Elizabethan; Wordsworth and Shelley—Romantic; Dickens and Tennyson—Victorian.
