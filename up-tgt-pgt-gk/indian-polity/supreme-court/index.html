Supreme Court and High Courts: Jurisdiction and Judicial Review

The Supreme Court is the apex constitutional court of India and High Courts are the highest courts in the States or groups of States.

UP TGT PGT GK6 concepts42 quiz questions10-question test

Topic overview

  • The Supreme Court is the apex constitutional court of India and High Courts are the highest courts in the States or groups of States.
  • The judiciary protects the Constitution through original, appellate, advisory, writ and judicial-review jurisdiction.
  • Questions commonly test Articles 124-147 and 214-231, appointment and removal of judges, writs, jurisdiction and judicial independence.
  • The Supreme Court has a wider constitutional and appellate reach, while High Courts have writ jurisdiction over Fundamental Rights and other legal rights within their territorial jurisdiction.
  • Judicial review, constitutional remedies, binding precedent and the basic-structure doctrine are central examination themes.

Concept 1

Supreme Court Composition and Appointment

The Supreme Court is the apex judicial institution with constitutionally protected judges and jurisdiction.

Step-by-step explanation

  • Article 124 establishes the Supreme Court consisting of the Chief Justice of India and such number of other judges as Parliament prescribes by law.
  • Judges are appointed by the President within the constitutional consultation and judicial-appointment framework.
  • A Supreme Court judge must be a citizen and satisfy the experience qualifications of a High Court judge for five years, an advocate for ten years or a distinguished jurist in the President's opinion.
  • The retirement age of a Supreme Court judge is 65 years; High Court judges retire at 62 years under the Constitution.
  • The Supreme Court is a court of record under Article 129 and may punish for contempt of itself.
  • The Court can include acting, ad hoc or retired judges in the circumstances provided by the Constitution.
  • The Chief Justice allocates benches and administrative work within the institutional framework; judicial decisions are delivered by constitutionally constituted benches.
  • The Court's independence is supported by security of tenure, fixed service conditions and a difficult removal procedure.

Key points

  • Article 124 - Supreme Court
  • President appoints within constitutional framework
  • Retirement age 65
  • Article 129 - court of record
  • Removal only for proved misbehaviour or incapacity.

Concept 2

Supreme Court Jurisdiction and Appeals

The Supreme Court combines federal, constitutional, civil, criminal, special-leave and advisory jurisdiction.

Step-by-step explanation

  • Article 131 gives original jurisdiction in disputes between the Union and States or between States involving a legal right, subject to constitutional limits.
  • Articles 132-134 provide appeals involving constitutional, civil and criminal matters from High Courts under specified conditions.
  • Article 136 gives the Supreme Court discretionary Special Leave to Appeal from judgments or orders of courts and tribunals, except the constitutionally excluded armed-forces matters.
  • Article 137 allows the Court to review its judgments or orders subject to parliamentary law and court rules.
  • Article 143 permits the President to seek an advisory opinion on a question of law or fact of public importance.
  • Article 139A allows transfer of cases in specified circumstances to secure substantial questions of law or the interests of justice.
  • Article 142 enables the Court to do complete justice in a pending matter within its jurisdiction.
  • Article 141 makes the law declared by the Supreme Court binding on all courts within India.

Key points

  • Article 131 - original federal disputes
  • Articles 132-134 - constitutional, civil and criminal appeals
  • Article 136 - SLP
  • Article 143 - advisory opinion
  • Article 141 - binding precedent.

Concept 3

Writs, Article 32 and Judicial Review

Constitutional remedies allow courts to protect rights and test public action against the Constitution.

Step-by-step explanation

  • Article 32 guarantees the right to move the Supreme Court for enforcement of Fundamental Rights.
  • The Supreme Court may issue habeas corpus, mandamus, prohibition, certiorari and quo warranto for the Article 32 purpose.
  • Habeas corpus challenges unlawful detention; mandamus commands a public authority to perform a public duty.
  • Prohibition prevents a subordinate court or tribunal from exceeding jurisdiction before the proceeding is completed.
  • Certiorari quashes an order of a lower court or tribunal for jurisdictional or legal error after the order or proceeding.
  • Quo warranto questions the legal authority of a person occupying a public office.
  • Article 226 gives High Courts writ power for Fundamental Rights and for any other purpose, so its scope is wider in subject matter.
  • Judicial review allows courts to invalidate unconstitutional laws or executive action, subject to constitutional doctrine and institutional limits.

Key points

  • Article 32 - Supreme Court remedy
  • Five writs
  • Article 226 - wider High Court purpose
  • Judicial review checks unconstitutional action
  • Habeas corpus protects personal liberty.

Concept 4

Judicial Independence and Removal of Judges

Security of tenure and a difficult removal process protect judicial independence.

Step-by-step explanation

  • Judges have constitutionally protected tenure and cannot be removed by the executive through an ordinary administrative order.
  • A Supreme Court judge may be removed by the President after an address by each House of Parliament supported by the required special majority.
  • The grounds are proved misbehaviour or incapacity; Parliament regulates the investigation and proof procedure by law.
  • Article 218 applies the Supreme Court judge removal provisions to High Court judges.
  • Salaries and service conditions are protected and cannot be varied to the disadvantage of a judge after appointment except as constitutionally permitted.
  • The judiciary has institutional separation from the executive, while appointments and accountability operate through constitutional consultation and parliamentary procedure.
  • Judicial review and contempt powers support the authority of courts but do not create personal immunity from lawful accountability.
  • Independence must be balanced with reasoned judgments, open procedure and constitutional limits.

Key points

  • Removal for proved misbehaviour or incapacity
  • Special majority in both Houses
  • President issues removal order after parliamentary address
  • Article 218 applies route to High Courts
  • Security of tenure supports independence.

Concept 5

High Courts: Jurisdiction and Superintendence

High Courts protect rights, hear appeals and supervise subordinate courts within their constitutional field.

Step-by-step explanation

  • Article 214 provides a High Court for each State, while the Constitution permits a common High Court for two or more States or for States and a Union Territory.
  • High Courts have original and appellate jurisdiction under the Constitution and laws, including civil, criminal and constitutional matters.
  • Article 226 gives High Courts power to issue writs for Fundamental Rights and for any other purpose.
  • Article 227 gives the High Court superintendence over courts and tribunals within its territory, except those under laws relating to the armed forces.
  • High Courts are courts of record under Article 215 and may punish for contempt of themselves.
  • High Court judges are appointed by the President after the constitutional consultation process and retire at 62 years.
  • High Court writ jurisdiction can reach a legal right even where no Fundamental Right is directly involved, subject to public-law limitations.
  • High Courts also control subordinate judiciary through rules, records, inspections and administrative supervision within constitutional limits.

Key points

  • Article 214 - High Court
  • Article 226 - writs for FR and other purpose
  • Article 227 - superintendence
  • Article 215 - court of record
  • Retirement age 62.

Concept 6

Supreme Court–High Court Comparison and Precedent

The two constitutional courts work together while differing in territorial reach, remedies and final authority.

Step-by-step explanation

  • The Supreme Court is the final court of appeal and its declared law binds all courts under Article 141.
  • A High Court is the highest court within its territorial jurisdiction but remains subject to Supreme Court precedent.
  • Both courts can issue writs, but Article 32 is focused on Fundamental Rights whereas Article 226 extends to any other legal purpose.
  • The Supreme Court decides federal disputes under Article 131; High Courts generally do not exercise that Union-State original jurisdiction.
  • Both courts are courts of record and possess contempt jurisdiction over themselves.
  • Constitution Benches of at least five judges hear substantial questions of constitutional interpretation in the Supreme Court.
  • Judicial review includes review of legislative and executive action, while precedent promotes consistency and equality before law.
  • The basic-structure doctrine limits constitutional amendment and is a major example of judicial review in constitutional cases.

Key points

  • Supreme Court final appeal
  • Article 141 binding law
  • Article 32 versus Article 226
  • Article 131 federal disputes
  • Constitution Bench minimum five judges.
