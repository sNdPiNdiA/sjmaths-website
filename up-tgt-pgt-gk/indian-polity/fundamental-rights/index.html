Indian Polity and Constitution

Fundamental Rights in India: Articles 12-35 and Writs

Fundamental Rights are guaranteed in Part III of the Constitution, mainly through Articles 12 to 35.

UP TGT PGT GK6 concepts42 quiz questions10-question test

Topic overview

  • They protect liberty, equality, dignity, religious freedom and minority interests against arbitrary State action.
  • Some rights are available to every person, while several rights are specifically guaranteed to citizens.
  • Rights are not absolute: the Constitution permits reasonable restrictions and special provisions for public interest and social justice.
  • Article 32 provides a constitutional remedy through writs, while High Courts exercise wider writ jurisdiction under Article 226.

Concept 1

Articles 12-13 and the Nature of Fundamental Rights

Articles 12 and 13 identify the authority bound by Fundamental Rights and establish constitutional control over inconsistent laws.

Step-by-step explanation

  • Article 12 gives the meaning of State for Part III and includes the Government and Parliament of India, State governments and legislatures, local authorities and other authorities under Indian control.
  • Article 13 covers pre-Constitution laws and later laws that take away or abridge Fundamental Rights.
  • A law inconsistent with a Fundamental Right is void to the extent of the inconsistency; the entire law is not automatically void if the offending part can be separated.
  • The word law in Article 13 includes ordinances, orders, bye-laws, rules, regulations, notifications, customs and usages having the force of law.
  • Fundamental Rights primarily operate against State action, although constitutional provisions and laws may also address private exploitation and discrimination.
  • Judicial review enables courts to test legislation and executive action against constitutional rights.
  • Rights are enforceable through constitutional remedies, but restrictions may be permitted where the Constitution itself authorises them.
  • Article 13 should be read with the amending power under Article 368 and the basic-structure doctrine.

Key points

  • Article 12 - meaning of State
  • Article 13 - laws inconsistent with rights
  • Void to the extent of inconsistency
  • Judicial review
  • Rights are subject to constitutional limitations.
Worked examples
  • Classifying a local authority under Article 12
  • Applying severability to a partly invalid law

Additional examples

  • Classifying a local authority under Article 12
  • Applying severability to a partly invalid law
Important distinctions
  • Article 12 identifies the authority bound by Part III, while Article 13 controls laws inconsistent with Part III.
  • A Fundamental Right is constitutionally guaranteed; an ordinary statutory right depends on legislation and can be changed by ordinary law.
Common misconceptions
  • Article 12 means only the Union Government.
  • Every law containing one invalid provision becomes wholly void automatically.
Exam focus
  • Remember the Article 12 categories.
  • Use the phrase void to the extent of inconsistency for Article 13.
  • Distinguish judicial review from ordinary administrative appeal.
Concept 2

Right to Equality: Articles 14-18

The Right to Equality combines equal legal protection with safeguards against discrimination, untouchability and hereditary titles.

Step-by-step explanation

  • Article 14 guarantees equality before law and equal protection of laws to every person, not only citizens.
  • Equality before law expresses a negative idea of absence of special privilege, while equal protection permits reasonable classification to treat unequal situations differently.
  • Article 15 prohibits discrimination against citizens only on religion, race, caste, sex, place of birth or any of them.
  • Article 15 permits special provisions for women, children, socially and educationally backward classes, Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes, and economically weaker sections as authorised by constitutional amendments.
  • Article 16 guarantees equality of opportunity in public employment and permits constitutionally authorised reservations and residence requirements in limited situations.
  • Article 17 abolishes untouchability and makes its practice in any form an offence punishable by law.
  • Article 18 abolishes titles except military or academic distinctions and restricts acceptance of titles and presents from foreign States in specified cases.
  • The equality code seeks substantive equality, not mechanical treatment of every person in exactly the same way.

Key points

  • Article 14 - equality before law and equal protection
  • Article 15 - discrimination and special provisions
  • Article 16 - public employment
  • Article 17 - untouchability abolished
  • Article 18 - titles abolished.
Worked examples
  • Reasonable classification under Article 14
  • Distinguishing reservation from discrimination prohibited by Article 15

Additional examples

  • Reasonable classification under Article 14
  • Distinguishing reservation from discrimination prohibited by Article 15
Important distinctions
  • Article 14 applies to every person; Articles 15 and 16 mainly use the expression citizen.
  • Equality before law is formal equality, while equal protection allows reasonable classification for substantive equality.
Common misconceptions
  • Article 14 prohibits every classification.
  • Article 17 only discourages untouchability but does not create a punishable offence.
Exam focus
  • Match Articles 14-18 with their exact subjects.
  • Remember the difference between Article 15 and Article 16.
  • Use reasonable classification, not arbitrary classification, in answers.
Concept 3

Right to Freedom: Articles 19-22 and 21A

The Right to Freedom protects expression, movement, life, personal liberty and safeguards against arbitrary criminal process and detention.

Step-by-step explanation

  • Article 19 guarantees six freedoms to citizens: speech and expression, peaceful assembly without arms, forming associations or unions, free movement, residence and settlement, and profession or occupation, trade or business.
  • Article 19 freedoms are subject to reasonable restrictions based on grounds specified in the Constitution, such as security of the State, public order, morality, defamation and incitement.
  • Article 20 protects against ex-post-facto criminal laws, double jeopardy and compelled self-incrimination.
  • Article 21 protects life and personal liberty except according to procedure established by law; judicial interpretation has given it a broad dignity-based meaning.
  • Article 21A provides free and compulsory education for children between six and fourteen years in the manner determined by law.
  • Article 22 gives safeguards against arrest and detention, including information about grounds of arrest, consultation with a legal practitioner and production before a magistrate within 24 hours, subject to constitutional exceptions.
  • Preventive detention is constitutionally recognised but is subject to safeguards and limits provided by the Constitution and legislation.
  • The Right to Freedom is not an unrestricted licence; lawful restrictions must satisfy constitutional requirements.

Key points

  • Article 19 - six freedoms for citizens
  • Article 20 - criminal conviction safeguards
  • Article 21 - life and personal liberty
  • Article 21A - education for ages 6-14
  • Article 22 - arrest and detention safeguards.
Worked examples
  • Testing a restriction on speech against Article 19(2)
  • Distinguishing preventive detention from punishment after conviction

Additional examples

  • Testing a restriction on speech against Article 19(2)
  • Distinguishing preventive detention from punishment after conviction
Important distinctions
  • Article 19 is available only to citizens, while Article 21 protects every person.
  • Preventive detention is intended to prevent a future threat; punitive detention follows a finding of criminal liability.
Common misconceptions
  • Article 19 protects an unlimited right to say anything anywhere.
  • Article 21A makes every form of higher education a Fundamental Right.
Exam focus

  • Match Articles 20, 21, 21A and 22 with their safeguards.
  • Mention reasonable restrictions in every answer on Article 19.
Concept 4

Rights against Exploitation and Freedom of Religion

Articles 23-28 protect human dignity from exploitation and secure religious freedom subject to constitutional regulation.

Step-by-step explanation

  • Article 23 prohibits traffic in human beings, begar and other similar forms of forced labour and treats contravention as an offence punishable by law.
  • Article 23 allows compulsory service for public purposes if the State does not discriminate only on religion, race, caste or class.
  • Article 24 prohibits employment of a child below fourteen years in a factory, mine or hazardous employment.
  • Article 25 guarantees freedom of conscience and the right to profess, practise and propagate religion subject to public order, morality, health and the other provisions of Part III.
  • Article 26 gives religious denominations rights to establish and maintain institutions, manage religious affairs, own property and administer property according to law and public order, morality and health.
  • Article 27 prevents compelling a person to pay taxes specifically appropriated for promoting or maintaining a particular religion.
  • Article 28 regulates religious instruction in educational institutions wholly maintained out of State funds and recognises exceptions for institutions administered under endowments or trusts.
  • Religious freedom in India is balanced with social reform, equality, public order, morality and health.

Key points

  • Article 23 - trafficking, begar and forced labour
  • Article 24 - child labour in hazardous work
  • Article 25 - conscience and religious practice
  • Article 26 - denominational management
  • Articles 27-28 - religion and State finance/instruction.
Worked examples
  • Distinguishing forced labour from compulsory public service
  • Applying Article 25 restrictions to a public-order situation

Additional examples

  • Distinguishing forced labour from compulsory public service
  • Applying Article 25 restrictions to a public-order situation
Important distinctions
  • Article 23 protects against exploitation by private persons as well as State action, while many other rights are framed mainly against the State.
  • Article 25 protects individuals; Article 26 protects religious denominations as collective entities.
Common misconceptions
  • Article 25 makes religious practice immune from all regulation.
  • Article 24 prohibits every form of work by every person below fourteen in every situation.
Exam focus
  • Match Articles 23-24 with exploitation subjects.
  • Remember the four constitutional limits under Article 25.
  • Distinguish Articles 25 and 26.
Concept 5

Cultural and Educational Rights and the Right to Property

Articles 29-30 protect cultural identity and minority educational institutions, while the former Fundamental Right to property now has a different constitutional status.

Step-by-step explanation

  • Article 29 protects the right of any section of citizens with a distinct language, script or culture to conserve it.
  • Article 29(2) prevents denial of admission to State-maintained or State-aided educational institutions on grounds only of religion, race, caste or language.
  • Article 30 gives religious and linguistic minorities the right to establish and administer educational institutions of their choice.
  • State regulation of minority institutions may be permitted for academic standards, public order and other legitimate constitutional purposes, but it cannot destroy the core right of administration.
  • The right to property was originally a Fundamental Right under Articles 19(1)(f) and 31.
  • The Forty-Fourth Amendment removed property from Part III and inserted Article 300A, under which no person can be deprived of property except by authority of law.
  • Article 300A is a constitutional legal right, not a Fundamental Right; Article 32 is therefore not the ordinary remedy for its enforcement.
  • The distinction between cultural rights, minority institution rights and property rights is a frequent examination trap.

Key points

  • Article 29 - language, script and culture
  • Article 30 - minority educational institutions
  • Article 31 - historical property right removed
  • Article 300A - constitutional legal right to property
  • Article 32 is not a normal remedy for Article 300A.
Worked examples
  • Applying Article 29(2) to admission discrimination
  • Comparing Article 30 minority institutions with ordinary private institutions

Additional examples

  • Applying Article 29(2) to admission discrimination
  • Comparing Article 30 minority institutions with ordinary private institutions
Important distinctions
  • Article 29 protects cultural interests of any section of citizens; Article 30 specifically protects religious and linguistic minorities establishing educational institutions.
  • Article 300A protects property by constitutional law but does not place it among the Fundamental Rights in Part III.
Common misconceptions
  • Only minorities can claim Article 29(1).
  • The Right to Property remains a Fundamental Right under Article 31.
Exam focus
  • Distinguish Articles 29 and 30.
  • Remember the Forty-Fourth Amendment and Article 300A.
  • Do not list Right to Property among the current six Fundamental Rights.
Concept 6

Constitutional Remedies, Writs and Articles 32-35

Article 32 makes enforcement of Fundamental Rights a constitutional remedy and the writ system gives courts practical tools to protect rights.

Step-by-step explanation

  • Article 32 guarantees the right to move the Supreme Court for enforcement of the rights conferred by Part III.
  • The Supreme Court may issue directions, orders and writs including habeas corpus, mandamus, prohibition, certiorari and quo warranto.
  • Habeas corpus commands that a detained person be produced before the court so the legality of detention can be examined.
  • Mandamus commands a public authority to perform a public legal duty that it has failed or refused to perform.
  • Prohibition is issued by a higher court to stop a lower court or tribunal from continuing proceedings beyond its jurisdiction.
  • Certiorari is used to quash an order of a lower court or tribunal for jurisdictional error or other recognised legal defect.
  • Quo warranto asks by what authority a person holds a public office and can remove an unlawful usurper from that office.
  • Article 226 gives High Courts wider power because they may issue writs for Fundamental Rights and for other legal purposes.
  • Articles 33-35 permit special rules for disciplined forces and reserve specified legislation and punishment matters to Parliament.

Key points

  • Article 32 - Supreme Court remedy
  • Article 226 - wider High Court writ jurisdiction
  • Habeas corpus - unlawful detention
  • Mandamus - public duty
  • Prohibition, certiorari and quo warranto - jurisdiction, orders and public office.
Worked examples
  • Choosing habeas corpus for unlawful detention
  • Distinguishing prohibition from certiorari

Additional examples

  • Choosing habeas corpus for unlawful detention
  • Distinguishing prohibition from certiorari
Important distinctions
  • Article 32 is itself a Fundamental Right and is focused on Part III rights; Article 226 is a wider High Court power for Fundamental Rights and other legal rights.
  • Prohibition stops proceedings before a final decision; certiorari generally quashes an order already made.
Common misconceptions
  • All writs have the same purpose.
  • Article 226 is narrower than Article 32 because High Courts are below the Supreme Court.
Exam focus
  • Memorise the purpose of all five writs.
  • Compare Articles 32 and 226.
  • Remember Parliament's special powers under Articles 33-35.
