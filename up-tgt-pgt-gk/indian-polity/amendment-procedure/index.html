Indian Polity and Constitution

Constitutional Amendments and the Basic Structure Doctrine

Article 368 provides the principal procedure for amending the Constitution while allowing different thresholds for different provisions.

UP TGT PGT GK6 concepts42 quiz questions10-question test

Topic overview

  • Some provisions can be changed by a simple majority, some by a special majority of Parliament and some by Parliament plus ratification by at least half of the States.
  • The Supreme Court has held that Parliament can amend the Constitution but cannot destroy its basic structure.
  • The 42nd, 44th, 73rd and 86th Amendments are especially important for UP TGT and PGT examinations.
  • The amendment process balances constitutional stability with the need to respond to social, political and institutional change.

Concept 1

Article 368 and Three Amendment Routes

The Constitution uses different amendment thresholds so that ordinary adjustments do not require the same process as changes to the federal compact.

Step-by-step explanation

  • Article 368 gives Parliament the constituent power to amend the Constitution through addition, variation or repeal of provisions.
  • A Constitution Amendment Bill may be introduced in either House of Parliament; it does not require prior permission of the President.
  • The Bill must be passed separately by each House; there is no provision for a joint sitting to resolve a deadlock.
  • The Constitution is commonly studied through three routes: simple majority, special majority and special majority plus ratification by at least half of the States.
  • Simple-majority changes apply to provisions expressly kept outside Article 368, such as certain matters concerning States and Schedules.
  • The special majority requires a majority of the total membership of each House and two-thirds of members present and voting.
  • Federal provisions require ratification by at least half of the State legislatures after Parliament passes the Bill and before assent.
  • The President must give assent to a Constitution Amendment Bill duly passed under Article 368.

Key points

  • Article 368 - amendment power and procedure
  • Bill may begin in either House
  • No joint sitting
  • Special majority - total membership plus two-thirds present and voting
  • Federal changes - ratification by at least half of States.

Concept 2

Amendment Process and Federal Ratification

The amendment process protects the federal balance by requiring State participation when the distribution of constitutional powers is affected.

Step-by-step explanation

  • A Constitution Amendment Bill can be introduced by a minister or a private member in either House of Parliament.
  • It requires the prescribed majority in both Houses, voting separately; the Lok Sabha and Rajya Sabha cannot be bypassed by a joint sitting.
  • For federal provisions, at least half of the State legislatures must ratify the Bill by a simple majority of members present and voting.
  • The Constitution does not prescribe a time limit within which State legislatures must ratify a federal amendment.
  • State ratification is required for changes relating to the election of the President, the Union and State executive relationship and the Union-State legislative powers.
  • It is also required for the Supreme Court and High Courts, the distribution of legislative powers, the Seventh Schedule and representation of States in Parliament.
  • After the required approvals, the President gives assent and the amendment becomes part of the Constitution from the date or manner specified.
  • The process reflects constitutional flexibility with a higher threshold for changes affecting the federal structure.

Key points

  • Either House may introduce the Bill
  • Both Houses pass it separately
  • No joint sitting
  • At least half of States ratify specified federal changes
  • President gives assent after constitutional requirements.

Concept 3

Basic Structure Doctrine and Landmark Cases

The basic-structure doctrine preserves the Constitution's identity while allowing Parliament to amend its text.

Step-by-step explanation

  • In Shankari Prasad (1951) and Sajjan Singh (1965), the Supreme Court accepted the power to amend Fundamental Rights through constitutional amendments.
  • In Golaknath (1967), the Supreme Court held that Parliament could not amend Fundamental Rights, leading to a major constitutional debate.
  • The 24th Amendment responded by affirming Parliament's amending power and requiring the President to give assent to a Constitution Amendment Bill duly passed.
  • In Kesavananda Bharati (1973), the Supreme Court held that Parliament can amend every part of the Constitution but cannot destroy its basic structure.
  • Basic-structure examples include supremacy of the Constitution, republican and democratic government, secularism, federalism, rule of law, judicial review, separation of powers and free and fair elections.
  • The list is not closed; the Court identifies basic features according to the constitutional context of a case.
  • In Indira Gandhi v Raj Narain (1975), free and fair elections and rule of law were treated as basic features in the relevant context.
  • Minerva Mills (1980) held that limited amending power and harmony between Fundamental Rights and DPSPs are part of the basic structure.

Key points

  • Golaknath - 1967
  • Kesavananda Bharati - 1973
  • Parliament can amend but cannot destroy basic structure
  • Judicial review and federalism are examples
  • Minerva Mills - limited amending power and FR-DPSP balance.

Concept 4

42nd Constitutional Amendment Act, 1976

The 42nd Amendment is called the Mini-Constitution because of its wide-ranging changes to the constitutional text and institutions.

Step-by-step explanation

  • The 42nd Amendment was enacted in 1976 during the Emergency period.
  • It added the words Socialist, Secular and Integrity to the Preamble.
  • It inserted Part IVA containing Fundamental Duties under Article 51A.
  • It added Article 39A on equal justice and free legal aid, Article 43A on worker participation in management and Article 48A on environment, forests and wildlife.
  • It attempted to strengthen Directive Principles in relation to certain Fundamental Rights, but parts of the changes were later limited by judicial decisions and subsequent amendments.
  • It made changes concerning the tenure of legislatures and the relationship between Parliament, courts and constitutional review; several controversial changes were later reversed.
  • The 42nd Amendment also shifted some subjects in the Seventh Schedule from the State List to the Concurrent List.
  • In examination answers, the 42nd Amendment should be connected with the Preamble, Duties, legal aid, environment and worker participation.

Key points

  • 1976 - 42nd Amendment
  • Added Socialist, Secular and Integrity
  • Added Fundamental Duties
  • Added Articles 39A, 43A and 48A
  • Known as the Mini-Constitution.

Concept 5

44th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1978

The 44th Amendment reversed or revised several Emergency-era changes and changed the constitutional status of property.

Step-by-step explanation

  • The 44th Amendment followed the Emergency period and aimed to restore safeguards for liberty and constitutional government.
  • It removed the Right to Property from the list of Fundamental Rights by deleting Articles 19(1)(f) and 31.
  • It inserted Article 300A, making property a constitutional legal right: no person can be deprived of property except by authority of law.
  • It replaced internal disturbance with armed rebellion as the ground for a National Emergency under Article 352.
  • It required written advice of the Union Cabinet for proclamation of a National Emergency.
  • It protected Articles 20 and 21 from suspension of enforcement even during an Emergency.
  • It restored the normal term of the Lok Sabha and State Legislative Assemblies to five years after the temporary extension made during the Emergency period.
  • The amendment is important for questions comparing Fundamental Rights, Emergency safeguards and property rights.

Key points

  • 1978 - 44th Amendment
  • Property moved to Article 300A
  • Armed rebellion replaced internal disturbance
  • Articles 20 and 21 protected during Emergency
  • Five-year legislative term restored.

Concept 6

73rd, 74th, 86th and Other Key Amendments

Later amendments expanded democratic decentralisation, education, taxation and representation while responding to new constitutional needs.

Step-by-step explanation

  • The 73rd Amendment constitutionalised Panchayati Raj institutions through Part IX and the Eleventh Schedule.
  • The 74th Amendment constitutionalised Municipalities through Part IXA and the Twelfth Schedule.
  • The 86th Amendment inserted Article 21A, changed Article 45 to early childhood care below six and added Article 51A(k).
  • The 52nd Amendment introduced the anti-defection framework through the Tenth Schedule.
  • The 61st Amendment reduced the voting age from 21 to 18 years.
  • The 101st Amendment introduced the Goods and Services Tax framework through the GST Council and related provisions.
  • The 103rd Amendment enabled constitutionally authorised reservation for Economically Weaker Sections in education and public employment, subject to judicial review.
  • Amendments must be remembered with their year, constitutional location and principal institutional effect.

Key points

  • 73rd - Panchayats and Eleventh Schedule
  • 74th - Municipalities and Twelfth Schedule
  • 86th - Article 21A and education duty
  • 52nd - anti-defection
  • 61st - voting age 18.
Worked examples
  • Matching an amendment with a Schedule or Part
  • Distinguishing the 73rd and 74th Amendments

Additional examples

  • Matching an amendment with a Schedule or Part
  • Distinguishing the 73rd and 74th Amendments
Important distinctions
  • The 73rd Amendment concerns rural local government; the 74th concerns urban local government.
  • The 86th Amendment combines a Fundamental Right, a DPSP change and a Fundamental Duty relating to education.
Common misconceptions
  • The 73rd Amendment created Municipalities and the 74th created Panchayats.
  • The 86th Amendment made all education free up to university level.

  • Match 73rd with Eleventh Schedule and 74th with Twelfth Schedule.
  • Remember the three-part education change under the 86th Amendment.

The special majority combines a majority of total membership with two-thirds of members present and voting in each House.

Question 2Assertion–Reason

Assertion (A): A Constitution Amendment Bill cannot be passed through a joint sitting. Reason (R): Each House must pass it separately with the prescribed majority.

Article 368 requires separate approval by both Houses and does not provide a joint-sitting route.

Question 3Match the following

Which option correctly matches the amendment route with its requirement?

The special-majority formula applies separately in each House.

Question 4Case-based

Case: A Bill changes representation of States in Parliament and the distribution of legislative powers. Which additional step is required after Parliament passes it?

Specified federal provisions require ratification by at least half of the States.

Question 5MCQ

Which matter generally requires ratification by at least half of the State legislatures?

Changes affecting the election of the President are among the federal provisions requiring State ratification.

Question 6Assertion–Reason

Assertion (A): State ratification is a federal safeguard. Reason (R): It gives States a constitutionally defined role when the distribution of Union-State powers is amended.

Ratification protects the federal balance for specified constitutional changes.

Question 7Match the following

Which option correctly pairs the federal subject with the ratification rule?

Distribution of legislative powers is a federal subject covered by the ratification requirement.

Question 8Case-based

Case: Parliament passes an amendment affecting the Seventh Schedule. A student says Parliament alone is enough. What is the correct response?

The Seventh Schedule is among the federal matters requiring State ratification.

Question 9MCQ

Which case established that Parliament cannot destroy the basic structure of the Constitution?

Kesavananda Bharati (1973) established the basic-structure limitation.

Question 10Assertion–Reason

Assertion (A): The basic-structure doctrine does not make the Constitution completely unamendable. Reason (R): Parliament may amend constitutional provisions but cannot destroy the Constitution's basic features.

The doctrine preserves the identity of the Constitution while allowing amendment.
