Indian Polity and Constitution

Emergency Provisions: National, State and Financial Emergency

Part XVIII of the Constitution contains emergency provisions for exceptional threats to national security, constitutional government in a State and financial stability.

UP TGT PGT GK6 concepts42 quiz questions10-question test

Topic overview

  • Article 352 covers National Emergency, Article 356 covers failure of constitutional machinery in a State and Article 360 covers Financial Emergency.
  • Emergency provisions alter the federal balance and affect executive, legislative and Fundamental Rights arrangements, so constitutional safeguards are essential.
  • The 44th Amendment strengthened approval, renewal, Cabinet-advice and liberty safeguards after the experience of the 1975-77 Emergency.
  • UP examinations frequently test grounds, approval periods, duration, effects, revocation, Article 358 versus 359 and the S.R. Bommai principle.

Concept 1

National Emergency: Grounds and Procedure

Article 352 responds to war, external aggression or armed rebellion through a tightly controlled Union process.

Step-by-step explanation

  • A National Emergency may be proclaimed when the security of India or any part is threatened by war, external aggression or armed rebellion.
  • The President issues the Proclamation, but the 44th Amendment requires a written decision of the Union Cabinet before the advice is tendered.
  • The Proclamation may cover the whole of India or only a part of its territory where the threat exists.
  • Each House of Parliament must approve the Proclamation within one month; without approval it ceases to operate.
  • Approval requires a majority of the total membership of the House and not less than two-thirds of members present and voting.
  • Once approved, the Emergency continues for six months at a time and each continuation requires parliamentary approval with the prescribed majority.
  • Lok Sabha can disapprove continuation through the special procedure, and the President may revoke the Proclamation.
  • The grounds must not be confused: armed rebellion replaced internal disturbance after the 44th Amendment.

Key points

  • Article 352 - war, external aggression, armed rebellion
  • Written Union Cabinet decision
  • Approval within one month
  • Special majority
  • Six-month renewal.

Concept 2

National Emergency: Effects on Federalism and Rights

National Emergency expands Union authority but operates within constitutional and rights safeguards.

Step-by-step explanation

  • Article 353 extends the Union executive power to giving directions to States and allows Parliament to make laws on matters in the State List within the emergency framework.
  • A law made by Parliament under the emergency expansion does not remain permanently effective merely because the Emergency existed; constitutional effects are limited by the relevant provisions.
  • The President may modify distribution of revenues between Union and States for the period of Emergency subject to constitutional presentation and parliamentary consideration.
  • Article 358 concerns automatic operation of Article 19 only during an Emergency based on war or external aggression and with the required connection to the proclamation.
  • Article 359 permits suspension of the right to move courts for enforcement of specified Fundamental Rights through a Presidential order.
  • After the 44th Amendment, the right to move courts for Articles 20 and 21 cannot be suspended under Article 359.
  • Emergency does not suspend the Constitution itself, and judicial review and parliamentary control remain important safeguards.
  • The Lok Sabha and State Assembly terms may be extended during a National Emergency by Parliament, but not indefinitely beyond the constitutional limit after the Emergency ends.

Key points

  • Article 353 - Union directions and State List laws
  • Article 358 - Article 19 limited to war/external aggression
  • Article 359 - suspension of court remedy for specified rights
  • Articles 20 and 21 protected
  • Federal balance becomes more centralised.

Concept 3

President's Rule: Article 356

Article 356 addresses failure of constitutional machinery in a State through a Presidential Proclamation and parliamentary control.

Step-by-step explanation

  • Article 356 may be invoked when the President is satisfied that the government of a State cannot be carried on according to the Constitution.
  • The satisfaction may be based on a report from the Governor or otherwise; a Governor's report is not the only possible material.
  • The President may assume State executive functions and declare that Parliament will exercise the powers of the State Legislature.
  • The President may make incidental provisions necessary to give effect to the Proclamation, but cannot assume the powers of a High Court.
  • The Proclamation must be approved by both Houses within two months or it ceases to operate.
  • Once approved, it continues for six months at a time and normally cannot exceed three years in total.
  • Continuation beyond one year requires a National Emergency in operation in the whole or any part of India and an Election Commission certification that elections cannot be held.
  • The S.R. Bommai judgment emphasised floor tests, federalism and judicial review of Article 356 action.

Key points

  • Failure of constitutional machinery
  • Governor report or other material
  • Approval within two months
  • Cannot assume High Court powers
  • Three-year maximum subject to conditions.

Concept 4

Financial Emergency: Article 360

Article 360 protects financial stability or credit through Union directions and parliamentary approval.

Step-by-step explanation

  • A Financial Emergency may be proclaimed if the financial stability or credit of India or any part of its territory is threatened.
  • The President issues the Proclamation, and both Houses must approve it within two months.
  • During a Financial Emergency, the Union may direct States to observe financial propriety and other financial canons specified in the direction.
  • Directions may include reduction of salaries and allowances of persons serving the Union or a State, including categories of judges within the constitutional text.
  • The President may require State Money Bills and other State financial Bills to be reserved for consideration.
  • The Proclamation continues until revoked or disapproved; the Constitution does not prescribe a six-month renewal cycle like Articles 352 and 356.
  • A Financial Emergency has never been proclaimed in India, but its provisions remain examinable.
  • Financial Emergency differs from a fiscal dispute or ordinary budget deficit because Article 360 requires a threat to financial stability or credit.

Key points

  • Article 360 - financial stability or credit
  • Approval within two months
  • Directions for financial propriety
  • Salary reduction power
  • Never proclaimed in India so far.

Concept 5

44th Amendment and Emergency Safeguards

The 44th Amendment made emergency powers more accountable and strengthened protection of personal liberty.

Step-by-step explanation

  • The 44th Amendment replaced internal disturbance with armed rebellion as a ground for National Emergency.
  • It required that the Union Cabinet decision authorising emergency advice be communicated in writing.
  • It reduced the time for parliamentary approval of a National Emergency from two months to one month.
  • It introduced the special-majority requirement for approval and continuation of a National Emergency.
  • It protected Articles 20 and 21 from suspension of the right to move courts under Article 359.
  • It limited Article 358 so that automatic Article 19 effects apply only to war or external aggression emergencies and connected laws or executive action.
  • It made it easier for Lok Sabha to require consideration of disapproval of a National Emergency through the one-tenth notice mechanism.
  • These safeguards show that emergency provisions remain subject to constitutionalism, parliamentary accountability and judicial review.

Key points

  • Internal disturbance replaced by armed rebellion
  • Written Cabinet advice
  • One-month approval
  • Special majority
  • Articles 20-21 protected.

Concept 6

Emergency Comparison, Revocation and Federalism

A comparative framework prevents confusion among Articles 352, 356 and 360.

Step-by-step explanation

  • Article 352 responds to war, external aggression or armed rebellion threatening India or part of India.
  • Article 356 responds to failure of constitutional machinery in a particular State.
  • Article 360 responds to threatened financial stability or credit of India or any part of its territory.
  • National Emergency needs parliamentary approval within one month; President's Rule and Financial Emergency need approval within two months.
  • National Emergency and President's Rule operate through six-month continuation periods, while Article 360 has no prescribed six-month renewal cycle.
  • Revocation and disapproval mechanisms preserve parliamentary control; the President is not the sole unchecked authority.
  • Emergency centralisation can affect federalism, but State institutions, courts, legislative procedure and constitutional limitations continue in the manner prescribed.
  • S.R. Bommai and post-44th safeguards are essential for explaining why Article 356 cannot be used as an unrestricted political weapon.

Key points

  • 352 - national security
  • 356 - State constitutional breakdown
  • 360 - financial stability or credit
  • Approval: one month for 352, two months for 356/360
  • Federalism and judicial review remain relevant.
