Indian Polity and Constitution

Directive Principles of State Policy and Fundamental Duties

Directive Principles of State Policy are contained in Part IV, Articles 36-51, and guide the State in building a just social order.

UP TGT PGT GK6 concepts42 quiz questions10-question test

Topic overview

  • DPSPs are not enforceable by courts, but Article 37 declares them fundamental in the governance of the country.
  • They cover social justice, economic democracy, village self-government, public health, environment, education and international peace.
  • Fundamental Duties are in Part IVA, Article 51A, and remind citizens of constitutional, civic, social and environmental responsibilities.
  • The relationship between Fundamental Rights and DPSPs has developed through constitutional amendments and Supreme Court interpretation.

Concept 1

Nature of DPSPs and Articles 36-39

DPSPs are constitutional instructions for a welfare-oriented State and set social and economic goals for governance.

Step-by-step explanation

  • Article 36 gives Part IV the same meaning of State as Part III unless the context requires otherwise.
  • Article 37 says DPSPs are not enforceable by any court, but they are fundamental in the governance of the country.
  • The State has a duty to apply these principles when making laws and public policies.
  • Article 38 asks the State to promote welfare through a social order informed by social, economic and political justice.
  • Article 38 also directs the State to minimise inequalities in income, status, facilities and opportunities.
  • Article 39 directs policy toward adequate livelihood, common good distribution of resources, prevention of wealth concentration and equal pay for equal work.
  • Article 39 also protects the health and strength of workers and the tender age of children from abuse and unsuitable employment.
  • DPSPs are goals for public policy, while Fundamental Rights are generally enforceable claims against unconstitutional State action.

Key points

  • Article 36 - State for Part IV
  • Article 37 - non-justiciable but fundamental in governance
  • Article 38 - welfare and reduced inequalities
  • Article 39 - livelihood, resources, equal pay and protection
  • DPSPs guide law and policy.
Worked examples
  • Writing a policy answer using Article 38
  • Distinguishing Article 39 policy goals from enforceable rights

Additional examples

  • Writing a policy answer using Article 38
  • Distinguishing Article 39 policy goals from enforceable rights
Important distinctions
  • DPSPs are non-justiciable directions; Fundamental Rights are judicially enforceable within constitutional limits.
  • Article 37 describes legal enforceability, not the importance of DPSPs in governance.
Common misconceptions
  • DPSPs are legally meaningless because courts cannot directly enforce them.
  • Article 37 makes every DPSP directly enforceable by an individual petition.
Exam focus

  • Use the phrase fundamental in governance.
  • Connect Article 39 with welfare-state and economic-justice questions.
Concept 2

Equal Justice, Panchayats, Work and Welfare: Articles 39A-43B

These principles connect access to justice, local democracy, labour welfare, living standards and cooperative organisation.

Step-by-step explanation

  • Article 39A directs the State to secure equal justice and provide free legal aid so that economic or other disabilities do not deny access to justice.
  • Article 40 directs the State to organise village panchayats and give them powers needed to function as units of self-government.
  • Article 41 concerns the right to work, education and public assistance within the limits of economic capacity and development.
  • Article 42 requires just and humane conditions of work and maternity relief.
  • Article 43 seeks a living wage, decent standard of life, leisure and social and cultural opportunities for workers.
  • Article 43A seeks participation of workers in the management of industries.
  • Article 43B promotes voluntary formation, autonomous functioning, democratic control and professional management of cooperative societies.
  • These principles are reflected through legal aid institutions, Panchayati Raj, labour laws, maternity benefits and cooperative legislation.

Key points

  • Article 39A - equal justice and free legal aid
  • Article 40 - village panchayats
  • Articles 41-42 - work, assistance and humane work conditions
  • Article 43 - living wage
  • Articles 43A-43B - worker participation and cooperatives.
Worked examples
  • Free legal services as an Article 39A objective
  • Panchayat as a unit of self-government under Article 40

Additional examples

  • Free legal services as an Article 39A objective
  • Panchayat as a unit of self-government under Article 40
Important distinctions
  • Article 39A concerns access to courts and legal aid; Article 40 concerns local self-government.
  • Article 43 seeks a living wage, which is higher than merely maintaining a minimum wage.
Common misconceptions
  • Article 40 itself creates directly elected Panchayats without legislation.
  • Article 41 creates an unlimited justiciable right to employment in every economic condition.
Exam focus
  • Match Articles 39A, 40, 42, 43, 43A and 43B.
  • Remember the economic-capacity qualification in Article 41.
  • Link Article 43B with cooperative societies and the 97th Amendment.
Concept 3

Uniform Civil Code, Children and Weaker Sections: Articles 44-46

These principles address common civil law, early childhood care and the advancement of disadvantaged communities.

Step-by-step explanation

  • Article 44 directs the State to secure a uniform civil code for citizens throughout India.
  • Article 44 is a Directive Principle and does not itself create an immediately enforceable individual right to a uniform code.
  • After the 86th Amendment, Article 45 focuses on early childhood care and education for children below six years.
  • The enforceable education guarantee for children aged six to fourteen is Article 21A, not the current Article 45.
  • Article 46 directs the State to promote the educational and economic interests of weaker sections of the people.
  • Article 46 gives special attention to Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes and seeks protection from social injustice and exploitation.
  • The three Articles show the relationship between a common civil framework, child development and social justice.
  • Policy measures under these Articles must remain consistent with Fundamental Rights and other constitutional provisions.

Key points

  • Article 44 - Uniform Civil Code
  • Article 45 - early childhood care below six
  • Article 21A - education for ages 6-14
  • Article 46 - weaker sections, SCs and STs
  • All are DPSP goals, not identical enforceable rights.
Worked examples
  • Distinguishing Article 45 from Article 21A
  • Using Article 46 to explain affirmative welfare policy

Additional examples

  • Distinguishing Article 45 from Article 21A
  • Using Article 46 to explain affirmative welfare policy
Important distinctions
  • Article 44 concerns civil law; Article 45 concerns early childhood care; Article 46 concerns weaker-section advancement.
  • Article 21A is a Fundamental Right, while current Article 45 is a Directive Principle.
Common misconceptions
  • Article 44 is already an enforceable Fundamental Right.
  • Article 45 currently guarantees free education for every child up to fourteen.
Exam focus

  • Match Article 46 with SC, ST and weaker-section welfare.
  • Do not confuse Article 21A with Article 45.
Concept 4

Health, Agriculture, Environment and Heritage: Articles 47-49

DPSPs also direct the State to improve public health, agriculture, animal welfare, environmental protection and national heritage.

Step-by-step explanation

  • Article 47 makes improvement of nutrition, standard of living and public health primary duties of the State.
  • Article 47 also directs the State to endeavour to prohibit intoxicating drinks and drugs injurious to health, except for medicinal purposes.
  • Article 48 directs the State to organise agriculture and animal husbandry on modern and scientific lines.
  • Article 48 includes preservation and improvement of breeds and prohibition of slaughter of cows, calves and other milch and draught cattle as constitutionally stated.
  • Article 48A directs protection and improvement of the environment and safeguarding of forests and wildlife.
  • Article 49 requires the State to protect monuments, places and objects of artistic or historic interest declared to be of national importance.
  • Article 48A was added by the 42nd Amendment, while environmental protection is also supported by Fundamental Duties and judicial interpretation.
  • These principles are used in public health, nutrition, environmental, wildlife, heritage and sustainable-development policies.

Key points

  • Article 47 - nutrition, public health and prohibition
  • Article 48 - scientific agriculture and animal husbandry
  • Article 48A - environment, forests and wildlife
  • Article 49 - monuments of national importance
  • 42nd Amendment added Article 48A.
Worked examples
  • Environmental protection using Articles 48A and 51A(g)
  • Public nutrition policy under Article 47

Additional examples

  • Environmental protection using Articles 48A and 51A(g)
  • Public nutrition policy under Article 47
Important distinctions
  • Article 47 concerns health and nutrition; Article 48 concerns agriculture and animal husbandry; Article 48A concerns environment.
  • Article 49 protects declared national monuments, while Article 51A(f) creates a citizen duty to value heritage more broadly.
Common misconceptions
  • Article 48A was part of the original 1950 Constitution.
  • Article 47 makes prohibition an unconditional Fundamental Right.
Exam focus
  • Match Articles 47-49 with their exact fields.
  • Remember the 42nd Amendment and Article 48A.
  • Connect environmental DPSP with the related Fundamental Duty.
Concept 5

Separation of Judiciary and International Peace: Articles 50-51

The final DPSPs seek institutional independence within the State and peaceful relations with the international community.

Step-by-step explanation

  • Article 50 directs the State to separate the judiciary from the executive in the public services of the State.
  • The principle supports independent adjudication and reduces executive control over judicial functions at subordinate levels.
  • Article 51 directs the State to promote international peace and security.
  • Article 51 encourages just and honourable relations between nations, respect for international law and treaty obligations, and settlement of international disputes by arbitration.
  • These provisions are policy directions rather than directly enforceable individual claims.
  • Article 50 is concerned with domestic institutional separation, while Article 51 concerns India's international conduct.
  • The principles connect constitutional governance with rule of law, judicial independence and peaceful foreign relations.
  • Courts may use these provisions as interpretive support when applying constitutional values and legislation.

Key points

  • Article 50 - separation of judiciary from executive
  • Article 51 - international peace and security
  • Respect for international law and treaties
  • Arbitration for international disputes
  • Both are non-justiciable DPSPs.
Worked examples
  • Distinguishing Article 50 from separation of powers in the whole Constitution
  • Using Article 51 in an international-relations answer

Additional examples

  • Distinguishing Article 50 from separation of powers in the whole Constitution
  • Using Article 51 in an international-relations answer
Important distinctions
  • Article 50 concerns domestic public services; Article 51 concerns international relations.
  • DPSP-based institutional goals guide governance but do not by themselves issue a court order to create a policy.
Common misconceptions

  • Article 51 makes every international treaty automatically enforceable in domestic courts.

Concept 6

Fundamental Duties and the Balance with Fundamental Rights

Article 51A lists citizen duties, while constitutional interpretation seeks harmony between rights, directives and duties.

Step-by-step explanation

  • Fundamental Duties are contained in Part IVA, Article 51A and apply to citizens of India.
  • The original Constitution did not contain Fundamental Duties; they were added by the 42nd Constitutional Amendment Act, 1976 on the recommendation of the Swaran Singh Committee.
  • The original list contained ten duties; the 86th Amendment added the duty of a parent or guardian to provide opportunities for education to a child or ward between six and fourteen years.
  • Duties include respecting the Constitution, the National Flag and National Anthem, cherishing freedom-struggle ideals and upholding sovereignty, unity and integrity.
  • They also include defending the country, promoting harmony, renouncing practices derogatory to women, preserving composite culture and developing scientific temper and humanism.
  • Article 51A(g) requires protection and improvement of the natural environment and compassion for living creatures; Article 51A(h) concerns scientific temper.
  • Fundamental Duties are generally non-justiciable by themselves, but laws may give effect to them and courts may use them in interpretation.
  • The relationship between rights and DPSPs is intended to be harmonious; neither set should be read as automatically destroying the constitutional balance.

Key points

  • Part IVA, Article 51A
  • 42nd Amendment added ten duties in 1976
  • 86th Amendment added the education-related duty
  • Duties apply to citizens
  • Rights, DPSPs and duties should be read harmoniously.
Worked examples
  • Connecting Article 51A(g) with Article 48A
  • Explaining why a duty may support legislation without becoming a direct writ claim

Additional examples

  • Connecting Article 51A(g) with Article 48A
  • Explaining why a duty may support legislation without becoming a direct writ claim
Important distinctions
  • Fundamental Rights are mainly enforceable claims; DPSPs are governance directions; Fundamental Duties are civic responsibilities.
  • Article 51A applies to citizens, while many Fundamental Rights are available to every person.
Common misconceptions
  • Fundamental Duties were present in the original Constitution.
  • A citizen can file a writ solely to enforce any Fundamental Duty without a supporting law.
Exam focus
  • Remember the 42nd and 86th Amendments.
  • Revise environment, scientific temper, harmony and education duties.
  • Use the phrase harmonious construction for FR-DPSP-duty questions.
