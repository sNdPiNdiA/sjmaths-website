Indian Polity and Constitution

State Legislature: Vidhan Sabha, Vidhan Parishad and Procedure

The State Legislature consists of the Governor and, in States with bicameral legislatures, the Legislative Assembly and Legislative Council.

UP TGT PGT GK6 concepts42 quiz questions10-question test

Topic overview

  • The Vidhan Sabha is the directly elected popular House; the Vidhan Parishad is a continuing second chamber where it exists.
  • Uttar Pradesh has a bicameral legislature, so UP examinations require special attention to the Assembly-Council composition and powers.
  • The Assembly has the decisive role in money matters and government confidence, while the Council provides revision, representation and continuity.
  • Questions commonly test Articles 168-212, creation or abolition of a Council, Governor assent, State Bills, budget and the distinction between the two Houses.

Concept 1

Structure and Creation of a Legislative Council

A State may have one or two Houses, with the Legislative Assembly as the essential popular chamber.

Step-by-step explanation

  • Article 168 provides for a State Legislature consisting of the Governor and either one House or two Houses.
  • The Legislative Assembly is present in every State; the Legislative Council exists only in some States, including Uttar Pradesh.
  • Under Article 169, Parliament may create or abolish a State Legislative Council if the Assembly passes the required resolution.
  • The Assembly resolution requires a majority of the total membership and a majority of not less than two-thirds of members present and voting.
  • The Parliament law under Article 169 is not treated as a constitutional amendment under Article 368, even though it changes the State Legislature structure.
  • The Council is a continuing House and is not dissolved; approximately one-third of its members retire every second year.
  • The Assembly is the directly elected House and determines the ministry's confidence and the decisive control of State finance.
  • Bicameralism in a State creates a revising and representative second chamber, but it does not give the Council equal power in every field.

Key points

  • Article 168 - State Legislature
  • Assembly exists in every State
  • Article 169 - creation or abolition of Council
  • Council is permanent with staggered retirement
  • UP has a bicameral legislature.

Concept 2

Membership, Constituencies and Qualifications

The two State Houses use different representation methods and age qualifications.

Step-by-step explanation

  • Article 170 provides for directly elected Assembly members from territorial constituencies, subject to constitutional limits and delimitation law.
  • Article 171 provides different categories for Council membership: local authorities, graduates, teachers, Assembly members and Governor nominees.
  • The Council's nominated members should have special knowledge or practical experience in literature, science, art, cooperative movement and social service.
  • Article 173 requires citizenship, oath or affirmation and minimum age of 25 for Assembly and 30 for Council membership.
  • Other qualifications are prescribed by Parliament through election law, including elector and nomination requirements.
  • Article 191 lists disqualifications such as office of profit, unsound mind, insolvency, loss of citizenship and statutory disqualifications.
  • Questions about Council composition often test the six-year term, one-third retirement and the mixed electoral constituencies.
  • In UP, the Assembly and Council represent different routes of entry but together form the bicameral State Legislature.

Key points

  • Assembly - direct territorial election
  • Council - five categories of representation
  • Minimum age 25 Assembly and 30 Council
  • Article 173 qualifications
  • Article 191 disqualifications.

Concept 3

Sessions, Presiding Officers and Privileges

State legislative functioning depends on sessions, presiding officers and privileges of the Houses.

Step-by-step explanation

  • The Governor summons and prorogues the State Legislature and ensures that the gap between sessions does not exceed six months.
  • The Governor may dissolve the Legislative Assembly, but the Legislative Council as a continuing House is not dissolved.
  • The Assembly elects a Speaker and Deputy Speaker from among its members under Article 178.
  • The Council elects a Chairman and Deputy Chairman from among its members under Article 182.
  • The Speaker presides over Assembly proceedings, maintains order and decides procedural questions under the House rules.
  • The Chairman performs the corresponding presiding role in the Council, subject to the constitutional rules of that House.
  • State Houses have freedom of speech and privileges under Article 194, subject to the Constitution and House rules.
  • Adjournment, prorogation and dissolution have distinct meanings at State level just as they do in Parliament.

Key points

  • Article 174 - State sessions
  • Six-month maximum gap
  • Assembly elects Speaker
  • Council elects Chairman
  • Article 194 - State legislative privileges.

Concept 4

Bills and the Legislative Procedure

The Assembly has the stronger position in a bicameral State, but the Council can revise and delay ordinary legislation.

Step-by-step explanation

  • An ordinary Bill may originate in either House of a bicameral State Legislature.
  • Both Houses must pass an ordinary Bill in the same form before it is presented for the Governor's action.
  • The Council may reject, amend or delay an Assembly Bill, but the Constitution limits the duration of its delaying power.
  • If the Council does not pass an Assembly Bill within the first period, the Assembly may pass it again and the Bill can proceed after the prescribed second period.
  • There is no joint sitting provision for resolving a State bicameral deadlock; the Assembly's repeated passage operates within Article 197.
  • A Bill pending in the Council does not lapse merely because the Assembly is dissolved, while a Bill pending in the Assembly may lapse subject to constitutional exceptions.
  • The Governor may assent, withhold assent, return a non-Money Bill or reserve it for the President under Article 200.
  • A Bill reserved for the President is governed by Article 201 and is not treated as an ordinary Governor-assent situation.

Key points

  • Ordinary Bill may begin in either House
  • Council has delaying, not permanent veto, power
  • Article 197 limits Council delay
  • No State joint sitting provision
  • Governor acts under Articles 200-201.

Concept 5

Money Bills, Budget and State Finance

The Legislative Assembly controls State grants and taxation while the Council has a restricted financial role.

Step-by-step explanation

  • Article 199 defines a State Money Bill by matters such as taxation, borrowing, Consolidated Fund custody and appropriation.
  • A State Money Bill can be introduced only in the Legislative Assembly and only on the Governor's recommendation.
  • The Assembly Speaker certifies whether a Bill is a Money Bill in a bicameral State.
  • The Legislative Council may make recommendations on a Money Bill and must return it within 14 days; the Assembly may accept or reject them.
  • Article 202 requires the Governor to cause the Annual Financial Statement of the State to be laid before the Legislature.
  • Demands for grants are voted by the Legislative Assembly; expenditure charged on the Consolidated Fund is discussed but not voted.
  • The Appropriation Bill authorises withdrawal from the Consolidated Fund of the State, while the Finance Bill gives effect to taxation proposals.
  • The Council cannot vote on demands for grants or use its limited Money Bill role to remove the Assembly's financial primacy.

Key points

  • Article 199 - State Money Bill
  • Only Assembly introduces and votes grants
  • Council has 14-day recommendation role
  • Article 202 - State Annual Financial Statement
  • Governor recommendation required.

Concept 6

Governor Assent and Uttar Pradesh Focus

UP's bicameral legislature makes Governor assent, Council delay and Assembly confidence especially important.

Step-by-step explanation

  • The Governor may assent to a Bill, withhold assent, return a non-Money Bill for reconsideration or reserve it for the President under Article 200.
  • A Money Bill cannot be returned for reconsideration in the same manner as a non-Money Bill.
  • If the Bill is reserved, the President may assent or withhold assent under Article 201; the State House does not receive an ordinary Governor-assent outcome.
  • The Governor normally acts on the aid and advice of the State Council of Ministers, but the Constitution gives limited discretion in specified situations.
  • The Chief Minister and State Council must maintain the confidence of the Legislative Assembly, not the Council.
  • The Council remains useful for review and representation but cannot form the confidence basis of the ministry.
  • Uttar Pradesh has both Vidhan Sabha and Vidhan Parishad, so questions may ask which House controls money, confidence or delay.
  • The State Legislature also exercises law-making, budgetary, deliberative and executive-accountability functions within the constitutional distribution.

Key points

  • Article 200 - Governor options
  • Article 201 - reserved Bill
  • Assembly controls confidence
  • UP is bicameral
  • Council reviews but cannot determine ministry survival.
Worked examples
  • Applying Governor options to a non-Money Bill

Additional examples

Important distinctions
  • Governor assent is State-level; President assent under Article 201 follows reservation.
  • Vidhan Sabha controls confidence and grants; Vidhan Parishad reviews and delays within limits.
Common misconceptions
  • The Chief Minister is responsible to the Council rather than the Assembly.
  • A Governor can turn every State Bill into a permanent veto without constitutional limits.
Exam focus
