Classical Dance Forms of India: UP TGT/PGT Complete Study Guide

India possesses eight classical dance forms recognized officially by the Sangeet Natak Akademi.

UP TGT PGT GK6 concepts42 quiz questions10-question test

Topic overview

  • India possesses eight classical dance forms recognized officially by the Sangeet Natak Akademi.
  • These dance forms trace their roots to ancient texts such as Natya Shastra by Sage Bharata.
  • Each classical dance combines two core elements: Natya (dramatic representation) and Nritta (pure dance movements).
  • These traditions are vital for UP TGT/PGT examinations, frequently featuring questions on regions, exponents, and foundational texts.

Concept 1 · foundation

Foundations: Natya Shastra and Theoretical Frameworks

The Natya Shastra authored by Sage Bharata serves as the primary theoretical fountainhead for all Indian classical dance forms.

Prerequisites

  • Basic understanding of ancient Indian textual traditions
  • Familiarity with Vedic and post-Vedic performing arts concepts
  • Awareness of the distinction between folk and classical arts

Definitions and scope

  • Natya Shastra: An ancient Indian treatise on the performing arts, attributed to Sage Bharata Muni.
  • Natya: The dramatic element involving speech, mime, and music.
  • Nritta: Pure rhythmic dance movements without specific narrative meaning.
  • Nritya: Expressive dance combining mime and mood to convey a story or theme.

Step-by-step explanation

  • The Natya Shastra is often referred to as the Fifth Veda, created by Brahma by drawing elements from the four Vedas.
  • It outlines the theory of Tandava (masculine, vigorous dance associated with Shiva) and Lasya (feminine, graceful dance associated with Parvati).
  • Abhinaya (expression) is classified into four types: Angika (bodily), Vachika (verbal), Aharya (costume/makeup), and Satvika (psychological/emotional).
  • The concept of Rasa (aesthetic flavor or sentiment) and Bhava (emotions) form the psychological foundation of classical Indian dance.
  • There are traditionally eight Rasas outlined in Natya Shastra, later expanded to nine (Navarasa) with the addition of Shanta (peace).
  • Hand gestures or mudras (Asamyuta and Samyuta Hastas) are codified systematically in texts like Natya Shastra and Abhinaya Darpana.
  • Footwork (Padachalana) and body postures (Stthanas and Charis) provide structural rigidity to classical training.
  • The evolution from temple rituals (Devadasi traditions) to court patronage and modern proscenium stages forms the historical trajectory of these arts.

Key points

  • Authored by Sage Bharata Muni.
  • Known as the Fifth Veda.
  • Divides performance into Natya, Nritta, and Nritya.
  • Defines Tandava and Lasya styles.
  • Introduces the concept of Navarasa (nine sentiments).
  • Codifies hastas (hand gestures) and abhinaya (expression).
Worked examples
  • Example 1: A dancer portraying grief (Karuna Rasa) uses Satvika abhinaya along with drooping shoulders and slow footwork as prescribed in Natya Shastra.
  • Example 2: A pure rhythmic sequence performed to complex tala cycles without storytelling illustrates Nritta.

Applications and connections

  • Examinations: Frequently asked questions on authorship of Natya Shastra and classification of abhinaya.
  • Choreography: Used by modern directors to structure classical and semi-classical ballet performances.
  • Cultural Studies: Essential for decoding temple sculptures and iconographic representations across India.

Advanced points, exceptions and limitations

  • The text incorporates detailed stage architecture and acoustics.
  • It establishes a deep connection between musicology (Sangeet) and movement.
  • Regional texts like Nandikeshvara's Abhinaya Darpana complement Natya Shastra for practical execution.

Additional examples

  • Brahma creating Natya Veda taking pathya from Rigveda, abhinaya from Yajurveda, geeta from Samaveda, and rasa from Atharvaveda.
  • Shiva performing Tandava at Chidambaram.
  • Parvati teaching Lasya to Banasura's daughter Usha.
Important distinctions
  • Natya Shastra vs. Abhinaya Darpana: Natya Shastra is a comprehensive encyclopedic treatise, whereas Abhinaya Darpana is a concise manual focused primarily on gestures and body movements.
  • Nritta vs. Nritya: Nritta is abstract rhythmic movement, while Nritya incorporates expressive storytelling.
  • Tandava vs. Lasya: Tandava is energetic and masculine; Lasya is delicate and feminine.
Common misconceptions
  • Misconception: Natya Shastra applies only to Bharatanatyam. Fact: It is the foundational text for all recognized Indian classical dance forms.
  • Misconception: Tandava is performed exclusively by men. Fact: Female dancers frequently execute dynamic Tandava passages.
Exam focus
  • Author of Natya Shastra (Sage Bharata).
  • Four types of Abhinaya.
  • Definition of Navarasa.
  • Distinction between Nritta, Nritya, and Natya.
  • Vedic association of the Natya Shastra.
Concept 2 · core

Core Classical Forms I: Bharatanatyam and Kathak

Bharatanatyam and Kathak represent the two major classical pillars of Southern and Northern India respectively, each with distinct historical lineages.

Prerequisites

  • Knowledge of basic Natya Shastra terminology
  • Understanding of regional geography of India
  • Familiarity with temple architecture of Tamil Nadu and North Indian court cultures

Definitions and scope

  • Bharatanatyam: A solo classical dance from Tamil Nadu based on Bha (Bhava), Ra (Raga), Ta (Tala), and Natyam.
  • Kathak: A North Indian classical dance originating from the word 'Katha' (story), traditionally performed by storytellers.
  • Adavu: Basic dance units in Bharatanatyam combining footwork, body posture, and hand gestures.
  • Tatkar: Basic footwork syllables in Kathak (e.g., ta thai thai tat).

Step-by-step explanation

  • Bharatanatyam originated in the Hindu temples of Tamil Nadu, formerly performed by Devadasis.
  • The repertoire of Bharatanatyam traditionally follows a margam sequence: Alarippu, Jatiswaram, Shabdam, Varnam, Padam, Stuti, and Thillana.
  • Physically, Bharatanatyam features the Aramandi posture (half-sitting position with knees turned outward) and symmetrical geometry.
  • Kathak evolved in northern India under the influence of Bhakti movement and later flourished in Mughal imperial courts.
  • Kathak is characterized by spins (chakkars), intricate footwork adorned with ankle bells (ghungroos), and subtle facial expressions.
  • Kathak is categorized into major gharanas: Lucknow Gharana (grace and expression), Jaipur Gharana (speed and technical footwork), and Raigarh/Banaras Gharanas.
  • Eminent revivalists like Rukmini Devi Arundale purified Bharatanatyam and established Kalakshetra in Chennai.
  • Legendary exponents like Birju Maharaj popularized the Lucknow gharana of Kathak globally through cinematic and traditional presentations.

Key points

  • Bharatanatyam belongs to Tamil Nadu; Kathak belongs to Northern India / Uttar Pradesh.
  • Bharatanatyam utilizes the Aramandi posture and Alarippu-to-Thillana sequence.
  • Kathak features rapid chakkars, intricate ghungroo work, and narrative abhinaya.
  • Key Kathak gharanas: Lucknow, Jaipur, Banaras.
  • Key Bharatanatyam revivalist: Rukmini Devi Arundale.
  • Key Kathak maestro: Pandit Birju Maharaj.
Worked examples
  • Worked Example 1: In a Bharatanatyam recital, the dancer begins with Alarippu (invocation) to warm up the body and invoke divine blessings before moving to complex rhythm cycles in Varnam.
  • Worked Example 2: In Kathak, a dancer executes an 'amad' (introduction) followed by Tukras, Toras, and rhythmic variations ending in a sharp 'tihai'.

Applications and connections

  • UP TGT/PGT Exams: Direct questions regarding Kathak gharanas, UP roots of Kathak, and Bharatanatyam repertoire terms.
  • Cultural Festivals: Standard items in national integration and tourism festivals across India.
  • Performing Arts Education: Forms the core curriculum in national academies like Sangeet Natak Akademi and Kathak Kendra.

Advanced points, exceptions and limitations

  • Bharatanatyam emphasizes sculpturesque poses mirroring Chidambaram temple reliefs.
  • Kathak incorporates Tarana and Thumri abhinaya, reflecting Persian and Sufi influences acquired during the Mughal era.
  • Both forms have evolved from devotional temple/court settings to proscenium stages with orchestral accompaniment.

Additional examples

  • Rukmini Devi Arundale performing Bharatanatyam at Kalakshetra.
  • Birju Maharaj choreographing classical sequences in Bollywood films like Shatranj ke Khiladi and Devdas.
  • Yamini Krishnamurthy excelling in both Bharatanatyam and Kuchipudi.
Important distinctions
  • Bharatanatyam vs. Kathak: Bharatanatyam is earth-bound with bent knees (Aramandi); Kathak stands erect with straight legs and emphasizes fluid spins.
  • Accompaniment: Bharatanatyam uses Carnatic music; Kathak uses Hindustani classical music with pakhawaj and tabla.
  • Geographic origin: Tamil Nadu (South) vs. Uttar Pradesh/North India.
Common misconceptions
  • Misconception: Kathak is purely a Moghul court dance. Fact: It originated in ancient Hindu temples as a storytelling medium before receiving Mughal patronage.
  • Misconception: Bharatanatyam has no space for pure rhythm. Fact: Jatiswaram and Thillana are dedicated entirely to Nritta.
Exam focus
  • UP relevance: Kathak's primary North Indian / UP heritage.
  • Gharanas of Kathak (Lucknow, Jaipur, Banaras).
  • Margam structure of Bharatanatyam.
  • Role of Kalakshetra and Rukmini Devi Arundale.
  • Terminology: Aramandi, Tatkar, Tihai.
Concept 3 · core

Classical Forms II: Kerala Traditions (Kathakali and Mohiniyattam)

Kerala contributes two contrasting classical dance forms: Kathakali, a vigorous martial dance-drama, and Mohiniyattam, a gentle solo feminine dance.

Prerequisites

  • Understanding of Kerala's cultural heritage and ritual art forms
  • Basic knowledge of Sanskrit and Malayalam theatrical literature
  • Familiarity with traditional makeup and costume traditions

Definitions and scope

  • Kathakali: A classical dance-drama of Kerala renowned for elaborate facial makeup, vibrant costumes, and martial roots.
  • Mohiniyattam: The classical solo dance of Kerala performed by women, characterized by graceful swaying body movements.
  • Chutty: Elaborate white facial makeup applied using rice paste and lime in Kathakali.
  • Kasavu: Traditional off-white saree with a gold zari border worn in Mohiniyattam.

Step-by-step explanation

  • Kathakali integrates dance, music, acting, and martial arts (like Kalarippayattu) to enact epics like Ramayana and Mahabharata.
  • Characters in Kathakali are classified through makeup types: Pacha (green/noble), Kathi (knife/villain), Kari (black/demoness), and Minukku (radiant/holy).
  • Vocal accompaniment in Kathakali uses Sopana Sangeet, sung from the steps of the sanctum sanctorum.
  • Mohiniyattam translates to 'dance of the enchantress (Mohini)', representing Vishnu's female avatar.
  • It features circular, pendulum-like body movements (Ati) and circular footwork, reflecting the waves of the Arabian Sea and swaying palm trees.
  • Mohiniyattam was revived in the 19th century by Travancore rulers Swathi Thirunal and poet Vallathol Narayana Menon (founder of Kerala Kalamandalam).
  • Both art forms draw heavily on regional literary traditions such as Manipravalam (a mix of Malayalam and Sanskrit).
  • Training in Kerala Kalamandalam emphasizes rigorous eye exercises, oil massage, and physical conditioning.

Key points

  • Kathakali is a vigorous dance-drama from Kerala with elaborate face paint.
  • Mohiniyattam is a graceful solo female dance from Kerala.
  • Kathakali utilizes martial training and character types (Pacha, Kathi, Minukku).
  • Mohiniyattam features circular swaying movements and Kasavu attire.
  • Vallathol Narayana Menon founded Kerala Kalamandalam to revive these arts.
  • Swathi Thirunal contributed immensely to musical compositions for Mohiniyattam.
Worked examples
  • Worked Example 1: A Kathakali actor portrays Ravana using the 'Kathi' makeup, conveying arrogance and power through wide eye movements and hand mudras.
  • Worked Example 2: A Mohiniyattam dancer executes a 'Cholukettu' or 'Padam', swaying gracefully in a white-and-gold saree with subtle emotional expressions.

Applications and connections

  • Examinations: Frequently asked questions pairing Kerala with Kathakali/Mohiniyattam and identifying makeup categories.
  • Cultural Preservation: Kerala Kalamandalam serves as a model institution for traditional arts training.
  • Interdisciplinary Arts: Studies in comparative dramaturgy and ritual theatre.

Advanced points, exceptions and limitations

  • Kathakali actors do not speak; the narrative is sung by off-stage vocalists (Bhagavathar) accompanied by Chenda and Maddalam drums.
  • Mohiniyattam lacks the heavy stamping of Kathakali, focusing instead on soft, undulating torso movements.
  • Both forms survived near-extinction during colonial times before institutional revival in the 20th century.

Additional examples

  • Kalamandalam Gopi portraying Nala or Arjuna in Kathakali.
  • Kanak Rele performing Mohiniyattam and standardizing its adavus.
  • Performances staged during the Onam festival in Kerala temples.
Important distinctions
  • Kathakali vs. Mohiniyattam: Kathakali is a male-dominated, heavy dance-drama with elaborate makeup; Mohiniyattam is a solo female dance with subtle facial expressions and simple white attire.
  • Music style: Kathakali uses Sopana Sangeet; Mohiniyattam uses Carnatic music with Malayalam/Sanskrit lyrics.
  • Physical intensity: Kathakali requires rigorous martial agility; Mohiniyattam focuses on lyrical grace.
Common misconceptions
  • Misconception: Kathakali actors speak their dialogues on stage. Fact: All dialogues and narrative text are sung by playback vocalists while actors use sign language and facial expressions.
  • Misconception: Mohiniyattam is identical to Bharatanatyam because both are South Indian. Fact: Mohiniyattam's torso movements and swaying postures are distinct from Bharatanatyam's geometrical precision.
Exam focus
  • Character makeup types in Kathakali (Pacha, Kathi, Kari, Minukku).
  • Role of Kerala Kalamandalam and Vallathol Narayana Menon.
  • Musical genre: Sopana Sangeet.
  • Costume and attire: Kasavu saree in Mohiniyattam.
  • Geographic roots in Kerala.
Concept 4 · core

Classical Forms III: Odissi and Kuchipudi

Odissi from Odisha and Kuchipudi from Andhra Pradesh bridge temple sculptural traditions with dynamic dramatic storytelling.

Prerequisites

  • Familiarity with East and South Indian temple architecture (e.g., Sun Temple at Konark)
  • Knowledge of Bhakti movement literature (e.g., Jayadeva's Gita Govinda)
  • Understanding of regional folk drama traditions

Definitions and scope

  • Odissi: A classical dance from Odisha characterized by sculptural Tribhanga postures and lyrical fluidity.
  • Kuchipudi: A classical dance-drama from Andhra Pradesh known for fast rhythms, dialogue, and dramatic characterization.
  • Tribhanga: A triple-bend posture of the body (neck, torso, and knees) prominent in Odissi.
  • Tarangam: A spectacular feat in Kuchipudi where the dancer dances on the rim of a brass plate while balancing a pot of water on the head.

Step-by-step explanation

  • Odissi is historically linked to the Maharis (temple dancers) and Gotipuas (young boys dressed as girls who preserved the tradition).
  • The sculptures of the Sun Temple at Konark and Jagannath Temple at Puri served as visual blueprints for reviving Odissi in the 1950s.
  • Key exponents like Kelucharan Mohapatra, Sanjukta Panigrahi, and Sonal Mansingh revived and codified Odissi into a global art form.
  • The repertoire of Odissi includes Mangalacharan, Batu Nritya, Pallavi, Thangam, and Moksha (final liberation).
  • Kuchipudi originated in the village of Kuchelapuram in Andhra Pradesh, formalized by Siddhendra Yogi in the 17th century.
  • Traditionally, Kuchipudi was performed exclusively by Brahmin male troupes as a dance-drama (Bhagavata Mela tradition).
  • Modern Kuchipudi is performed widely by solo female artists, retaining its unique blend of pure dance (Nritta) and expressive drama (Natya).
  • Eminent maestros like Vempati Chinna Satyam established academies (like Kuchipudi Art Academy in Chennai) to systematize training.

Key points

  • Odissi originates from Odisha; Kuchipudi originates from Andhra Pradesh.
  • Odissi is famous for the Tribhanga posture and Gita Govinda themes.
  • Kuchipudi features dramatic dialogues, fast footwork, and Tarangam (plate dancing).
  • Gotipuas and Maharis preserved early Odissi traditions.
  • Siddhendra Yogi is credited with formalizing Kuchipudi.
  • Key maestros: Kelucharan Mohapatra (Odissi) and Vempati Chinna Satyam (Kuchipudi).
Worked examples
  • Worked Example 1: An Odissi dancer strikes the Tribhanga posture while enacting verses from Jayadeva's Gita Govinda describing Radha's yearning for Krishna.
  • Worked Example 2: A Kuchipudi dancer places feet on the edges of a brass plate, balancing a pot of water on the head while executing intricate tala patterns.

Applications and connections

  • Examinations: Common MCQs on Tribhanga posture, Konark temple links, and Tarangam association with Kuchipudi.
  • Cultural Heritage: Showcasing regional temple art traditions on international platforms.
  • Dance Pedagogy: Standard syllabi in university fine arts departments.

Advanced points, exceptions and limitations

  • Odissi incorporates Chowk (square masculine stance) and Tribhanga (feminine lyrical stance), creating a unique spatial dynamic.
  • Kuchipudi uniquely retains spoken dialogues (Vachika abhinaya) in traditional dance-drama formats, distinguishing it from other solo-oriented classical forms.
  • Both forms draw deeply on Vaishnavite philosophy and Bhakti literature.

Additional examples

  • Kelucharan Mohapatra sculpting Odissi technique through meticulous research of temple reliefs.
  • Sanjukta Panigrahi performing Jayadeva's Ashtapadis in Odissi.
  • Yamini Krishnamurthy and Vempati Chinna Satyam popularizing Kuchipudi.
Important distinctions
  • Odissi vs. Kuchipudi: Odissi emphasizes sculptural fluidity and the Tribhanga stance; Kuchipudi emphasizes brisk rhythmic footwork, dramatic dialogue, and acrobatic items like Tarangam.
  • Geographic roots: Odisha (East) vs. Andhra Pradesh (South-East).
  • Historical preservation: Gotipuas in Odisha vs. Bhagavata Mela troupes in Andhra Pradesh.
Common misconceptions
  • Misconception: Odissi is an ancient dance performed continuously without interruption. Fact: While based on ancient temple sculptures, modern Odissi was reconstructed and codified in the mid-20th century by scholars and gurus.
  • Misconception: Kuchipudi is only a solo female dance. Fact: It originated as a male group dance-drama tradition.
Exam focus
  • Tribhanga posture association (Odissi).
  • Tarangam and brass plate performance (Kuchipudi).
  • Role of Gotipuas and Maharis.
  • Gita Govinda and Jayadeva's influence.
  • Key gurus: Kelucharan Mohapatra and Vempati Chinna Satyam.
Concept 5 · core

Classical Forms IV: Manipuri, Sattriya, and Regional Traditions

Manipuri from the Northeast and Sattriya from Assam represent profound devotional traditions rooted in Vaishnavite theology.

Prerequisites

  • Geographical familiarity with Northeast India (Manipur and Assam)
  • Understanding of Gaudiya Vaishnavism and Bhakti movement saints
  • Awareness of tribal and folk cultural confluence in eastern India

Definitions and scope

  • Manipuri: A classical dance from Manipur characterized by gentle, flowing movements and the distinctive barrel-shaped Kumil skirt.
  • Sattriya: A classical dance from Assam introduced by saint Srimanta Sankaradeva, traditionally performed in Sattras (monasteries).
  • Kumil: The rigid, cylindrical embroidered skirt worn by female dancers in Manipuri Ras Lila.
  • Sattras: Monastic institutions established in Assam by Sankaradeva that preserve Assamese art and culture.

Step-by-step explanation

  • Manipuri dance is deeply spiritual, focusing on the divine love of Radha and Krishna through themes like Ras Lila.
  • King Bhagyachandra of Manipur played a pivotal role in the 18th century by designing the Kumil costume and patronizing theatrical presentations.
  • The dance avoids abrupt jerks, featuring soft, curving hand and foot movements that resemble floating water.
  • Instruments central to Manipuri include the Pung (Manipuri barrel drum), played energetically by male dancers while executing acrobatics.
  • Sattriya was created by the 15th-century Bhakti reformer Srimanta Sankaradeva in Assam as a medium for propagating Neo-Vaishnavism.
  • It was traditionally performed by male monks (Bhokots) inside Sattras as part of daily religious rituals and Ankiya Naat (one-act plays).
  • Sattriya was officially recognized as the eighth classical dance of India by the Sangeet Natak Akademi in the year 2000.
  • Eminent proponents like Jatin Goswami and Bhabananda Barbayan have brought Sattriya from monastery floors to global proscenium stages.

Key points

  • Manipuri originates from Manipur; Sattriya originates from Assam.
  • Manipuri features the Kumil costume, Pung drumming, and Ras Lila themes.
  • Sattriya originates from Assamese Sattras and was founded by Srimanta Sankaradeva.
  • Sattriya was recognized as classical in 2000.
  • King Bhagyachandra patronized Manipuri dance.
  • Both forms are deeply anchored in Vaishnavite philosophy.
Worked examples
  • Worked Example 1: In a Manipuri Ras Lila, female dancers wear the rigid embroidered Kumil and transparent veil, moving with delicate, circular footwork around Krishna.
  • Worked Example 2: In a Sattriya performance, male monks (Bhokots) perform vigorous footwork and abhinaya depicting celestial tales from the Bhagavata Purana.

Applications and connections

  • Examinations: Frequently tested on the founding saint of Sattriya (Sankaradeva), the recognition year (2000), and Manipuri's distinctive drum (Pung).
  • Cultural Diplomacy: Representing India's northeastern heritage in national and international festivals.
  • Ethnomusicology: Studying the integration of tribal rhythms with classical devotion.

Advanced points, exceptions and limitations

  • Manipuri incorporates both Tandava and Lasya, with male dancers performing martial cholom dances while playing the Pung.
  • Sattriya encompasses diverse styles including Mati Akhara (basic foundational exercises) and Ojapali traditions.
  • Unlike forms derived purely from temple courtesans, Sattriya evolved within an institutional monastic framework.

Additional examples

  • Jhaveri Sisters performing and preserving Manipuri dance across India.
  • Monks performing Sattriya inside the Majuli island Sattras.
  • Kartal Cholom and Pung Cholom performed during Yaoshang festival in Manipur.
Important distinctions
  • Manipuri vs. Sattriya: Manipuri is known for soft floating movements and the cylindrical Kumil skirt; Sattriya is rooted in Assamese monastic Sattras and blends devotional vigor with graceful storytelling.
  • Geographical base: Manipur (Northeast hill valley) vs. Assam (Brahmaputra valley).
  • Institutional origin: Royal court patronage under Bhagyachandra (Manipuri) vs. Monastic Sattras under Sankaradeva (Sattriya).
Common misconceptions
  • Misconception: Sattriya is a folk dance of Assam like Bihu. Fact: Sattriya is a codified classical dance form with strict grammar, mudras, and spiritual literature, distinct from folk dances like Bihu.
  • Misconception: Manipuri dance has no athletic movements. Fact: Male dancers perform high-flying leaps and acrobatics while playing the Pung drum in Pung Cholom.
Exam focus
  • Founder of Sattriya (Srimanta Sankaradeva).
  • Year Sattriya gained classical status (2000).
  • Manipuri costume (Kumil) and drum (Pung).
  • Monastic context of Assamese Sattras.
  • Vaishnavite themes (Radha-Krishna lila).
Concept 6 · exam-synthesis

Advanced Comparative Synthesis and UP TGT/PGT Examination Strategy

Mastering classical dances for UP TGT/PGT requires synthesizing regional roots, institutional origins, key gurus, and textual authorities.

Prerequisites

  • Complete mastery of all eight classical dance forms
  • Familiarity with Sangeet Natak Akademi listings
  • Understanding of historical revival movements in 20th-century India

Definitions and scope

  • Sangeet Natak Akademi: India's national academy for music, dance, and drama, responsible for official classical dance recognition.
  • Gharana: A system of social organization in the Indian performing arts linking disciples to a lineage of teachers.
  • Abhinaya: The art of expression in Indian performance, translating textual meaning to audience emotion.

Step-by-step explanation

  • UP TGT/PGT examinations frequently test matching questions: Dance Form versus State of Origin (e.g., Sattriya - Assam, Kathakali - Kerala).
  • Another high-yield area is matching legendary exponents to their respective dance forms (e.g., Kelucharan Mohapatra - Odissi, Birju Maharaj - Kathak).
  • Chronological and institutional queries often feature revivalists like Rukmini Devi Arundale (Bharatanatyam), Vallathol Narayana Menon (Kathakali/Mohiniyattam), and Sankaradeva (Sattriya).
  • Questions frequently differentiate between pure dance (Nritta), expressive dance (Nritya), and dramatic enactment (Natya).
  • Aspirants must note that while 8 classical dances are officially recognized by the Sangeet Natak Akademi, other regional forms like Chhau are sometimes categorized under semi-classical or martial traditions.
  • Understanding textual sources such as Natya Shastra, Abhinaya Darpana, and Sangita Ratnakara is essential for answering theoretical assertion-reason questions.
  • Thorough revision of regional musical accompaniments (Carnatic vs. Hindustani) prevents common confusion in exam halls.
  • Practicing previous years' patterns ensures quick identification of trap options regarding state origins and founder-saints.

Key points

  • 8 official classical dance forms recognized by Sangeet Natak Akademi.
  • State mapping is the most common direct question type.
  • Guru-exponent pairings carry high weightage in PGT exams.
  • Textual authorities (Natya Shastra) form the bedrock of theoretical questions.
  • Distinguishing folk from classical (e.g., Bihu vs. Sattriya) is critical.
  • UP connection: Kathak's strong North Indian and regional heritage.
Worked examples
  • Worked Example 1 (Match-following question): Match List I (Dance) with List II (State) -> A. Sattriya (Assam), B. Kuchipudi (Andhra Pradesh), C. Mohiniyattam (Kerala), D. Odissi (Odisha).
  • Worked Example 2 (Assertion-Reason): Assertion: Kathakali actors speak their dialogues on stage. Reason: Kathakali utilizes Vachika abhinaya spoken by the actor. (Correct evaluation: Both Assertion and Reason are false, as vocalists sing off-stage).

Applications and connections

  • UP TGT/PGT Arts and General Studies papers.
  • Civil services and state commission examinations.
  • Cultural lectures and academic research in Indian aesthetics.

Advanced points, exceptions and limitations

  • Recognition criteria by Sangeet Natak Akademi involve historical antiquity, textual backing, and widespread institutional training.
  • The evolution from temple rituals to state-sponsored proscenium arts reflects modern India's cultural preservation policies.
  • Interdisciplinary analysis of sculpture, music, and literature enhances scoring capability in descriptive PGT mains.

Additional examples

  • Solving a multi-statement question on Sankaradeva's contributions to Sattriya.
  • Analyzing the gharana differences in Kathak between Lucknow and Jaipur.
  • Identifying correct abhinaya classifications in a case-based question.
Important distinctions
  • Official SNA Classical Dances (8 forms) vs. Folk/Semi-Classical Traditions (Chhau, Bhangra, Bihu): SNA classical forms possess codified textual grammar (Natya Shastra), whereas folk dances are community-based celebrations without rigid classical treatises.
  • North Indian (Kathak, Sattriya, Manipuri) vs. South Indian (Bharatanatyam, Kathakali, Mohiniyattam, Kuchipudi, Odissi) stylistic divisions.
  • Solo traditions (Bharatanatyam, Mohiniyattam, Odissi) vs. Dance-drama traditions (Kathakali, Kuchipudi).
Common misconceptions
  • Misconception: There are more than 8 classical dances recognized by the Government of India. Fact: Sangeet Natak Akademi officially recognizes exactly 8 classical dance forms, though Ministry of Culture occasionally includes others like Chhau in expanded lists.
  • Misconception: All classical dances use the same musical system. Fact: Northern forms use Hindustani music, while Southern and Eastern forms (Bharatanatyam, Kuchipudi, Mohiniyattam, Kathakali, Odissi) primarily use Carnatic or regional classical music systems.
Exam focus
  • Complete list of 8 dances and their states.
  • Major exponents for each of the 8 forms.
  • Foundational texts and authors.
  • UP specific cultural relevance (Kathak).
  • Distinction between Nritta, Nritya, and Natya.

Rapid revision: Classical Dance Forms

Review every concept point-wise before attempting the quiz and timed test.

Concept 1

Foundations: Natya Shastra and Theoretical Frameworks

Definition and scope

  • Natya Shastra is the foundational treatise on performing arts attributed to Sage Bharata.
  • Divides performance into Natya, Nritta, and Nritya.
  • Establishes Navarasa (nine sentiments) and four-fold Abhinaya.

Must remember

  • Author: Sage Bharata Muni
  • Referred to as the Fifth Veda
  • Tandava (Shiva) vs Lasya (Parvati)
  • Four Abhinayas: Angika, Vachika, Aharya, Satvika
  • Nine Rasas including Shanta

Concept-specific exam traps

  • Confusing Abhinaya Darpana with Natya Shastra authorship.
  • Assuming Tandava is exclusively male.
Concept 2

Core Classical Forms I: Bharatanatyam and Kathak

Definition and scope

  • Bharatanatyam is Tamil Nadu's classical solo dance following the Margam sequence.
  • Kathak is North India's storytelling dance characterized by footwork and chakkars.
  • Gharanas define regional stylistic lineages in Kathak.

Must remember

  • Bharatanatyam origin: Tamil Nadu
  • Kathak origin: Uttar Pradesh / North India
  • Key Kathak gharanas: Lucknow, Jaipur, Banaras
  • Rukmini Devi Arundale revived Bharatanatyam (Kalakshetra)
  • Birju Maharaj was a legendary Kathak exponent

Concept-specific exam traps

  • Thinking Kathak originated purely in Mughal courts rather than Hindu temples.
  • Confusing Bharatanatyam's Aramandi posture with Odissi's Tribhanga.
Concept 3

Classical Forms II: Kerala Traditions (Kathakali and Mohiniyattam)

Definition and scope

  • Kathakali is a vigorous Kerala dance-drama with elaborate facial makeup.
  • Mohiniyattam is Kerala's graceful solo female dance.
  • Kerala Kalamandalam is the premier institution for both.

Must remember

  • Kathakali makeup types: Pacha, Kathi, Kari, Minukku
  • Mohiniyattam attire: Kasavu saree
  • Founder of Kerala Kalamandalam: Vallathol Narayana Menon
  • Musical style: Sopana Sangeet in Kathakali
  • Off-stage vocalists narrate the Kathakali story

Concept-specific exam traps

  • Believing Kathakali actors speak dialogues on stage.
  • Confusing Mohiniyattam with Bharatanatyam due to South Indian roots.
Concept 4

Classical Forms III: Odissi and Kuchipudi

Definition and scope

  • Odissi features Tribhanga postures inspired by Konark temple sculptures.
  • Kuchipudi is an Andhra Pradesh dance-drama known for Tarangam.
  • Gotipuas and Maharis preserved early Odissi.

Must remember

  • Odissi origin: Odisha
  • Kuchipudi origin: Andhra Pradesh
  • Tribhanga posture belongs to Odissi
  • Tarangam (brass plate dance) belongs to Kuchipudi
  • Key gurus: Kelucharan Mohapatra and Vempati Chinna Satyam

Concept-specific exam traps

  • Assuming Odissi is an uninterrupted ancient dance rather than mid-20th century revival.
  • Thinking Kuchipudi is exclusively a solo female dance today without group drama roots.
Concept 5

Classical Forms IV: Manipuri, Sattriya, and Regional Traditions

Definition and scope

  • Manipuri features soft movements, Kumil skirt, and Pung drumming.
  • Sattriya is an Assamese monastic dance created by Sankaradeva.
  • Sattriya gained classical status in 2000.

Must remember

  • Manipuri origin: Manipur
  • Sattriya origin: Assam
  • Sattriya founder: Srimanta Sankaradeva
  • Sattriya recognition year: 2000
  • Manipuri costume: Kumil; Drum: Pung

Concept-specific exam traps

  • Confusing Sattriya with Assamese folk dance Bihu.
  • Assuming Manipuri lacks athletic elements (forgetting Pung Cholom).
Concept 6

Advanced Comparative Synthesis and UP TGT/PGT Examination Strategy

Definition and scope

  • Sangeet Natak Akademi officially recognizes 8 classical dance forms.
  • Exam questions test state mappings, exponent pairings, and textual authorities.
  • Distinguishing Nritta, Nritya, and Natya is critical for scoring.

Must remember

  • Total recognized classical dances: 8
  • Match states to dance forms accurately
  • Learn top 2-3 exponents for each dance
  • Review UP-specific relevance of Kathak
  • Master Natya Shastra terminology

Concept-specific exam traps

  • Assuming folk dances like Bhangra or Garba are classical.
  • Mixing up Carnatic and Hindustani music associations.

Quick facts

  • Sage Bharata Muni authored the Natya Shastra, known as the Fifth Veda.
  • Sangeet Natak Akademi officially recognizes eight classical dance forms in India.
  • Sattriya of Assam was the last dance form to be granted classical status in 2000.
  • Kathak is the only classical dance form with distinct ties to North India and Uttar Pradesh.
  • Rukmini Devi Arundale founded Kalakshetra to revive Bharatanatyam.
  • Kelucharan Mohapatra is celebrated as the modern maestro of Odissi.
  • Vallathol Narayana Menon founded Kerala Kalamandalam for Kathakali and Mohiniyattam.

Glossary

Natya ShastraAncient Indian treatise on dramaturgy and performing arts by Sage Bharata.
NrittaPure rhythmic dance movements without narrative meaning.
NrityaExpressive dance combining mime and mood to convey a story.
AramandiHalf-sitting basic posture in Bharatanatyam with knees turned outward.
TribhangaThree-fold bending posture of the body prominent in Odissi.
TarangamA Kuchipudi item where the dancer balances on a brass plate.
KumilRigid barrel-shaped embroidered skirt worn in Manipuri dance.
SattraAssamese Vaishnavite monastery where Sattriya dance originated.

Important distinctions

BharatanatyamvsKathak
  • Bharatanatyam originates from Tamil Nadu; Kathak originates from North India / Uttar Pradesh.
  • Bharatanatyam uses bent knees (Aramandi) and Carnatic music; Kathak stands erect, features rapid spins, and uses Hindustani music.
KathakalivsMohiniyattam
  • Kathakali is a vigorous dance-drama with heavy facial makeup; Mohiniyattam is a graceful solo female dance.
  • Kathakali is traditionally male-dominated with martial roots; Mohiniyattam is performed by women with swaying torso movements.
OdissivsKuchipudi
  • Odissi emphasizes sculptural fluidity and Tribhanga stance; Kuchipudi features brisk footwork, dialogue, and Tarangam.
  • Odissi originates from Odisha; Kuchipudi originates from Andhra Pradesh.

Memory hooks

  • Mnemonic for 8 Classical Dances (BSO-KK-MMM): Bharatanatyam, Sattriya, Odissi, Kathak, Kathakali, Manipuri, Mohiniyattam, Kuchipudi.
  • State Association Hook: Kerala gives us two K's (Kathakali & Mohiniyattam) and Tamil Nadu gives Bharatanatyam.
  • Sattriya Founder Hook: Srimanta Sankaradeva started Sattriya in Assam in the 15th century.

Exam traps and cautions

  • Trap: Confusing Sattriya (Assam) with Santhal (Tribal folk dance).
  • Trap: Assuming Kathakali actors speak dialogues on stage instead of off-stage singers.
  • Trap: Believing the Government recognizes more than 8 official classical dance forms.
  • Trap: Forgetting that Kathak has deep UP gharana roots relevant for state exams.
