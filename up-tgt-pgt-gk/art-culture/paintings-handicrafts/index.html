Traditional Paintings and Handicrafts of India for UP TGT and PGT Art and Culture

Traditional Indian paintings and handicrafts represent centuries of indigenous artistic heritage, religious devotion, and rural craftsmanship across diverse geographic regions.

Topic overview

  • The UP TGT and PGT examinations frequently test regional origins, primary materials, distinct color sources, motifs, and Geographical Indication (GI) tags of these art forms.
  • Major traditional styles covered in the syllabus include Madhubani, Warli, Pattachitra, and Tanjore paintings, alongside numerous GI-tagged crafts.
  • Understanding these art forms requires mastery over technical processes, historical patrons, ritualistic foundations, and stylistic variations.

Concept 1 · foundation

Madhubani (Mithila) Painting

Madhubani painting is a vibrant folk art form originating from the Mithila region of Bihar, traditionally created by women on freshly plastered mud walls.

Prerequisites

  • Basic knowledge of Indian folk art traditions
  • Geographical awareness of the Mithila region spanning Bihar and Nepal

Definitions and scope

  • Madhubani: Literally means 'forest of honey' (Madhu = honey, vana = forest).
  • Mithila Painting: Alternate term reflecting its regional provenance.
  • Bharni, Kachni, Tantrik, Godna, and Kohbar: Five distinct styles within Madhubani art.

Step-by-step explanation

  • Mithila painting originated as a ritualistic wall art practiced during weddings and festivals like Diwali and Chhath.
  • Traditionally, women prepared walls using cow dung and mud plaster, known as 'gobar', before painting.
  • The Bharni style utilizes vibrant colors to fill large spaces depicting deities and natural elements.
  • The Kachni style is monochromatic or delicate, relying on fine line work and cross-hatching without heavy color filling.
  • The Tantrik style represents ritualistic diagrams, yantras, and mythological themes associated with specific religious sects.
  • Godna style features repetitive, tattoo-like motifs arranged in parallel bands or circles.
  • Kohbar style focuses on fertility, marriage, and union motifs, traditionally painted in the nuptial chamber.
  • Colors are derived entirely from natural sources: soot for black, turmeric for yellow, leaf extracts for green, and rice paste for white.

Key points

  • Region: Mithila (Bihar, India and Terai region of Nepal).
  • Surface: Originally mud walls, now cloth, handmade paper, and canvas.
  • Border: Distinctive double-line borders, often filled with cross-hatch or floral patterns.
  • Subject Matter: Hindu deities (Krishna, Rama, Durga), celestial bodies, flora, and fauna.
  • Absence of Empty Space: Every gap is filled with floral motifs, dots, or cross-hatching.
  • Pioneers: Sita Devi and Jagdamba Devi received national recognition and Padma Shri awards.
Worked examples
  • Step 1: Prepare handmade paper by treating it with cow dung solution or keeping it in its natural textured state. Step 2: Draw a double-line border. Step 3: Sketch central mythological figures using bamboo twigs or nib pens. Step 4: Fill enclosed spaces using organic pigments.
  • Exam application: Identifying that the Kohbar motif specifically relates to nuptial chambers and fertility symbols in Mithila folk art.

Applications and connections

  • Revival of rural artisan livelihoods through government-sponsored GI tagging and craft clusters.
  • Interior decoration and textile printing adapting authentic Madhubani motifs.
  • Cultural representation in international exhibitions and state museums.

Advanced points, exceptions and limitations

  • Transition from ephemeral wall paintings to durable commercial artifacts created a major socioeconomic shift for women artists in Bihar.
  • Inclusion of contemporary social themes (girl child education, environmental conservation) alongside traditional mythology.
  • Strict adherence to traditional iconographic rules while allowing individual stylistic expression in line work.

Additional examples

  • Depiction of Radha and Krishna amidst dense banana groves.
  • Images of the 'Tree of Life' symbolizing fertility and eternal life.
  • Ritual depictions of the Sun and Moon deities.
Important distinctions
  • Unlike Warli art which uses minimalist geometric stick figures, Madhubani uses detailed figurative and floral compositions.
  • Unlike Tanjore paintings which use heavy gold leaf and relief work, Madhubani relies on flat planes of color and intricate lines.
  • Madhubani was traditionally practiced exclusively by women, whereas many classical traditions had male artisan lineages.
Common misconceptions
  • Misconception: Madhubani uses synthetic acrylic paints. Correction: Traditional Madhubani strictly utilizes natural dyes and vegetable colors.
  • Misconception: It originated in urban Patna. Correction: It is strictly rural in origin, rooted in the villages of Darbhanga, Madhubani, and surrounding Mithila areas.
Exam focus
  • Remember the five sub-styles: Bharni, Kachni, Tantrik, Godna, Kohbar.
  • Note the GI tag status of Madhubani painting.
  • Identify the primary medium as natural dyes on handmade paper or mud walls.
  • Recall prominent Padma Shri recipient Sita Devi.
  • Understand the distinctive feature of zero negative space in composition.
Concept 2 · foundation

Warli Painting

Warli painting is a tribal art form of Maharashtra characterized by stark white pigments on mud walls, utilizing fundamental geometric shapes to depict communal life.

Prerequisites

  • Understanding of tribal art traditions in Western India
  • Basic familiarity with prehistoric rock art and folk motifs

Definitions and scope

  • Warli: Indigenous tribe inhabiting the mountainous and coastal areas along the Maharashtra-Gujarat border.
  • Tarpa Dance: A central ritualistic motif involving a circular dance around a musical instrument.
  • Palaghag: The marriage god depicted in central ritual paintings.

Step-by-step explanation

  • Warli paintings date back roots to the 10th century CE, though the visual tradition was formally documented extensively in the 1970s.
  • The traditional background consists of a mixture of mud, cow dung, and red earth, creating an opaque red-brown canvas.
  • The white pigment is prepared using a simple mixture of rice paste, water, and gum as a binding agent.
  • Tools used are rudimentary: bamboo sticks chewed at the end to serve as a paintbrush.
  • The artistic vocabulary rests on three fundamental geometric shapes: the circle (representing the sun and moon), the triangle (derived from mountains and conical trees), and the square (representing a sacred enclosure or piece of land).
  • Human and animal figures are constructed using two triangles joined at their apexes—representing the torso—along with simple lines for limbs.
  • The paintings lack perspective or proportion, focusing instead on narrative clarity, rhythm, and communal harmony.
  • Jivya Soma Mashe is internationally celebrated as the master artist who transitioned Warli art from mud walls to canvas and paper.

Key points

  • Region: Sahyadri mountain range, Palghar and Thane districts of Maharashtra.
  • Color Palette: Strictly bi-color (Red-brown background and white foreground).
  • Primary Shapes: Circle, triangle, square.
  • Core Theme: Celebration of nature, harvest, fertility, and community dances (Tarpa).
  • Medium: Mud wall base with rice paste paint.
  • Key Proponent: Jivya Soma Mashe.
Worked examples
  • Step 1: Coat a canvas or wall with reddish-brown earth and cow dung mixture. Step 2: Mix ground rice flour with water and natural gum. Step 3: Draw concentric circles and spiraling lines to depict the Tarpa dance. Step 4: Add triangular human figures holding hands in rhythm.
  • Exam application: Identifying the use of triangles and circles as the foundational geometry of Warli art.

Applications and connections

  • Rural development and eco-tourism branding in Maharashtra.
  • Modern apparel, home furnishing, and interior architectural panels.
  • Anthropological documentation of indigenous worldviews.

Advanced points, exceptions and limitations

  • Warli art exhibits striking structural similarities to prehistoric rock shelter paintings found globally, particularly in central India.
  • The shift to commercial paper production allowed Warli artists to gain economic independence while preserving sacred motifs.
  • Strict communal ownership of motifs means individual artists traditionally signed works only after modern commercialization.

Additional examples

  • The Tarpa dance spiral depicting the cycle of life and seasons.
  • Depiction of Mother Nature as a fertility symbol surrounded by flora and fauna.
  • Harvest scenes featuring farmers working with bulls and ploughs.
Important distinctions
  • Unlike Madhubani's multi-colored, densely packed panels, Warli uses a stark two-color palette and minimalist geometry.
  • Unlike Pattachitra's intricate mythological narratives, Warli depicts daily tribal life, dances, and agrarian cycles.
  • Warli figures are symbolic abstractions rather than naturalistic or anatomical representations.
Common misconceptions
  • Misconception: Warli paintings use modern white acrylic markers. Correction: Traditional execution requires rice paste applied with bamboo twigs.
  • Misconception: Warli art depicts complex Vedic mythology. Correction: It primarily focuses on animistic beliefs, nature worship, and tribal rituals.
Exam focus
  • Identify the primary geometry: circle, triangle, square.
  • Remember the association with the Warli tribe of Maharashtra.
  • Note the signature bi-color scheme (reddish-brown and white).
  • Recall Jivya Soma Mashe as the pioneering artist.
  • Understand the significance of the Tarpa dance motif.
Concept 3 · core

Pattachitra Painting

Pattachitra is a traditional cloth-based scroll painting tradition of Odisha and West Bengal, renowned for intricate details, mythological narratives, and natural mineral colors.

Prerequisites

  • Familiarity with eastern Indian temple architecture and iconography
  • Basic knowledge of Jagannath cult and Vaishnavite mythology

Definitions and scope

  • Patta: Canvas or cloth.
  • Chitra: Picture or painting.
  • Talapatra Pattachitra: Engravings etched on palm leaves.
  • Chitrakars: Traditional artisan community practicing Pattachitra.

Step-by-step explanation

  • The Pattachitra tradition is closely linked to the worship of Lord Jagannath at Puri, Odisha, dating back to the 12th century CE.
  • Preparation of the patta involves soaking cotton cloth in tamarind seed paste, coating it with a mixture of chalk and gum, and burnishing it with smooth stones until leather-like.
  • Colors are derived from natural mineral and organic sources: white from conch shells, black from lamp soot, red from hingula (cinnabar), and yellow from haritala (orpiment).
  • Brushes are traditionally crafted from animal hair, including squirrel tail hair and mouse hair, bound to bamboo handles.
  • Every painting is bordered by intricate floral or geometric frames, and figures are depicted with distinct profile faces and large, expressive eyes.
  • Themes center around the 'Gita Govinda' of Jayadeva, epics like Ramayana and Mahabharata, and various avatars of Lord Vishnu.
  • Talapatra Pattachitra involves scratching drawings onto dried palm leaves using an iron stylus and rubbing black carbon or turmeric into the grooves.
  • Raghurajpur in Puri district is recognized as a heritage crafts village where entire families are engaged in Pattachitra creation.

Key points

  • Region: Odisha (Puri, Raghurajpur) and West Bengal (Midnapore, Purulia).
  • Medium: Treated cotton cloth (Patta) or dried palm leaves (Talapatra).
  • Color Source: Natural minerals, stones, conch shells, and vegetable dyes.
  • Finish: Treated with natural tree resin (lacquer) to render it waterproof and glossy.
  • Key Motifs: Lord Jagannath, Balaram, Subhadra, Radha-Krishna, Dashavatara.
  • Artisan Community: Chitrakars.
Worked examples
  • Step 1: Take fine cotton cloth and apply tamarind paste adhesive. Step 2: Apply chalk powder and gum coating, then polish with a smooth stone. Step 3: Outline figures in bold black using hair brushes. Step 4: Fill in vibrant mineral colors and apply a lacquer coating over fire for gloss.
  • Exam application: Identifying the use of conch shell powder for white and tamarind paste for canvas preparation in Odishan Pattachitra.

Applications and connections

  • Temple ritual hangings ('Anavasara patti' displayed during Lord Jagannath's ritual illness).
  • High-value decorative wall art and cultural export items.
  • Manuscript illustration and archival preservation of traditional epics.

Advanced points, exceptions and limitations

  • West Bengal Pattachitra (Patachua) differs from Odishan Pattachitra by being performed alongside traditional ballad singing by traveling bards known as Patuas.
  • The preparation of the cloth canvas is a rigorous process taking up to a week before any painting begins.
  • The final lacquer coating (Jala) protects the natural pigments from humidity and fading over decades.

Additional examples

  • Thazhia pattachitra depicting the ten incarnations (Dashavatara) of Lord Vishnu.
  • The 'Rasa Lila' depicting Krishna dancing with the gopis.
  • Palm-leaf etched manuscripts detailing the Ramayana.
Important distinctions
  • Unlike Madhubani's folk aesthetic, Pattachitra reflects classical iconography and formal stylistic conventions.
  • Unlike Tanjore's heavy relief and gold foil, Pattachitra is flat cloth painting finished with a lacquer sheen.
  • Unlike Warli's minimalist tribal lines, Pattachitra exhibits ornate detailing, vibrant color palettes, and complex narrative staging.
Common misconceptions
  • Misconception: Pattachitra is painted on paper. Correction: Traditional Pattachitra is executed on treated cloth (Patta) or palm leaf (Talapatra).
  • Misconception: It is secular art. Correction: It is deeply rooted in Vaishnavite temple traditions and the Jagannath cult.
Exam focus
  • Remember the canvas preparation using tamarind paste and chalk.
  • Note the association with Raghurajpur heritage crafts village in Odisha.
  • Identify the brush material (squirrel/mouse hair).
  • Differentiate between Odishan cloth Pattachitra and Bengal Patachitra song traditions.
  • Recall the use of palm leaves in Talapatra Pattachitra.
Concept 4 · core

Tanjore Painting

Tanjore painting is a classical South Indian painting style distinguished by rich gold leafing, semi-precious stone embellishments, and raised gesso relief work on wooden panels.

Prerequisites

  • Understanding of South Indian temple architecture and Vijayanagara art heritage
  • Basic knowledge of gold leafing and embossing techniques

Definitions and scope

  • Palagai Padam: Traditional Tanjore painting executed on wooden planks ('palagai').
  • Makuri Gold: Pure 22-carat gold foil used for gilding.
  • Gesso Work: Relief work created using a mixture of chalk and arabic gum.
  • Thanjavur: Historic capital of the Cholas, Nayaks, and Marathas where the style flourished.

Step-by-step explanation

  • Tanjore painting originated in Thanjavur (Tanjore) under the patronage of the Nayak rulers in the 16th century and was later patronized by the Maratha court.
  • The base consists of a solid wooden plank, usually jackfruit or teak wood, covered with a layer of cloth pasted using Arabic gum.
  • A paste of limestone and gum (called 'sukku paste' or gesso) is applied to create raised relief work on ornaments, clothing, and architectural arches.
  • Pure 22-carat gold foils are carefully pasted over the embossed gesso areas to give a luminous, metallic sheen that never tarnishes.
  • Genuine semi-precious stones, including rubies, emeralds, and imitation Jaipur stones, are embedded within the gold foil to highlight jewelry and crowns.
  • Vibrant, rich primary colors are painted over the rest of the composition, traditionally using natural vegetable and mineral dyes, though modern artists use durable poster or acrylic colors.
  • The primary subject matter is Hindu deities, with Lord Balaji (Venkateswara), Krishna, and Goddess Lakshmi being the most popular central figures.
  • Encased in heavy teak wood frames with glass fronts, Tanjore paintings are celebrated for their durability and opulent visual richness.

Key points

  • Region: Thanjavur, Tamil Nadu.
  • Base Material: Solid wood panel (jackfruit/teak) covered with cloth and chalk paste.
  • Embellishment: 22-carat gold foil and semi-precious stones.
  • Technique: Raised relief work (gesso) providing a 3D effect.
  • Patronage: Nayak rulers and Maratha kings of Tanjore.
  • Dominant Theme: Divine icons with large, round faces and expressive eyes.
Worked examples
  • Step 1: Secure a teak wood plank and paste a layer of cotton cloth over it. Step 2: Apply chalk-gum paste to build up 3D architectural columns and jewelry reliefs. Step 3: Affix 22-carat gold foil onto the embossed areas. Step 4: Paint the deity in rich hues and stud the crown with glass stones.
  • Exam application: Identifying the use of gesso relief and gold leafing as diagnostic features of Tanjore art.

Applications and connections

  • Household shrines and daily worship in South Indian homes.
  • Prestigious wedding gifts and corporate heirloom artifacts.
  • Museum preservation and classical interior design.

Advanced points, exceptions and limitations

  • The weight and structural rigidity of the wooden plank distinguish Tanjore paintings from all other Indian paper or cloth scroll traditions.
  • The chemical purity of the gold foil used ensures the paintings retain their brilliance across centuries without polishing.
  • Master artisans undergo rigorous apprenticeship to perfect the intricate embossing and stone-setting techniques.

Additional examples

  • Iconic depiction of Lord Ganesha seated in a regal arch.
  • Thanjavur style coronation of Lord Rama with Sita and Lakshmana.
  • Standing figure of Lord Balaji adorned with detailed temple jewellery.
Important distinctions
  • Unlike Madhubani and Warli which are flat 2D folk arts, Tanjore painting is a high-relief classical craft incorporating gold and jewels.
  • Unlike Pattachitra cloth scrolls, Tanjore paintings are rigid, heavy wood-panel icons.
  • Tanjore art shares stylistic affinities with the inlay and gold-work traditions of Vijayanagara murals.
Common misconceptions
  • Misconception: Tanjore paintings are printed on paper. Correction: They are handcrafted relief works built on wooden panels.
  • Misconception: The gold used is imitation brass paint. Correction: Authentic traditional Tanjore paintings use genuine 22-carat gold foil.
Exam focus
  • Remember the base material: solid wood panel with cloth and chalk paste.
  • Identify the hallmark features: 22-carat gold foil and semi-precious stones.
  • Note the patronage of Nayak and Maratha rulers of Thanjavur.
  • Understand the gesso relief technique.
  • Recognize popular iconography, especially Lord Balaji.
Concept 5 · advanced

Geographical Indication (GI) Tagged Traditional Crafts of India

Geographical Indication (GI) tags protect traditional Indian crafts by legally recognizing their specific regional origin, historical authenticity, and unique manufacturing techniques.

Prerequisites

  • Basic understanding of Intellectual Property Rights (IPR) and WTO TRIPS agreement
  • Geographical familiarity with Indian states and handicraft clusters

Definitions and scope

  • Geographical Indication (GI): A sign used on products that have a specific geographical origin and possess qualities or a reputation due to that origin (governed by GI of Goods Registration and Protection Act, 1999).
  • Handicraft Cluster: A concentration of artisan families practicing a localized craft generation after generation.
  • Authenticity Seal: Legal protection preventing unauthorized use of regional craft names.

Step-by-step explanation

  • The Geographical Indications of Goods (Registration and Protection) Act came into force in India in September 2003, administered by the Controller General of Patents, Designs and Trademarks.
  • Uttar Pradesh holds numerous prestigious GI tags for traditional crafts, including Banarasi Brocade sarees, Lucknow Chikankari embroidery, Bhadohi handmade carpets, and Moradabad brassware.
  • Terracotta crafts like the Gorakhpur terracotta pottery have received GI tags, protecting the specialized clay baking and black-firing techniques.
  • In South India, Mysore Silk, Kanchipuram Silk, and Aranmula Kannadi (metal mirrors of Kerala) hold vital GI protection.
  • East Indian crafts such as Pipli applique work of Odisha and Madhubani paintings of Bihar are legally protected under GI registries.
  • West Indian crafts like Pithora tribal paintings of Gujarat and Paithani sarees of Maharashtra represent vital regional GI entries.
  • The primary objective of a GI tag is to eliminate counterfeit market products, ensure fair wages for traditional artisans, and preserve intangible cultural heritage.
  • GI registration is valid for 10 years and can be renewed indefinitely upon application.

Key points

  • Governing Act: GI of Goods (Registration and Protection) Act, 1999.
  • Validity: 10 years, renewable.
  • UP Focus Tags: Banarasi Brocade, Lucknow Chikankari, Bhadohi Carpets, Moradabad Brass, Gorakhpur Terracotta.
  • Economic Impact: Prevents cultural misappropriation and boosts export value.
  • Administering Body: Geographical Indication Registry based in Chennai.
  • Ownership: Belongs collectively to the authorized community of producers, not an individual.
Worked examples
  • Step 1: Artisan association files an application demonstrating historical origin exceeding 100 years. Step 2: Scientific and cultural verification establishes unique regional qualities. Step 3: GI Registry grants legal certification under the 1999 Act. Step 4: Artisans gain exclusive rights to use the regional designation.
  • Exam application: Matching Uttar Pradesh crafts like Gorakhpur Terracotta and Lucknow Chikankari with their respective GI registry attributes.

Applications and connections

  • Legal enforcement against counterfeit mass-produced factory goods.
  • Export promotion and global branding of authentic Indian heritage crafts.
  • Empowerment of rural artisan cooperatives through authenticated marketing.

Advanced points, exceptions and limitations

  • A GI tag is a collective intellectual property right, meaning no single individual can own the tag; rather, it belongs to all authorized producers within the specified geographic boundary.
  • Infringement of a GI tag attracts statutory penalties and seizure of counterfeit goods under Indian law.
  • The international recognition of GI aligns with WTO standards, facilitating smoother global trade for authentic artisan clusters.

Additional examples

  • Lucknow Chikankari: White-on-white shadow needlework originating in Awadh, UP.
  • Gorakhpur Terracotta: Hand-shaped red clay animal figures baked without artificial glazing.
  • Aranmula Kannadi: Unique metal alloy mirror made exclusively in Aranmula, Kerala.
Important distinctions
  • Unlike a Patent which protects a new technical invention for a limited time, a GI protects a collective regional quality tied to geography indefinitely upon renewal.
  • Unlike a Trademark which identifies a specific corporate brand, a GI identifies the geographical source and collective heritage of a community.
  • GI tags focus on traditional heritage and provenance rather than modern industrial innovation.
Common misconceptions
  • Misconception: A GI tag is awarded to an individual master artisan. Correction: It is granted to a region and its collective body of authorized producers.
  • Misconception: GI tags are permanent without renewal. Correction: They must be renewed every 10 years.
Exam focus
  • Memorize major UP-specific GI crafts: Chikankari, Banarasi Saree, Bhadohi Carpet, Moradabad Brass, Gorakhpur Terracotta.
  • Recall the enacting year of the GI Act: 1999 (enforced in 2003).
  • Know the headquarters of the GI Registry: Chennai, Tamil Nadu.
  • Understand the distinction between GI, Patent, and Trademark.
  • Note the 10-year validity period for GI registration.

Rapid revision: Traditional Paintings & Handicrafts

Review every concept point-wise before attempting the quiz and timed test.

Concept 1

Madhubani (Mithila) Painting

Definition and scope

  • Folk art form originating from the Mithila region of Bihar.
  • Traditionally executed by women on freshly plastered mud walls.
  • Categorized into five distinct sub-styles: Bharni, Kachni, Tantrik, Godna, and Kohbar.

Must remember

  • Uses natural dyes and organic pigments exclusively.
  • Characterized by double-line borders and zero negative space.
  • Prominent artists include Padma Shri recipient Sita Devi.
  • Holds a recognized Geographical Indication (GI) tag.
  • Primary themes include Hindu mythology, celestial bodies, and fertility.

Concept-specific exam traps

  • Confusing Madhubani with tribal Warli art geometry.
  • Assuming synthetic acrylic paints are permitted in traditional Madhubani.
Concept 2

Warli Painting

Definition and scope

  • Tribal art of Maharashtra practiced by the Warli indigenous community.
  • Built on a stark bi-color palette of red-brown mud background and white foreground.
  • Utilizes fundamental geometric shapes: circle, triangle, and square.

Must remember

  • White pigment is made from rice paste and water with natural gum.
  • Tarpa dance is a central ritualistic motif depicting community harmony.
  • Jivya Soma Mashe is the pioneering artist who popularized Warli globally.
  • Figures are constructed using two triangles joined at their apexes.
  • Depicts agrarian cycles, nature worship, and tribal life rather than complex mythology.

Concept-specific exam traps

  • Thinking Warli paintings depict multi-colored scenes.
  • Confusing Warli stick figures with Madhubani figurative detailing.
Concept 3

Pattachitra Painting

Definition and scope

  • Cloth and palm-leaf scroll painting tradition of Odisha and West Bengal.
  • Deeply connected to the Jagannath cult and Vaishnavite mythology.
  • Canvas is prepared using tamarind seed paste, chalk, and stone polishing.

Must remember

  • Brushes are traditionally made from animal hair (squirrel and mouse).
  • Finished with a natural tree resin (lacquer) coating for waterproof gloss.
  • Raghurajpur in Odisha is the famous heritage crafts village for Pattachitra.
  • Talapatra Pattachitra involves etching on dried palm leaves with an iron stylus.
  • Figures feature distinct profile faces and large, prominent eyes.

Concept-specific exam traps

  • Assuming Pattachitra is painted on ordinary paper.
  • Forgetting the connection to Raghurajpur village.
Concept 4

Tanjore Painting

Definition and scope

  • Classical South Indian painting style originating in Thanjavur, Tamil Nadu.
  • Executed on solid wooden panels using gesso relief work.
  • Embellished with 22-carat gold foil and semi-precious stones.

Must remember

  • Patronized by Nayak and Maratha rulers of Tanjore.
  • Base material is a solid wooden plank (jackfruit/teak) covered with cloth and chalk paste.
  • Central subjects are Hindu deities, especially Lord Balaji.
  • Raised relief gesso work gives a distinct 3D effect.
  • Framed traditionally in heavy teak wood with glass protection.

Concept-specific exam traps

  • Mistaking Tanjore paintings for paper scroll or cloth paintings.
  • Believing imitation brass paint constitutes authentic traditional Tanjore art.
Concept 5

Geographical Indication (GI) Tagged Traditional Crafts

Definition and scope

  • Legal protection for traditional crafts possessing specific regional origin and quality.
  • Governed by the GI of Goods (Registration and Protection) Act, 1999.
  • Administered by the GI Registry based in Chennai.

Must remember

  • Valid for 10 years and indefinitely renewable.
  • Uttar Pradesh GI tags include Banarasi Saree, Chikankari, Bhadohi Carpets, Moradabad Brass, and Gorakhpur Terracotta.
  • Prevents commercial counterfeiting and protects collective artisan heritage.
  • Belongs to a community of producers, not a single individual.
  • Aligns with WTO TRIPS international intellectual property standards.

Concept-specific exam traps

  • Believing GI tags are granted permanently without renewal.
  • Assuming a GI tag is a patent held by an individual artisan.

Quick facts

  • Madhubani art originates from the Mithila region of Bihar and is traditionally executed by women.
  • Warli painting uses a bi-color scheme of red-brown earth and white rice-paste pigment.
  • Jivya Soma Mashe is internationally renowned for popularizing Warli tribal art.
  • Pattachitra canvas is prepared using tamarind seed paste and chalk powder.
  • Raghurajpur in Odisha is a designated heritage crafts village famous for Pattachitra.
  • Tanjore paintings feature genuine 22-carat gold foil and gesso relief work on wooden panels.
  • The GI Act of India was enacted in 1999 and came into force in 2003.
  • Lucknow Chikankari and Gorakhpur Terracotta are prominent GI-tagged crafts of Uttar Pradesh.

Glossary

KohbarA ritualistic marriage and fertility motif painted in the nuptial chamber in Madhubani art.
TarpaA trumpet-like musical instrument played during the circular communal dance depicted in Warli art.
PattaTreated cotton cloth used as a canvas base in Odishan Pattachitra paintings.
GessoA mixture of limestone/chalk and arabic gum used for 3D relief work in Tanjore paintings.
Geographical Indication (GI)A legal sign certifying that a product originates from a specific region and possesses distinct regional qualities.
TalapatraDried palm leaf used for etching engravings in traditional Odishan palm-leaf manuscripts and paintings.

Important distinctions

Madhubani PaintingvsWarli Painting
  • Madhubani is multi-colored and densely filled with flora and fauna; Warli is strictly bi-color (red and white) and minimalist.
  • Madhubani features detailed figurative mythological forms; Warli uses basic geometric shapes like circles, triangles, and squares.
Pattachitra PaintingvsTanjore Painting
  • Pattachitra is executed on flexible cloth scrolls or palm leaves; Tanjore is executed on rigid wooden planks.
  • Pattachitra uses flat mineral colors with a lacquer finish; Tanjore incorporates heavy 22-carat gold foil and raised gesso relief.

Memory hooks

  • Madhubani = Mithila Mamas (Mithila mud walls, multi-colored).
  • Warli = White on Red (Bi-color geometric tribal art).
  • Pattachitra = Palm & Patta (Cloth and palm leaves of Odisha).
  • Tanjore = Teak & Gold (Wooden panels with gold foil and stones).

Exam traps and cautions

  • Do not confuse Madhubani's multi-color folk style with Warli's minimalist two-color tribal geometry.
  • Remember that Tanjore paintings are wooden plank reliefs, whereas Pattachitra and Madhubani are flat cloth/paper scroll arts.
  • Do not assume GI tags are permanent patents; they must be renewed every 10 years.
  • Bear in mind that traditional Pattachitra brushes are made from animal hair, not synthetic nylon.
