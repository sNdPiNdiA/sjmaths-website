Concept 1 · foundation

Plant vs Animal Cell

Plant and animal cells share basic eukaryotic organization but differ fundamentally in extracellular structures, storage forms, and energy organelles.

Prerequisites

  • Basic understanding of eukaryotic vs prokaryotic cell structure.
  • Familiarity with the Cell Theory proposed by Schleiden and Schwann.
  • Basic knowledge of microscopic techniques and cell staining.

Definitions and scope

  • Plant Cell: A rigid eukaryotic cell enclosed by a cellulosic cell wall containing plastids and large central vacuoles.
  • Animal Cell: A flexible eukaryotic cell lacking a cell wall, possessing centrioles and multiple small temporary vacuoles.
  • Protoplasm: The living content of a cell surrounded by the plasma membrane, comprising cytoplasm and nucleoplasm.

Step-by-step explanation

  • Plant cells possess a rigid, outer cell wall composed primarily of cellulose, hemicellulose, and pectin, providing structural rigidity and turgor pressure resistance.
  • Animal cells lack a cell wall, maintaining flexibility through a specialized extracellular matrix and membrane-associated cholesterol.
  • Chloroplasts and other plastids are present in plant cells for photosynthesis; animal cells completely lack plastids.
  • A mature plant cell contains a large central vacuole surrounded by a tonoplast, occupying up to 90% of the cell volume and storing water and metabolites.
  • Animal cells contain multiple small, transient vacuoles primarily used for pinocytosis, phagocytosis, or exocytosis.
  • Centrioles and centrosomes are typically present in animal cells to organize mitotic spindles; higher plant cells lack centrioles and organize spindles through acentriolar microtubule organizing centers.
  • Plant cells store surplus carbohydrates as insoluble starch grains within amyloplasts.
  • Animal cells store surplus carbohydrates as soluble glycogen granules within the cytosol of liver and muscle cells.
  • Cytokinesis in plant cells occurs via centrifugal cell plate formation directed by the phragmoplast.
  • Cytokinesis in animal cells occurs via centripetal constriction mediated by an actin-myosin contractile ring.

Key points

  • Plant cells have rigid cell walls of cellulose; animal cells lack cell walls.
  • Plastids are exclusive to plant cells and certain protozoans.
  • Plant cells feature a single large central vacuole; animal cells have scattered small vacuoles.
  • Centrioles are present in animal cells and absent in higher plant cells.
  • Carbohydrate storage is in the form of starch in plants and glycogen in animals.
  • Plant cytokinesis involves a cell plate; animal cytokinesis involves furrowing.
Worked examples
  • Identifying an onion peel cell under a compound microscope based on rectangular shape, distinct cell wall, and prominent central vacuole.
  • Differentiating a human cheek epithelial cell from an Elodea leaf cell by observing the absence of chloroplasts and cell walls in the cheek cell.

Applications and connections

  • Botanical and histological slide preparation in educational institutions.
  • Taxonomic identification of plant tissues versus animal tissues.
  • Agricultural biotechnology and plant tissue culture applications.

Advanced points, exceptions and limitations

  • Plasmodesmata enable symplastic continuity between adjacent plant cells, whereas animal cells communicate via gap junctions.
  • Secondary cell walls in plants contain lignin for enhanced mechanical strength, a structure entirely absent in animal cells.
  • Peroxisomes in plant cells contain specialized enzymes for photorespiration (glycolate cycle), differing functionally from animal peroxisomes.

Additional examples

  • Onion epidermal cell showing distinct cell walls.
  • Human buccal mucosal cell showing smooth plasma membrane.
  • Spirogyra cell showing spiral chloroplasts.
Important distinctions
  • Cell Wall: Present in plants (cellulose), absent in animals.
  • Vacuole: Large central vacuole in plants, small scattered vacuoles in animals.
  • Centrioles: Absent in higher plants, present in animals.
Common misconceptions
  • Believing plant cells do not undergo cellular respiration because they perform photosynthesis.
  • Assuming animal cells have no vacuoles at all.
  • Thinking all plant cells contain chloroplasts (roots and non-green tissues do not).
Exam focus
  • Direct questions on structural differences between plant and animal cells.
  • Identification of carbohydrate reserve differences (starch vs glycogen).
  • Mechanism of plant cell cytokinesis (cell plate/phragmoplast).
  • Presence or absence of centrioles in plant mitosis.
Concept 2 · core

DNA Structure and Replication

Deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA) is the hereditary macromolecule organized as a double helix that replicates semi-conservatively to ensure genetic continuity.

Prerequisites

  • Basic chemical composition of nucleic acids (pentose sugar, nitrogenous base, phosphate group).
  • Understanding of chemical bonding (hydrogen bonds, phosphodiester bonds).
  • Basic concepts of cell cycle and interphase.

Definitions and scope

  • Nucleotide: The basic monomer of nucleic acids comprising a nitrogenous base, a deoxyribose sugar, and a phosphate group.
  • Double Helix: The coiled 3D structure of DNA proposed by Watson and Crick in 1953.
  • Semi-conservative Replication: The mechanism where each newly synthesized DNA molecule consists of one parental strand and one newly synthesized strand.

Step-by-step explanation

  • DNA consists of two polynucleotide chains wound around a common axis in a right-handed double helix.
  • The sugar-phosphate backbone forms the outer structural framework, while nitrogenous bases project inward.
  • Purines (Adenine and Guanine) pair exclusively with pyrimidines (Thymine and Cytosine) through specific hydrogen bonding (A=T with two bonds, G≡C with three bonds).
  • The two strands run in opposite antiparallel directions (5' to 3' and 3' to 5').
  • Meselson and Stahl's experiment using 15N isotope in Escherichia coli definitively proved the semi-conservative mode of DNA replication.
  • DNA replication initiates at specific genomic sites called origins of replication.
  • DNA Helicase unwinds the double helix, creating a replication fork by breaking hydrogen bonds.
  • DNA Polymerase III synthesizes the new strand by adding deoxyribonucleoside triphosphates exclusively in the 5' to 3' direction.
  • The leading strand is synthesized continuously, whereas the lagging strand is synthesized discontinuously in short segments known as Okazaki fragments.
  • DNA Ligase seals the nicks in the sugar-phosphate backbone by joining Okazaki fragments.

Key points

  • Watson and Crick proposed the 3D double helix model of DNA in 1953.
  • Chargaff's Rule states that [A] = [T] and [G] = [C], and [A+G] = [T+C].
  • DNA strands are antiparallel (5' to 3' and 3' to 5').
  • Replication is semi-conservative as proved by Meselson and Stahl.
  • DNA polymerase requires a free 3'-OH group to initiate synthesis and needs an RNA primer.
  • Okazaki fragments are synthesized on the lagging strand.
Worked examples
  • Calculating base percentages: If a double-stranded DNA molecule contains 20% Adenine, calculate the percentage of Cytosine (Solution: A=20%, T=20%, total AT=40%, remaining GC=60%, so Cytosine=30%).
  • Tracing the action of DNA ligase in sealing phosphodiester gaps between Okazaki fragments on the lagging strand.

Applications and connections

  • Forensic DNA profiling and paternity testing.
  • Genetic engineering, recombinant DNA technology, and gene cloning.
  • Medical diagnostics for genetic disorders and infectious diseases.

Advanced points, exceptions and limitations

  • Topoisomerases (DNA gyrase in prokaryotes) relieve supercoiling tension ahead of the replication fork.
  • Telomerase solves the end-replication problem in eukaryotic linear chromosomes by adding species-specific repetitive DNA sequences to chromosome ends.
  • Proofreading 3' to 5' exonuclease activity of DNA polymerase ensures high fidelity during replication.

Additional examples

  • E. coli replication machinery.
  • Human genomic DNA replication during S phase of cell cycle.
  • Plasmid replication in bacteria.
Important distinctions
  • Leading vs Lagging Strand: Continuous synthesis vs discontinuous Okazaki fragments.
  • Purines vs Pyrimidines: Double-ringed (A, G) vs single-ringed (C, T, U).
  • DNA vs RNA: Deoxyribose/Thymine/Double-stranded vs Ribose/Uracil/Single-stranded.
Common misconceptions
  • Believing DNA replication proceeds in both 5' to 3' and 3' to 5' directions on the same template.
  • Confusing Chargaff's rule applicability to single-stranded RNA or DNA viruses.
  • Assuming RNA primer remains permanently part of the replicated DNA molecule.
Exam focus
  • Chargaff's equivalence rule calculations.
  • Meselson and Stahl experiment details (heavy and light nitrogen isotopes).
  • Enzymes involved in replication (helicase, primase, DNA polymerase, ligase).
  • Directionality of DNA synthesis (always 5' to 3').
Concept 3 · core

RNA Types and Transcription

Ribonucleic acid (RNA) mediates the expression of genetic information from DNA to proteins through distinct structural forms synthesized via transcription.

Prerequisites

  • Structure of DNA and nucleotide chemistry.
  • Understanding of the Central Dogma of Molecular Biology (DNA → RNA → Protein).
  • Basic knowledge of gene promoters and terminators.

Definitions and scope

  • Transcription: The enzymatic process of synthesizing a complementary RNA strand from a DNA template strand.
  • mRNA (Messenger RNA): RNA transcript that carries genetic code from DNA to ribosomes for translation.
  • tRNA (Transfer RNA): Adaptor RNA molecule that decodes mRNA codons into specific amino acid sequences.

Step-by-step explanation

  • RNA differs chemically from DNA by containing ribose sugar instead of deoxyribose and uracil instead of thymine.
  • The Central Dogma, formulated by Francis Crick, outlines the directional flow of genetic information: DNA undergoes replication, transcribes into RNA, and translates into proteins.
  • mRNA carries the specific codon sequence transcribed from the structural gene template strand.
  • tRNA possesses a cloverleaf secondary structure with an anticodon loop that pairs with mRNA codons and an amino acid attachment site at the 3' end.
  • rRNA (Ribosomal RNA) is structural and catalytic, forming the core architecture of ribosomes and catalyzing peptide bond formation.
  • Transcription initiates when RNA polymerase binds to a specific promoter region on the DNA template with the aid of transcription factors.
  • RNA polymerase elongates the transcript in the 5' to 3' direction using ribonucleoside triphosphates.
  • Transcription terminates when specific terminator sequences or proteins cause RNA polymerase to detach from the DNA template.
  • Eukaryotic pre-mRNA undergoes extensive post-transcriptional modifications including 5' capping, 3' polyadenylation, and RNA splicing to remove introns.
  • Spliceosomes remove non-coding introns and ligate coding exons, allowing alternative splicing to generate multiple protein isoforms from a single gene.

Key points

  • RNA contains ribose sugar and uracil instead of thymine.
  • Three major types of RNA are mRNA, tRNA, and rRNA.
  • Transcription is catalyzed by RNA polymerase in the 5' to 3' direction.
  • Prokaryotic mRNA is polycistronic, while eukaryotic mRNA is monocistronic.
  • Eukaryotic primary transcripts undergo 5' capping, poly-A tailing, and splicing.
  • Introns are non-coding sequences; exons are coding sequences.
Worked examples
  • Transcribing a DNA template strand 3'-TACGGT-5' into its corresponding mRNA codon sequence (Solution: 5'-AUGCCA-3').
  • Identifying the cloverleaf structure of tRNA and matching an mRNA codon (5'-AUG-3') with its complementary tRNA anticodon (3'-UAC-5').

Applications and connections

  • Understanding viral replication mechanisms in RNA viruses like retroviruses and coronaviruses.
  • Designing mRNA vaccines and therapeutic oligonucleotide treatments.
  • Gene expression profiling and transcriptomics in cancer research.

Advanced points, exceptions and limitations

  • Non-coding RNAs (ncRNAs) such as microRNAs (miRNAs) and small interfering RNAs (siRNAs) regulate gene expression post-transcriptionally via RNA interference (RNAi).
  • Ribozymes are catalytic RNA molecules demonstrating that RNA can possess enzymatic activity (e.g., self-splicing introns and peptidyl transferase activity of 23S rRNA).
  • Reverse transcription violates the classical unidirectional flow by synthesizing DNA from an RNA template using reverse transcriptase enzyme.

Additional examples

  • Beta-globin mRNA processing in human erythroblasts.
  • tRNA-Phe charging with phenylalanine.
  • rRNA functioning as a ribozyme in the ribosome.
Important distinctions
  • mRNA vs tRNA vs rRNA: Coding transcript vs adaptor molecule vs structural/catalytic component.
  • Prokaryotic vs Eukaryotic Transcription: Occurs in cytoplasm/no introns vs occurs in nucleus/intron splicing.
  • DNA Polymerase vs RNA Polymerase: Requires primer/proofreads vs no primer required/de novo synthesis.
Common misconceptions
  • Believing both strands of DNA are transcribed simultaneously into mRNA.
  • Assuming all transcribed RNA molecules are translated into proteins (neglecting ncRNAs, tRNA, rRNA).
  • Confusing exons with introns regarding which one is retained in mature mRNA.
Exam focus
  • Central Dogma flow and its exceptions (reverse transcription).
  • Post-transcriptional modifications in eukaryotes (capping, tailing, splicing).
  • Functions of mRNA, tRNA, and rRNA.
  • Identification of template vs coding strands in transcription problems.
Concept 4 · advanced

Chromosomes and Cell Division

Chromosomes are compact nucleoprotein structures that ensure accurate genetic segregation during mitotic and meiotic cell divisions.

Prerequisites

  • Basic structure of DNA and histone proteins.
  • Concept of eukaryotic nucleus and cell cycle phases (G1, S, G2, M).
  • Basic understanding of hereditary transmission.

Definitions and scope

  • Chromatin: The complex of DNA and histone proteins making up eukaryotic chromosomes.
  • Centromere: The specialized constricted region of a chromosome where sister chromatids are joined and spindle fibers attach via kinetochores.
  • Mitosis: Equational cell division producing two genetically identical diploid daughter cells.

Step-by-step explanation

  • Eukaryotic DNA is wound around an octamer of basic histone proteins (H2A, H2B, H3, H4) to form repeating nucleosome units, appearing as 'beads on a string'.
  • Chromosomes are classified based on centromere position into metacentric, sub-metacentric, acrocentric, and telocentric types.
  • The cell cycle consists of interphase (G1, S, G2) for growth and DNA replication, followed by M phase (mitosis/meiosis and cytokinesis).
  • Mitosis comprises prophase, metaphase, anaphase, and telophase, ensuring equal distribution of duplicate chromosomes.
  • Metaphase is characterized by maximum chromosome condensation and alignment of chromosomes at the equatorial plate (metaphase plate).
  • Anaphase involves the splitting of centromeres and the poleward migration of sister chromatids.
  • Meiosis consists of two successive nuclear divisions (Meiosis I and Meiosis II) following a single round of DNA replication, reducing chromosome number by half.
  • Prophase I of meiosis is subdivided into leptotene, zygotene, pachytene, diplotene, and diakinesis.
  • Crossing over (genetic recombination) occurs between non-sister chromatids of homologous chromosomes during the pachytene stage, mediated by recombinase enzymes.
  • Chiasmata represent the X-shaped physical sites of crossing over visible during diplotene before terminalization in diakinesis.

Key points

  • Nucleosomes consist of DNA wrapped around an octamer of histone proteins.
  • Centromere position defines chromosome shape (metacentric, sub-metacentric, acrocentric, telocentric).
  • Mitosis produces two identical diploid cells; meiosis produces four genetically diverse haploid cells.
  • Crossing over occurs during pachytene of Prophase I in meiosis, increasing genetic variation.
  • Synapsis is the pairing of homologous chromosomes during zygotene.
  • Anaphase I separates homologous chromosomes, while Anaphase II separates sister chromatids.
Worked examples
  • Determining the number of chromosomes and DNA molecules in a human cell (2n = 46) during G2 phase: 46 chromosomes, 92 DNA molecules (since DNA replicated in S phase).
  • Calculating the number of meiotic divisions required to produce 100 viable pollen grains: 25 meiocytes undergo meiosis to yield 100 microspores.

Applications and connections

  • Karyotyping for diagnosing chromosomal abnormalities (e.g., Down syndrome, Turner syndrome).
  • Plant breeding programs utilizing polyploidy induced by colchicine.
  • Cancer diagnosis and staging based on mitotic index and chromosomal aberrations.

Advanced points, exceptions and limitations

  • Non-disjunction during anaphase I or II leads to aneuploidy (trisomy, monosomy) such as Klinefelter syndrome (XXY) or Turner syndrome (XO).
  • Heterochromatin is tightly packed, transcriptionally inactive DNA, whereas euchromatin is loosely packed and transcriptionally active.
  • Telomeres protect chromosome ends from degradation, and their progressive shortening is linked to cellular senescence and aging.

Additional examples

  • Onion root tip squash showing mitotic stages.
  • Grasshopper testis squash showing meiotic stages.
  • Human karyogram showing 22 pairs of autosomes and 1 pair of sex chromosomes.
Important distinctions
  • Mitosis vs Meiosis: Somatic/equational/2 cells vs Germ cells/reductional/4 cells.
  • Anaphase of Mitosis vs Anaphase I of Meiosis: Sister chromatids separate vs Homologous chromosomes separate.
  • Leptotene vs Pachytene: Condensation begins vs Crossing over occurs.
Common misconceptions
  • Assuming sister chromatids separate in Anaphase I of meiosis (they separate in Anaphase II).
  • Believing chromosomes are visible throughout the entire cell cycle (they condense only during M phase).
  • Confusing chromatin with chromatid.
Exam focus
  • Substages of Prophase I in meiosis and events occurring in each (especially pachytene and diplotene).
  • Difference between chromosome separation in mitosis and meiosis.
  • Chromosomal classification based on centromere position.
  • Calculation problems involving cell divisions and gamete formation.
Concept 5 · exam-synthesis

Mendel's Laws of Inheritance

Gregor Mendel established the fundamental principles of particulate inheritance through controlled hybridization experiments on pea plants.

Prerequisites

  • Basic terminology: gene, allele, genotype, phenotype, homozygous, heterozygous.
  • Basic understanding of sexual reproduction in flowering plants.
  • Basic probability rules for genetic crosses.

Definitions and scope

  • Allele: Alternative forms of a gene occupying the same locus on homologous chromosomes.
  • Law of Segregation: Alleles of a gene separate during gamete formation so that each gamete carries only one allele.
  • Law of Independent Assortment: Alleles of two or more different genes assort independently of one another during gamete formation.

Step-by-step explanation

  • Gregor Mendel performed hybridization experiments on Pisum sativum (garden pea) between 1856 and 1863, analyzing seven contrasting characters.
  • The Law of Dominance states that in a heterozygote, one allele (dominant) masks the phenotypic expression of another allele (recessive) at the same locus.
  • The Law of Segregation (Mendel's First Law) is based on the fact that allele pairs separate during gametogenesis without blending, ensuring pure gametes.
  • Monohybrid cross between homozygous tall (TT) and dwarf (tt) pea plants yields all tall heterozygous (Tt) F1 offspring, and a 3:1 phenotypic ratio in F2.
  • The Law of Independent Assortment (Mendel's Second Law) states that the segregation of one pair of factors is independent of another pair during gamete formation.
  • Dihybrid cross between plants differing in two traits (e.g., round yellow vs wrinkled green seeds) yields a 9:3:3:1 phenotypic ratio in the F2 generation.
  • Test cross involves crossing an individual of unknown dominant phenotype with a homozygous recessive individual to determine its zygosity (homozygous or heterozygous).
  • Back cross involves crossing an F1 offspring with either of its parents.
  • Incomplete dominance (e.g., Mirabilis jalapa / Four O'clock plant) violates strict dominance where heterozygous flowers are pink, yielding a 1:2:1 phenotypic and genotypic ratio in F2.
  • Co-dominance (e.g., ABO blood groups in humans) occurs when both alleles are expressed fully and equally in the heterozygote (e.g., IAIB blood type).

Key points

  • Mendel used Pisum sativum for his classical genetic experiments.
  • Monohybrid F2 phenotypic ratio is 3:1, genotypic ratio is 1:2:1.
  • Dihybrid F2 phenotypic ratio is 9:3:3:1.
  • Law of Segregation has no exceptions; it is universal for sexually reproducing organisms.
  • Law of Independent Assortment applies only to genes located on different chromosomes or situated far apart on the same chromosome.
  • Test cross for monohybrid yields 1:1 ratio if heterozygous.
Worked examples
  • Solving a dihybrid cross: Crossing RrYy x RrYy and calculating the proportion of homozygous recessive offspring (rryy) in F2 (Solution: 1/16).
  • Determining genotype using a test cross: If a tall pea plant crossed with a dwarf plant produces 50% tall and 50% dwarf offspring, the unknown parent's genotype is heterozygous (Tt).

Applications and connections

  • Plant and animal breeding for desirable agricultural traits (disease resistance, high yield).
  • Genetic counseling for predicting inheritance patterns of monogenic disorders in humans.
  • Pedigree analysis and forensic identification.

Advanced points, exceptions and limitations

  • Linkage violates the Law of Independent Assortment when genes are located close together on the same chromosome, leading to parental combinations exceeding recombinant frequencies.
  • Gene interactions such as complementary genes (9:7 ratio), epistatic interactions (dominant epistasis 12:3:1, recessive epistasis 9:3:4), and polygenic inheritance modify standard Mendelian ratios.
  • Pleiotropy occurs when a single gene influences multiple phenotypic traits (e.g., sickle cell anemia or phenylketonuria).

Additional examples

  • ABO blood group system in humans demonstrating co-dominance and multiple alleles.
  • Flower color in Mirabilis jalapa showing incomplete dominance.
  • Coat color in mice showing recessive epistasis.
Important distinctions
  • Incomplete Dominance vs Co-dominance: Blending of traits (pink flowers) vs simultaneous equal expression (AB blood type).
  • Monohybrid vs Dihybrid Cross: Single trait (3:1 ratio) vs two traits (9:3:3:1 ratio).
  • Test Cross vs Back Cross: Cross with homozygous recessive vs cross with any parent.
Common misconceptions
  • Believing the Law of Independent Assortment applies to all genes regardless of location (it fails for linked genes).
  • Confusing codominance with incomplete dominance.
  • Assuming dominant traits are always most common in natural populations.
Exam focus
  • F2 phenotypic and genotypic ratios for monohybrid and dihybrid crosses.
  • Exceptions to Mendel's laws (incomplete dominance, codominance, linkage, pleiotropy).
  • Purpose and outcome of a test cross.
  • Calculation of gamete types ($2^n$, where n is number of heterozygous loci).

Concept 1

Plant vs Animal Cell

Definition and scope

  • Plant cells possess rigid cellulose cell walls, plastids, and large central vacuoles.
  • Animal cells lack cell walls and plastids, but possess centrioles and small vacuoles.
  • Plant cells store carbohydrates as starch; animal cells store them as glycogen.

Must remember

  • Cell wall composition: cellulose, hemicellulose, pectin.
  • Cytokinesis in plants via cell plate/phragmoplast; in animals via cleavage furrow.
  • Vacuole membrane in plants is called the tonoplast.
  • Higher plants lack centrioles.
  • Plastids are absent in animal cells.

Concept-specific exam traps

  • Assuming all plant cells contain chloroplasts.
  • Confusing carbohydrate storage forms between plants and animals.
Concept 2

DNA Structure and Replication

Definition and scope

  • DNA is a double-stranded helical structure with antiparallel strands.
  • Replication is semi-conservatively executed by DNA polymerase.
  • Chargaff's rule states [A]=[T] and [G]=[C].

Must remember

  • Watson and Crick proposed double helix in 1953.
  • Meselson and Stahl proved semi-conservative replication using 15N.
  • DNA synthesis proceeds exclusively in 5' to 3' direction.
  • Leading strand is continuous; lagging strand produces Okazaki fragments.
  • Enzymes: Helicase unwinds, Primase adds RNA primer, Ligase seals nicks.

Concept-specific exam traps

  • Forgetting the 5' to 3' synthesis constraint.
  • Miscalculating nucleotide base percentages without applying Chargaff's rules correctly.
Concept 3

RNA Types and Transcription

Definition and scope

  • RNA contains ribose sugar and uracil instead of thymine.
  • Transcription synthesizes RNA from a DNA template using RNA polymerase.
  • Three main types are mRNA, tRNA, and rRNA.

Must remember

  • Central Dogma: DNA -> RNA -> Protein.
  • Eukaryotic pre-mRNA modifications: 5' capping, polyadenylation, splicing.
  • Introns are removed, exons are retained.
  • tRNA has a cloverleaf shape with an anticodon loop.
  • Reverse transcription violates unidirectional flow (RNA to DNA).

Concept-specific exam traps

  • Confusing introns with exons regarding coding capacity.
  • Assuming transcription copies both DNA strands simultaneously.
Concept 4

Chromosomes and Cell Division

Definition and scope

  • Chromosomes consist of DNA wound around histone octamers forming nucleosomes.
  • Mitosis produces two identical diploid cells; meiosis produces four haploid cells.
  • Crossing over occurs during pachytene of meiosis Prophase I.

Must remember

  • Centromere positions define metacentric, sub-metacentric, acrocentric, telocentric.
  • Homologous chromosomes pair up (synapsis) during zygotene.
  • Crossing over creates genetic recombination during pachytene.
  • Anaphase I separates homologs; Anaphase II separates sister chromatids.
  • Non-disjunction leads to chromosomal abnormalities like Down syndrome.

Concept-specific exam traps

  • Confusing chromatid separation stages between mitosis and meiosis.
  • Assuming chromosomes are visible throughout interphase.
Concept 5

Mendel's Laws of Inheritance

Definition and scope

  • Mendel formulated the laws of dominance, segregation, and independent assortment.
  • Monohybrid F2 phenotypic ratio is 3:1; dihybrid F2 phenotypic ratio is 9:3:3:1.
  • Test cross determines zygosity using a homozygous recessive parent.

Must remember

  • Experiment organism: Pisum sativum (garden pea).
  • Law of Segregation has no exceptions.
  • Law of Independent Assortment fails for linked genes.
  • Incomplete dominance yields 1:2:1 phenotypic ratio (e.g., Mirabilis jalapa).
  • Codominance exhibits simultaneous expression (e.g., ABO blood groups).

Concept-specific exam traps

  • Applying independent assortment to tightly linked genes on the same chromosome.
  • Confusing test cross with back cross.

Quick facts

  • Plant cell walls are made of cellulose, while fungal cell walls are made of chitin.
  • Meselson and Stahl used Escherichia coli and 15N isotope to prove semi-conservative replication.
  • The term 'Central Dogma' was coined by Francis Crick in 1958.
  • Crossing over during meiosis is facilitated by the enzyme recombinase.
  • Mendel's work on garden peas was rediscovered in 1900 by de Vries, Correns, and Tschermak.
  • Human somatic cells contain 46 chromosomes (23 pairs).

Glossary

TonoplastThe cytoplasmic membrane surrounding the large central vacuole of a plant cell.
Okazaki FragmentShort segment of newly synthesized DNA formed on the lagging strand during replication.
SpliceosomeA large macromolecular complex that removes introns from transcribed pre-mRNA.
SynapsisThe pairing of homologous chromosomes during zygotene stage of meiosis Prophase I.
PleiotropyPhenomenon where a single gene influences multiple unrelated phenotypic traits.

Important distinctions

Plant CellvsAnimal Cell
  • Plant cells have a rigid cellulose cell wall; animal cells lack cell walls.
  • Plant cells contain plastids (chloroplasts); animal cells do not.
  • Plant cells feature a large central vacuole; animal cells have small scattered vacuoles.
MitosisvsMeiosis
  • Mitosis produces two diploid daughter cells; meiosis produces four haploid cells.
  • Mitosis occurs in somatic cells; meiosis occurs in germ cells.
  • Crossing over does not occur in mitosis but is a hallmark of meiosis I.
Incomplete DominancevsCodominance
  • Incomplete dominance results in a blended intermediate phenotype (e.g., pink flowers).
  • Codominance results in both alleles being independently and fully expressed (e.g., AB blood type).

Memory hooks

  • CELL WALL: Plants Stand Tall Because of Cell Walls.
  • REPLICATION: Helicase Unwinds, Polymerase Builds, Ligase Seals.
  • MEIOSIS PHASES: PMAT twice, with crossing over in Prophase One (Pachytene).

Exam traps and cautions

  • Do not confuse the phragmoplast in plant cytokinesis with the cleavage furrow in animal cytokinesis.
  • Remember that RNA polymerase does not require a primer, unlike DNA polymerase.
  • Do not assume independent assortment holds true for genes close together on the same chromosome (linkage).
  • Always check whether a question asks for chromosome number or DNA molecule count during specific cell cycle phases.

Question 1MCQ

Which of the following cellular features distinguishes plant cells from animal cells?

Plant cells are uniquely characterized by a rigid cellulose cell wall and a large central vacuole.

Question 2MCQ

In DNA replication, why is the synthesis on the lagging strand discontinuous?

Because DNA strands are antiparallel and replication must proceed in the 5' to 3' direction, the lagging strand is synthesized discontinuously as Okazaki fragments.

Question 3MCQ

What is the primary function of mRNA splicing in eukaryotic cells?

RNA splicing removes non-coding introns and connects exons to produce mature, translatable mRNA.

Question 4MCQ

During which substage of meiosis Prophase I does crossing over occur?

Crossing over and genetic recombination take place during the pachytene stage of Prophase I.

Question 5MCQ

What is the expected F2 phenotypic ratio in a classical Mendelian dihybrid cross?

A dihybrid cross yields a 9:3:3:1 phenotypic ratio in the F2 generation due to independent assortment.

Question 6Assertion–Reason

Assertion (A): According to Chargaff's rules, the amount of adenine equals thymine in double-stranded DNA. Reason (R): Adenine pairs specifically with thymine via two hydrogen bonds in the DNA double helix.

Specific base pairing (A=T and G≡C) dictates that the quantity of adenine equals thymine.

Question 7Assertion–Reason

Assertion (A): Mitosis is known as equational division. Reason (R): Mitosis produces daughter cells with the same chromosome number as the parent cell.

Mitosis maintains ploidy level, yielding genetically identical diploid cells from a diploid parent cell.

Question 8Case-based

In a test cross between a tall pea plant of unknown genotype and a homozygous dwarf pea plant (tt), 50% of the offspring are tall and 50% are dwarf. What is the genotype of the tall parent?

A 1:1 phenotypic ratio in a test cross indicates that the unknown parent is heterozygous (Tt).

Question 9Match the following

Match the cellular storage product to the correct organism type:

Plants store starch; animals store glycogen.

Question 10MCQ

Which molecule acts as an adaptor molecule translating mRNA codons into amino acid sequences?

tRNA serves as the adaptor molecule matching codons with specific amino acids during translation.
