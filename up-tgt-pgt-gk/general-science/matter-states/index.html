Concept 1 · foundation

States of Matter and Interparticle Forces

Matter exists in distinct physical states governed by thermal energy and intermolecular forces.

Prerequisites

  • Basic understanding of mass, volume, and density.
  • Concept of temperature and kinetic energy.
  • General properties of solids, liquids, and gases.

Definitions and scope

  • Matter is anything that occupies space and has mass.
  • Solid state possesses definite shape and volume due to strong intermolecular forces and fixed constituent positions.
  • Liquid state has definite volume but indefinite shape, taking the shape of its container.
  • Gaseous state has neither definite shape nor definite volume due to high kinetic energy and negligible intermolecular forces.

Step-by-step explanation

  • Solids have constituent particles held closely by strong electrostatic or covalent forces, exhibiting vibrational motion only.
  • Liquids possess intermediate thermal energy; particles can slide past one another, resulting in fluidity and incompressibility.
  • Gases have high thermal energy overcoming intermolecular attraction, causing particles to move randomly at high velocities.
  • Plasma, the fourth state, consists of superheated ionized gas containing free electrons and positive ions at extreme temperatures.
  • Bose-Einstein Condensate (BEC), the fifth state, occurs at temperatures extremely close to absolute zero where particles clump together and act as a single quantum entity.
  • Intermolecular forces include dipole-dipole interactions, hydrogen bonding, London dispersion forces, and ion-dipole interactions.
  • Thermal energy promotes random motion and tends to separate particles, whereas intermolecular forces pull particles together.
  • The physical state of a substance at a given temperature and pressure depends on the net balance between thermal energy and intermolecular forces.

Key points

  • Solids: Fixed shape, fixed volume, high density, incompressible.
  • Liquids: Variable shape, fixed volume, moderate density, slightly compressible.
  • Gases: Variable shape, variable volume, low density, highly compressible.
  • Plasma: Ionized gas found in stars and lightning.
  • BEC: Formed near absolute zero (0 Kelvin) predicted by Satyendra Nath Bose and Albert Einstein.
  • Phase transitions occur when energy is added or removed at constant temperature during melting, boiling, or sublimation.
Worked examples
  • Water exists as ice below 0°C (solid), liquid water between 0°C and 100°C, and steam above 100°C at 1 atm pressure.
  • Dry ice (solid carbon dioxide) sublimes directly into gaseous carbon dioxide at atmospheric pressure without melting into a liquid.

Applications and connections

  • Cryogenic storage of biological samples using liquid nitrogen.
  • Refrigeration and air conditioning cycles relying on phase changes of refrigerants.
  • Neon signs and fluorescent lighting utilizing plasma physics.

Advanced points, exceptions and limitations

  • Supercritical fluids exist above the critical temperature and pressure, possessing properties of both liquids and gases.
  • Glass is often classified as an amorphous solid or a supercooled liquid with extremely high viscosity.
  • Quantum degeneracy pressure prevents white dwarfs and neutron stars from collapsing under gravity.

Additional examples

  • Table salt (NaCl) crystals as rigid ionic solids.
  • Mercury metal as a liquid at room temperature.
  • Oxygen and nitrogen as diatomic gases in the atmosphere.
Important distinctions
  • Crystalline solids have long-range order; amorphous solids have short-range order.
  • Gases expand infinitely to fill containers; liquids occupy a definite volume with a free surface.
  • Plasma conducts electricity; neutral gases are poor electrical conductors under normal conditions.
Common misconceptions
  • Mistaking glass as a true crystalline solid rather than an amorphous solid.
  • Believing liquids are completely incompressible (they have very low, but non-zero, compressibility).
  • Assuming all gases obey the ideal gas law under all conditions of high pressure and low temperature.
Exam focus
  • Identify conditions favoring plasma and Bose-Einstein condensate formation.
  • Differentiate between crystalline and amorphous solid properties.
  • Explain the role of hydrogen bonding in water's anomalous expansion.
  • Recognize phase transition terminology (melting, freezing, vaporization, condensation, sublimation, deposition).
  • Analyze pressure-temperature phase diagrams for pure substances.
Concept 2 · core

Fundamental Subatomic Particles

Atoms are composed of three primary subatomic particles: protons, neutrons, and electrons.

Prerequisites

  • Dalton's atomic theory and its limitations.
  • Basic electrical charge concepts (positive and negative charges).
  • Discovery of cathode rays and anode rays.

Definitions and scope

  • Proton is a positively charged subatomic particle residing in the nucleus with a relative charge of +1 and mass of approximately 1 amu.
  • Neutron is a neutrally charged subatomic particle residing in the nucleus with zero charge and mass nearly equal to a proton.
  • Electron is a negatively charged subatomic particle orbiting the nucleus with a relative charge of -1 and negligible mass (1/1836th of a proton).

Step-by-step explanation

  • J.J. Thomson discovered the electron in 1897 using cathode ray tubes and determined the charge-to-mass ratio.
  • Eugen Goldstein observed canal rays (anode rays), leading to the identification of the proton.
  • James Chadwick discovered the neutron in 1932 by bombarding beryllium with alpha particles.
  • Rutherford's gold foil experiment (1911) proved that the atom has a tiny, dense, positively charged nucleus containing protons and neutrons.
  • Mass of an electron is 9.109 × 10^-31 kg; mass of a proton is 1.673 × 10^-27 kg; mass of a neutron is 1.675 × 10^-27 kg.
  • Absolute charge of an electron and proton is 1.602 × 10^-19 Coulombs (negative for electron, positive for proton).
  • Nuclear force (strong nuclear force) binds protons and neutrons together inside the nucleus, overcoming electrostatic repulsion between protons.
  • Quarks are elementary particles that make up protons and hadrons (up quarks and down quarks).

Key points

  • Electron: Discovered by J.J. Thomson; mass ~0 amu; charge -1.
  • Proton: Discovered by Rutherford/Goldstein; mass ~1 amu; charge +1.
  • Neutron: Discovered by James Chadwick; mass ~1 amu; charge 0.
  • Nucleus contains nucleons (protons + neutrons) and accounts for nearly 99.9% of atomic mass.
  • Electrons occupy extra-nuclear space in specific energy levels or shells.
  • Atom as a whole is electrically neutral because the number of protons equals the number of electrons.
Worked examples
  • Calculate the total mass of an atom with 6 protons, 6 neutrons, and 6 electrons: (6 × 1.673 × 10^-27) + (6 × 1.675 × 10^-27) + (6 × 9.109 × 10^-31) ≈ 2.0 × 10^-26 kg.
  • Determine the charge of an ion with 12 protons and 10 electrons: Net charge = (+12) + (-10) = +2 (Magnesium ion, Mg²⁺).

Applications and connections

  • Cathode ray tubes used historically in television and oscilloscope displays.
  • Particle accelerators (like the Large Hadron Collider) studying fundamental subatomic interactions.
  • Medical radiation therapy utilizing high-energy subatomic particle beams.

Advanced points, exceptions and limitations

  • Free neutrons are unstable and undergo beta decay into a proton, electron, and anti-neutrino with a half-life of about 10 minutes.
  • Protons are considered stable in standard physics, though some grand unified theories predict proton decay.
  • Leptons (electrons) and quarks are fundamental, whereas hadrons (protons, neutrons) are composite particles.

Additional examples

  • Hydrogen-1 (Protium) is the only stable atom lacking neutrons in its nucleus.
  • Carbon-12 contains 6 protons, 6 neutrons, and 6 electrons.
  • Uranium-238 contains 92 protons, 146 neutrons, and 92 electrons.
Important distinctions
  • Protons and neutrons have comparable masses, whereas electrons are roughly 1836 times lighter.
  • Electrons reside outside the nucleus in orbitals; protons and neutrons reside tightly bound inside the nucleus.
  • Neutrons experience only the strong nuclear force and gravity, whereas charged particles (protons, electrons) also experience electromagnetic forces.
Common misconceptions
  • Believing electrons orbit the nucleus in fixed circular planetary tracks (superseded by quantum mechanical wave mechanical model).
  • Assuming neutrons have no function other than adding weight (they provide essential nuclear stability via strong force mediation).
  • Thinking the volume of the nucleus is comparable to the volume of the entire atom (the nucleus is 100,000 times smaller than the atom).
Exam focus
  • Memorize discoverers of electron, proton, and neutron (Thomson, Goldstein/Rutherford, Chadwick).
  • Compare relative and absolute masses and charges of subatomic particles.
  • Understand the significance of Rutherford's alpha-scattering experiment.
  • Calculate number of protons, neutrons, and electrons from atomic number and mass number.
  • Identify nucleon constituents and properties of the strong nuclear force.
Concept 3 · core

Atomic Number, Mass Number, and Nuclide Notation

Atomic number defines chemical identity, while mass number dictates the total nucleon count.

Prerequisites

  • Identification of protons, neutrons, and electrons.
  • Basic chemical symbol representation.
  • Concept of whole numbers in atomic counting.

Definitions and scope

  • Atomic Number (Z) is the total number of protons present in the nucleus of an atom.
  • Mass Number (A) is the total sum of protons and neutrons (nucleons) in the nucleus of an atom.
  • Nuclide notation represents an element with its chemical symbol X, mass number A as a superscript, and atomic number Z as a subscript: ^A_Z X.

Step-by-step explanation

  • Atomic number (Z) uniquely identifies an element; all atoms of the same element have the same atomic number.
  • In a neutral atom, number of electrons equals atomic number (Z = p = e).
  • Mass number (A) = Number of protons (Z) + Number of neutrons (N).
  • Number of neutrons (N) can be calculated as N = A - Z.
  • Most of the mass of an atom is concentrated in its nucleus, making mass number approximately equal to atomic mass in atomic mass units (amu).
  • Fractional atomic weights observed on the periodic table are weighted averages of naturally occurring isotopes.
  • Nuclide representation allows quick determination of all fundamental subatomic particle counts.
  • Ions are formed by gaining or losing electrons, which changes the electron count but leaves Z and A unchanged.

Key points

  • Atomic Number = Protons = Electrons (in neutral atom).
  • Mass Number (A) = Protons (Z) + Neutrons (N).
  • Number of Neutrons (N) = A - Z.
  • Nuclide format: ^12_6 C represents Carbon with mass 12 and atomic number 6.
  • Elements are arranged in the modern periodic table in order of increasing atomic number.
  • Changing the atomic number changes the element itself.
Worked examples
  • For Chlorine (^35_17 Cl): Z = 17 (protons = 17, electrons = 17 in neutral atom), A = 35, Neutrons = 35 - 17 = 18.
  • For Sodium ion (^23_11 Na^+): Z = 11 (protons = 11), A = 23, Neutrons = 23 - 11 = 12, Electrons = 11 - 1 = 10.

Applications and connections

  • Determining elemental composition in chemical analysis and stoichiometry.
  • Identifying radioactive decay products and nuclear reaction equations.
  • Medical diagnostics utilizing specific atomic mass tracers.

Advanced points, exceptions and limitations

  • Mass defect: The actual mass of a nucleus is slightly less than the sum of its individual isolated protons and neutrons due to mass-energy equivalence (E=mc^2) binding energy.
  • Nuclides with 'magic numbers' of protons or neutrons (2, 8, 20, 28, 50, 82, 126) exhibit exceptional nuclear stability.
  • Superheavy elements (transuranic elements with Z > 92) are synthetically produced in particle accelerators.

Additional examples

  • Oxygen (^16_8 O): 8 protons, 8 neutrons, 8 electrons.
  • Iron (^56_26 Fe): 26 protons, 30 neutrons, 26 electrons.
  • Gold (^197_79 Au): 79 protons, 118 neutrons, 79 electrons.
Important distinctions
  • Atomic number (Z) determines chemical properties; mass number (A) determines physical nuclear mass.
  • Neutrons vary among isotopes of the same element, keeping Z constant while altering A.
  • Atomic weight is a weighted average of isotopes; mass number is always an integer.
Common misconceptions
  • Confusing atomic number with atomic mass or atomic weight.
  • Assuming the number of neutrons is always equal to the number of protons.
  • Believing loss or gain of electrons alters the atomic number or mass number.
Exam focus
  • Practice calculating protons, neutrons, and electrons from given Z and A values.
  • Interpret standard nuclide notation (^A_Z X) rapidly under exam conditions.
  • Understand why atomic weights on the periodic table are not whole numbers.
  • Differentiate between atomic number, mass number, atomic mass, and gram atomic mass.
  • Solve numerical problems on isotopic abundance and average atomic mass calculation.
Concept 4 · advanced

Isotopes, Isobars, Isotones, and Isoelectronic Species

Nuclear variations result in specific atomic relationships classified by protons, neutrons, and mass numbers.

Prerequisites

  • Definition of atomic number and mass number.
  • Calculation of neutron count (A - Z).
  • Basic periodic table familiarity.

Definitions and scope

  • Isotopes are atoms of the same element having the same atomic number (Z) but different mass numbers (A) due to varying neutron counts.
  • Isobars are atoms of different elements having the same mass number (A) but different atomic numbers (Z).
  • Isotones are atoms of different elements having the same number of neutrons (N) but different atomic and mass numbers.
  • Isoelectronic species are atoms, ions, or molecules that possess the exact same total number of electrons.

Step-by-step explanation

  • Isotopes exhibit identical chemical properties because they have the same electronic configuration, but different physical properties (like density and melting point).
  • Hydrogen has three main isotopes: Protium (^1_1 H), Deuterium (^2_1 H or D), and Tritium (^3_1 H or T).
  • Carbon isotopes include Carbon-12, Carbon-13, and radioactive Carbon-14 used in radiometric dating.
  • Isobars have different chemical and physical properties because they belong to different elements (e.g., ^40_18 Ar and ^40_20 Ca both have mass number 40).
  • Isotones share only the neutron count; for instance, Boron-11 (^11_5 B) and Carbon-12 (^12_6 C) both have 6 neutrons.
  • Isoelectronic species show periodic trends in ionic radii; for example, N^3-, O^2-, F^-, Ne, Na^+, Mg^2+, and Al^3+ all possess 10 electrons.
  • Fractional atomic masses on the periodic table occur due to the relative natural abundance of isotopes.
  • Radioisotopes (unstable isotopes) emit radiation and decay into stable nuclides.

Key points

  • Isotopes: Same Z, different A (same element, different neutrons).
  • Isobars: Same A, different Z (different elements, same total nucleons).
  • Isotones: Same N (A - Z), different Z and A.
  • Isoelectronic: Same total number of electrons.
  • Chemical properties depend on Z (electrons); thus isotopes have identical chemical behavior.
  • Physical properties depend on mass; thus isotopes have different physical properties.
Worked examples
  • Chlorine isotopes (^35_17 Cl at 75% and ^37_17 Cl at 25%): Average atomic mass = (35 × 0.75) + (37 × 0.25) = 26.25 + 9.25 = 35.5 amu.
  • Identify the relationship between ^14_6 C and ^14_7 N: They are isobars because both have mass number 14, but different atomic numbers (6 and 7).

Applications and connections

  • Carbon-14 dating for archaeological artifacts.
  • Cobalt-60 isotope used in cancer radiotherapy.
  • Uranium-235 enrichment for nuclear power generation and weapons.
  • Tracer techniques in biochemical and medical research using radioactive isotopes.

Advanced points, exceptions and limitations

  • Odd-even rule in nuclear stability: Nuclides with even numbers of both protons and neutrons are exceptionally stable.
  • Nuclear isomers are nuclides with the same atomic and mass numbers but existing in different excited nuclear energy states.
  • Isotopic effect causes slight differences in reaction rates (kinetic isotope effect), especially with deuterium substitution.

Additional examples

  • Protium, Deuterium, and Tritium (Isotopes of Hydrogen).
  • Argon (^40_18 Ar), Potassium (^40_19 K), and Calcium (^40_20 Ca) (Isobars).
  • Silicon (^30_14 Si), Phosphorus (^31_15 P), and Sulfur (^32_16 S) with 16 neutrons (Isotones).
Important distinctions
  • Isotopes have same Z and different N; Isobars have different Z and different N but same A.
  • Isotones have same N; Isobars have same A.
  • Isoelectronic series involve electrons; isotopes involve neutrons.
Common misconceptions
  • Believing isotopes have different chemical properties (they react identically because their valence electron configuration is identical).
  • Assuming isobars belong to the same element (isobars are always different elements because Z differs).
  • Confusing isotones (same neutrons) with isotopes (same protons).
Exam focus
  • Master identification questions for isotopes, isobars, and isotones from given atomic symbols.
  • Calculate average atomic mass given percentage abundances of isotopes.
  • Identify isoelectronic ions and rank them by ionic radius (larger negative charge = larger radius).
  • Recall important medical and industrial applications of specific radioisotopes (Co-60, I-131, U-235, C-14).
  • Solve numerical matching and multiple-choice questions frequently asked in UP TGT/PGT exams.

Rapid revision: Matter & Atomic Structure

Review every concept point-wise before attempting the quiz and timed test.

Concept 1

States of Matter and Interparticle Forces

Definition and scope

  • Matter exists in solid, liquid, gaseous, plasma, and Bose-Einstein condensate states.
  • State depends on the balance between thermal energy and intermolecular forces.
  • Phase transitions occur when energy changes at constant temperature.

Must remember

  • Solids have fixed shape and volume; liquids have fixed volume only; gases have neither.
  • Plasma consists of ionized gas at ultra-high temperatures.
  • BEC occurs near absolute zero as a single quantum state.
  • Amorphous solids lack long-range crystalline order.
  • Hydrogen bonding is a special strong dipole-dipole interaction.

Concept-specific exam traps

  • Confusing glass as a crystalline solid rather than an amorphous solid.
  • Assuming liquids are completely compressible.
Concept 2

Fundamental Subatomic Particles

Definition and scope

  • Atoms consist of protons (+), neutrons (0), and electrons (-).
  • Nucleus contains protons and neutrons (nucleons).
  • Electrons orbit the nucleus in energy levels.

Must remember

  • Electron discovered by J.J. Thomson.
  • Proton discovered by Goldstein/Rutherford.
  • Neutron discovered by James Chadwick.
  • Protons and neutrons have mass ~1 amu; electrons have negligible mass.
  • Strong nuclear force holds the nucleus together.

Concept-specific exam traps

  • Assuming electrons orbit in fixed circular planetary paths.
  • Forgetting that the nucleus occupies a tiny fraction of total atomic volume.
Concept 3

Atomic Number, Mass Number, and Nuclide Notation

Definition and scope

  • Atomic number (Z) equals the number of protons.
  • Mass number (A) equals protons plus neutrons.
  • Nuclide notation is written as ^A_Z X.

Must remember

  • Z uniquely identifies an element.
  • Number of neutrons N = A - Z.
  • In a neutral atom, protons equal electrons.
  • Ions differ in electron count, not Z or A.
  • Fractional atomic weights arise from isotopic mixtures.

Concept-specific exam traps

  • Confusing atomic number with atomic mass or atomic weight.
  • Assuming neutron count equals proton count for all atoms.
Concept 4

Isotopes, Isobars, Isotones, and Isoelectronic Species

Definition and scope

  • Isotopes: Same Z, different A.
  • Isobars: Same A, different Z.
  • Isotones: Same neutron count N.

Must remember

  • Isotopes have identical chemical properties due to same electron configuration.
  • Isoelectronic species have the exact same number of electrons.
  • Carbon-14 is used for radiometric dating.
  • Cobalt-60 is used in cancer therapy.
  • Isobars belong to different elements.

Concept-specific exam traps

  • Believing isotopes have different chemical properties.
  • Assuming isobars belong to the same element.

Quick facts

  • Matter states are governed by the interplay between thermal energy and intermolecular forces.
  • J.J. Thomson discovered the electron using cathode ray tubes in 1897.
  • James Chadwick discovered the neutron in 1932.
  • Atomic number (Z) equals the number of protons in an atom's nucleus.
  • Mass number (A) is the sum of protons and neutrons (nucleons).
  • Isotopes have the same atomic number but different mass numbers.
  • Isobars have the same mass number but different atomic numbers.
  • Isotones have the same number of neutrons.
  • Isoelectronic species share identical electron counts.
  • Protium is the only stable isotope without neutrons.

Glossary

MatterAnything that has mass and occupies space.
PlasmaAn ionized state of matter comprising free electrons and positive ions at high temperatures.
NucleonA subatomic particle residing in the atomic nucleus (proton or neutron).
Atomic NumberThe total number of protons in the nucleus of an atom.
Mass NumberThe total sum of protons and neutrons in an atomic nucleus.
IsotopeAtoms of the same element with identical atomic numbers but different mass numbers.
IsobarAtoms of different elements with the same mass number.
IsotoneAtoms of different elements with the same number of neutrons.
IsoelectronicSpecies possessing the exact same number of electrons.
SublimationDirect phase transition of a substance from solid to gas without passing through the liquid state.

Important distinctions

IsotopesvsIsobars
  • Isotopes have the same atomic number (Z); Isobars have different atomic numbers.
  • Isotopes belong to the same element; Isobars belong to different elements.
ProtonvsElectron
  • Protons reside in the nucleus with positive charge; electrons orbit outside with negative charge.
  • Protons have a mass of ~1 amu; electrons are ~1836 times lighter with negligible mass.
SolidvsGas
  • Solids have definite shape and volume; gases have indefinite shape and volume.
  • Solids have strong interparticle forces and low thermal energy; gases have negligible interparticle forces and high thermal energy.

Memory hooks

  • Remember isotopes with 'P' (Same Protons), isobars with 'A' (Same Mass Number A), and isotones with 'N' (Same Neutrons).
  • PEN: Proton = positive, Electron = negative, Neutron = neutral.
  • Z is for Zoo (Z = atomic number = protons); A is for Aggregate mass (A = mass number).

Exam traps and cautions

  • Do not confuse mass number (integer sum of nucleons) with atomic weight (weighted average on periodic table).
  • Remember that isotopes have identical chemical properties, not physical properties.
  • Do not assume all atoms have neutrons; ordinary hydrogen (Protium) has none.
  • Check whether an ion or neutral atom is specified when counting electrons.

Question 8Case-based

Case: A positively charged ion (cation) derived from an element with atomic number 12 and mass number 24 loses 2 electrons. What is the number of protons, neutrons, and electrons in this ion?

Protons = 12 (Z), Neutrons = 24 - 12 = 12, Electrons = 12 - 2 (lost) = 10.
