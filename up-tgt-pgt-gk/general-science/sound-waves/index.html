Concept 1 · foundation

Mechanical and Longitudinal Wave Motion

Sound waves are mechanical longitudinal waves requiring a material medium for propagation through particle oscillations parallel to energy transfer.

Prerequisites

  • Basic understanding of simple harmonic motion and restoring forces.
  • Concept of elasticity and inertia in material media.

Definitions and scope

  • Mechanical wave: A disturbance that travels through a medium due to elastic properties of the medium.
  • Longitudinal wave: A wave in which particles of the medium vibrate back and forth in the same direction as the propagation of the wave.
  • Compression: Region of high pressure and density where particles are closest together.
  • Rarefaction: Region of low pressure and density where particles are furthest apart.

Step-by-step explanation

  • Sound originates from vibrating bodies that impart mechanical disturbances to adjacent medium particles.
  • The medium must possess elasticity so that displaced particles experience restoring forces to return to equilibrium.
  • The medium must possess inertia or mass so that particles store kinetic energy and overshoot their equilibrium positions.
  • As a longitudinal wave travels, energy is transferred through successive compressions and rarefactions without bulk transport of matter.
  • Sound waves cannot travel through a vacuum because there are no particles to transmit mechanical vibrations.
  • The speed of sound depends directly on the square root of the bulk modulus of elasticity and inversely on the density of the medium.
  • Solids possess high elasticity compared to gases, which explains why sound travels fastest in solid steel than in water or air.
  • Temperature increase in gases increases molecular velocity, thereby increasing the speed of sound through the medium.

Key points

  • Sound requires a material medium for propagation.
  • Particle displacement is parallel to wave velocity in longitudinal waves.
  • Compressions and rarefactions constitute the wave profile.
  • Speed of sound is maximum in solids and minimum in gases.
  • Vacuum acts as a total barrier to sound transmission.
  • Frequency remains constant when a wave crosses from one medium to another.
Worked examples
  • Calculating wavelength given velocity v = 340 m/s and frequency f = 500 Hz: lambda = v / f = 340 / 500 = 0.68 meters.
  • Determining time taken by sound to travel 1.5 km in air where speed is 330 m/s: t = distance / speed = 1500 / 330 = 4.54 seconds.

Applications and connections

  • Acoustic design of auditoriums and concert halls.
  • Stethoscope design utilizing solid and hollow tube conduction.
  • Seismic monitoring using primary (P) waves which are longitudinal seismic waves.

Advanced points, exceptions and limitations

  • Newton's formula for sound speed assumed isothermal change, yielding 280 m/s in air, which Laplace corrected using adiabatic assumptions to yield ~332 m/s.
  • Displacement wave and pressure wave are out of phase by pi/2 radians; maximum particle displacement occurs at pressure nodes.
  • Dispersion effects in non-homogeneous media cause wave front distortion over long distances.

Additional examples

  • A vibrating tuning fork creating compressions and rarefactions in surrounding air.
  • Human vocal cords producing longitudinal pressure variations in the vocal tract.
  • A ringing bell causing air molecules to oscillate longitudinally.
Important distinctions
  • Longitudinal waves involve particle motion parallel to propagation; transverse waves involve particle motion perpendicular to propagation.
  • Longitudinal waves can propagate in solids, liquids, and gases; transverse elastic waves typically propagate efficiently only in solids and on liquid surfaces.
  • Longitudinal waves form compressions/rarefactions; transverse waves form crests/troughs.
Common misconceptions
  • Believing that air molecules travel all the way from the sound source to the ear.
  • Assuming sound travels faster in vacuum because there is no air resistance.
  • Thinking transverse waves and longitudinal waves have identical particle displacement characteristics.
Exam focus
  • Laplace correction formula for speed of sound in gases.
  • Effect of temperature, humidity, and pressure on sound velocity.
  • Phase relationship between displacement and pressure waves.
  • Inability of sound waves to travel through interstellar vacuum.
  • Dependence of wave speed on medium properties rather than frequency.
Concept 2 · core

Ultrasonic Waves and Their Industrial Applications

Ultrasonic waves are high-frequency mechanical waves above the human audible limit (20 kHz) possessing high energy and directional properties.

Prerequisites

  • Definition of audible frequency spectrum (20 Hz to 20,000 Hz).
  • Piezoelectric effect and magnetostriction principles.

Definitions and scope

  • Infrasonic waves: Sound waves with frequencies below 20 Hz.
  • Ultrasonic waves (Ultrasound): Sound waves with frequencies exceeding 20,000 Hz (20 kHz).
  • Piezoelectric effect: Generation of electric charges by mechanical stress in quartz or similar crystals, and vice versa.
  • SONAR: Sound Navigation and Ranging utilizing ultrasonic pulses for underwater detection.

Step-by-step explanation

  • The human ear cannot perceive ultrasound due to biological limitations of the basilar membrane in the cochlea.
  • Ultrasound can be generated in the laboratory using piezoelectric oscillators or magnetostriction oscillators.
  • Because of their extremely short wavelengths, ultrasonic waves can be beamed in highly directional rays without significant diffraction.
  • Ultrasound can penetrate dense media over considerable distances, making it ideal for underwater exploration.
  • When high-intensity ultrasound passes through liquids, it produces microscopic vapor cavities that collapse violently, a process called cavitation.
  • Cavitation cleaning utilizes ultrasonic baths to scrub microscopic dirt from delicate instruments and jewelry.
  • Non-destructive testing (NDT) uses ultrasound to detect internal cracks, blowholes, and flaws in metal castings and machine parts.
  • Medical ultrasonography uses reflected ultrasonic pulses to image soft tissues, fetuses, and blood flow via the Doppler shift.

Key points

  • Audible range: 20 Hz to 20 kHz; Ultrasound: > 20 kHz.
  • Generated via piezoelectric and magnetostrictive oscillators.
  • Highly directional due to short wavelength.
  • Used in SONAR for depth sounding and obstacle detection.
  • Employs cavitation principle for cleaning and homogenizing.
  • Essential tool in medical diagnostics (sonography, echocardiography).
Worked examples
  • A SONAR pulse sent to the ocean floor returns in 0.4 seconds. If the speed of sound in seawater is 1500 m/s, calculate depth: Depth = (v * t) / 2 = (1500 * 0.4) / 2 = 300 meters.
  • Determining if a frequency of 35 kHz is audible to humans: Yes/No, since 35,000 Hz > 20,000 Hz, it is ultrasonic and inaudible.

Applications and connections

  • Medical ultrasound imaging and ultrasonic therapy.
  • SONAR for submarine navigation and mapping ocean topography.
  • Ultrasonic welding of plastics and cleaning of precision mechanical parts.

Advanced points, exceptions and limitations

  • High-intensity focused ultrasound (HIFU) can thermally destroy malignant tumors without invasive surgery.
  • Bats use echolocation with ultrasonic clicks ranging from 20 kHz to over 100 kHz to navigate and hunt flying insects in complete darkness.
  • Attenuation of ultrasound increases exponentially with frequency, limiting maximum penetration depth at very high frequencies (e.g., above 15 MHz).

Additional examples

  • Galton's whistle producing ultrasonic sounds used to call dogs.
  • Bats emitting high-frequency squeaks to detect obstacles.
  • Ultrasonic humidifiers producing fine water aerosols.
Important distinctions
  • Infrasonic waves have large wavelengths and low frequencies (<20 Hz); ultrasonic waves have tiny wavelengths and high frequencies (>20 kHz).
  • Audible sound diffracts easily around large obstacles; ultrasound travels in sharp, directional beams.
  • Bats utilize ultrasound for echolocation; whales and elephants communicate over long distances using infrasound.
Common misconceptions
  • Assuming ultrasonic waves are electromagnetic waves like X-rays or radio waves.
  • Believing that humans can hear ultrasound if it is loud enough.
  • Thinking ultrasonic waves can travel through a vacuum because they are high frequency.
Exam focus
  • Frequency threshold of ultrasound (>20 kHz).
  • Piezoelectric crystals (quartz, tourmaline) used in ultrasound generators.
  • Working principle of SONAR and calculation of depth.
  • Industrial and medical applications of cavitation.
  • Comparison with infrasonic and audible frequency domains.
Concept 3 · application

Doppler Effect in Sound and Frequency Shifts

The apparent change in frequency of a sound wave due to relative motion between the source and the observer is known as the Doppler effect.

Prerequisites

  • Wave frequency, wavelength, and wave speed relationship (v = f * lambda).
  • Concept of relative velocity in one dimension.

Definitions and scope

  • Doppler Effect: Apparent shift in frequency perceived by an observer when there is relative motion between source and observer.
  • Apparent frequency (f'): The frequency heard by the moving or stationary observer.
  • Source velocity (v_s): Speed of the sound-emitting object relative to the medium.
  • Observer velocity (v_o): Speed of the sound-receiving observer relative to the medium.

Step-by-step explanation

  • When a sound source moves towards a stationary observer, wave crests are compressed closer together, resulting in a shorter apparent wavelength and higher perceived pitch.
  • When a sound source moves away from a stationary observer, wave crests are spread apart, leading to a longer apparent wavelength and lower perceived pitch.
  • The general Doppler formula is f' = f * [(v +- v_o) / (v -+ v_s)], where v is the speed of sound in the medium.
  • Sign convention: Use top signs (plus for observer towards, minus for source towards) when motion decreases distance between source and observer.
  • If the observer moves towards a stationary source, the relative speed of sound waves increases, leading to a higher apparent frequency.
  • If the wind is blowing, the velocity of the wind must be vectorially added to the speed of sound v in the Doppler calculation.
  • The Doppler effect is asymmetric: the formula differs slightly depending on whether the source is moving towards the observer or the observer is moving towards the source at the same speed.
  • When the relative speed equals or exceeds the speed of sound (Mach 1), a sonic boom is produced by shock waves.

Key points

  • Apparent frequency increases when source/observer approach each other.
  • Apparent frequency decreases when source/observer move apart.
  • Formula: f' = f * [(v +- v_o) / (v -+ v_s)].
  • Applies to both sound and light, though light requires relativistic formulas.
  • Wind velocity affects the effective speed of sound in Doppler equations.
  • No Doppler shift occurs if source and observer move at the same speed in the same direction.
Worked examples
  • A train blowing a whistle of frequency 400 Hz moves towards a stationary observer at 20 m/s. Speed of sound is 340 m/s. Find apparent frequency: f' = 400 * [340 / (340 - 20)] = 400 * (340 / 320) = 425 Hz.
  • An observer moves away from a stationary siren of 600 Hz at 15 m/s. Speed of sound is 330 m/s. Find f': f' = 600 * [(330 - 15) / 330] = 600 * (315 / 330) = 572.73 Hz.

Applications and connections

  • Radar and sonar speed traps used by traffic police and defense tracking.
  • Astrophysical red-shift and blue-shift measurements of stars and galaxies.
  • Medical echocardiography and blood flow velocity measurement using ultrasound Doppler.

Advanced points, exceptions and limitations

  • The Doppler effect for electromagnetic waves involves relativistic corrections because light does not require a medium and depends on Lorentz transformations.
  • When a supersonic jet flies overhead, a conical shock wave sweeps across the ground, producing a sudden explosive sound known as a sonic boom.
  • Transverse Doppler effect occurs when motion is perpendicular to the line of sight, which is strictly a relativistic phenomenon for light.

Additional examples

  • Pitch of an ambulance siren rising as it approaches and dropping sharply as it passes by.
  • Change in engine note of a race car passing a grandstand.
  • Whistle of a passing express train changing pitch for a platform passenger.
Important distinctions
  • Doppler effect in sound depends on the medium and velocities relative to the medium; in light, it depends solely on relative velocity between source and observer.
  • Sound Doppler shift formulas have separate terms for source and observer motion; optical Doppler shift uses a single relativistic relative velocity term.
  • Sound requires a material medium (air, water); light waves do not require a medium.
Common misconceptions
  • Assuming the actual frequency emitted by the source changes due to its motion.
  • Thinking the Doppler shift is only observed when the source moves, ignoring observer motion.
  • Believing the Doppler effect occurs instantaneously across all points in space regardless of wave travel time.
Exam focus
  • Memorization and correct sign application in the standard Doppler formula.
  • Distinguishing between source motion and observer motion scenarios.
  • Sonic boom generation and Mach number concept (v_source / v_sound).
  • Effect of wind direction on Doppler frequency calculations.
  • Numerical problems involving ambulance/train whistles.
Concept 4 · application

Echo, Reverberation, and Architectural Acoustics

An echo is the repetition of sound produced by the reflection of sound waves from a hard obstacle, subject to persistence of hearing limitations.

Prerequisites

  • Reflection laws of wave motion.
  • Human auditory persistence (approximately 0.1 seconds).

Definitions and scope

  • Echo: Distinct repetition of original sound heard after reflection from a rigid obstacle.
  • Reverberation: Persistence of sound in an enclosed space due to multiple reflections from walls, floor, and ceiling.
  • Persistence of hearing: The retention of a sensory impression in the human brain for 1/10th of a second (0.1 s).
  • Sabine's formula: Mathematical relation determining reverberation time of an auditorium based on volume and total absorption.

Step-by-step explanation

  • For a distinct echo to be heard, the reflected sound must reach the human ear at least 0.1 seconds after the original direct sound.
  • Taking the speed of sound in air as approximately 340 m/s, the minimum distance required to hear an echo is (340 m/s * 0.1 s) / 2 = 17 meters.
  • If the reflecting surface is closer than 17 meters, the reflected sound blends with the original sound, causing reverberation or confusion.
  • Excessive reverberation makes speech unintelligible in auditoriums due to overlapping sound syllables.
  • To reduce undesirable reverberation, walls and ceilings are covered with sound-absorbing materials like compressed fiberboard, heavy curtains, and acoustic tiles.
  • Megaphones, horns, and curved soundboards utilize multiple reflections to guide sound waves in a desired direction without loss of intensity.
  • Concert halls are designed with curved ceilings that reflect and distribute sound evenly to every seat in the audience.
  • Multiple echoes can occur when sound bounces back and forth between two parallel reflecting surfaces, such as in canyons or long corridors.

Key points

  • Minimum distance for an echo in air is 17 meters.
  • Persistence of human hearing is 0.1 seconds.
  • Reverberation is caused by multiple reflections in a closed room.
  • Acoustic absorption materials eliminate unwanted echoes and flutter echoes.
  • Soundboards focus and project speaker voices across large halls.
  • Calculated formula for echo distance: d = (v * t) / 2.
Worked examples
  • A person standing in front of a cliff claps hands and hears an echo after 2.0 seconds. If sound speed is 340 m/s, find distance to cliff: d = (340 * 2) / 2 = 340 meters.
  • Calculate the minimum distance in water (speed = 1500 m/s) required to hear an echo: d = (1500 * 0.1) / 2 = 75 meters.

Applications and connections

  • Acoustic design of theaters, broadcasting studios, and lecture halls.
  • Depth sounding and ocean floor mapping via echo-sounding.
  • Echolocation used by bats and marine mammals to navigate.

Advanced points, exceptions and limitations

  • Sabine's reverberation time formula: T = 0.161 * V / A, where V is room volume in cubic meters and A is total absorption in metric sabins.
  • Optimum reverberation time for speech is around 0.5 to 1.0 seconds, while for orchestral music it ranges from 1.5 to 2.2 seconds.
  • Flutter echo occurs in rooms with parallel hard walls, producing a high-pitched ringing sound following a hand clap.

Additional examples

  • Hearing one's voice bounce back from a distant mountain cliff.
  • A sharp echo heard in an empty unfurnished room before carpets are installed.
  • Sound reflection inside a well or deep cave.
Important distinctions
  • Echo is a single, distinct repetition of sound; reverberation is a prolonged blending of multiple reflected sounds.
  • Echo requires a minimum obstacle distance of 17 m in air; reverberation occurs in enclosed spaces regardless of distance due to multiple boundary reflections.
  • Echoes are desirable in sonar/radar but disruptive in speech acoustics; controlled reverberation enhances musical warmth.
Common misconceptions
  • Believing an echo can be heard at any distance as long as the sound is loud enough.
  • Confusing echo with refraction of sound waves.
  • Thinking acoustic tiles completely block sound transmission rather than absorbing reflected energy.
Exam focus
  • Calculation of 17 meters minimum distance for echo in air.
  • Persistence of hearing time (0.1 s).
  • Methods to control reverberation in architectural acoustics.
  • Sabine's concept of reverberation time.
  • Distinction between echo and reverberation.
Concept 5 · advanced

Characteristics of Musical Sound: Pitch, Loudness, and Quality

Musical sounds are distinguished by three subjective psychological attributes: pitch, loudness, and quality (timbre), which correspond to physical wave parameters.

Prerequisites

  • Wave frequency, amplitude, and waveform harmonic structure.
  • Logarithmic scaling and decibel scale.

Definitions and scope

  • Pitch: The psychological attribute of sound that allows us to classify it as high or low, determined primarily by frequency.
  • Loudness: The subjective perception of sound intensity by the human ear, measured in decibels (dB).
  • Quality (Timbre): The characteristic of sound that enables a listener to distinguish between two sounds of the same pitch and loudness produced by different instruments.
  • Harmonics: Integer multiples of the fundamental frequency produced by a vibrating body.

Step-by-step explanation

  • Pitch depends directly on frequency: higher frequency produces a shrill, high-pitched sound (e.g., a whistle), while lower frequency produces a grave, low-pitched sound (e.g., a drum).
  • Women and children generally have vocal cords vibrating at higher frequencies, giving their voices a higher pitch than adult males.
  • Loudness depends on the intensity of the sound wave, which is proportional to the square of the amplitude (I proportional to A^2).
  • Sound intensity is measured in watts per square meter (W/m^2), but human hearing response is logarithmic, leading to the decibel (dB) scale.
  • The threshold of hearing is 0 dB (10^-12 W/m^2), while the threshold of pain is around 120 dB to 140 dB.
  • Quality (timbre) depends on the number, relative prominence, and frequency of overtones or harmonics accompanying the fundamental frequency.
  • A tuning fork produces a pure tone consisting of a single frequency with no overtones, whereas a violin or flute produces rich harmonic waveforms.
  • Noise consists of a random mixture of many frequencies without a definite pitch or harmonious harmonic relationship.

Key points

  • Pitch depends on frequency; loudness depends on amplitude/intensity.
  • Quality (timbre) depends on harmonic overtone structure.
  • Human ear loudness scale is logarithmic, measured in decibels (dB).
  • Pure tone has only one frequency; musical notes have a fundamental plus harmonics.
  • Intensity is proportional to the square of wave amplitude.
  • Normal conversation level is roughly 60 dB.
Worked examples
  • If the amplitude of a sound wave is tripled, how does its intensity change? Intensity I proportional to A^2, so new intensity is 3^2 = 9 times the original intensity.
  • Comparing two sound sources where intensity increases by a factor of 10, the decibel level increases by 10 dB.

Applications and connections

  • Acoustic engineering of musical instruments and sound reproduction systems.
  • Audiometry and hearing testing for occupational health safety.
  • Noise pollution monitoring and legal decibel limits in urban planning.

Advanced points, exceptions and limitations

  • Fletcher-Munson curves demonstrate equal-loudness contours for the human ear, showing that human hearing sensitivity varies across different frequencies.
  • Combination tones (sum and difference frequencies) can be perceived when two loud, high-frequency pure tones are played simultaneously.
  • Fourier analysis proves that any complex periodic sound wave can be mathematically resolved into a sum of simple sine waves corresponding to fundamental and harmonic frequencies.

Additional examples

  • Distinguishing the sound of a violin from a piano playing the exact same musical note (Middle C).
  • A mosquito buzzing having a high pitch compared to a lion's roar having a low pitch.
  • Whispering at 20 dB versus a jet engine takeoff at 130 dB.
Important distinctions
  • Pitch is subjective to frequency; loudness is subjective to amplitude; quality is subjective to waveform/harmonics.
  • Intensity is an objective physical quantity measured in W/m^2; loudness is the subjective human sensation measured in decibels or phons.
  • Noise has random, non-periodic waveforms; musical sound has periodic waveforms with harmonic relationships.
Common misconceptions
  • Confusing loudness (amplitude-dependent) with pitch (frequency-dependent).
  • Believing that decibels scale linearly rather than logarithmically with sound intensity.
  • Thinking a tuning fork produces complex overtones instead of a nearly pure single frequency.
Exam focus
  • Dependence of pitch on frequency and loudness on amplitude.
  • Relationship between intensity and square of amplitude.
  • Role of overtones and harmonics in determining sound quality (timbre).
  • Decibel scale definition and human hearing thresholds.
  • Distinction between musical sound and noise.

  • Intensity is proportional to square of amplitude.
  • Decibel scale is logarithmic.
  • Pure tones have single frequencies; musical notes have harmonics.
  • Threshold of hearing is 0 dB.
  • Fourier analysis resolves complex waveforms into harmonics.

Concept-specific exam traps

  • Confusing pitch with loudness.
  • Assuming decibels scale linearly with sound intensity.

Quick facts

  • Speed of sound in dry air at 0°C is approximately 332 m/s, and at 20°C it is ~343 m/s.
  • Sound travels roughly 4.5 times faster in pure water than in air.
  • The human ear is most sensitive to frequencies between 2,000 Hz and 4,000 Hz.
  • Infrasonic waves are generated during earthquakes by tectonic plate movements.
  • Bats navigate using ultrasonic pulses ranging from 20 kHz to 100 kHz.

Glossary

CompressionRegion in a longitudinal wave where particles are compressed, resulting in high pressure.
RarefactionRegion in a longitudinal wave where particles are spread apart, resulting in low pressure.
Piezoelectric EffectProperty of certain crystals to generate electrical charges under mechanical stress and vice versa.
CavitationFormation and violent collapse of vapor bubbles in a liquid subjected to intense ultrasound.
TimbreQuality of sound determined by harmonic overtones that differentiates instruments.

Important distinctions

Longitudinal WavesvsTransverse Waves
  • Particle displacement is parallel to wave propagation in longitudinal waves and perpendicular in transverse waves.
  • Longitudinal waves form compressions and rarefactions; transverse waves form crests and troughs.
  • Longitudinal waves propagate in all states of matter; transverse elastic waves are restricted mostly to solids.
EchovsReverberation
  • Echo is a single, distinct repetition of sound; reverberation is a prolonged blending of multiple reflections.
  • Echo requires a minimum obstacle distance of 17 meters; reverberation occurs in enclosed spaces regardless of obstacle distance.
  • Echoes are useful in SONAR and distance measurement; reverberation must be controlled in auditoriums for speech clarity.
PitchvsLoudness
  • Pitch is determined by the frequency of the sound wave; loudness is determined by its amplitude/intensity.
  • High pitch sounds shrill; high loudness sounds intense or loud.
  • Pitch is measured in Hertz (Hz); loudness is measured in decibels (dB).

Memory hooks

  • SONAR uses Sound Navigation And Ranging with high-frequency Ultrasound.
  • ECHO magic number: 17 meters in 0.1 seconds (340 * 0.1 / 2).
  • Pitch = Frequency (both start with P/F or remember 'Pitch the Frequency high').

Exam traps and cautions

  • Doppler sign convention errors: always check whether distance is decreasing or increasing.
  • Forgetting the factor of 2 in echo and sonar reflection calculations.
  • Confusing threshold of hearing (0 dB) with total silence or negative decibels.
  • Assuming sound speed is constant regardless of temperature and medium density.
