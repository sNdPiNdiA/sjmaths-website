Concept 1 · foundation

Bacterial Pathogens and Associated Diseases

Bacterial diseases are caused by single-celled prokaryotic microorganisms that release endotoxins or exotoxins disrupting host cellular metabolism.

Prerequisites

  • Basic structure of prokaryotic cells including peptidoglycan cell walls.
  • Distinction between Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria.
  • Understanding of modes of transmission such as airborne droplets, waterborne, and vector-borne.

Definitions and scope

  • Bacterial infections involve colonization of host tissues by pathogenic bacteria leading to pathological damage.
  • Exotoxins are secreted proteins produced mostly by Gram-positive bacteria, whereas endotoxins are lipopolysaccharides integral to the outer membrane of Gram-negative bacteria.

Step-by-step explanation

  • Mycobacterium tuberculosis causes tuberculosis, primarily affecting the lungs through aerosol transmission and forming granulomas called tubercles.
  • Vibrio cholerae is a comma-shaped, Gram-negative bacterium causing cholera via contaminated water, leading to severe rice-water stools and dehydration.
  • Salmonella typhi causes typhoid fever, characterized by sustained high fever, rose spots, and intestinal perforation, diagnosed via the Widal test.
  • Clostridium tetani produces tetanospasmin, a neurotoxin causing spastic paralysis, lockjaw, and muscle spasms.
  • Corynebacterium diphtheriae causes diphtheria, forming a pseudomembrane in the throat and producing an exotoxin that damages the heart and nerves.
  • Bordetella pertussis causes whooping cough (pertussis), characterized by paroxysmal coughing fits ending in a characteristic whoop.
  • Treponema pallidum is a spirochete causing syphilis, a sexually transmitted infection progressing through primary, secondary, latent, and tertiary stages.
  • Yersinia pestis, transmitted by rat fleas, causes plague, manifesting as bubonic, septicemic, or pneumonic forms.

Key points

  • Tuberculosis is treated with DOTS therapy involving Rifampicin and Isoniazid.
  • Cholera causes rapid dehydration requiring oral rehydration therapy and tetracyclines.
  • Typhoid targets the Peyer's patches in the small intestine.
  • Tetanus toxin blocks inhibitory neurotransmitters in the central nervous system.
  • Diphtheria involves the formation of a grayish pseudomembrane.
  • Widal test is used for the serological diagnosis of typhoid fever.
Worked examples
  • Example 1: A patient presents with prolonged cough, hemoptysis, and low-grade evening fever; acid-fast staining of sputum confirms Mycobacterium tuberculosis.
  • Example 2: A patient presents with abrupt watery diarrhea and severe dehydration after consuming contaminated municipal water; stool culture isolates curved Gram-negative rods identified as Vibrio cholerae.

Applications and connections

  • Clinical diagnosis and differential identification of bacterial infections in public health laboratories.
  • Implementation of sanitation protocols and water purification to prevent waterborne bacterial epidemics.
  • Execution of national immunization programs involving DPT and BCG vaccines.

Advanced points, exceptions and limitations

  • Horizontal gene transfer via plasmids confers multidrug resistance (MDR) in strains like Mycobacterium tuberculosis and Vibrio cholerae.
  • Biofilm formation by pathogenic bacteria on medical implants protects them from host immune clearance and antibiotic action.
  • Endotoxin shock (septic shock) triggered by lipopolysaccharides can lead to fatal systemic hypotension.

Additional examples

  • Mycobacterium tuberculosis causing pulmonary TB.
  • Vibrio cholerae causing epidemic cholera.
  • Clostridium tetani causing lockjaw.
Important distinctions
  • Bacterial diseases respond to antibiotics, whereas viral diseases do not.
  • Bacteria are cellular organisms with independent metabolic machinery, unlike obligate intracellular viruses.
  • Gram-negative bacteria possess an outer lipopolysaccharide membrane absent in typical Gram-positive bacteria.
Common misconceptions
  • Antibiotics can cure viral infections like the common cold.
  • All bacteria are harmful pathogens.
  • Boiling water removes chemical toxins as effectively as it kills bacterial pathogens.
Exam focus
  • Causative agents and transmission vectors for TB, Cholera, Typhoid, and Tetanus.
  • Diagnostic tests such as the Widal test for typhoid and Acid-Fast Bacilli (AFB) stain for TB.
  • Mode of action of bacterial toxins like tetanospasmin.
Concept 2 · core

Viral Pathogens and Systemic Infections

Viral diseases are caused by obligate intracellular infectious agents consisting of genetic material enclosed in a protein capsid.

Prerequisites

  • Basic structure of viruses including capsid, envelope, and nucleic acid core (DNA or RNA).
  • Replication cycle of viruses: attachment, penetration, uncoating, replication, assembly, and release.
  • Concept of host cell specificity and tropism.

Definitions and scope

  • Viruses are submicroscopic infectious agents that replicate only inside living host cells, highjacking cellular machinery.
  • Retroviruses are RNA viruses that use reverse transcriptase to integrate their genome into the host DNA.

Step-by-step explanation

  • Poliovirus causes poliomyelitis, spreading via the fecal-oral route, attacking motor neurons in the spinal cord and causing flaccid paralysis.
  • Rabies virus (Lyssavirus) affects the central nervous system, transmitted through the saliva of rabid animals, causing hydrophobia and fatal encephalomyelitis.
  • Human Immunodeficiency Virus (HIV) infects CD4+ T-helper cells, progressively destroying cell-mediated immunity and leading to Acquired Immunodeficiency Syndrome (AIDS).
  • Influenza virus causes seasonal flu, mutating via antigenic drift and antigenic shift, leading to respiratory illness.
  • Dengue virus, transmitted by Aedes aegypti mosquitoes, causes breakbone fever with severe joint pain, thrombocytopenia, and potential hemorrhagic fever.
  • Hepatitis viruses (A, B, C, D, E) cause inflammation of the liver; Hepatitis B and C are bloodborne and sexually transmitted, while A and E are enteric.
  • Measles virus causes rubeola, characterized by high fever, maculopapular rash, and Koplik spots on the buccal mucosa.
  • Smallpox, eradicated globally, was caused by the Variola virus, characterized by pustular rashes leaving characteristic scars.

Key points

  • Poliovirus is prevented using oral polio vaccine (OPV) and inactivated polio vaccine (IPV).
  • Rabies has a nearly 100% fatality rate once clinical symptoms appear, requiring immediate post-exposure prophylaxis.
  • HIV utilizes reverse transcriptase enzyme to transcribe its RNA into complementary DNA.
  • Dengue vector Aedes aegypti breeds in clean stagnant water and bites during daylight.
  • Koplik spots are pathognomonic clinical signs of measles.
  • Hepatitis B infection can progress to chronic carrier state, cirrhosis, and hepatocellular carcinoma.
Worked examples
  • Example 1: A patient presenting with high fever, retro-orbital pain, bleeding gums, and petechiae after an Aedes mosquito bite is diagnosed with Dengue Hemorrhagic Fever.
  • Example 2: A lab test revealing a drop in CD4+ T-cell count below 200 cells/mm³ in an HIV-positive patient confirms progression to AIDS.

Applications and connections

  • Epidemiological surveillance and vector control programs to manage arboviral outbreaks.
  • Antiretroviral therapy (ART) design targeting viral enzymes like reverse transcriptase and protease.
  • Global vaccination campaigns leading to the eradication of smallpox and near-eradication of polio.

Advanced points, exceptions and limitations

  • Antigenic shift through gene reassortment in influenza viruses can cause global pandemics.
  • Lysogenic versus lytic viral cycles determine whether host cells are immediately lysed or harbor dormant viral genomes (provirus).
  • Oncogenic viruses like HPV and Hepatitis B integrate into host genomes, inactivating tumor suppressor genes.

Additional examples

  • Poliovirus causing acute flaccid paralysis.
  • HIV causing immune system depletion.
  • Influenza virus causing respiratory epidemics.
Important distinctions
  • Viral infections are managed with antivirals and vaccines, whereas bacterial infections require antibiotics.
  • Viruses are metabolically inert outside host cells, unlike bacteria.
  • DNA viruses have lower mutation rates compared to RNA viruses like HIV and Influenza.
Common misconceptions
  • Antibiotics can cure viral infections like influenza or dengue.
  • HIV can be transmitted through casual social contact or mosquito bites.
  • Viral infections always manifest symptoms immediately without an incubation period.
Exam focus
  • Vectors for viral diseases (Aedes for Dengue/Chikungunya, Anopheles for Malaria which is protozoan).
  • Pathognomonic signs such as Koplik spots for measles and hydrophobia for rabies.
  • Mechanism of retroviruses and CD4+ cell destruction in HIV/AIDS.
Concept 3 · core

Fungal and Protozoan Parasitic Infections

Fungal and protozoan infections involve eukaryotic pathogens ranging from multicellular molds and yeasts to unicellular microparasites.

Prerequisites

  • Basic characteristics of eukaryotic pathogens possessing membrane-bound organelles and nuclei.
  • Understanding of saprophytic and parasitic modes of nutrition in fungi.
  • Life cycles of protozoan parasites involving primary and intermediate hosts.

Definitions and scope

  • Mycoses are diseases caused by fungal infections, categorized into superficial, cutaneous, subcutaneous, and systemic types.
  • Protozoan diseases are caused by single-celled eukaryotic animals, often transmitted via vectors or contaminated food and water.

Step-by-step explanation

  • Ringworm (dermatophytosis) is caused by dermatophytes like Microsporum, Trichophyton, and Epidermophyton, leading to scaly, circular skin lesions.
  • Candidiasis is caused by Candida albicans, an opportunistic yeast causing oral thrush, vaginitis, and systemic infections in immunocompromised hosts.
  • Plasmodium species (P. vivax, P. falciparum, P. malariae, P. ovale) cause malaria, transmitted by female Anopheles mosquitoes.
  • The life cycle of Plasmodium involves asexual schizogony in humans and sexual sporogony in the mosquito vector, with hemozoin pigment causing chills and fever.
  • Entamoeba histolytica causes amoebic dysentery (amoebiasis), characterized by abdominal pain, constipation alternating with diarrhea, and bloody stools, transmitted via cysts in contaminated food/water.
  • Leishmania donovani causes Kala-azar (visceral leishmaniasis), transmitted by sandflies, affecting the reticuloendothelial system and causing hepatosplenomegaly.
  • Trypanosoma species cause sleeping sickness (African trypanosomiasis) and Chagas disease, transmitted by tsetse flies and triatomine bugs respectively.
  • Giardia lamblia causes giardiasis, colonizing the small intestine and causing foul-smelling diarrhea and malabsorption.

Key points

  • Malaria paroxysm (chills and high fever) coincides with the rupture of red blood cells releasing merozoites and hemozoin toxin.
  • Plasmodium falciparum causes the most severe and potentially fatal cerebral malaria.
  • Entamoeba histolytica infects the large intestine and can form liver abscesses.
  • Dermatophytes utilize keratin as a nutrient source, limiting infections to skin, hair, and nails.
  • Candida albicans is part of normal human flora that turns pathogenic under immunosuppression or antibiotic abuse.
  • Kala-azar is characterized by massive enlargement of the spleen and liver alongside skin hyperpigmentation.
Worked examples
  • Example 1: A patient presenting with periodic chills, sweating, and high fever every 48 hours is diagnosed with tertian malaria caused by Plasmodium vivax.
  • Example 2: Examination of skin scrapings in potassium hydroxide (KOH) mount showing branching hyphae confirms dermatophytosis.

Applications and connections

  • Vector eradication and larvicidal treatments to control Anopheles and sandfly populations.
  • Development of antimalarial drugs like Chloroquine, Artemisinin-combination therapies (ACT).
  • Clinical diagnosis using stool microscopy for cysts and trophozoites of Entamoeba and Giardia.

Advanced points, exceptions and limitations

  • Antigenic variation in protozoan parasites like Plasmodium and Trypanosoma helps them evade host immune clearance.
  • Fungal cell walls contain chitin and ergosterol, serving as targets for antifungal drugs like azoles and polyenes.
  • Cryptosporidium and Giardia cysts possess thick outer shells rendering them resistant to standard chlorination of water supplies.

Additional examples

  • Plasmodium falciparum causing malignant tertian malaria.
  • Trichophyton causing tinea corporis (ringworm).
  • Entamoeba histolytica causing amoebic dysentery.
Important distinctions
  • Protozoa are unicellular eukaryotes, whereas bacteria are prokaryotes and fungi can be multicellular or unicellular eukaryotes.
  • Antifungals target fungal cell membranes (ergosterol) and cell walls (chitin), whereas antibiotics target bacterial structures.
  • Malaria is vector-borne via Anopheles, while amoebiasis is waterborne via fecal-oral contamination.
Common misconceptions
  • Ringworm is caused by a parasitic worm (helminth) rather than a fungus.
  • Malaria is caused by mosquito bites directly; the mosquito is actually a vector carrying the protozoan parasite.
  • Fungal infections cannot become systemic or life-threatening.
Exam focus
  • Causative species of malaria and their periodicity (Vivax vs Falciparum).
  • Diagnostic features of amoebic dysentery vs bacterial dysentery.
  • Fungal genera responsible for dermatophytosis.
Concept 4 · application

Principles of Immunology, Vaccines, and Passive/Active Immunity

Immunization stimulates the adaptive immune system to develop specific memory against pathogens without causing full-blown disease.

Prerequisites

  • Difference between innate immunity (non-specific) and adaptive immunity (specific).
  • Roles of B-lymphocytes (humoral immunity) and T-lymphocytes (cell-mediated immunity).
  • Structure and function of antibodies (immunoglobulins).

Definitions and scope

  • Active immunity is induced when the host's immune system encounters antigens and generates its own antibodies and memory cells.
  • Passive immunity involves the transfer of preformed antibodies into a recipient, providing immediate but temporary protection.

Step-by-step explanation

  • Natural active immunity results from recovering from an actual clinical or subclinical infection.
  • Artificial active immunity is achieved through vaccination using attenuated, inactivated, subunit, or toxoid antigens.
  • Natural passive immunity occurs via transplacental transfer of IgG from mother to fetus and colostrum transfer of IgA.
  • Artificial passive immunity involves administration of immune globulins or anti-venom containing preformed antibodies against specific toxins or pathogens.
  • Live-attenuated vaccines contain weakened live pathogens that replicate poorly in the host, inducing robust cellular and humoral immunity (e.g., BCG, MMR, OPV).
  • Inactivated (killed) vaccines contain pathogens destroyed by heat, chemicals, or radiation, requiring booster doses (e.g., Salk polio, Rabies, Hepatitis B subunit).
  • Toxoid vaccines contain inactivated bacterial toxins that stimulate antitoxin production (e.g., Diphtheria and Tetanus toxoids).
  • Adjuvants like aluminum salts are added to vaccines to enhance the immune response by prolonging antigen exposure.

Key points

  • Live-attenuated vaccines provide lifelong immunity with fewer doses but pose reversal risks in immunocompromised hosts.
  • Inactivated vaccines are safer for immunocompromised individuals but often require booster doses.
  • Herd immunity protects vulnerable unvaccinated populations when a high percentage of the community is immunized.
  • Colostrum is rich in secretory IgA, protecting the neonatal gut from enteric infections.
  • Anti-snake venom serum is an example of artificial passive immunization.
  • Memory B and T cells ensure a rapid secondary immune response upon subsequent pathogen exposure.
Worked examples
  • Example 1: A newborn infant receives maternal IgG antibodies across the placenta, protecting against measles for the first few months of life (Natural Passive Immunity).
  • Example 2: An infant is administered the BCG vaccine intradermally at birth to induce cell-mediated protection against tuberculosis (Artificial Active Immunity).

Applications and connections

  • Universal Immunization Programme (UIP) execution to eradicate vaccine-preventable diseases.
  • Prophylactic management of accidental exposures using rabies immunoglobulin and tetanus antitoxin.
  • Epidemic control through mass vaccination drives and maintenance of cold chain storage.

Advanced points, exceptions and limitations

  • mRNA vaccines deliver lipid nanoparticle-encapsulated mRNA instructing host cells to transiently express viral spike proteins, eliciting robust immune responses.
  • Antigenic variation can render vaccines ineffective, necessitating strain updates as seen in seasonal influenza vaccines.
  • Original antigenic sin describes the immune system's preference to utilize memory cells from a prior infection rather than mount a de novo response to a mutated variant.

Additional examples

  • BCG vaccine for tuberculosis.
  • MMR vaccine for Measles, Mumps, and Rubella.
  • Tetanus toxoid vaccine.
Important distinctions
  • Active immunity is long-lasting due to memory cells, whereas passive immunity is temporary and fades as antibodies degrade.
  • Active immunity takes weeks to develop, whereas passive immunity provides immediate protection.
  • Live vaccines replicate in the body, while killed or subunit vaccines do not replicate.
Common misconceptions
  • Vaccines can cause the actual disease they are designed to prevent.
  • Passive immunity creates immunological memory.
  • Natural infections provide safer immunity than vaccination.
Exam focus
  • Classification of vaccines (Live-attenuated vs Inactivated vs Toxoid).
  • Examples of natural vs artificial, active vs passive immunity.
  • Role of adjuvants and herd immunity thresholds.
Concept 5 · advanced

Antibiotics, Mechanisms of Action, and Antimicrobial Resistance

Antibiotics are microbial metabolites or synthetic compounds that selectively inhibit or kill bacterial pathogens without harming host tissues.

Prerequisites

  • Understanding of structural differences between prokaryotic and eukaryotic cells.
  • Concept of selective toxicity in pharmacology.
  • Basic bacteriological staining and culture techniques.

Definitions and scope

  • Bactericidal antibiotics kill bacteria outright, whereas bacteriostatic antibiotics inhibit bacterial growth and replication, relying on host immunity to clear the infection.
  • Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) occurs when bacteria mutate or acquire resistance genes, rendering standard antibiotic treatments ineffective.

Step-by-step explanation

  • Penicillins and cephalosporins (Beta-lactams) inhibit bacterial cell wall synthesis by binding to penicillin-binding proteins (PBPs) and disrupting peptidoglycan cross-linking.
  • Aminoglycosides (e.g., Streptomycin, Gentamicin) and Tetracyclines bind to bacterial ribosomes (30S subunit), inhibiting protein synthesis.
  • Macrolides (e.g., Erythromycin) and Chloramphenicol bind to the 50S ribosomal subunit, halting peptide chain elongation.
  • Quinolones and Fluoroquinolones (e.g., Ciprofloxacin) inhibit DNA gyrase and topoisomerase IV, preventing bacterial DNA replication.
  • Sulfonamides act as competitive inhibitors of dihydropteroate synthase, blocking folic acid synthesis essential for nucleotide production.
  • Mechanism of AMR includes enzymatic inactivation of drugs (e.g., beta-lactamases breaking down penicillin), target site modification, and active efflux pumps.
  • Plasmids, transposons, and bacteriophages facilitate horizontal gene transfer of resistance determinants among bacterial populations.
  • Misuse and overuse of broad-spectrum antibiotics in human medicine and agriculture accelerate the selection of multidrug-resistant superbugs.

Key points

  • Alexander Fleming discovered penicillin produced by the fungus Penicillium notatum.
  • Beta-lactamase inhibitors like clavulanic acid are combined with penicillins to overcome bacterial resistance.
  • Tetracyclines are contraindicated in pregnant women and children due to bone growth inhibition and tooth discoloration.
  • Streptomycin was the first effective antibiotic against Mycobacterium tuberculosis.
  • Plasmids often carry multiple resistance genes (R-factors), conferring resistance to several antibiotic classes simultaneously.
  • Superbugs like Methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) pose severe hospital-acquired infection challenges.
Worked examples
  • Example 1: A bacterial infection is treated with amoxicillin combined with clavulanic acid to prevent beta-lactamase enzymes from destroying the antibiotic.
  • Example 2: A urinary tract infection caused by E. coli is treated with ciprofloxacin, which inhibits DNA gyrase and halts bacterial DNA replication.

Applications and connections

  • Rational antibiotic stewardship programs in clinical settings to preserve drug efficacy.
  • Development of novel antimicrobial agents targeting bacterial virulence factors rather than viability.
  • Infection control practices in hospitals to prevent the spread of hospital-acquired (nosocomial) resistant strains.

Advanced points, exceptions and limitations

  • Colistin is often deployed as a last-resort antibiotic for multidrug-resistant Gram-negative bacteria, though plasmid-mediated mcr-1 genes now confer resistance to it.
  • Bacteriophage therapy uses lytic viruses to target specific pathogenic bacteria without disrupting the commensal microbiome.
  • Efflux pumps can expel structurally unrelated classes of antibiotics, leading to multidrug resistance phenotypes.

Additional examples

  • Penicillin inhibiting peptidoglycan synthesis.
  • Ciprofloxacin inhibiting DNA gyrase.
  • Streptomycin binding to 30S ribosomal subunit.
Important distinctions
  • Bactericidal drugs kill bacteria directly, while bacteriostatic drugs halt multiplication, leaving clearance to host phagocytes.
  • Antibiotics target bacterial structures and pathways, whereas antivirals target viral replication enzymes.
  • Narrow-spectrum antibiotics target specific bacterial groups, while broad-spectrum antibiotics affect a wide range of Gram-positive and Gram-negative species.
Common misconceptions
  • Stopping an antibiotic course early is acceptable once symptoms disappear.
  • Antibiotics are effective against viral infections like the common cold or flu.
  • Human cells develop resistance to antibiotics directly.
Exam focus
  • Discovery of penicillin and major antibiotic classes along with their cellular targets.
  • Mechanism of antimicrobial resistance and horizontal gene transfer.
  • Distinction between bactericidal and bacteriostatic agents.

Concept 1

Bacterial Pathogens and Associated Diseases

Definition and scope

  • Infections caused by prokaryotic microorganisms releasing toxins.
  • Exotoxins are secreted by Gram-positive bacteria; endotoxins are lipopolysaccharides in Gram-negative cell walls.
  • Treatable using targeted antibiotic regimens.

Must remember

  • M. tuberculosis causes TB, treated with DOTS.
  • V. cholerae causes cholera via contaminated water.
  • S. typhi is diagnosed using the Widal test.
  • C. tetani produces tetanospasmin causing spastic paralysis.
  • Diphtheria involves a characteristic throat pseudomembrane.

Concept-specific exam traps

  • Confusing Widal test (typhoid) with Wassermann test (syphilis).
  • Assuming all bacterial infections present identical symptoms.

Viral Pathogens and Systemic Infections

Definition and scope

  • Obligate intracellular infectious agents requiring host cell machinery.
  • Consist of nucleic acid core enclosed in a protein capsid.
  • Unaffected by standard antibiotic treatments.

Must remember

  • Poliovirus targets spinal motor neurons causing flaccid paralysis.
  • HIV infects CD4+ T-helper cells utilizing reverse transcriptase.
  • Dengue is transmitted by Aedes aegypti mosquitoes.
  • Koplik spots are pathognomonic for measles.
  • Rabies causes fatal encephalomyelitis and hydrophobia.

Concept-specific exam traps

  • Believing mosquito vectors cause viral diseases directly without parasitic or viral incubation.
  • Expecting antibiotics to cure viral infections.
Concept 3

Fungal and Protozoan Parasitic Infections

Definition and scope

  • Infections caused by eukaryotic pathogens including molds, yeasts, and unicellular parasites.
  • Fungi possess chitinous cell walls and ergosterol membranes.
  • Protozoa often involve complex multi-host life cycles.

Must remember

  • Plasmodium species cause malaria, transmitted by Anopheles mosquitoes.
  • Entamoeba histolytica causes amoebic dysentery via cysts.
  • Ringworm is a superficial fungal infection caused by dermatophytes.
  • Kala-azar is caused by Leishmania donovani via sandfly bites.
  • Hemozoin pigment released during red blood cell rupture causes malaria chills.

Concept-specific exam traps

  • Classifying ringworm as a worm infection rather than a fungal dermatophytosis.
  • Confusing amoebic dysentery with bacterial dysentery transmission routes.
Concept 4

Principles of Immunology, Vaccines, and Passive/Active Immunity

Definition and scope

  • Immunization stimulates adaptive immunity to create immunological memory.
  • Active immunity involves host antigen encounter; passive involves preformed antibodies.
  • Vaccines use attenuated, killed, subunit, or toxoid antigens.

Must remember

  • Live-attenuated vaccines provide robust immunity but carry reversion risks.
  • Inactivated vaccines are safer but require booster doses.
  • Transplacental IgG and colostrum IgA provide natural passive immunity.
  • Adjuvants enhance immune response potency.
  • Herd immunity protects non-vaccinated vulnerable individuals.

Concept-specific exam traps

  • Assuming passive immunity creates long-term immunological memory.
  • Believing live vaccines cannot cause infection in immunocompromised hosts.
Concept 5

Antibiotics, Mechanisms of Action, and Antimicrobial Resistance

Definition and scope

  • Antimicrobial agents that inhibit or kill bacterial pathogens selectively.
  • Bactericidal drugs kill bacteria; bacteriostatic drugs inhibit multiplication.
  • AMR arises via mutations and horizontal gene transfer.

Must remember

  • Penicillins inhibit bacterial cell wall synthesis by binding PBPs.
  • Streptomycin and tetracyclines target bacterial ribosomes.
  • Alexander Fleming discovered penicillin from Penicillium notatum.
  • Plasmids facilitate horizontal transfer of resistance genes.
  • Beta-lactamase inhibitors protect antibiotics from enzymatic degradation.

Concept-specific exam traps

  • Stopping antibiotic courses prematurely leading to resistant bacterial selection.
  • Assuming bacteriostatic drugs actively lyse bacterial cell walls.

Quick facts

  • Mycobacterium tuberculosis is an acid-fast bacillus identified via Ziehl-Neelsen stain.
  • Plasmodium falciparum causes the most severe form of malaria.
  • Alexander Fleming discovered penicillin in 1928.
  • Koplik spots are characteristic diagnostic signs of measles.
  • Anti-snake venom provides artificial passive immunity.
  • Widal test is used for serodiagnosis of typhoid fever.
  • Aedes aegypti is the vector for Dengue, Chikungunya, and Zika viruses.

Glossary

PathogenA biological agent that causes disease or illness to its host.
AntigenA toxin or foreign substance that induces an immune response in the body.
AntibodyA blood protein produced in response to and counteracting a specific antigen.
VectorAn organism that transmits a disease parasite from one animal or human to another.
BactericidalAn agent that kills bacteria outright.
BacteriostaticAn agent that inhibits bacterial growth and reproduction.
AdjuvantA pharmacological agent added to a vaccine to modify and boost immune response.

Important distinctions

Active ImmunityvsPassive Immunity
  • Active immunity is produced by the host's own immune system after antigen exposure; passive immunity involves transfer of preformed antibodies.
  • Active immunity is long-lasting due to memory cells; passive immunity is temporary and short-lived.
Bactericidal AntibioticsvsBacteriostatic Antibiotics
  • Bactericidal drugs kill bacterial cells directly; bacteriostatic drugs inhibit growth and replication.
  • Bacteriostatic drugs rely on host immune mechanisms to completely clear the remaining inhibited bacteria.

Memory hooks

  • Bacterial infections like TB, Cholera, and Typhoid respond to Antibiotics; Viral infections like Polio, Rabies, and HIV do not.
  • Live vaccines give Long life; Killed vaccines Need boosters.
  • Malaria Mosquito Mechanism: Female Anopheles carries Plasmodium protozoa.

Exam traps and cautions

  • Confusing viral vector-borne diseases with bacterial waterborne diseases.
  • Assuming all fungi are harmless or only affect plants.
  • Believing that incomplete antibiotic courses have no consequence on resistance development.
  • Confusing natural active immunity with artificial passive immunization examples.
