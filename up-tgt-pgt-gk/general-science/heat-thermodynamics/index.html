Concept 1 · foundation

Temperature, Thermal Equilibrium and Zeroth Law

Temperature is the degree of hotness measured on a standardized scale, while thermal equilibrium forms the foundational basis of the Zeroth Law of Thermodynamics.

Prerequisites

  • Basic concept of matter and kinetic energy of molecules
  • Understanding of macroscopic physical properties

Definitions and scope

  • Temperature: A macroscopic property determining the direction of net heat flow between bodies in contact.
  • Thermal Equilibrium: A state where two bodies in thermal contact experience no net exchange of heat energy.
  • Zeroth Law of Thermodynamics: If system A is in thermal equilibrium with C, and B is separately in thermal equilibrium with C, then A and B are in thermal equilibrium with each other.

Step-by-step explanation

  • Molecules in any substance possess microscopic kinetic energy, which manifests macroscopically as temperature.
  • When two bodies of different temperatures are placed in contact, heat flows spontaneously from the higher temperature body to the lower temperature body.
  • Thermal equilibrium is achieved when both bodies attain the same temperature and net heat flow drops to zero.
  • The Zeroth Law of Thermodynamics defines the property of temperature and justifies the use of thermometers.
  • Thermometers rely on thermometric properties such as length of a mercury column, electrical resistance, or pressure of a constant-volume gas.
  • Celsius scale uses the ice point (0°C) and steam point (100°C) under standard atmospheric pressure.
  • Fahrenheit scale sets the ice point at 32°F and steam point at 212°F.
  • Kelvin scale is the absolute thermodynamic scale starting from absolute zero (0 K), where molecular motion ceases.

Key points

  • Temperature determines the direction of heat flow.
  • Zeroth law introduces the concept of temperature.
  • Conversion formula: (C/5) = (F-32)/9 = (K-273)/5.
  • Absolute zero is 0 K or -273.15°C.
  • At absolute zero, ideal gas volume theoretically shrinks to zero.
  • Thermometers measure their own temperature after reaching thermal equilibrium with the system.
Worked examples
  • Convert 35°C to Fahrenheit: F = (35 × 9/5) + 32 = 63 + 32 = 95°F.
  • Find the temperature where Celsius and Fahrenheit scales read the same: C = (C - 32) × 5/9 => 9C = 5C - 160 => 4C = -160 => C = -40°.

Applications and connections

  • Calibration of clinical and laboratory thermometers.
  • Environmental meteorological measurements.
  • Industrial temperature control processes.

Advanced points, exceptions and limitations

  • Absolute zero cannot be attained experimentally due to the Third Law of Thermodynamics.
  • Gas thermometers provide the most fundamental definition of the ideal gas temperature scale.
  • Quantum fluctuations prevent complete cessation of zero-point energy even at 0 K.

Additional examples

  • A mercury thermometer inserted into boiling water until the mercury level stabilizes.
  • Two cups of coffee left on a table eventually reaching room temperature.
  • Checking body fever using a clinical thermometer.
Important distinctions
  • Heat is energy in transit, whereas temperature is a state property of a system.
  • Heat depends on mass and material; temperature is an intensive property independent of mass.
  • Heat is measured in Joules or calories; temperature is measured in Kelvin, Celsius, or Fahrenheit.
Common misconceptions
  • Mistaking temperature for total heat content of a body.
  • Believing that 0°C represents the total absence of thermal energy.
  • Assuming two objects of different materials feel equally cold/hot at the same temperature due to different thermal conductivities.
Exam focus
  • Memorize the exact conversion formula between Celsius, Fahrenheit, and Kelvin scales.
  • Identify the Zeroth Law as the basis for temperature measurement.
  • Solve numericals on the temperature where Celsius equals Fahrenheit (-40°).
  • Understand thermometric properties like resistance and volume expansion.
  • Recognize absolute zero properties.
Concept 2 · core

Thermal Expansion in Solids, Liquids and Gases

Materials expand upon heating due to an increase in average interatomic separation caused by asymmetric potential energy wells.

Prerequisites

  • Atomic structure and interatomic forces
  • Basic linear and volumetric geometry

Definitions and scope

  • Linear Expansion: Fractional change in length per degree change in temperature, quantified by coefficient of linear expansion (α).
  • Superficial Expansion: Fractional change in surface area per degree change, quantified by coefficient of area expansion (β).
  • Cubical Expansion: Fractional change in volume per degree change, quantified by coefficient of volume expansion (γ).

Step-by-step explanation

  • When solids are heated, atoms vibrate with greater amplitude, increasing the average distance between atoms.
  • Linear expansion formula: ΔL = L₀ α ΔT.
  • Area expansion formula: ΔA = A₀ β ΔT, where β ≈ 2α for isotropic solids.
  • Volume expansion formula: ΔV = V₀ γ ΔT, where γ ≈ 3α for isotropic solids.
  • Liquids do not have a fixed shape; hence they only exhibit volume expansion.
  • Apparent expansion of a liquid is observed in a container, whereas real expansion includes the expansion of the container itself (γ_real = γ_apparent + γ_container).
  • Anomalous expansion of water: Water contracts when heated from 0°C to 4°C, reaching maximum density at 4°C, then expands above 4°C.
  • Thermal stress is induced in constrained structures when temperature changes, calculated as Stress = Y × α × ΔT.

Key points

  • Relations between coefficients: α : β : γ = 1 : 2 : 3.
  • Water shows anomalous behavior between 0°C and 4°C.
  • Gases have high coefficients of volume expansion compared to solids and liquids.
  • Gaps are left in railway tracks and bridges to accommodate expansion.
  • Pendulum clocks lose time in summer due to elongation of the rod.
  • Real expansion of liquid = Apparent expansion + Expansion of vessel.
Worked examples
  • A metal rod of length 2 m at 0°C is heated to 100°C (α = 1.2 × 10^-5 /°C). Find change in length: ΔL = 2 × 1.2 × 10^-5 × 100 = 2.4 × 10^-3 m.
  • A glass flask of volume 1000 cc is filled with mercury. Calculate apparent expansion if γ_Hg = 1.8 × 10^-4 /°C and γ_glass = 2.7 × 10^-5 /°C for a 50°C rise.

Applications and connections

  • Fitting metal rims on wooden cart wheels by thermal contraction.
  • Bimetallic strips used in thermostats and automatic fire alarms.
  • Concrete road expansion joints.

Advanced points, exceptions and limitations

  • Anisotropic crystals exhibit different coefficients of linear expansion along different crystallographic axes.
  • Negative thermal expansion is observed in certain materials like zirconium tungstate over specific temperature ranges.
  • Thermal stress calculations require integration if Young's modulus varies significantly with temperature.

Additional examples

  • Sagging of overhead electric transmission lines during summer.
  • Cracking of thick glass tumblers when boiling water is poured inside.
  • Aquatic life surviving under frozen lake surfaces due to water's maximum density at 4°C.
Important distinctions
  • Solids show linear, area, and volume expansion; liquids and gases show only volume expansion.
  • Apparent expansion of liquid depends on the material of the container, whereas real expansion is an intrinsic property of the liquid.
  • Coefficients of expansion for gases are much larger than those for solids and liquids.
Common misconceptions
  • Assuming holes in metal plates shrink upon heating (they expand just like solid material).
  • Believing water expands uniformly upon cooling to 0°C.
  • Confusing apparent expansion of liquid with true expansion without accounting for vessel expansion.
Exam focus
  • Master the ratio α : β : γ = 1 : 2 : 3.
  • Explain the ecological significance of anomalous expansion of water.
  • Calculate thermal stress formulas in fixed metal rods.
  • Distinguish between real and apparent expansion of liquids.
  • Solve numericals on pendulum clock time loss/gain.
Concept 3 · core

Specific Heat Capacity, Heat Capacity and Latent Heat

Specific heat capacity determines how much thermal energy is required to change a substance's temperature, while latent heat governs phase changes without temperature change.

Prerequisites

  • Conservation of energy
  • Units of heat (Joules and calories)

Definitions and scope

  • Heat Capacity (C): Amount of heat required to raise the temperature of an entire body by 1°C (C = Q / ΔT).
  • Specific Heat Capacity (s): Amount of heat required to raise the temperature of unit mass (1 kg) of a substance by 1°C (s = Q / (m × ΔT)).
  • Molar Specific Heat (C_m): Heat required to raise the temperature of 1 mole of a substance by 1°C.
  • Latent Heat (L): Heat absorbed or released per unit mass during a phase transition at constant temperature (Q = m × L).

Step-by-step explanation

  • The SI unit of specific heat capacity is J kg^-1 K^-1, and the CGS unit is cal g^-1 °C^-1.
  • Water has an unusually high specific heat capacity (~4184 J kg^-1 K^-1), making it an excellent coolant and moderating global climates.
  • Principle of calorimetry: Heat lost by hot bodies equals heat gained by cold bodies in an isolated system (m₁s₁(T₁ - T) = m₂s₂(T - T₂)).
  • Latent heat of fusion (L_f) is the heat required to convert solid to liquid at melting point.
  • Latent heat of vaporization (L_v) is the heat required to convert liquid to gas at boiling point.
  • Steam causes more severe burns than boiling water at 100°C due to its high latent heat of vaporization.
  • Molar heat capacity for gases is divided into C_p (at constant pressure) and C_v (at constant volume), related by Mayer's relation (C_p - C_v = R).
  • Phase diagrams represent the boundaries between solid, liquid, and gas phases as functions of pressure and temperature.

Key points

  • Water has the highest specific heat among common liquids.
  • During phase change, temperature remains constant.
  • Latent heat of vaporization is generally much greater than latent heat of fusion.
  • Calorimetry is based on the law of conservation of energy.
  • Mayer's relation applies to ideal gases: C_p - C_v = R.
  • High specific heat of water moderates coastal climates.
Worked examples
  • Calculate heat needed to raise 2 kg of water from 20°C to 70°C (s = 4200 J/kg·K): Q = m s ΔT = 2 × 4200 × (70 - 20) = 4,20,000 J.
  • Find final temperature when 100 g of water at 80°C is mixed with 200 g of water at 20°C: 100×1×(80-T) = 200×1×(T-20) => 80-T = 2(T-20) => 3T = 120 => T = 40°C.

Applications and connections

  • Water used in car radiators as a coolant.
  • Hot water bags used for fomentation in winters.
  • Meteorological climate moderation near oceans.

Advanced points, exceptions and limitations

  • Dulong-Petit law states that the molar heat capacity of solid elements at high temperatures is approximately 3R.
  • Quantum modifications (Einstein and Debye models) explain the decrease in specific heat of solids at very low temperatures.
  • Critical point marks the termination of the liquid-gas coexistence curve.

Additional examples

  • Melting of ice cubes in a glass of juice without changing temperature.
  • Boiling water turning into steam at 100°C.
  • Land and sea breezes driven by differing specific heat capacities of land and water.
Important distinctions
  • Heat capacity is for an entire body, whereas specific heat capacity is per unit mass.
  • Latent heat involves phase change without temperature change, whereas specific heat involves temperature change without phase change.
  • Specific heat applies to a single phase, while latent heat is absorbed/released during phase boundaries.
Common misconceptions
  • Assuming temperature rises when heat is added during a phase change.
  • Confusing heat capacity with specific heat capacity.
  • Believing boiling water is hotter than steam because steam originates from water.
Exam focus
  • Solve numericals based on the principle of mixtures in calorimetry.
  • Explain why steam causes worse burns than boiling water using latent heat.
  • State and apply Mayer's relation (C_p - C_v = R).
  • Recall units and dimensional formulas of specific heat and latent heat.
  • Analyze cooling/heating curves showing phase transitions.
Concept 4 · advanced

Modes of Heat Transfer: Conduction, Convection and Radiation

Heat transfers through three distinct mechanisms: conduction via lattice vibrations and free electrons, convection via bulk fluid motion, and radiation via electromagnetic waves.

Prerequisites

  • Wave nature of light and electromagnetic spectrum
  • Fluid dynamics and density variations
  • Atomic lattice structures

Definitions and scope

  • Conduction: Transfer of heat through stationary matter without bulk motion of the medium.
  • Convection: Transfer of heat through the bulk movement of fluids (liquids and gases).
  • Radiation: Transfer of heat energy via electromagnetic waves requiring no material medium.

Step-by-step explanation

  • Fourier's Law of Conduction: Rate of heat flow (dQ/dt) = -kA (dT/dx), where k is thermal conductivity.
  • Thermal resistance of a slab R_th = L / (kA), analogous to electrical resistance.
  • In metals, free electrons are primarily responsible for high thermal and electrical conductivity (Wiedemann-Franz law).
  • Convection occurs due to density differences; heated fluid expands, becomes less buoyant, rises, and is replaced by cooler fluid.
  • Natural convection is driven by buoyancy forces, whereas forced convection uses pumps or fans.
  • Radiation involves emission of infrared and thermal electromagnetic waves by all bodies above absolute zero (T > 0 K).
  • Stefan-Boltzmann Law: Total emissive power E = σT^4, where σ is Stefan's constant.
  • Wien's Displacement Law: λ_max T = b (constant), explaining why hotter objects emit radiation of shorter wavelengths (e.g., glowing white vs. red hot).
  • Newton's Law of Cooling: Rate of cooling of a body is proportional to the temperature difference between the body and its surroundings for small temperature differences.

Key points

  • Conduction requires a material medium and occurs predominantly in solids.
  • Convection requires fluid flow and cannot occur in a vacuum or solid.
  • Radiation can travel through a vacuum at the speed of light.
  • Silver is the best thermal conductor among metals.
  • Wool and feathers trap air pockets to act as good thermal insulators.
  • Black bodies are ideal absorbers and emitters of radiation.
Worked examples
  • Calculate heat conducted through a glass window (k = 0.8 W/m·K) of area 2 m² and thickness 4 mm when inside is 20°C and outside is 0°C: Q/t = 0.8 × 2 × 20 / 0.004 = 8000 W.
  • Verify Wien's law: If an object emits peak radiation at λ_max = 2900 nm, calculate its temperature (b ≈ 2.9 × 10^-3 m·K): T = (2.9 × 10^-3) / (2.9 × 10^-6) = 1000 K.

Applications and connections

  • Double-glazed windows for thermal insulation in buildings.
  • Design of thermos flasks (vacuum wall + silvered surfaces) to prevent all three modes of heat transfer.
  • Land and sea breeze formation along coastal areas.

Advanced points, exceptions and limitations

  • Greenhouse effect operates by transmitting incoming shortwave solar radiation and trapping outgoing longwave terrestrial radiation.
  • Kirchhoff's Law of Radiation states that good absorbers are good emitters at a given temperature.
  • Transient heat conduction involves time-dependent temperature distributions governed by the heat equation.

Additional examples

  • A metal spoon getting hot when dipped in a cup of hot tea (conduction).
  • Boiling water circulating in a pan (convection).
  • Feeling the warmth of the sun on your face through empty space (radiation).
Important distinctions
  • Conduction needs physical contact and stationary medium; convection requires fluid flow; radiation needs no medium at all.
  • Conduction rate depends on cross-section and temperature gradient; radiation rate depends on surface area and T^4.
  • Convection is directional (upward due to buoyancy), whereas radiation radiates in all directions.
Common misconceptions
  • Believing warm air sinks because heat rises (heated air rises due to buoyancy, not because heat inherently moves upward).
  • Assuming radiation requires air to travel.
  • Thinking wool generates heat instead of acting as an insulator preventing heat loss.
Exam focus
  • State and apply Fourier's law of thermal conduction.
  • Memorize Stefan-Boltzmann law (E = σT^4) and Wien's displacement law.
  • Explain the construction and working of a thermos flask addressing all three heat transfer modes.
  • Derive or apply Newton's Law of Cooling for temperature-time graphs.
  • Distinguish clearly between natural and forced convection.
Concept 5 · exam-synthesis

First and Second Laws of Thermodynamics and Thermodynamic Processes

Thermodynamics relates heat and work to macroscopic properties of systems, governed by conservation of energy and directional constraints.

Prerequisites

  • Work done by a gas (W = ∫P dV)
  • Internal energy as a state function
  • PV diagram interpretation

Definitions and scope

  • First Law of Thermodynamics: Conservation of energy applied to thermal systems (ΔQ = ΔU + ΔW).
  • Isothermal Process: Constant temperature process (ΔT = 0, ΔU = 0).
  • Adiabatic Process: No heat exchange with surroundings (ΔQ = 0).
  • Isobaric Process: Constant pressure process.
  • Isochoric Process: Constant volume process (ΔV = 0, ΔW = 0).
  • Second Law of Thermodynamics: Heat cannot spontaneously flow from a colder body to a warmer body without external work (Clausius statement), and no cyclic engine can convert 100% of absorbed heat into work (Kelvin-Planck statement).

Step-by-step explanation

  • Internal energy (U) is a state function depending only on the state of the system, not its path.
  • Heat (ΔQ) and work (ΔW) are path functions depending on the thermodynamic process used.
  • In an isothermal expansion, heat supplied equals work done by the gas (Q = W).
  • In an adiabatic process governed by PV^γ = constant, expansion causes cooling of the gas.
  • In an isochoric process, all added heat increases internal energy (ΔQ = ΔU).
  • Carnot Engine is an idealized reversible heat engine operating between two temperatures, setting the upper limit for efficiency (η = 1 - (T_2 / T_1)).
  • Entropy (S) is a measure of molecular disorder, which always increases in irreversible natural processes.
  • Third Law of Thermodynamics states that the entropy of a pure crystalline substance approaches zero as temperature approaches absolute zero.

Key points

  • First law is based on the conservation of energy.
  • Adiabatic curves are steeper than isothermal curves on a PV diagram (slope_adiabatic = γ × slope_isothermal).
  • Carnot efficiency depends only on source and sink temperatures.
  • Second law introduces the concept of entropy and irreversibility.
  • Perpetual motion machine of the second kind (PMM-2) violates the second law.
  • Internal energy of an ideal gas depends solely on its temperature.
Worked examples
  • A gas absorbs 500 J of heat and does 200 J of work. Find change in internal energy: ΔU = ΔQ - ΔW = 500 - 200 = 300 J.
  • Find the efficiency of a Carnot engine operating between 500 K and 300 K: η = 1 - (300/500) = 1 - 0.6 = 0.4 or 40%.

Applications and connections

  • Design of internal combustion engines (Otto and Diesel cycles).
  • Refrigerators and heat pumps operating on reversed Carnot cycles.
  • Power plants generating electricity from steam turbines.

Advanced points, exceptions and limitations

  • Maxwell's thermodynamic relations connect partial derivatives of thermodynamic potentials.
  • Joule-Thomson effect describes temperature change of a real gas when throttled through a porous plug.
  • Statistical mechanics interprets entropy as the logarithm of the number of microstates.

Additional examples

  • Rapid deflation of a bicycle tire resulting in a cold valve (adiabatic expansion).
  • Heating a sealed pressure cooker where volume remains constant (isochoric process).
  • Melting of ice in a warm room as an irreversible spontaneous process increasing entropy.
Important distinctions
  • Isothermal processes occur very slowly maintaining thermal equilibrium; adiabatic processes occur very rapidly without heat exchange.
  • Internal energy change is zero in isothermal processes; heat exchange is zero in adiabatic processes.
  • First law deals with energy conservation and energy transformation; second law dictates the direction of spontaneous processes.
Common misconceptions
  • Assuming a 100% efficient heat engine is possible if friction is completely eliminated.
  • Believing heat and work are state functions.
  • Confusing temperature with internal energy for real non-ideal gases.
Exam focus
  • Memorize the sign conventions for heat (ΔQ), work (ΔW), and internal energy (ΔU).
  • Calculate Carnot engine efficiency and coefficient of performance (COP) for refrigerators.
  • Compare slopes of isothermal and adiabatic curves on PV diagrams.
  • State Clausius and Kelvin-Planck statements of the Second Law.
  • Understand entropy as a measure of disorder and irreversibility.

Concept 1

Temperature, Thermal Equilibrium and Zeroth Law

Definition and scope

  • Temperature measures macroscopic hotness and dictates net heat flow direction.
  • Thermal equilibrium occurs when two bodies reach identical temperature with zero net heat transfer.
  • Zeroth Law establishes temperature as a valid property and underpins thermometry.

Must remember

  • Conversion: (C/5) = (F-32)/9 = (K-273)/5.
  • Celsius equals Fahrenheit at -40°.
  • Absolute zero is 0 K (-273.15°C).

  • Thermometric properties change predictably with temperature.

  • Assuming absolute zero is attainable.

  • Specific heat capacity is the heat required to raise unit mass by 1 K.
  • Latent heat governs phase changes at constant temperature.
  • Calorimetry applies the conservation of heat energy in isolated systems.

Must remember

  • Water has the highest specific heat among common liquids (~4184 J/kg·K).
  • During phase change, temperature remains constant.
  • Steam causes worse burns than boiling water due to high latent heat of vaporization.
  • Mayer's relation: C_p - C_v = R.
  • Principle of mixtures: Heat lost = Heat gained.

Concept-specific exam traps

  • Applying specific heat formula (ΔQ = m s ΔT) during phase change where temperature is constant.
  • Mixing units of calories and joules in calorimetry numericals.
Concept 4

Modes of Heat Transfer: Conduction, Convection and Radiation

Definition and scope

  • Conduction transfers heat through stationary matter via lattice vibrations and free electrons.
  • Convection involves bulk fluid motion driven by density and buoyancy gradients.
  • Radiation transmits energy via electromagnetic waves through vacuum.

Must remember

  • Fourier's law governs conduction: Q/t = -kA(dT/dx).
  • Stefan-Boltzmann law: E = σT^4.
  • Wien's displacement law: λ_max T = b.
  • Silver is the best thermal conductor.
  • Radiation requires no material medium.

Concept-specific exam traps

  • Thinking radiation requires air to propagate.
  • Confusing forced convection with conduction.
Concept 5

First and Second Laws of Thermodynamics and Thermodynamic Processes

Definition and scope

  • First Law expresses energy conservation: ΔQ = ΔU + ΔW.
  • Second Law limits thermal efficiency and dictates natural process direction via entropy increase.
  • Carnot engine establishes maximum possible theoretical efficiency between two temperatures.

Must remember

  • Carnot efficiency: η = 1 - (T_2 / T_1).
  • Adiabatic equation: PV^γ = constant.
  • Adiabatic curve is steeper than isothermal curve by factor γ.
  • Internal energy of an ideal gas is a function of temperature alone.
  • Entropy increases in all natural irreversible processes.

Concept-specific exam traps

  • Getting sign conventions wrong for work done by/on a gas.
  • Assuming 100% efficiency is achievable in real engines.

Quick facts

  • Water has its maximum density at 4°C.
  • The value of absolute zero is 0 K or -273.15°C.
  • At -40°, Celsius and Fahrenheit scales show identical readings.
  • The ratio of coefficients of linear, area, and volume expansion is 1 : 2 : 3.
  • Stefan-Boltzmann constant σ ≈ 5.67 × 10^-8 W/m²K⁴.
  • Mayer's relation for ideal gases is C_p - C_v = R.

Glossary

Adiabatic ProcessA thermodynamic process in which no heat enters or leaves the system.
CalorimetryMeasurement of heat transfer and specific heats of substances.
ConductionHeat transfer through stationary matter without bulk motion.
ConvectionHeat transfer via bulk movement of fluids.
EntropyA thermodynamic measure of molecular disorder and unavailability of thermal energy for work.
Isothermal ProcessA thermodynamic process occurring at constant temperature.
Latent HeatHeat absorbed or released during a phase change at constant temperature.
Specific HeatHeat required to raise the temperature of unit mass by 1 degree.

Important distinctions

ConductionvsConvection
  • Conduction occurs in stationary matter (mostly solids), while convection requires bulk fluid movement.
  • Conduction relies on atomic collisions and free electrons, whereas convection relies on buoyancy and density differences.
Isothermal ProcessvsAdiabatic Process
  • Isothermal processes happen at constant temperature (ΔT = 0), while adiabatic processes involve no heat exchange (ΔQ = 0).
  • PV curves for adiabatic expansions are steeper than those for isothermal expansions.
HeatvsTemperature
  • Heat is total kinetic energy in transit measured in Joules, while temperature is average kinetic energy measured in Kelvin.
  • Heat depends on mass, whereas temperature is an intensive property independent of mass.

Memory hooks

  • ACOR for heat transfer modes: Adiabatic, Conduction, Convection, Radiation (remember Conduction, Convection, Radiation).
  • ALPHA ratio: Linear (1), Area (2), Volume (3) -> 1:2:3.
  • CARNOT efficiency formula: 1 minus (Sink over Source) -> 1 - (T_2 / T_1).

Exam traps and cautions

  • Forgetting that water's anomalous expansion causes ice to float and lakes to freeze from top to bottom.
  • Confusing molar specific heat capacity with gram specific heat capacity.
  • Misidentifying path functions (Heat and Work) as state functions (Internal Energy and Temperature).
  • Failing to convert Celsius to Kelvin when applying gas laws or thermal radiation formulas.
