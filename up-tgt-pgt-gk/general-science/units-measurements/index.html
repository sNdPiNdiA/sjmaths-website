Concept 1 · foundation

SI Units and Derived Units

Fundamental quantities form the basis of the International System of Units (SI), from which all derived units are constructed.

Prerequisites

  • Basic understanding of physical quantities
  • Distinction between scalar and vector quantities
  • Concept of measurement standards

Definitions and scope

  • Physical Quantity: A property of a material or system that can be quantified by measurement.
  • Fundamental Quantities: Independent physical quantities that cannot be defined in terms of other quantities.
  • Derived Quantities: Quantities that are expressed as a combination of fundamental quantities.
  • SI System: The modern form of the metric system globally agreed upon, consisting of 7 base units and 2 supplementary units.

Step-by-step explanation

  • The seven base SI units are Metre (m), Kilogram (kg), Second (s), Ampere (A), Kelvin (K), Mole (mol), and Candela (cd).
  • The two supplementary SI units are Radian (rad) for plane angle and Steradian (sr) for solid angle.
  • Derived units are obtained by multiplying or dividing fundamental units without numerical factors.
  • Force is measured in Newtons, which is derived as kg·m/s².
  • Work and energy are measured in Joules, equivalent to kg·m²/s².
  • Power is measured in Watts, equivalent to kg·m²/s³ or J/s.
  • Pressure is measured in Pascals, equivalent to N/m² or kg/(m·s²).
  • Every derived unit can be traced back to its dimensional representation in terms of M, L, T, A, K, mol, and cd.

Key points

  • Seven fundamental SI units are universally recognized.
  • Supplementary units are dimensionless quantities with units.
  • Newton is the derived unit of force.
  • Joule is the derived unit of work and energy.
  • Pascal is the derived unit of pressure.
  • All physical equations must be dimensionally homogeneous.
Worked examples
  • Derive the SI unit of gravitational constant (G) from Newton's law of gravitation: F = G(m1*m2)/r^2. G = F*r^2 / (m1*m2). Units: (N * m^2) / kg^2 = (kg * m / s^2 * m^2) / kg^2 = m^3 / (kg * s^2).
  • Express the unit of electric resistance (ohm) in base SI units using Ohm's Law V = IR. R = V/I. Since V = W/Q (Joules/Coulomb), R = (kg*m^2/s^2) / (A*s * A) = kg*m^2/(A^2*s^3).

Applications and connections

  • Standardizing international trade and scientific research.
  • Ensuring dimensional correctness in engineering blueprints and architectural calculations.
  • Calibrating laboratory instruments across global research facilities.

Advanced points, exceptions and limitations

  • In 2019, the SI base units were redefined in terms of fundamental physical constants (e.g., Planck's constant, speed of light, elementary charge) rather than physical artifacts.
  • Dimensionless combinations of physical constants (like the fine-structure constant) play a crucial role in theoretical physics.
  • Some specialized fields use CGS or FPS systems, requiring precise conversion factors to SI units.

Additional examples

  • Speed: m/s
  • Frequency: Hz (s^-1)
  • Magnetic Field: Tesla (kg/(A*s^2))
Important distinctions
  • Fundamental units are independent; derived units are dependent.
  • Base quantities cannot be resolved further; derived quantities are products or quotients of base quantities.
  • Base units have standalone standards; derived units inherit standards from base units.
Common misconceptions
  • Confusing supplementary units as fundamental quantities.
  • Believing that units like Liter or Atmosphere are strictly SI base units.
  • Assuming dimensions have units attached to them.
Exam focus
  • Memorize the 7 base units and their symbols.
  • Be ready to derive base units for complex quantities like magnetic flux or viscosity.
  • Understand the 2019 SI redefinitions based on universal constants.
  • Know the exact dimensions of pressure, energy, and torque.
Concept 2 · core

Dimensions and Dimensional Analysis

Dimensional analysis uses the fact that physical laws are independent of the choice of units, enabling equation verification and formula derivation.

Prerequisites

  • Knowledge of fundamental physical quantities
  • Basic algebra and exponent laws
  • Understanding of proportionality

Definitions and scope

  • Dimensions: The powers to which fundamental quantities are raised to express a physical quantity.
  • Dimensional Formula: An expression showing how and which of the base quantities represent the dimensions of a physical quantity, denoted as [M^a L^b T^c].
  • Principle of Homogeneity: The dimensions of all terms on the left side of an equation must equal the dimensions of all terms on the right side.
  • Dimensionless Quantities: Quantities that have no dimensions, such as strain, angle, and refractive index.

Step-by-step explanation

  • Mass, Length, Time, Electric Current, Thermodynamic Temperature, Luminous Intensity, and Amount of Substance are represented dimensionally by [M], [L], [T], [I], [K], [cd], and [मोल] or [mol] respectively.
  • Velocity has a dimensional formula of [M^0 L^1 T^-1].
  • Acceleration has a dimensional formula of [M^0 L^1 T^-2].
  • Force has a dimensional formula of [M^1 L^1 T^-2].
  • Work, Energy, and Torque share the exact same dimensional formula: [M^1 L^2 T^-2].
  • Pressure, Stress, and Young's Modulus share the dimensional formula: [M^1 L^-1 T^-2].
  • Dimensional analysis can check the correctness of any physical equation based on the principle of homogeneity.
  • It can derive relationships between physical quantities when the influencing factors are known using the method of dimensions.

Key points

  • Torque and Work have identical dimensions despite different physical meanings.
  • Pressure and Stress have identical dimensions.
  • Angles and trigonometric functions are dimensionless.
  • Only quantities with identical dimensions can be added or subtracted.
  • Addition or subtraction does not alter the dimensional formula of a term.
  • Dimensional analysis cannot determine dimensionless constants in an equation.
Worked examples
  • Check the correctness of the equation s = ut + (1/2)at^2 using dimensions. LHS: [s] = [L]. RHS: [ut] = [L/T]*[T] = [L]; [(1/2)at^2] = [L/T^2]*[T^2] = [L]. Since [L] = [L] + [L], the equation is dimensionally correct.
  • Derive the formula for the time period (T) of a simple pendulum, assuming it depends on mass (m), length (l), and acceleration due to gravity (g). T = k * m^x * l^y * g^z. [T^1] = [M]^x * [L]^y * ([L][T]^-2)^z = M^x L^(y+z) T^(-2z). Equating powers: x=0, y+z=0, -2z=1 -> z = -1/2, y = 1/2. Thus T = k * sqrt(l/g).

Applications and connections

  • Checking the validity of newly derived physics equations.
  • Converting units from one system (e.g., CGS) to another (SI).
  • Deriving empirical relations in fluid dynamics and aerodynamics using Buckingham Pi theorem.

Advanced points, exceptions and limitations

  • Equations containing exponential, logarithmic, or trigonometric terms cannot be fully derived by dimensional analysis alone because arguments of such functions must be dimensionless.
  • Quantities with the same dimensions may not represent the same physical property (e.g., torque and energy).
  • Dimensional analysis fails when a physical quantity depends on more than three fundamental mechanical dimensions if only M, L, T are used.

Additional examples

  • Frequency: [T^-1]
  • Surface Tension: [M^1 L^0 T^-2]
  • Universal Gravitational Constant: [M^-1 L^3 T^-2]
Important distinctions
  • Work is scalar; torque is vector, yet both have dimensions [M^1 L^2 T^-2].
  • Speed and velocity differ in vector nature, but share the same dimensions [L T^-1].
  • Pressure and energy density share the same dimensions [M^1 L^-1 T^-2].
Common misconceptions
  • Believing that dimensionally correct equations are always physically correct (ignoring dimensionless constants like 1/2 or 2*pi).
  • Adding quantities with different dimensions.
  • Assuming dimensionless quantities cannot have units (e.g., radian).
Exam focus
  • Memorize dimensional formulas for Planck's constant, universal gravitational constant, and coefficient of viscosity.
  • Practice checking dimensional homogeneity of complex thermodynamic or wave equations.
  • Identify pairs of physical quantities that share identical dimensions.
  • Know the limitations of dimensional analysis.
Concept 3 · application

Vernier Calipers: Principle, Least Count and Errors

Vernier calipers provide precision linear measurements down to fractions of a millimeter using the difference between main scale and vernier scale divisions.

Prerequisites

  • Basic metric measurement units (mm and cm)
  • Concept of fractional scale divisions
  • Understanding of precision and accuracy

Definitions and scope

  • Main Scale Division (MSD): The smallest value measured accurately by the main scale, typically 1 mm.
  • Vernier Scale Division (VSD): Divisions marked on the sliding vernier scale.
  • Least Count (LC): The smallest measurement that can be read accurately with the instrument.
  • Zero Error: The discrepancy where the zero of the vernier scale does not coincide with the zero of the main scale when jaws are closed.

Step-by-step explanation

  • The working principle of Vernier calipers relies on the difference between the lengths of a Main Scale Division (MSD) and a Vernier Scale Division (VSD).
  • Generally, n divisions of the vernier scale (n VSD) are made equal in length to (n - 1) divisions of the main scale ((n - 1) MSD).
  • Least Count formula: LC = 1 MSD - 1 VSD.
  • For standard calipers where 10 VSD = 9 MSD, LC = 1 mm - (9/10) mm = 1/10 mm = 0.1 mm = 0.01 cm.
  • Total Reading = Main Scale Reading (MSR) + (Vernier Coincidence (VC) * Least Count).
  • Positive Zero Error occurs when the vernier zero lies to the right of the main scale zero with jaws closed; it must be subtracted.
  • Negative Zero Error occurs when the vernier zero lies to the left of the main scale zero with jaws closed; its magnitude must be added after proper calculation.
  • External jaws measure outer diameters, internal jaws measure inner diameters, and the depth probe measures depth.

Key points

  • Standard least count of a school laboratory Vernier caliper is 0.01 cm (0.1 mm).
  • LC = (Value of 1 MSD) / (Total number of divisions on Vernier scale).
  • Positive zero error is subtracted from the observed reading.
  • Negative zero error is added (as true reading = observed reading - zero error).
  • Vernier calipers eliminate parallax error through careful alignment.
  • Used for measuring cylinders, spheres, and hollow pipes.
Worked examples
  • Calculate the least count of a Vernier caliper where 20 VSD coincide with 19 MSD, and 1 MSD = 1 mm. LC = 1 MSD - (19/20) MSD = (1/20) MSD = 0.05 mm = 0.005 cm.
  • A student measures a rod: MSR = 2.3 cm, Vernier coincidence = 6, Least count = 0.01 cm. If there is a positive zero error of +0.02 cm, find the correct reading. Observed reading = 2.3 + (6 * 0.01) = 2.36 cm. Correct reading = 2.36 - (+0.02) = 2.34 cm.

Applications and connections

  • Measuring internal and external diameters of test tubes, beakers, and cylinders.
  • Determining the depth of small holes or hollow containers.
  • Manufacturing and mechanical workshop quality control inspections.

Advanced points, exceptions and limitations

  • Digital Vernier calipers replace physical vernier scales with capacitive or optical encoders for direct digital readout.
  • Thermal expansion of the metal scale can introduce systematic errors in high-precision temperature environments.
  • Wear and tear on the measuring jaws can cause calibration drift over extended use.

Additional examples

  • Measuring sphere diameter = 2.45 cm
  • Measuring beaker inner diameter = 5.12 cm
  • Measuring test tube depth = 12.40 cm
Important distinctions
  • Vernier calipers measure linear dimensions up to 0.01 cm; screw gauges measure up to 0.001 cm.
  • Vernier calipers use a sliding secondary scale; screw gauges use a circular rotating screw pitch.
  • Vernier calipers handle both internal/external spans and depths; standard screw gauges are limited to thickness and external diameters.
Common misconceptions
  • Forgetting to apply zero error correction.
  • Confusing positive and negative zero error signs.
  • Assuming 10 VSD always equals 9 MSD for every custom instrument.
Exam focus
  • Memorize the standard least count formula: LC = 1 MSD - 1 VSD.
  • Practice zero error numerical problems frequently asked in TGT/PGT.
  • Know the exact function of external jaws, internal jaws, and depth strips.
  • Calculate correct readings given MSR, VC, and zero error.
Concept 4 · application

Screw Gauge (Micrometer): Principle and Applications

A screw gauge utilizes the mechanical advantage of a screw thread to measure extremely small thicknesses and diameters with millesimal precision.

Prerequisites

  • Understanding of pitch and threads in screws
  • Decimal arithmetic and rounding off
  • Concept of least count and precision

Definitions and scope

  • Pitch: The linear distance moved by the screw per complete rotation of the circular cap, typically 1 mm.
  • Circular Scale Divisions (CSD): The number of divisions marked on the circular head, commonly 50 or 100.
  • Least Count (LC): Pitch divided by the total number of divisions on the circular scale.
  • Backlash Error: Error arising from wear in the screw threads resulting in lost motion when reversing direction.

Step-by-step explanation

  • The screw gauge works on the principle of the screw, where rotational motion is converted into linear motion.
  • Pitch = (Distance moved on main scale) / (Number of full rotations given).
  • Least Count = Pitch / (Total number of divisions on circular scale).
  • For a pitch of 1 mm and 100 circular divisions, LC = 1 mm / 100 = 0.01 mm = 0.001 cm.
  • Total Reading = Pitch Scale Reading (PSR) + (Circular Scale Reading (CSR) * Least Count).
  • Ratchet mechanism is provided to prevent excessive tightening and protect the screw threads from damage.
  • Zero error in a screw gauge is checked when the anvil and spindle are brought into gentle contact.
  • Positive zero error occurs when the zero of the circular scale lies below the reference line; it is subtracted.

Key points

  • Standard least count of a laboratory screw gauge is 0.001 cm (0.01 mm).
  • Pitch is usually 1 mm per rotation.
  • Ratchet ensures uniform pressure during measurement.
  • Negative zero error means the circular scale zero is above the reference line.
  • Used for measuring wire thickness and thin metal sheets.
  • Backlash error is avoided by always rotating the screw in the same direction while taking final readings.
Worked examples
  • Calculate the least count of a screw gauge with a pitch of 0.5 mm and 50 divisions on the circular scale. LC = 0.5 mm / 50 = 0.01 mm = 0.001 cm.
  • A micrometer screw gauge measures wire diameter: PSR = 2 mm, Circular scale division coinciding = 45, LC = 0.001 cm (0.01 mm). If zero error is -0.003 cm, find the true diameter. Observed reading = 0.2 cm + (45 * 0.001 cm) = 0.245 cm. True reading = Observed - (-0.003) = 0.248 cm.

Applications and connections

  • Measuring diameter of thin copper wires for electrical experiments.
  • Determining thickness of microscope glass cover slips and metal foils.
  • Precision machining quality checks in mechanical workshops.

Advanced points, exceptions and limitations

  • Micrometer screw gauges can be fitted with electronic digital displays using linear encoders.
  • Abbe's error can occur if the measuring axis is not aligned with the scale axis, though minimized in high-grade micrometers.
  • Temperature control is vital because a 1-degree temperature change can alter the reading of a steel micrometer significantly over large spans.

Additional examples

  • Measuring wire diameter = 0.035 cm
  • Measuring glass slide thickness = 0.124 cm
  • Measuring aluminum foil thickness = 0.018 cm
Important distinctions
  • Screw gauge provides higher precision (0.001 cm) than Vernier calipers (0.01 cm).
  • Screw gauge uses circular scale rotation; Vernier calipers use linear sliding.
  • Screw gauge is susceptible to backlash error; Vernier calipers do not suffer from backlash.
Common misconceptions
  • Confusing pitch with least count.
  • Over-tightening the spindle without using the ratchet, causing inaccurate readings and equipment damage.
  • Ignoring backlash error when reversing the screw direction.
Exam focus
  • Master the least count formula: LC = Pitch / Number of circular divisions.
  • Know how to identify and correct positive and negative zero errors.
  • Understand the purpose of the ratchet and how to avoid backlash error.
  • Solve numerical problems involving wire diameter and sheet thickness.
Concept 5 · advanced

Barometers: Fortin's and Aneroid Barometers

Barometers measure atmospheric pressure, acting as vital meteorological tools for weather prediction and altitude determination.

Prerequisites

  • Concept of atmospheric pressure and hydrostatic pressure
  • Pascal's law and Torricelli's experiment
  • Units of pressure (atm, mmHg, Pascal, bar)

Definitions and scope

  • Atmospheric Pressure: The hydrostatic pressure exerted by the weight of the air column of the Earth's atmosphere.
  • Torricelli's Barometer: A glass tube filled with mercury inverted in a mercury reservoir, where column height measures atmospheric pressure.
  • Fortin's Barometer: A precision mercury barometer equipped with an ivory pointer and adjustable leather bag for accurate level setting.
  • Aneroid Barometer: A portable barometer containing a sealed flexible metal capsule that expands or contracts with pressure changes without using liquid mercury.

Step-by-step explanation

  • Torricelli first demonstrated in 1643 that atmospheric pressure supports a mercury column of approximately 76 cm at sea level.
  • Standard atmospheric pressure = 1 atm = 1.013 * 10^5 Pa = 760 mmHg = 76 cm of Hg.
  • Fortin's Barometer features a vernier scale attached to a brass sleeve surrounding the glass tube, allowing readings down to 0.1 mm of Hg.
  • The mercury reservoir in Fortin's Barometer has a flexible leather bottom adjusted by a thumb screw so that the mercury level touches the ivory pointer (the 'fiducial point') before every reading.
  • An aneroid barometer uses an evacuated corrugated metal box (aneroid capsule) connected via levers and a spring to a pointer on a dial.
  • Changes in external air pressure deflect the capsule walls, and the mechanical linkage magnifies this movement to point to pressure values on the dial.
  • Barometers are extensively used in meteorology to predict weather: sudden drop in pressure indicates impending storms or cyclones, while slow rise indicates fair weather.
  • They are also used as altimeters in aviation because atmospheric pressure decreases predictably with altitude.

Key points

  • Standard sea-level pressure is 76 cm of Hg or 1.013 * 10^5 Pascals.
  • Fortin's Barometer uses mercury and an ivory pointer for high precision.
  • Aneroid barometer contains no liquid and is portable.
  • Sudden drop in barometric reading forecasts a storm.
  • Torricelli's experiment proved the existence of atmospheric pressure.
  • Altimeters in aircraft are calibrated aneroid barometers.
Worked examples
  • Convert a barometric reading of 750 mm of Hg into Pascals. Given density of mercury rho = 13600 kg/m^3, g = 9.8 m/s^2, h = 0.75 m. Pressure P = rho * g * h = 13600 * 9.8 * 0.75 = 99,960 Pa = 99.96 kPa.
  • If a barometer shows a continuous gradual fall in pressure over 12 hours, what meteorological conclusion is drawn? A gradual and steady fall indicates the approach of rain, moisture-laden winds, or low-pressure depression.

Applications and connections

  • Weather forecasting and meteorological data collection.
  • Aviation altimetry for measuring aircraft height above sea level.
  • Physics laboratories for standardizing gas laws and pressure experiments.

Advanced points, exceptions and limitations

  • Corrections must be applied to mercury barometer readings for temperature (due to thermal expansion of mercury and brass scale) and gravity (variation of 'g' with latitude and altitude).
  • The vacuum space above the mercury column in a barometer tube is called Torricelli's vacuum; the presence of air vapor in this space introduces errors.
  • Aneroid barometers require periodic recalibration against standard mercury barometers due to metal fatigue in the capsule.

Additional examples

  • Sea level pressure = 760 mmHg
  • High altitude mountain pressure = 500 mmHg
  • Storm approach indicator = Rapid pressure drop
Important distinctions
  • Fortin's barometer uses liquid mercury; aneroid barometer uses a mechanical vacuum capsule.
  • Fortin's barometer offers high laboratory precision; aneroid barometer offers portability and ease of field use.
  • Mercury barometers require vertical mounting and temperature corrections; aneroid barometers can be mounted in any orientation with built-in bimetallic compensation.
Common misconceptions
  • Believing that the space above mercury in a barometer tube contains air (it is Torricelli's vacuum).
  • Assuming aneroid barometers contain water or mercury.
  • Confusing pressure measurement with temperature measurement.
Exam focus
  • Know the value of standard atmospheric pressure in various units (mmHg, atm, Pascal, bar).
  • Understand the specific role of the ivory pointer and leather bag in Fortin's Barometer.
  • Recognize weather indications from barometric pressure changes (sudden drop vs. gradual rise).
  • Calculate hydrostatic pressure using P = hdg.

Rapid revision: Units, Dimensions & Measuring Instruments

Review every concept point-wise before attempting the quiz and timed test.

Concept 1

SI Units and Derived Units

Definition and scope

  • SI system consists of 7 base units and 2 supplementary units.
  • Derived units are formulated from products or quotients of base units.
  • Fundamental quantities are physically independent.

Must remember

  • Base units: Metre, Kilogram, Second, Ampere, Kelvin, Mole, Candela.
  • Supplementary units: Radian and Steradian.
  • Force unit: Newton (kg m/s²).
  • Energy unit: Joule (kg m²/s²).
  • Pressure unit: Pascal (N/m²).

Concept-specific exam traps

  • Treating supplementary units as fundamental or dimensionless without unit distinction.
  • Confusing joule and watt dimensions.
Concept 2

Dimensions and Dimensional Analysis

Definition and scope

  • Dimensional formula expresses physical quantities in terms of base dimensions [M, L, T, etc.].
  • Principle of homogeneity requires dimensionally identical terms in equations.
  • Dimensionless quantities carry no dimensional powers.

Must remember

  • Torque and Work share dimensions [M¹ L² T⁻²].
  • Pressure and Stress share dimensions [M¹ L⁻¹ T⁻²].
  • Angles and strain are dimensionless.
  • Dimensional analysis cannot find dimensionless constants.
  • Only identically dimensioned terms can be added.

Concept-specific exam traps

  • Assuming quantities with same dimensions must be physically identical.
  • Failing to check dimensionless arguments in exponential or trigonometric functions.
Concept 3

Vernier Calipers: Principle, Least Count and Errors

Definition and scope

  • Least count LC = 1 MSD - 1 VSD.
  • Total reading = MSR + (VC * LC).
  • Zero errors require additive or subtractive correction.

Must remember

  • Standard school LC = 0.01 cm.
  • Positive zero error is subtracted from observed reading.
  • Negative zero error is added to observed reading.
  • External jaws, internal jaws, and depth rod serve distinct measuring functions.

Concept-specific exam traps

  • Adding positive zero error instead of subtracting it.
  • Mixing up centimeter and millimeter least count values.
Concept 4

Screw Gauge (Micrometer): Principle and Applications

Definition and scope

  • Least count LC = Pitch / Total circular scale divisions.
  • Total reading = PSR + (CSR * LC).
  • Ratchet ensures uniform pressure and prevents thread damage.

Must remember

  • Standard school screw gauge LC = 0.001 cm (0.01 mm).
  • Pitch is typically 1 mm.
  • Backlash error is caused by worn threads; avoid by turning in one direction.
  • Used for measuring thin wires and sheet thicknesses.

Concept-specific exam traps

  • Confusing screw gauge LC (0.001 cm) with Vernier caliper LC (0.01 cm).
  • Ignoring zero error adjustments in numerical problems.
Concept 5

Barometers: Fortin's and Aneroid Barometers

Definition and scope

  • Barometers measure atmospheric pressure.
  • Fortin's barometer uses mercury with an adjustable leather reservoir and ivory pointer.
  • Aneroid barometer uses a flexible evacuated metal capsule.

Must remember

  • Standard atmospheric pressure = 76 cm of Hg = 1.013 * 10^5 Pa.
  • Torricelli's vacuum exists above the mercury column.
  • Sudden drop in barometric pressure forecasts a storm.
  • Aneroid barometers serve as altimeters in aviation.

Concept-specific exam traps

  • Believing the space above mercury in a barometer tube is filled with air.
  • Confusing Fortin's barometer liquid with water instead of mercury.

Quick facts

  • The SI system has 7 base units and 2 supplementary units.
  • Work, energy, and torque have the same dimensional formula: [M¹ L² T⁻²].
  • Pressure, stress, and modulus of elasticity share the dimensional formula: [M¹ L⁻¹ T⁻²].
  • Least count of a standard school Vernier caliper is 0.01 cm.
  • Least count of a standard school screw gauge is 0.001 cm.
  • Standard atmospheric pressure is 76 cm of Hg or 1.013 * 10^5 Pascals.
  • A sudden fall in barometric reading indicates an approaching storm.

Glossary

Least CountThe smallest measurement that can be read accurately with a given measuring instrument.
Dimensional HomogeneityThe principle that every term in a correct physical equation must have the same dimensional formula.
PitchThe linear distance advanced by a screw thread in one complete rotation.
Torricelli's VacuumThe empty space above the mercury column in a barometer tube.
Backlash ErrorApparent lost motion in a screw mechanism when reversing the direction of rotation due to thread wear.

Important distinctions

Vernier CalipersvsScrew Gauge
  • Vernier calipers have a least count of 0.01 cm; screw gauges have 0.001 cm.
  • Vernier calipers measure internal, external, and depth spans; screw gauges measure external thickness and diameter.
  • Vernier calipers use a sliding scale; screw gauges use a rotating screw pitch.
Fortin's BarometervsAneroid Barometer
  • Fortin's barometer uses liquid mercury; aneroid barometer uses a mechanical vacuum capsule.
  • Fortin's barometer requires vertical mounting and laboratory calibration; aneroid barometer is portable and field-ready.
  • Fortin's barometer offers higher precision; aneroid barometer offers greater convenience.

Memory hooks

  • Base Units mnemonic: 'M-K-S-A-K-M-C' (Metre, Kilogram, Second, Ampere, Kelvin, Mole, Candela).
  • Precision order: Vernier = 0.01 cm (two zeros after decimal), Screw Gauge = 0.001 cm (three zeros after decimal).
  • Barometer weather rule: 'Fall = Foul' (falling pressure means bad weather/storm).

Exam traps and cautions

  • Do not confuse the dimensions of Work ([M L² T⁻²]) with Power ([M L² T⁻³]).
  • Remember to subtract positive zero error and add negative zero error.
  • Do not assume a dimensionally correct formula is 100% physically correct, as dimensionless constants cannot be checked.
  • Always check the unit prefixes (mm vs cm) when calculating least counts.
