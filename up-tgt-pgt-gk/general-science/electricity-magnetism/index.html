Concept 1 · foundation

Ohm's Law, Resistance, and Resistivity

Ohm's law establishes the linear relationship between potential difference and electric current under constant physical conditions.

Prerequisites

  • Basic understanding of electric charge, electric current ($I$), and potential difference ($V$).
  • Concept of atomic structure, free electrons, and drift velocity.

Definitions and scope

  • Ohm's Law states that the electric current flowing through a metallic conductor is directly proportional to the potential difference across its ends, provided physical conditions like temperature remain constant ($V \propto I$).
  • Resistance ($R$) is the obstruction offered by a conductor to the flow of electric current, measured in Ohms ($\Omega$).
  • Resistivity ($\rho$) is the inherent property of a material to resist current flow, defined as resistance per unit length and unit cross-sectional area.

Step-by-step explanation

  • The mathematical expression for Ohm's law is $V = IR$, where $V$ is voltage, $I$ is current, and $R$ is resistance.
  • Resistance depends directly on the length ($l$) of the conductor and inversely on its cross-sectional area ($A$), expressed as $R = \rho \frac{l}{A}$.
  • The SI unit of resistivity is Ohm-meter ($\Omega\cdot\text{m}$). Conductivity ($\sigma$) is the reciprocal of resistivity, measured in Siemens per meter (S/m).
  • Ohm's law is an empirical law; it does not hold universally for all materials (e.g., diodes, transistors, and electrolytes are non-ohmic conductors).
  • Effect of temperature on resistance: For metals, resistance increases linearly with temperature according to $R_t = R_0(1 + \alpha \Delta T)$, where $\alpha$ is the temperature coefficient of resistance.
  • For semiconductors and insulators, resistance decreases with an increase in temperature due to an increase in charge carrier density.
  • Drift velocity ($v_d$) of electrons is related to current by $I = nAe v_d$, where $n$ is electron density and $e$ is electronic charge.
  • Microscopic form of Ohm's law relates current density ($J$), electric field ($E$), and conductivity ($\sigma$) as $\vec{J} = \sigma \vec{E}$.

Key points

  • Formula: $V = IR$ and $R = \rho \frac{l}{A}$.
  • Ohm's law applies only under constant physical conditions (especially temperature).
  • Resistivity depends only on material and temperature, not on dimensions.
  • Metals have positive temperature coefficient of resistance ($\alpha > 0$).
  • Non-ohmic devices do not obey $V$-$I$ linearity.
  • Microscopic Ohm's law: $\vec{J} = \sigma \vec{E}$.
Worked examples
  • Find the resistance of a copper wire of length 2 m and cross-sectional area $10^{-6}\text{ m}^2$. Given resistivity of copper $\rho = 1.7 \times 10^{-8} \Omega\cdot\text{m}$. Solution: $R = \rho \frac{l}{A} = \frac{1.7 \times 10^{-8} \times 2}{10^{-6}} = 3.4 \times 10^{-2} \Omega = 0.034 \Omega$.
  • Calculate the current flowing through a resistor of $20 \Omega$ connected across a 120 V DC source. Solution: Using $I = \frac{V}{R} = \frac{120}{20} = 6\text{ A}.

Applications and connections

  • Design of standard resistors and heating elements in appliances.
  • Voltage regulation and circuit analysis using Kirchhoff's laws.
  • Selection of wire gauges for safe electrical installations.

Advanced points, exceptions and limitations

  • Superconductors exhibit zero resistance below a critical temperature ($T_c$).
  • Mobility of charge carriers ($\mu = \frac{v_d}{E}$) determines semiconductor performance.
  • High-frequency AC currents suffer from skin effect, concentrating current near the conductor surface.

Additional examples

  • Nichrome wire used in room heaters due to high resistivity.
  • Copper cables used for power transmission due to low resistivity.
  • Carbon resistors used in electronic circuits with color-coding.
Important distinctions
  • Resistance depends on dimensions and material; resistivity depends strictly on material and temperature.
  • Ohmic conductors maintain constant $R$ across $V$; non-ohmic conductors show variable dynamic resistance ($dV/dI$).
  • Conductivity measures ease of current flow; resistivity measures opposition.
Common misconceptions
  • Believing resistivity changes when a wire is stretched (resistivity remains constant; resistance changes).
  • Assuming Ohm's law is a fundamental law of nature like Newton's laws.
  • Confusing resistance with resistivity.
Exam focus
  • Numerical problems involving stretching of wires ($R \propto l^2$ or $R \propto 1/A^2$).
  • Color coding of carbon resistors and tolerance values.
  • Temperature coefficient of resistance derivations and units.
  • Graph interpretation of $V$-$I$ characteristics for ohmic and non-ohmic devices.
  • Microscopic derivation of Ohm's law from relaxation time.
Concept 2 · core

Electric Power and Joule's Heating Effect

Electric power quantifies the rate of electrical energy transfer, while Joule's law governs the conversion of electrical energy into thermal energy.

Prerequisites

  • Understanding of work, energy, and power from mechanics.
  • Familiarity with potential difference and electric current.

Definitions and scope

  • Electric Power ($P$) is the rate at which electrical work is done or energy is consumed in an electrical circuit, measured in Watts (W).
  • Joule's Heating Effect states that the heat produced in a resistor is directly proportional to the square of current, resistance, and time ($H = I^2Rt$).

Step-by-step explanation

  • Power formulas: $P = VI = I^2R = \frac{V^2}{R}$.
  • Electrical energy consumed is $E = P \times t = VIt$.
  • Commercial unit of electrical energy is the Kilowatt-hour (kWh), commonly known as a 'unit' of electricity ($1\text{ kWh} = 3.6 \times 10^6\text{ Joules}$).
  • Joule's law of heating: $H = I^2Rt$ Joules, or in calories, $H = \frac{I^2Rt}{4.18}$ cal.
  • Power rating of appliances indicates energy consumed per second at rated voltage (e.g., a 100W bulb consumes 100 Joules per second).
  • Transmission losses in power lines: Power loss is given by $P_{\text{loss}} = I^2R$. To minimize loss, power is transmitted at very high voltage and low current.
  • Electric fuses work on the heating effect; when excessive current flows, the fuse wire melts and breaks the circuit.
  • Filament lamps operate at high temperatures where tungsten glows incandescently in an inert gas atmosphere.

Key points

  • Power formulas: $P = VI$, $P = I^2R$, $P = V^2/R$.
  • 1 Unit = 1 kWh = $3.6 \times 10^6\text{ J}$.
  • Heat generated: $H = I^2Rt$.
  • High voltage transmission reduces $I^2R$ line losses.
  • Fuse wire requires low melting point and high resistivity (typically lead-tin alloy).
  • Tungsten filament has high melting point (approx 3380°C).
Worked examples
  • An electric iron of resistance $50 \Omega$ draws a current of 4 A. Calculate the heat developed in 5 minutes. Solution: $I = 4\text{ A}$, $R = 50 \Omega$, $t = 300\text{ s}$. $H = I^2Rt = (4)^2 \times 50 \times 300 = 16 \times 50 \times 300 = 240,000\text{ Joules} = 240\text{ kJ}$.
  • Calculate the cost of operating five 100W bulbs for 10 hours daily in a month of 30 days, if electricity costs ₹5 per kWh. Solution: Total energy = $(5 \times 100\text{W} \times 10\text{h} \times 30) / 1000 = 150\text{ kWh}$. Cost = $150 \times 5 = \text{₹}750$.

Applications and connections

  • Electric heaters, geysers, toasters, and soldering irons utilizing heating coils.
  • Incandescent lighting and halogen lamps.
  • Circuit protection devices such as fuses and miniature circuit breakers (MCBs).

Advanced points, exceptions and limitations

  • Power transfer theorem: Maximum power is transferred from a source to a load when internal resistance equals load resistance ($r = R$).
  • Skin effect increases AC resistance in high-power transmission cables.
  • Eddy current heating (induction heating) used in industrial metallurgy.

Additional examples

  • Room heater rated 2000W, 230V.
  • Fuse wire melting at 5A rating during a short circuit.
  • Electric kettle boiling water rapidly via immersed heating element.
Important distinctions
  • Power is rate of energy transfer (Watts); energy is total work done over time (Joules or kWh).
  • Series grouping of bulbs shares voltage and reduces total power; parallel grouping maintains rated voltage across each bulb.
  • Fuse wire has low melting point; transmission wires have high conductivity and high melting point.
Common misconceptions
  • Thinking a 100W bulb is brighter than a 60W bulb when connected in series (in series, the 60W bulb has higher resistance and glows brighter).
  • Confusing power rating with actual power consumed when operated at non-rated voltage.
  • Believing electricity is consumed and destroyed rather than converted.
Exam focus
  • Domestic electricity bill calculation problems.
  • Series vs. parallel bulb brightness comparisons.
  • Maximum power transfer theorem condition ($R = r$).
  • Properties of fuse wire materials.
  • Derivation of Joule's heating law.
Concept 3 · application

Domestic Electric Circuits and Safety Devices

Domestic wiring systems utilize parallel distribution networks supplied with AC mains, protected by earthing, fuses, and MCBs.

Prerequisites

  • Understanding of AC supply voltage (220V, 50Hz in India).
  • Basic circuit concepts of series and parallel connections.

Definitions and scope

  • Domestic Circuit is the standard household wiring system delivering electricity from the mains supply to individual appliances in parallel.
  • Earthing (Grounding) is the safety connection of metal appliance bodies to the earth to prevent severe electric shocks.
  • MCB (Miniature Circuit Breaker) is an electromechanical safety device that automatically trips during overload or short circuit.

Step-by-step explanation

  • Household wiring is always connected in parallel so that every appliance receives the full rated voltage (220V) and can be operated independently.
  • Three color-coded wires are used in standard Indian domestic circuits: Live (Red/Brown, carries current), Neutral (Black/Blue, completes circuit), and Earth (Green/Yellow, safety shield).
  • Live wire operates at high potential (220V AC), Neutral is at zero potential (connected to earth at local substation), and Earth is connected deep into the ground via a copper plate.
  • The electric meter measures total energy consumed in kWh; the distribution box contains main switches, fuses, and MCBs.
  • Short circuit occurs when Live and Neutral wires come in direct contact, causing resistance to drop to nearly zero and current to spike dangerously.
  • Overloading happens when too many high-power appliances are plugged into a single socket, drawing current beyond the safe capacity of the wiring.
  • Fuses and MCBs act as breaking mechanisms to disconnect power during faults, preventing electrical fires and equipment damage.
  • Polarity and switch placement: Switches must always be connected to the Live wire so that turning off the appliance disconnects it from high voltage.

Key points

  • Household appliances are connected in parallel.
  • Standard Indian AC supply: 220V, 50Hz.
  • Wire color codes: Live (Red), Neutral (Black), Earth (Green).
  • Switches are placed in the Live wire for safety.
  • Earthing protects users from body leakage current.
  • MCB replaces traditional fuse for automatic resetting and fast action.
Worked examples
  • Explain why connecting appliances in series is unsuitable for domestic wiring. Solution: In series, if one appliance fails, the entire circuit breaks; total voltage is shared unequally; and appliances cannot be operated independently.
  • Calculate the maximum number of 60W bulbs that can safely run on a 5A fuse in a 220V domestic circuit. Solution: Maximum power $P = VI = 220 \times 5 = 1100\text{W}$. Number of bulbs = $1100 / 60 \approx 18$ bulbs.

Applications and connections

  • Residential housing and commercial building electrical installations.
  • Industrial machinery power distribution panels.
  • Earth leakage circuit breakers (ELCB/RCCB) for personnel protection in wet areas.

Advanced points, exceptions and limitations

  • Three-phase supply ($440\text{V}$) used for heavy industrial loads and high-capacity air conditioning.
  • Harmonic distortions in domestic circuits caused by non-linear electronic loads (SMPS, LED drivers).
  • Surge protection devices (SPDs) to protect sensitive electronics against lightning strikes.

Additional examples

  • Operating a refrigerator, television, and ceiling fan simultaneously in different rooms.
  • Connecting the metal body of an electric iron to the green earth pin of a 3-pin plug.
  • Tripping of MCB when a heavy-duty water heater is switched on alongside an AC unit.
Important distinctions
  • Series circuits share voltage and current; parallel circuits maintain constant voltage across all branches.
  • Live wire carries high potential; neutral wire returns current; earth wire carries no normal current.
  • Fuse is a one-time sacrificial component; MCB is reusable and resettable.
Common misconceptions
  • Believing electricity is consumed in the neutral wire.
  • Connecting switches to the neutral wire (leaving the appliance live even when switched off).
  • Assuming earth wire carries return current during normal operation.
Exam focus
  • Color coding standards of domestic wiring in India.
  • Why parallel connection is preferred over series in homes.
  • Function and placement of switches, fuses, and earth pins.
  • Calculations involving safe current ratings and total power loads.
  • Differences between short circuit and overloading.
Concept 4 · core

Magnetic Fields, Biot-Savart Law, Ampere's Law, and Lorentz Force

Electric currents generate magnetic fields, and magnetic fields exert forces on moving charges and current-carrying conductors.

Prerequisites

  • Vector calculus basics (cross product, line integrals).
  • Concept of electric charge and moving current.

Definitions and scope

  • Magnetic Field ($B$) is the region around a magnet or current-carrying conductor where magnetic forces are experienced, measured in Tesla (T).
  • Lorentz Force is the total electromagnetic force acting on a moving charged particle in combined electric and magnetic fields: $\vec{F} = q(\vec{E} + \vec{v} \times \vec{B})$.
  • Biot-Savart Law gives the magnetic field produced by a current element: $d\vec{B} = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi} \frac{I d\vec{l} \times \hat{r}}{r^2}$.

Step-by-step explanation

  • Oersted's discovery proved that electric current produces a magnetic field around it.
  • Right-Hand Thumb Rule gives the direction of the magnetic field around a straight current-carrying conductor.
  • Biot-Savart law states that magnetic field element $dB$ is proportional to current $I$, length $dl$, sine of angle $\theta$, and inversely proportional to square of distance $r$.
  • Ampere's Circuital Law states that the line integral of magnetic field $\vec{B}$ around a closed loop equals $\mu_0$ times the total current passing through the loop: $\oint \vec{B} \cdot d\vec{l} = \mu_0 I_{\text{enclosed}}$.
  • Magnetic field at the center of a circular coil of radius $R$ carrying current $I$: $B = \frac{\mu_0 NI}{2R}$.
  • Magnetic field inside a long solenoid: $B = \mu_0 n I$, where $n$ is turns per unit length.
  • Force on a moving charge in a magnetic field: $\vec{F} = q(\vec{v} \times \vec{B})$, magnitude $F = qvB\sin\theta$.
  • Force on a current-carrying conductor of length $l$ in a uniform magnetic field: $\vec{F} = I(\vec{l} \times \vec{B})$, magnitude $F = IlB\sin\theta$.

Key points

  • SI unit of magnetic field is Tesla (T) or Weber/m² (1 Gauss = $10^{-4}$ T).
  • Biot-Savart formula: $dB = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi} \frac{I dl \sin\theta}{r^2}$.
  • Ampere's law: $\oint B \cdot dl = \mu_0 I$.
  • Solenoid magnetic field: $B = \mu_0 n I$.
  • Magnetic force on moving charge: $F = qvB\sin\theta$ (zero when moving parallel to field).
  • Fleming's Left-Hand Rule determines force direction on a current-carrying conductor.
Worked examples
  • Calculate the magnetic field at the center of a circular coil of 100 turns, radius 0.1 m, carrying a current of 5 A. Given $\mu_0 = 4\pi \times 10^{-7} \text{ T}\cdot\text{m/A}$. Solution: $B = \frac{\mu_0 NI}{2R} = \frac{4\pi \times 10^{-7} \times 100 \times 5}{2 \times 0.1} = \frac{2\pi \times 10^{-5}}{0.2} = \pi \times 10^{-4} \text{ T} \approx 3.14 \times 10^{-4} \text{ T}$.
  • A straight wire of length 0.5 m carries a current of 2 A and is placed perpendicular to a uniform magnetic field of 0.4 T. Calculate the magnetic force. Solution: $F = IlB\sin(90^\circ) = 2 \times 0.5 \times 0.4 \times 1 = 0.4\text{ N}$.

Applications and connections

  • Electric motors operating on magnetic force on current-carrying coils.
  • Particle accelerators and cyclotrons steering charged particle beams.
  • Moving coil galvanometers, ammeters, and voltmeters.

Advanced points, exceptions and limitations

  • Cyclotron frequency: $f = \frac{qB}{2\pi m}$, independent of particle velocity (for non-relativistic speeds).
  • Hall effect measures charge carrier concentration and sign in semiconductors.
  • Toroids provide uniform confinement of magnetic fields in toroidal geometry.

Additional examples

  • Deflection of electron beam in a cathode ray tube (CRT) by magnetic coils.
  • Magnetic levitation (Maglev) trains utilizing Lorentz force.
  • Force between two parallel current-carrying wires defining the Ampere standard.
Important distinctions
  • Electric field exerts force parallel/anti-parallel to field; magnetic field exerts force perpendicular to both velocity and field.
  • Electric force does work on stationary and moving charges; magnetic force does zero work on moving charges (since $\vec{F} \perp \vec{v}$).
  • Biot-Savart law is analogous to Coulomb's law in electrostatics.
Common misconceptions
  • Believing a magnetic field exerts a force on a stationary charge.
  • Assuming magnetic field lines can cross each other.
  • Confusing Fleming's Left-Hand Rule (motor rule) with Fleming's Right-Hand Rule (generator rule).
Exam focus
  • Derivations of magnetic field using Biot-Savart law (circular loop axis, straight wire).
  • Ampere's law applications for long wire, solenoid, and toroid.
  • Radius and time period of charged particle in a uniform magnetic field ($r = \frac{mv}{qB}$).
  • Torque on a current loop in a magnetic field ($\tau = NIAB\sin\theta$).
  • Numerical problems on Fleming's Left-Hand Rule.
Concept 5 · advanced

Electromagnetic Induction, Faraday's Law, Lenz's Law, and Transformers

Changing magnetic flux induces an electromotive force (EMF) in a circuit, a principle harnessed in generators and transformers.

Prerequisites

  • Concept of magnetic flux ($\Phi_B = \vec{B} \cdot \vec{A}$).
  • Understanding of electromagnetic fields and induced currents.

Definitions and scope

  • Electromagnetic Induction is the generation of an electric current and EMF in a circuit due to a changing magnetic flux linked with it.
  • Faraday's Law of Induction states that the magnitude of induced EMF is directly proportional to the rate of change of magnetic flux linked with the circuit.
  • A Transformer is a static electrical device that transfers electrical energy between two or more circuits through electromagnetic induction, altering voltage levels.

Step-by-step explanation

  • Magnetic flux linkage $\Phi_B = B A \cos\theta$, measured in Weber (Wb) or Tesla-meter².
  • Faraday's Law equation: $E = -N \frac{d\Phi_B}{dt}$, where $N$ is the number of turns.
  • Lenz's Law states that the polarity of induced EMF is such that it opposes the very cause (change in flux) that produces it. This embodies the law of conservation of energy.
  • Motional EMF: When a conductor of length $l$ moves with velocity $v$ perpendicular to magnetic field $B$, induced EMF is $E = Blv$.
  • Eddy currents are loops of electrical current induced within conductors by changing magnetic fields, causing damping and heating.
  • Transformer Principle: Operates solely on AC. Alternating current in the primary coil creates a changing magnetic flux in the soft iron core, inducing an AC EMF in the secondary coil.
  • Transformer Ratio equation: $\frac{V_s}{V_p} = \frac{N_s}{N_p} = \frac{I_p}{I_s} = k$, where $k$ is the transformation ratio.
  • Step-up transformer increases voltage ($N_s > N_p, k > 1$); step-down transformer decreases voltage ($N_s < N_p, k < 1$).

Key points

  • Faraday's law: $E = -N \frac{d\Phi}{dt}$.
  • Lenz's law provides the negative sign in Faraday's law, ensuring conservation of energy.
  • Motional EMF formula: $E = Blv$.
  • Transformers work only with AC; DC produces constant flux with zero induced EMF.
  • Ideal transformer efficiency: $P_{\text{in}} = P_{\text{out}}$ ($V_p I_p = V_s I_s$).
  • Core losses in transformers include hysteresis loss and eddy current loss (minimized by laminated soft iron core).
Worked examples
  • A transformer has 500 primary turns and 5000 secondary turns. If the primary voltage is 220V, calculate the secondary voltage. Solution: $\frac{V_s}{V_p} = \frac{N_s}{N_p} \Rightarrow V_s = 220 \times \frac{5000}{500} = 220 \times 10 = 2200\text{ V}$.
  • Magnetic flux linked with a coil changes from $5\text{ Wb}$ to $1\text{ Wb}$ in $0.2\text{ s}$. Find the induced EMF if the coil has 10 turns. Solution: $E = -N \frac{\Delta \Phi}{\Delta t} = -10 \times \frac{1 - 5}{0.2} = -10 \times \frac{-4}{0.2} = +200\text{ V}$.

Applications and connections

  • AC generators and alternators producing commercial electrical power.
  • Power transmission grids utilizing step-up and step-down transformers.
  • Induction cooktops, metal detectors, and magnetic braking systems in trains.

Advanced points, exceptions and limitations

  • Self-inductance ($L$) and mutual inductance ($M$) define back-EMF and transformer coupling.
  • Energy stored in an inductor: $U = \frac{1}{2} L I^2$.
  • Skin effect and proximity losses in high-frequency transformer windings.

Additional examples

  • Mobile phone chargers containing step-down transformers and rectifiers.
  • Power grid substations stepping down 132kV transmission voltage to 220V for residential use.
  • Dynamic braking in electric trains using eddy current damping.
Important distinctions
  • Step-up transformer increases voltage and decreases current; step-down transformer decreases voltage and increases current.
  • Self-induction involves a single coil opposing current changes; mutual induction involves two magnetically linked coils.
  • AC transformers operate via changing flux; DC circuits require switching/chopper circuits to induce EMF.
Common misconceptions
  • Believing transformers can step up DC voltage.
  • Assuming a transformer generates new electrical energy (it only transforms voltage/current; input power equals output power minus losses).
  • Thinking Lenz's law violates the law of conservation of energy.
Exam focus
  • Numerical problems on transformer turns ratio, voltage, and current relationships.
  • Causes of energy losses in transformers (copper loss, eddy current loss, hysteresis loss, flux leakage) and remedies.
  • Derivation of motional EMF ($E = Blv$).
  • Conceptual questions on Lenz's law direction of induced current.
  • Self-inductance of a long solenoid ($L = \mu_0 n^2 A l$).

Definition and scope

Concept 1 · foundation

Ohm's Law, Resistance, and Resistivity

Ohm's law establishes the linear relationship between potential difference and electric current under constant physical conditions.

Prerequisites

  • Basic understanding of electric charge, electric current ($I$), and potential difference ($V$).
  • Concept of atomic structure, free electrons, and drift velocity.

Definitions and scope

  • Ohm's Law states that the electric current flowing through a metallic conductor is directly proportional to the potential difference across its ends, provided physical conditions like temperature remain constant ($V \propto I$).
  • Resistance ($R$) is the obstruction offered by a conductor to the flow of electric current, measured in Ohms ($\Omega$).
  • Resistivity ($\rho$) is the inherent property of a material to resist current flow, defined as resistance per unit length and unit cross-sectional area.

Step-by-step explanation

  • The mathematical expression for Ohm's law is $V = IR$, where $V$ is voltage, $I$ is current, and $R$ is resistance.
  • Resistance depends directly on the length ($l$) of the conductor and inversely on its cross-sectional area ($A$), expressed as $R = \rho \frac{l}{A}$.
  • The SI unit of resistivity is Ohm-meter ($\Omega\cdot\text{m}$). Conductivity ($\sigma$) is the reciprocal of resistivity, measured in Siemens per meter (S/m).
  • Ohm's law is an empirical law; it does not hold universally for all materials (e.g., diodes, transistors, and electrolytes are non-ohmic conductors).
  • Effect of temperature on resistance: For metals, resistance increases linearly with temperature according to $R_t = R_0(1 + \alpha \Delta T)$, where $\alpha$ is the temperature coefficient of resistance.
  • For semiconductors and insulators, resistance decreases with an increase in temperature due to an increase in charge carrier density.
  • Drift velocity ($v_d$) of electrons is related to current by $I = nAe v_d$, where $n$ is electron density and $e$ is electronic charge.
  • Microscopic form of Ohm's law relates current density ($J$), electric field ($E$), and conductivity ($\sigma$) as $\vec{J} = \sigma \vec{E}$.

Key points

  • Formula: $V = IR$ and $R = \rho \frac{l}{A}$.
  • Ohm's law applies only under constant physical conditions (especially temperature).
  • Resistivity depends only on material and temperature, not on dimensions.
  • Metals have positive temperature coefficient of resistance ($\alpha > 0$).
  • Non-ohmic devices do not obey $V$-$I$ linearity.
  • Microscopic Ohm's law: $\vec{J} = \sigma \vec{E}$.
Worked examples
  • Find the resistance of a copper wire of length 2 m and cross-sectional area $10^{-6}\text{ m}^2$. Given resistivity of copper $\rho = 1.7 \times 10^{-8} \Omega\cdot\text{m}$. Solution: $R = \rho \frac{l}{A} = \frac{1.7 \times 10^{-8} \times 2}{10^{-6}} = 3.4 \times 10^{-2} \Omega = 0.034 \Omega$.
  • Calculate the current flowing through a resistor of $20 \Omega$ connected across a 120 V DC source. Solution: Using $I = \frac{V}{R} = \frac{120}{20} = 6\text{ A}.

Applications and connections

  • Design of standard resistors and heating elements in appliances.
  • Voltage regulation and circuit analysis using Kirchhoff's laws.
  • Selection of wire gauges for safe electrical installations.

Advanced points, exceptions and limitations

  • Superconductors exhibit zero resistance below a critical temperature ($T_c$).
  • Mobility of charge carriers ($\mu = \frac{v_d}{E}$) determines semiconductor performance.
  • High-frequency AC currents suffer from skin effect, concentrating current near the conductor surface.

Additional examples

  • Nichrome wire used in room heaters due to high resistivity.
  • Copper cables used for power transmission due to low resistivity.
  • Carbon resistors used in electronic circuits with color-coding.
Important distinctions
  • Resistance depends on dimensions and material; resistivity depends strictly on material and temperature.
  • Ohmic conductors maintain constant $R$ across $V$; non-ohmic conductors show variable dynamic resistance ($dV/dI$).
  • Conductivity measures ease of current flow; resistivity measures opposition.
Common misconceptions
  • Believing resistivity changes when a wire is stretched (resistivity remains constant; resistance changes).
  • Assuming Ohm's law is a fundamental law of nature like Newton's laws.
  • Confusing resistance with resistivity.
Exam focus
  • Numerical problems involving stretching of wires ($R \propto l^2$ or $R \propto 1/A^2$).
  • Color coding of carbon resistors and tolerance values.
  • Temperature coefficient of resistance derivations and units.
  • Graph interpretation of $V$-$I$ characteristics for ohmic and non-ohmic devices.
  • Microscopic derivation of Ohm's law from relaxation time.
Concept 2 · core

Electric Power and Joule's Heating Effect

Electric power quantifies the rate of electrical energy transfer, while Joule's law governs the conversion of electrical energy into thermal energy.

Prerequisites

  • Understanding of work, energy, and power from mechanics.
  • Familiarity with potential difference and electric current.

Definitions and scope

  • Electric Power ($P$) is the rate at which electrical work is done or energy is consumed in an electrical circuit, measured in Watts (W).
  • Joule's Heating Effect states that the heat produced in a resistor is directly proportional to the square of current, resistance, and time ($H = I^2Rt$).

Step-by-step explanation

  • Power formulas: $P = VI = I^2R = \frac{V^2}{R}$.
  • Electrical energy consumed is $E = P \times t = VIt$.
  • Commercial unit of electrical energy is the Kilowatt-hour (kWh), commonly known as a 'unit' of electricity ($1\text{ kWh} = 3.6 \times 10^6\text{ Joules}$).
  • Joule's law of heating: $H = I^2Rt$ Joules, or in calories, $H = \frac{I^2Rt}{4.18}$ cal.
  • Power rating of appliances indicates energy consumed per second at rated voltage (e.g., a 100W bulb consumes 100 Joules per second).
  • Transmission losses in power lines: Power loss is given by $P_{\text{loss}} = I^2R$. To minimize loss, power is transmitted at very high voltage and low current.
  • Electric fuses work on the heating effect; when excessive current flows, the fuse wire melts and breaks the circuit.
  • Filament lamps operate at high temperatures where tungsten glows incandescently in an inert gas atmosphere.

Key points

  • Power formulas: $P = VI$, $P = I^2R$, $P = V^2/R$.
  • 1 Unit = 1 kWh = $3.6 \times 10^6\text{ J}$.
  • Heat generated: $H = I^2Rt$.
  • High voltage transmission reduces $I^2R$ line losses.
  • Fuse wire requires low melting point and high resistivity (typically lead-tin alloy).
  • Tungsten filament has high melting point (approx 3380°C).
Worked examples
  • An electric iron of resistance $50 \Omega$ draws a current of 4 A. Calculate the heat developed in 5 minutes. Solution: $I = 4\text{ A}$, $R = 50 \Omega$, $t = 300\text{ s}$. $H = I^2Rt = (4)^2 \times 50 \times 300 = 16 \times 50 \times 300 = 240,000\text{ Joules} = 240\text{ kJ}$.
  • Calculate the cost of operating five 100W bulbs for 10 hours daily in a month of 30 days, if electricity costs ₹5 per kWh. Solution: Total energy = $(5 \times 100\text{W} \times 10\text{h} \times 30) / 1000 = 150\text{ kWh}$. Cost = $150 \times 5 = \text{₹}750$.

Applications and connections

  • Electric heaters, geysers, toasters, and soldering irons utilizing heating coils.
  • Incandescent lighting and halogen lamps.
  • Circuit protection devices such as fuses and miniature circuit breakers (MCBs).

Advanced points, exceptions and limitations

  • Power transfer theorem: Maximum power is transferred from a source to a load when internal resistance equals load resistance ($r = R$).
  • Skin effect increases AC resistance in high-power transmission cables.
  • Eddy current heating (induction heating) used in industrial metallurgy.

Additional examples

  • Room heater rated 2000W, 230V.
  • Fuse wire melting at 5A rating during a short circuit.
  • Electric kettle boiling water rapidly via immersed heating element.
Important distinctions
  • Power is rate of energy transfer (Watts); energy is total work done over time (Joules or kWh).
  • Series grouping of bulbs shares voltage and reduces total power; parallel grouping maintains rated voltage across each bulb.
  • Fuse wire has low melting point; transmission wires have high conductivity and high melting point.
Common misconceptions
  • Thinking a 100W bulb is brighter than a 60W bulb when connected in series (in series, the 60W bulb has higher resistance and glows brighter).
  • Confusing power rating with actual power consumed when operated at non-rated voltage.
  • Believing electricity is consumed and destroyed rather than converted.
Exam focus
  • Domestic electricity bill calculation problems.
  • Series vs. parallel bulb brightness comparisons.
  • Maximum power transfer theorem condition ($R = r$).
  • Properties of fuse wire materials.
  • Derivation of Joule's heating law.
Concept 3 · application

Domestic Electric Circuits and Safety Devices

Domestic wiring systems utilize parallel distribution networks supplied with AC mains, protected by earthing, fuses, and MCBs.

Prerequisites

  • Understanding of AC supply voltage (220V, 50Hz in India).
  • Basic circuit concepts of series and parallel connections.

Definitions and scope

  • Domestic Circuit is the standard household wiring system delivering electricity from the mains supply to individual appliances in parallel.
  • Earthing (Grounding) is the safety connection of metal appliance bodies to the earth to prevent severe electric shocks.
  • MCB (Miniature Circuit Breaker) is an electromechanical safety device that automatically trips during overload or short circuit.

Step-by-step explanation

  • Household wiring is always connected in parallel so that every appliance receives the full rated voltage (220V) and can be operated independently.
  • Three color-coded wires are used in standard Indian domestic circuits: Live (Red/Brown, carries current), Neutral (Black/Blue, completes circuit), and Earth (Green/Yellow, safety shield).
  • Live wire operates at high potential (220V AC), Neutral is at zero potential (connected to earth at local substation), and Earth is connected deep into the ground via a copper plate.
  • The electric meter measures total energy consumed in kWh; the distribution box contains main switches, fuses, and MCBs.
  • Short circuit occurs when Live and Neutral wires come in direct contact, causing resistance to drop to nearly zero and current to spike dangerously.
  • Overloading happens when too many high-power appliances are plugged into a single socket, drawing current beyond the safe capacity of the wiring.
  • Fuses and MCBs act as breaking mechanisms to disconnect power during faults, preventing electrical fires and equipment damage.
  • Polarity and switch placement: Switches must always be connected to the Live wire so that turning off the appliance disconnects it from high voltage.

Key points

  • Household appliances are connected in parallel.
  • Standard Indian AC supply: 220V, 50Hz.
  • Wire color codes: Live (Red), Neutral (Black), Earth (Green).
  • Switches are placed in the Live wire for safety.
  • Earthing protects users from body leakage current.
  • MCB replaces traditional fuse for automatic resetting and fast action.
Worked examples
  • Explain why connecting appliances in series is unsuitable for domestic wiring. Solution: In series, if one appliance fails, the entire circuit breaks; total voltage is shared unequally; and appliances cannot be operated independently.
  • Calculate the maximum number of 60W bulbs that can safely run on a 5A fuse in a 220V domestic circuit. Solution: Maximum power $P = VI = 220 \times 5 = 1100\text{W}$. Number of bulbs = $1100 / 60 \approx 18$ bulbs.

Applications and connections

  • Residential housing and commercial building electrical installations.
  • Industrial machinery power distribution panels.
  • Earth leakage circuit breakers (ELCB/RCCB) for personnel protection in wet areas.

Advanced points, exceptions and limitations

  • Three-phase supply ($440\text{V}$) used for heavy industrial loads and high-capacity air conditioning.
  • Harmonic distortions in domestic circuits caused by non-linear electronic loads (SMPS, LED drivers).
  • Surge protection devices (SPDs) to protect sensitive electronics against lightning strikes.

Additional examples

  • Operating a refrigerator, television, and ceiling fan simultaneously in different rooms.
  • Connecting the metal body of an electric iron to the green earth pin of a 3-pin plug.
  • Tripping of MCB when a heavy-duty water heater is switched on alongside an AC unit.
Important distinctions
  • Series circuits share voltage and current; parallel circuits maintain constant voltage across all branches.
  • Live wire carries high potential; neutral wire returns current; earth wire carries no normal current.
  • Fuse is a one-time sacrificial component; MCB is reusable and resettable.
Common misconceptions
  • Believing electricity is consumed in the neutral wire.
  • Connecting switches to the neutral wire (leaving the appliance live even when switched off).
  • Assuming earth wire carries return current during normal operation.
Exam focus
  • Color coding standards of domestic wiring in India.
  • Why parallel connection is preferred over series in homes.
  • Function and placement of switches, fuses, and earth pins.
  • Calculations involving safe current ratings and total power loads.
  • Differences between short circuit and overloading.
Concept 4 · core

Magnetic Fields, Biot-Savart Law, Ampere's Law, and Lorentz Force

Electric currents generate magnetic fields, and magnetic fields exert forces on moving charges and current-carrying conductors.

Prerequisites

  • Vector calculus basics (cross product, line integrals).
  • Concept of electric charge and moving current.

Definitions and scope

  • Magnetic Field ($B$) is the region around a magnet or current-carrying conductor where magnetic forces are experienced, measured in Tesla (T).
  • Lorentz Force is the total electromagnetic force acting on a moving charged particle in combined electric and magnetic fields: $\vec{F} = q(\vec{E} + \vec{v} \times \vec{B})$.
  • Biot-Savart Law gives the magnetic field produced by a current element: $d\vec{B} = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi} \frac{I d\vec{l} \times \hat{r}}{r^2}$.

Step-by-step explanation

  • Oersted's discovery proved that electric current produces a magnetic field around it.
  • Right-Hand Thumb Rule gives the direction of the magnetic field around a straight current-carrying conductor.
  • Biot-Savart law states that magnetic field element $dB$ is proportional to current $I$, length $dl$, sine of angle $\theta$, and inversely proportional to square of distance $r$.
  • Ampere's Circuital Law states that the line integral of magnetic field $\vec{B}$ around a closed loop equals $\mu_0$ times the total current passing through the loop: $\oint \vec{B} \cdot d\vec{l} = \mu_0 I_{\text{enclosed}}$.
  • Magnetic field at the center of a circular coil of radius $R$ carrying current $I$: $B = \frac{\mu_0 NI}{2R}$.
  • Magnetic field inside a long solenoid: $B = \mu_0 n I$, where $n$ is turns per unit length.
  • Force on a moving charge in a magnetic field: $\vec{F} = q(\vec{v} \times \vec{B})$, magnitude $F = qvB\sin\theta$.
  • Force on a current-carrying conductor of length $l$ in a uniform magnetic field: $\vec{F} = I(\vec{l} \times \vec{B})$, magnitude $F = IlB\sin\theta$.

Key points

  • SI unit of magnetic field is Tesla (T) or Weber/m² (1 Gauss = $10^{-4}$ T).
  • Biot-Savart formula: $dB = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi} \frac{I dl \sin\theta}{r^2}$.
  • Ampere's law: $\oint B \cdot dl = \mu_0 I$.
  • Solenoid magnetic field: $B = \mu_0 n I$.
  • Magnetic force on moving charge: $F = qvB\sin\theta$ (zero when moving parallel to field).
  • Fleming's Left-Hand Rule determines force direction on a current-carrying conductor.
Worked examples
  • Calculate the magnetic field at the center of a circular coil of 100 turns, radius 0.1 m, carrying a current of 5 A. Given $\mu_0 = 4\pi \times 10^{-7} \text{ T}\cdot\text{m/A}$. Solution: $B = \frac{\mu_0 NI}{2R} = \frac{4\pi \times 10^{-7} \times 100 \times 5}{2 \times 0.1} = \frac{2\pi \times 10^{-5}}{0.2} = \pi \times 10^{-4} \text{ T} \approx 3.14 \times 10^{-4} \text{ T}$.
  • A straight wire of length 0.5 m carries a current of 2 A and is placed perpendicular to a uniform magnetic field of 0.4 T. Calculate the magnetic force. Solution: $F = IlB\sin(90^\circ) = 2 \times 0.5 \times 0.4 \times 1 = 0.4\text{ N}$.

Applications and connections

  • Electric motors operating on magnetic force on current-carrying coils.
  • Particle accelerators and cyclotrons steering charged particle beams.
  • Moving coil galvanometers, ammeters, and voltmeters.

Advanced points, exceptions and limitations

  • Cyclotron frequency: $f = \frac{qB}{2\pi m}$, independent of particle velocity (for non-relativistic speeds).
  • Hall effect measures charge carrier concentration and sign in semiconductors.
  • Toroids provide uniform confinement of magnetic fields in toroidal geometry.

Additional examples

  • Deflection of electron beam in a cathode ray tube (CRT) by magnetic coils.
  • Magnetic levitation (Maglev) trains utilizing Lorentz force.
  • Force between two parallel current-carrying wires defining the Ampere standard.
Important distinctions
  • Electric field exerts force parallel/anti-parallel to field; magnetic field exerts force perpendicular to both velocity and field.
  • Electric force does work on stationary and moving charges; magnetic force does zero work on moving charges (since $\vec{F} \perp \vec{v}$).
  • Biot-Savart law is analogous to Coulomb's law in electrostatics.
Common misconceptions
  • Believing a magnetic field exerts a force on a stationary charge.
  • Assuming magnetic field lines can cross each other.
  • Confusing Fleming's Left-Hand Rule (motor rule) with Fleming's Right-Hand Rule (generator rule).
Exam focus
  • Derivations of magnetic field using Biot-Savart law (circular loop axis, straight wire).
  • Ampere's law applications for long wire, solenoid, and toroid.
  • Radius and time period of charged particle in a uniform magnetic field ($r = \frac{mv}{qB}$).
  • Torque on a current loop in a magnetic field ($\tau = NIAB\sin\theta$).
  • Numerical problems on Fleming's Left-Hand Rule.
Concept 5 · advanced

Electromagnetic Induction, Faraday's Law, Lenz's Law, and Transformers

Changing magnetic flux induces an electromotive force (EMF) in a circuit, a principle harnessed in generators and transformers.

Prerequisites

  • Concept of magnetic flux ($\Phi_B = \vec{B} \cdot \vec{A}$).
  • Understanding of electromagnetic fields and induced currents.

Definitions and scope

  • Electromagnetic Induction is the generation of an electric current and EMF in a circuit due to a changing magnetic flux linked with it.
  • Faraday's Law of Induction states that the magnitude of induced EMF is directly proportional to the rate of change of magnetic flux linked with the circuit.
  • A Transformer is a static electrical device that transfers electrical energy between two or more circuits through electromagnetic induction, altering voltage levels.

Step-by-step explanation

  • Magnetic flux linkage $\Phi_B = B A \cos\theta$, measured in Weber (Wb) or Tesla-meter².
  • Faraday's Law equation: $E = -N \frac{d\Phi_B}{dt}$, where $N$ is the number of turns.
  • Lenz's Law states that the polarity of induced EMF is such that it opposes the very cause (change in flux) that produces it. This embodies the law of conservation of energy.
  • Motional EMF: When a conductor of length $l$ moves with velocity $v$ perpendicular to magnetic field $B$, induced EMF is $E = Blv$.
  • Eddy currents are loops of electrical current induced within conductors by changing magnetic fields, causing damping and heating.
  • Transformer Principle: Operates solely on AC. Alternating current in the primary coil creates a changing magnetic flux in the soft iron core, inducing an AC EMF in the secondary coil.
  • Transformer Ratio equation: $\frac{V_s}{V_p} = \frac{N_s}{N_p} = \frac{I_p}{I_s} = k$, where $k$ is the transformation ratio.
  • Step-up transformer increases voltage ($N_s > N_p, k > 1$); step-down transformer decreases voltage ($N_s < N_p, k < 1$).

Key points

  • Faraday's law: $E = -N \frac{d\Phi}{dt}$.
  • Lenz's law provides the negative sign in Faraday's law, ensuring conservation of energy.
  • Motional EMF formula: $E = Blv$.
  • Transformers work only with AC; DC produces constant flux with zero induced EMF.
  • Ideal transformer efficiency: $P_{\text{in}} = P_{\text{out}}$ ($V_p I_p = V_s I_s$).
  • Core losses in transformers include hysteresis loss and eddy current loss (minimized by laminated soft iron core).
Worked examples
  • A transformer has 500 primary turns and 5000 secondary turns. If the primary voltage is 220V, calculate the secondary voltage. Solution: $\frac{V_s}{V_p} = \frac{N_s}{N_p} \Rightarrow V_s = 220 \times \frac{5000}{500} = 220 \times 10 = 2200\text{ V}$.
  • Magnetic flux linked with a coil changes from $5\text{ Wb}$ to $1\text{ Wb}$ in $0.2\text{ s}$. Find the induced EMF if the coil has 10 turns. Solution: $E = -N \frac{\Delta \Phi}{\Delta t} = -10 \times \frac{1 - 5}{0.2} = -10 \times \frac{-4}{0.2} = +200\text{ V}$.

Applications and connections

  • AC generators and alternators producing commercial electrical power.
  • Power transmission grids utilizing step-up and step-down transformers.
  • Induction cooktops, metal detectors, and magnetic braking systems in trains.

Advanced points, exceptions and limitations

  • Self-inductance ($L$) and mutual inductance ($M$) define back-EMF and transformer coupling.
  • Energy stored in an inductor: $U = \frac{1}{2} L I^2$.
  • Skin effect and proximity losses in high-frequency transformer windings.

Additional examples

  • Mobile phone chargers containing step-down transformers and rectifiers.
  • Power grid substations stepping down 132kV transmission voltage to 220V for residential use.
  • Dynamic braking in electric trains using eddy current damping.
Important distinctions
  • Step-up transformer increases voltage and decreases current; step-down transformer decreases voltage and increases current.
  • Self-induction involves a single coil opposing current changes; mutual induction involves two magnetically linked coils.
  • AC transformers operate via changing flux; DC circuits require switching/chopper circuits to induce EMF.
Common misconceptions
  • Believing transformers can step up DC voltage.
  • Assuming a transformer generates new electrical energy (it only transforms voltage/current; input power equals output power minus losses).
  • Thinking Lenz's law violates the law of conservation of energy.
Exam focus
  • Numerical problems on transformer turns ratio, voltage, and current relationships.
  • Causes of energy losses in transformers (copper loss, eddy current loss, hysteresis loss, flux leakage) and remedies.
  • Derivation of motional EMF ($E = Blv$).
  • Conceptual questions on Lenz's law direction of induced current.
  • Self-inductance of a long solenoid ($L = \mu_0 n^2 A l$).

Rapid revision: Electricity & Magnetism

Concept 1

Ohm's Law, Resistance, and Resistivity

Definition and scope

  • Ohm's law states $V = IR$ under constant physical conditions.
  • Resistivity $\rho$ is material-dependent and independent of dimensions.
  • Metals exhibit positive temperature coefficients of resistance.

Must remember

  • $R = \rho \frac{l}{A}$
  • Microscopic Ohm's law: $\vec{J} = \sigma \vec{E}$
  • Stretching wire of length $l_1$ to $l_2$ changes resistance as $R \propto l^2$.
  • SI unit of resistance is Ohm ($\Omega$); resistivity is $\Omega\cdot\text{m}$.
  • Non-ohmic devices do not follow linear $V$-$I$ curves.

Concept-specific exam traps

  • Assuming resistivity changes when wire dimensions are altered.
  • Applying Ohm's law to non-linear semiconductor diodes without dynamic resistance consideration.
Concept 2

Electric Power and Joule's Heating Effect

Definition and scope

  • Electric power $P = VI = I^2R = V^2/R$.
  • Commercial unit of energy is kWh (1 unit = $3.6 \times 10^6$ J).
  • Joule's heating law gives heat generated $H = I^2Rt$.

Must remember

  • High voltage power transmission minimizes $I^2R$ line losses.
  • Fuse wire requires low melting point and high resistivity.
  • Maximum power transfer theorem requires load resistance to equal internal resistance ($R = r$).
  • Tungsten filament lamps operate at high incandescence.
  • Series bulb brightness depends on resistance, not wattage rating.

Concept-specific exam traps

  • Assuming a 100W bulb always emits 100W regardless of applied voltage.
  • Confusing electrical power units (Watts) with electrical energy units (kWh).
Concept 3

Domestic Electric Circuits and Safety Devices

Definition and scope

  • Domestic circuits use parallel wiring to maintain constant voltage.
  • Color codes: Live (Red), Neutral (Black), Earth (Green).
  • Switches must be connected to the Live wire.

Must remember

  • Indian domestic supply is 220V AC at 50Hz.
  • Earthing protects users from body leakage shock.
  • MCBs provide automated trip protection against overloads and short circuits.
  • Short circuit occurs when Live and Neutral wires touch directly.
  • Overloading occurs when total connected load exceeds safe wire capacity.

Concept-specific exam traps

  • Believing appliances in homes are wired in series.
  • Placing switches in the neutral wire instead of the live wire.
Concept 4

Magnetic Fields, Biot-Savart Law, Ampere's Law, and Lorentz Force

Definition and scope

  • Magnetic field $B$ is measured in Tesla (T).
  • Biot-Savart law gives magnetic field due to current element.
  • Lorentz force accounts for combined electric and magnetic forces on moving charges.

Must remember

  • Ampere's Circuital Law: $\oint B \cdot dl = \mu_0 I$.
  • Solenoid magnetic field: $B = \mu_0 n I$.
  • Magnetic force on moving charge: $F = qvB\sin\theta$.
  • Magnetic force on current wire: $F = IlB\sin\theta$.
  • Magnetic force does zero work on moving charges.

Concept-specific exam traps

  • Confusing Fleming's Left-Hand Rule (motors) with Right-Hand Rule (generators).
  • Assuming magnetic force acts on stationary electric charges.
Concept 5

Electromagnetic Induction, Faraday's Law, Lenz's Law, and Transformers

Definition and scope

  • Faraday's law: Induced EMF $E = -N \frac{d\Phi}{dt}$.
  • Lenz's law dictates opposition to flux change (conservation of energy).
  • Transformer transfers AC energy via electromagnetic induction.

Must remember

  • Motional EMF formula: $E = Blv$.
  • Transformer ratio: $\frac{V_s}{V_p} = \frac{N_s}{N_p} = \frac{I_p}{I_s}$.
  • Transformers operate exclusively on AC supply.
  • Laminated soft iron cores minimize eddy current losses.
  • Step-up transformers increase voltage and decrease current.

Concept-specific exam traps

  • Believing transformers can step up DC voltage.
  • Forgetting the negative sign in Faraday's law when determining induced EMF direction.

Quick facts

  • Ohm's law ($V = IR$) applies only under constant physical conditions like temperature.
  • Resistivity depends strictly on material and temperature, not on length or cross-sectional area.
  • Commercial unit of electrical energy is 1 kWh ($3.6 \times 10^6$ Joules).
  • Domestic electrical circuits in India operate at 220V AC and 50Hz frequency.
  • Household appliances are connected in parallel to ensure independent operation and rated voltage.
  • Fuse wire is made of lead-tin alloy, featuring high resistivity and low melting point.
  • Magnetic force on a moving charged particle does zero work because force is perpendicular to velocity.
  • Transformers operate exclusively on alternating current (AC) through mutual induction.

Important distinctions

ResistancevsResistivity
  • Resistance depends on length, cross-sectional area, material, and temperature; resistivity depends only on material and temperature.
  • Unit of resistance is Ohm ($\Omega$); unit of resistivity is Ohm-meter ($\Omega\cdot\text{m}$).
Fleming's Left-Hand RulevsFleming's Right-Hand Rule

  • Right-Hand Rule is used for electric generators to find the direction of induced current in a moving conductor.

  • ELF: Electric field $\times$ Length = Force (Lorentz).
  • MOTORS use Left hand; GENERATORS use Right hand.

Exam traps and cautions

  • Do not assume resistivity changes when stretching a wire; volume remains constant, changing $R$ but not $\rho$.
  • Do not forget that transformers are strictly AC devices; DC causes zero secondary EMF.
  • Remember that household switches must always be wired into the live wire, never the neutral wire.
  • Recognize that magnetic force does zero work on a moving charge because force is perpendicular to displacement.

Concept 1 · foundation

Ohm's Law, Resistance, and Resistivity

Ohm's law establishes the linear relationship between potential difference and electric current under constant physical conditions.

Prerequisites

  • Basic understanding of electric charge, electric current ($I$), and potential difference ($V$).
  • Concept of atomic structure, free electrons, and drift velocity.

Definitions and scope

  • Ohm's Law states that the electric current flowing through a metallic conductor is directly proportional to the potential difference across its ends, provided physical conditions like temperature remain constant ($V \propto I$).
  • Resistance ($R$) is the obstruction offered by a conductor to the flow of electric current, measured in Ohms ($\Omega$).
  • Resistivity ($\rho$) is the inherent property of a material to resist current flow, defined as resistance per unit length and unit cross-sectional area.

Step-by-step explanation

  • The mathematical expression for Ohm's law is $V = IR$, where $V$ is voltage, $I$ is current, and $R$ is resistance.
  • Resistance depends directly on the length ($l$) of the conductor and inversely on its cross-sectional area ($A$), expressed as $R = \rho \frac{l}{A}$.
  • The SI unit of resistivity is Ohm-meter ($\Omega\cdot\text{m}$). Conductivity ($\sigma$) is the reciprocal of resistivity, measured in Siemens per meter (S/m).
  • Ohm's law is an empirical law; it does not hold universally for all materials (e.g., diodes, transistors, and electrolytes are non-ohmic conductors).
  • Effect of temperature on resistance: For metals, resistance increases linearly with temperature according to $R_t = R_0(1 + \alpha \Delta T)$, where $\alpha$ is the temperature coefficient of resistance.
  • For semiconductors and insulators, resistance decreases with an increase in temperature due to an increase in charge carrier density.
  • Drift velocity ($v_d$) of electrons is related to current by $I = nAe v_d$, where $n$ is electron density and $e$ is electronic charge.
  • Microscopic form of Ohm's law relates current density ($J$), electric field ($E$), and conductivity ($\sigma$) as $\vec{J} = \sigma \vec{E}$.

Key points

  • Formula: $V = IR$ and $R = \rho \frac{l}{A}$.
  • Ohm's law applies only under constant physical conditions (especially temperature).
  • Resistivity depends only on material and temperature, not on dimensions.
  • Metals have positive temperature coefficient of resistance ($\alpha > 0$).
  • Non-ohmic devices do not obey $V$-$I$ linearity.
  • Microscopic Ohm's law: $\vec{J} = \sigma \vec{E}$.
Worked examples
  • Find the resistance of a copper wire of length 2 m and cross-sectional area $10^{-6}\text{ m}^2$. Given resistivity of copper $\rho = 1.7 \times 10^{-8} \Omega\cdot\text{m}$. Solution: $R = \rho \frac{l}{A} = \frac{1.7 \times 10^{-8} \times 2}{10^{-6}} = 3.4 \times 10^{-2} \Omega = 0.034 \Omega$.
  • Calculate the current flowing through a resistor of $20 \Omega$ connected across a 120 V DC source. Solution: Using $I = \frac{V}{R} = \frac{120}{20} = 6\text{ A}.

Applications and connections

  • Design of standard resistors and heating elements in appliances.
  • Voltage regulation and circuit analysis using Kirchhoff's laws.
  • Selection of wire gauges for safe electrical installations.

Advanced points, exceptions and limitations

  • Superconductors exhibit zero resistance below a critical temperature ($T_c$).
  • Mobility of charge carriers ($\mu = \frac{v_d}{E}$) determines semiconductor performance.
  • High-frequency AC currents suffer from skin effect, concentrating current near the conductor surface.

Additional examples

  • Nichrome wire used in room heaters due to high resistivity.
  • Copper cables used for power transmission due to low resistivity.
  • Carbon resistors used in electronic circuits with color-coding.
Important distinctions
  • Resistance depends on dimensions and material; resistivity depends strictly on material and temperature.
  • Ohmic conductors maintain constant $R$ across $V$; non-ohmic conductors show variable dynamic resistance ($dV/dI$).
  • Conductivity measures ease of current flow; resistivity measures opposition.
Common misconceptions
  • Believing resistivity changes when a wire is stretched (resistivity remains constant; resistance changes).
  • Assuming Ohm's law is a fundamental law of nature like Newton's laws.
  • Confusing resistance with resistivity.
Exam focus
  • Numerical problems involving stretching of wires ($R \propto l^2$ or $R \propto 1/A^2$).
  • Color coding of carbon resistors and tolerance values.
  • Temperature coefficient of resistance derivations and units.
  • Graph interpretation of $V$-$I$ characteristics for ohmic and non-ohmic devices.
  • Microscopic derivation of Ohm's law from relaxation time.

Electric Power and Joule's Heating Effect

Electric power quantifies the rate of electrical energy transfer, while Joule's law governs the conversion of electrical energy into thermal energy.

Prerequisites

  • Understanding of work, energy, and power from mechanics.
  • Familiarity with potential difference and electric current.

Definitions and scope

  • Electric Power ($P$) is the rate at which electrical work is done or energy is consumed in an electrical circuit, measured in Watts (W).
  • Joule's Heating Effect states that the heat produced in a resistor is directly proportional to the square of current, resistance, and time ($H = I^2Rt$).

Step-by-step explanation

  • Power formulas: $P = VI = I^2R = \frac{V^2}{R}$.
  • Electrical energy consumed is $E = P \times t = VIt$.
  • Commercial unit of electrical energy is the Kilowatt-hour (kWh), commonly known as a 'unit' of electricity ($1\text{ kWh} = 3.6 \times 10^6\text{ Joules}$).
  • Joule's law of heating: $H = I^2Rt$ Joules, or in calories, $H = \frac{I^2Rt}{4.18}$ cal.
  • Power rating of appliances indicates energy consumed per second at rated voltage (e.g., a 100W bulb consumes 100 Joules per second).
  • Transmission losses in power lines: Power loss is given by $P_{\text{loss}} = I^2R$. To minimize loss, power is transmitted at very high voltage and low current.
  • Electric fuses work on the heating effect; when excessive current flows, the fuse wire melts and breaks the circuit.
  • Filament lamps operate at high temperatures where tungsten glows incandescently in an inert gas atmosphere.

Key points

  • Power formulas: $P = VI$, $P = I^2R$, $P = V^2/R$.
  • 1 Unit = 1 kWh = $3.6 \times 10^6\text{ J}$.
  • Heat generated: $H = I^2Rt$.
  • High voltage transmission reduces $I^2R$ line losses.
  • Fuse wire requires low melting point and high resistivity (typically lead-tin alloy).
  • Tungsten filament has high melting point (approx 3380°C).
Worked examples
  • An electric iron of resistance $50 \Omega$ draws a current of 4 A. Calculate the heat developed in 5 minutes. Solution: $I = 4\text{ A}$, $R = 50 \Omega$, $t = 300\text{ s}$. $H = I^2Rt = (4)^2 \times 50 \times 300 = 16 \times 50 \times 300 = 240,000\text{ Joules} = 240\text{ kJ}$.
  • Calculate the cost of operating five 100W bulbs for 10 hours daily in a month of 30 days, if electricity costs ₹5 per kWh. Solution: Total energy = $(5 \times 100\text{W} \times 10\text{h} \times 30) / 1000 = 150\text{ kWh}$. Cost = $150 \times 5 = \text{₹}750$.

Applications and connections

  • Electric heaters, geysers, toasters, and soldering irons utilizing heating coils.
  • Incandescent lighting and halogen lamps.
  • Circuit protection devices such as fuses and miniature circuit breakers (MCBs).

Advanced points, exceptions and limitations

  • Power transfer theorem: Maximum power is transferred from a source to a load when internal resistance equals load resistance ($r = R$).
  • Skin effect increases AC resistance in high-power transmission cables.
  • Eddy current heating (induction heating) used in industrial metallurgy.

Additional examples

  • Room heater rated 2000W, 230V.
  • Fuse wire melting at 5A rating during a short circuit.
  • Electric kettle boiling water rapidly via immersed heating element.
Important distinctions
  • Power is rate of energy transfer (Watts); energy is total work done over time (Joules or kWh).
  • Series grouping of bulbs shares voltage and reduces total power; parallel grouping maintains rated voltage across each bulb.
  • Fuse wire has low melting point; transmission wires have high conductivity and high melting point.
Common misconceptions
  • Thinking a 100W bulb is brighter than a 60W bulb when connected in series (in series, the 60W bulb has higher resistance and glows brighter).
  • Confusing power rating with actual power consumed when operated at non-rated voltage.
  • Believing electricity is consumed and destroyed rather than converted.
Exam focus
  • Domestic electricity bill calculation problems.
  • Series vs. parallel bulb brightness comparisons.
  • Maximum power transfer theorem condition ($R = r$).
  • Properties of fuse wire materials.
  • Derivation of Joule's heating law.
Concept 3 · application

Domestic Electric Circuits and Safety Devices

Domestic wiring systems utilize parallel distribution networks supplied with AC mains, protected by earthing, fuses, and MCBs.

Prerequisites

  • Understanding of AC supply voltage (220V, 50Hz in India).
  • Basic circuit concepts of series and parallel connections.

Definitions and scope

  • Domestic Circuit is the standard household wiring system delivering electricity from the mains supply to individual appliances in parallel.
  • Earthing (Grounding) is the safety connection of metal appliance bodies to the earth to prevent severe electric shocks.
  • MCB (Miniature Circuit Breaker) is an electromechanical safety device that automatically trips during overload or short circuit.

Step-by-step explanation

  • Household wiring is always connected in parallel so that every appliance receives the full rated voltage (220V) and can be operated independently.
  • Three color-coded wires are used in standard Indian domestic circuits: Live (Red/Brown, carries current), Neutral (Black/Blue, completes circuit), and Earth (Green/Yellow, safety shield).
  • Live wire operates at high potential (220V AC), Neutral is at zero potential (connected to earth at local substation), and Earth is connected deep into the ground via a copper plate.
  • The electric meter measures total energy consumed in kWh; the distribution box contains main switches, fuses, and MCBs.
  • Short circuit occurs when Live and Neutral wires come in direct contact, causing resistance to drop to nearly zero and current to spike dangerously.
  • Overloading happens when too many high-power appliances are plugged into a single socket, drawing current beyond the safe capacity of the wiring.
  • Fuses and MCBs act as breaking mechanisms to disconnect power during faults, preventing electrical fires and equipment damage.
  • Polarity and switch placement: Switches must always be connected to the Live wire so that turning off the appliance disconnects it from high voltage.

Key points

  • Household appliances are connected in parallel.
  • Standard Indian AC supply: 220V, 50Hz.
  • Wire color codes: Live (Red), Neutral (Black), Earth (Green).
  • Switches are placed in the Live wire for safety.
  • Earthing protects users from body leakage current.
  • MCB replaces traditional fuse for automatic resetting and fast action.
Worked examples
  • Explain why connecting appliances in series is unsuitable for domestic wiring. Solution: In series, if one appliance fails, the entire circuit breaks; total voltage is shared unequally; and appliances cannot be operated independently.
  • Calculate the maximum number of 60W bulbs that can safely run on a 5A fuse in a 220V domestic circuit. Solution: Maximum power $P = VI = 220 \times 5 = 1100\text{W}$. Number of bulbs = $1100 / 60 \approx 18$ bulbs.

Applications and connections

  • Residential housing and commercial building electrical installations.
  • Industrial machinery power distribution panels.
  • Earth leakage circuit breakers (ELCB/RCCB) for personnel protection in wet areas.

Advanced points, exceptions and limitations

  • Three-phase supply ($440\text{V}$) used for heavy industrial loads and high-capacity air conditioning.
  • Harmonic distortions in domestic circuits caused by non-linear electronic loads (SMPS, LED drivers).
  • Surge protection devices (SPDs) to protect sensitive electronics against lightning strikes.

Additional examples

  • Operating a refrigerator, television, and ceiling fan simultaneously in different rooms.
  • Connecting the metal body of an electric iron to the green earth pin of a 3-pin plug.
  • Tripping of MCB when a heavy-duty water heater is switched on alongside an AC unit.
Important distinctions
  • Series circuits share voltage and current; parallel circuits maintain constant voltage across all branches.
  • Live wire carries high potential; neutral wire returns current; earth wire carries no normal current.
  • Fuse is a one-time sacrificial component; MCB is reusable and resettable.
Common misconceptions
  • Believing electricity is consumed in the neutral wire.
  • Connecting switches to the neutral wire (leaving the appliance live even when switched off).
  • Assuming earth wire carries return current during normal operation.
Exam focus
  • Color coding standards of domestic wiring in India.
  • Why parallel connection is preferred over series in homes.
  • Function and placement of switches, fuses, and earth pins.
  • Calculations involving safe current ratings and total power loads.
  • Differences between short circuit and overloading.

Magnetic Fields, Biot-Savart Law, Ampere's Law, and Lorentz Force

Electric currents generate magnetic fields, and magnetic fields exert forces on moving charges and current-carrying conductors.

Prerequisites

  • Vector calculus basics (cross product, line integrals).
  • Concept of electric charge and moving current.

Definitions and scope

  • Magnetic Field ($B$) is the region around a magnet or current-carrying conductor where magnetic forces are experienced, measured in Tesla (T).
  • Lorentz Force is the total electromagnetic force acting on a moving charged particle in combined electric and magnetic fields: $\vec{F} = q(\vec{E} + \vec{v} \times \vec{B})$.
  • Biot-Savart Law gives the magnetic field produced by a current element: $d\vec{B} = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi} \frac{I d\vec{l} \times \hat{r}}{r^2}$.

Step-by-step explanation

  • Oersted's discovery proved that electric current produces a magnetic field around it.
  • Right-Hand Thumb Rule gives the direction of the magnetic field around a straight current-carrying conductor.
  • Biot-Savart law states that magnetic field element $dB$ is proportional to current $I$, length $dl$, sine of angle $\theta$, and inversely proportional to square of distance $r$.
  • Ampere's Circuital Law states that the line integral of magnetic field $\vec{B}$ around a closed loop equals $\mu_0$ times the total current passing through the loop: $\oint \vec{B} \cdot d\vec{l} = \mu_0 I_{\text{enclosed}}$.
  • Magnetic field at the center of a circular coil of radius $R$ carrying current $I$: $B = \frac{\mu_0 NI}{2R}$.
  • Magnetic field inside a long solenoid: $B = \mu_0 n I$, where $n$ is turns per unit length.
  • Force on a moving charge in a magnetic field: $\vec{F} = q(\vec{v} \times \vec{B})$, magnitude $F = qvB\sin\theta$.
  • Force on a current-carrying conductor of length $l$ in a uniform magnetic field: $\vec{F} = I(\vec{l} \times \vec{B})$, magnitude $F = IlB\sin\theta$.

Key points

  • SI unit of magnetic field is Tesla (T) or Weber/m² (1 Gauss = $10^{-4}$ T).
  • Biot-Savart formula: $dB = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi} \frac{I dl \sin\theta}{r^2}$.
  • Ampere's law: $\oint B \cdot dl = \mu_0 I$.
  • Solenoid magnetic field: $B = \mu_0 n I$.
  • Magnetic force on moving charge: $F = qvB\sin\theta$ (zero when moving parallel to field).
  • Fleming's Left-Hand Rule determines force direction on a current-carrying conductor.
Worked examples
  • Calculate the magnetic field at the center of a circular coil of 100 turns, radius 0.1 m, carrying a current of 5 A. Given $\mu_0 = 4\pi \times 10^{-7} \text{ T}\cdot\text{m/A}$. Solution: $B = \frac{\mu_0 NI}{2R} = \frac{4\pi \times 10^{-7} \times 100 \times 5}{2 \times 0.1} = \frac{2\pi \times 10^{-5}}{0.2} = \pi \times 10^{-4} \text{ T} \approx 3.14 \times 10^{-4} \text{ T}$.
  • A straight wire of length 0.5 m carries a current of 2 A and is placed perpendicular to a uniform magnetic field of 0.4 T. Calculate the magnetic force. Solution: $F = IlB\sin(90^\circ) = 2 \times 0.5 \times 0.4 \times 1 = 0.4\text{ N}$.

Applications and connections

  • Electric motors operating on magnetic force on current-carrying coils.
  • Particle accelerators and cyclotrons steering charged particle beams.
  • Moving coil galvanometers, ammeters, and voltmeters.

Advanced points, exceptions and limitations

  • Cyclotron frequency: $f = \frac{qB}{2\pi m}$, independent of particle velocity (for non-relativistic speeds).
  • Hall effect measures charge carrier concentration and sign in semiconductors.
  • Toroids provide uniform confinement of magnetic fields in toroidal geometry.

Additional examples

  • Deflection of electron beam in a cathode ray tube (CRT) by magnetic coils.
  • Magnetic levitation (Maglev) trains utilizing Lorentz force.
  • Force between two parallel current-carrying wires defining the Ampere standard.
Important distinctions
  • Electric field exerts force parallel/anti-parallel to field; magnetic field exerts force perpendicular to both velocity and field.
  • Electric force does work on stationary and moving charges; magnetic force does zero work on moving charges (since $\vec{F} \perp \vec{v}$).
  • Biot-Savart law is analogous to Coulomb's law in electrostatics.
Common misconceptions
  • Believing a magnetic field exerts a force on a stationary charge.
  • Assuming magnetic field lines can cross each other.
  • Confusing Fleming's Left-Hand Rule (motor rule) with Fleming's Right-Hand Rule (generator rule).
Exam focus
  • Derivations of magnetic field using Biot-Savart law (circular loop axis, straight wire).
  • Ampere's law applications for long wire, solenoid, and toroid.
  • Radius and time period of charged particle in a uniform magnetic field ($r = \frac{mv}{qB}$).
  • Torque on a current loop in a magnetic field ($\tau = NIAB\sin\theta$).
  • Numerical problems on Fleming's Left-Hand Rule.
Concept 5 · advanced

Electromagnetic Induction, Faraday's Law, Lenz's Law, and Transformers

Changing magnetic flux induces an electromotive force (EMF) in a circuit, a principle harnessed in generators and transformers.

Prerequisites

  • Concept of magnetic flux ($\Phi_B = \vec{B} \cdot \vec{A}$).
  • Understanding of electromagnetic fields and induced currents.

Definitions and scope

  • Electromagnetic Induction is the generation of an electric current and EMF in a circuit due to a changing magnetic flux linked with it.
  • Faraday's Law of Induction states that the magnitude of induced EMF is directly proportional to the rate of change of magnetic flux linked with the circuit.
  • A Transformer is a static electrical device that transfers electrical energy between two or more circuits through electromagnetic induction, altering voltage levels.

Step-by-step explanation

  • Magnetic flux linkage $\Phi_B = B A \cos\theta$, measured in Weber (Wb) or Tesla-meter².
  • Faraday's Law equation: $E = -N \frac{d\Phi_B}{dt}$, where $N$ is the number of turns.
  • Lenz's Law states that the polarity of induced EMF is such that it opposes the very cause (change in flux) that produces it. This embodies the law of conservation of energy.
  • Motional EMF: When a conductor of length $l$ moves with velocity $v$ perpendicular to magnetic field $B$, induced EMF is $E = Blv$.
  • Eddy currents are loops of electrical current induced within conductors by changing magnetic fields, causing damping and heating.
  • Transformer Principle: Operates solely on AC. Alternating current in the primary coil creates a changing magnetic flux in the soft iron core, inducing an AC EMF in the secondary coil.
  • Transformer Ratio equation: $\frac{V_s}{V_p} = \frac{N_s}{N_p} = \frac{I_p}{I_s} = k$, where $k$ is the transformation ratio.
  • Step-up transformer increases voltage ($N_s > N_p, k > 1$); step-down transformer decreases voltage ($N_s < N_p, k < 1$).

Key points

  • Faraday's law: $E = -N \frac{d\Phi}{dt}$.
  • Lenz's law provides the negative sign in Faraday's law, ensuring conservation of energy.
  • Motional EMF formula: $E = Blv$.
  • Transformers work only with AC; DC produces constant flux with zero induced EMF.
  • Ideal transformer efficiency: $P_{\text{in}} = P_{\text{out}}$ ($V_p I_p = V_s I_s$).
  • Core losses in transformers include hysteresis loss and eddy current loss (minimized by laminated soft iron core).
Worked examples
  • A transformer has 500 primary turns and 5000 secondary turns. If the primary voltage is 220V, calculate the secondary voltage. Solution: $\frac{V_s}{V_p} = \frac{N_s}{N_p} \Rightarrow V_s = 220 \times \frac{5000}{500} = 220 \times 10 = 2200\text{ V}$.
  • Magnetic flux linked with a coil changes from $5\text{ Wb}$ to $1\text{ Wb}$ in $0.2\text{ s}$. Find the induced EMF if the coil has 10 turns. Solution: $E = -N \frac{\Delta \Phi}{\Delta t} = -10 \times \frac{1 - 5}{0.2} = -10 \times \frac{-4}{0.2} = +200\text{ V}$.

Applications and connections

  • AC generators and alternators producing commercial electrical power.
  • Power transmission grids utilizing step-up and step-down transformers.
  • Induction cooktops, metal detectors, and magnetic braking systems in trains.

Advanced points, exceptions and limitations

  • Self-inductance ($L$) and mutual inductance ($M$) define back-EMF and transformer coupling.
  • Energy stored in an inductor: $U = \frac{1}{2} L I^2$.
  • Skin effect and proximity losses in high-frequency transformer windings.

Additional examples

  • Mobile phone chargers containing step-down transformers and rectifiers.
  • Power grid substations stepping down 132kV transmission voltage to 220V for residential use.
  • Dynamic braking in electric trains using eddy current damping.
Important distinctions
  • Step-up transformer increases voltage and decreases current; step-down transformer decreases voltage and increases current.
  • Self-induction involves a single coil opposing current changes; mutual induction involves two magnetically linked coils.
  • AC transformers operate via changing flux; DC circuits require switching/chopper circuits to induce EMF.
Common misconceptions
  • Believing transformers can step up DC voltage.
  • Assuming a transformer generates new electrical energy (it only transforms voltage/current; input power equals output power minus losses).
  • Thinking Lenz's law violates the law of conservation of energy.
Exam focus
  • Numerical problems on transformer turns ratio, voltage, and current relationships.
  • Causes of energy losses in transformers (copper loss, eddy current loss, hysteresis loss, flux leakage) and remedies.
  • Derivation of motional EMF ($E = Blv$).
  • Conceptual questions on Lenz's law direction of induced current.
  • Self-inductance of a long solenoid ($L = \mu_0 n^2 A l$).

Question 27Case-based

An electron enters a uniform magnetic field moving parallel to the magnetic field lines. What is the path of the electron?

Since velocity is parallel to the magnetic field ($\theta = 0^\circ$), magnetic force $F = qvB\sin(0^\circ) = 0$, so the electron continues in a straight line.

Question 28Short answer

How much work is done by a magnetic field on a moving charged particle?

Zero

Magnetic force is always perpendicular to velocity, so work done ($W = \vec{F} \cdot \vec{s}$) is zero.

Question 29MCQ

Which law states that the induced EMF opposes the change in magnetic flux producing it?

Lenz's law determines the direction of induced EMF, stating it opposes the change in flux responsible for its creation.

Question 30Assertion–Reason

Reason (R): DC produces a constant magnetic flux, resulting in zero rate of change of flux and no induced secondary EMF.

Transformers require changing flux ($d\Phi/dt \neq 0$) according to Faraday's law, which only AC can provide.

Question 31True / False

In an ideal step-up transformer, the secondary voltage is greater than the primary voltage, and secondary current is greater than primary current.

In a step-up transformer, voltage increases while current decreases to conserve power ($V_pI_p = V_sI_s$). Secondary current is smaller, not greater.

Question 34Case-based

A step-down transformer operates on a 220V mains supply to provide 11V for a doorbell. If the primary has 2000 turns, how many turns are in the secondary coil?

$N_s = N_p \times (V_s / V_p) = 2000 \times (11 / 220) = 2000 \times (1/20) = 100\text{ turns}$.

Question 35Short answer

What physical conservation law is embodied by Lenz's Law in electromagnetic induction?

Law of conservation of energy

Lenz's law embodies the conservation of energy, ensuring work must be done to move a magnet through a coil against induced magnetic opposition.

Rapid revision: Electricity & Magnetism

Review every concept point-wise before attempting the quiz and timed test.

Concept 1

Ohm's Law, Resistance, and Resistivity

Definition and scope

  • Ohm's law states $V = IR$ under constant physical conditions.
  • Resistivity $\rho$ is material-dependent and independent of dimensions.
  • Metals exhibit positive temperature coefficients of resistance.

Must remember

  • $R = \rho \frac{l}{A}$
  • Microscopic Ohm's law: $\vec{J} = \sigma \vec{E}$
  • Stretching wire of length $l_1$ to $l_2$ changes resistance as $R \propto l^2$.
  • SI unit of resistance is Ohm ($\Omega$); resistivity is $\Omega\cdot\text{m}$.
  • Non-ohmic devices do not follow linear $V$-$I$ curves.

Concept-specific exam traps

  • Assuming resistivity changes when wire dimensions are altered.
  • Applying Ohm's law to non-linear semiconductor diodes without dynamic resistance consideration.
Concept 2

Electric Power and Joule's Heating Effect

Definition and scope

  • Electric power $P = VI = I^2R = V^2/R$.
  • Commercial unit of energy is kWh (1 unit = $3.6 \times 10^6$ J).
  • Joule's heating law gives heat generated $H = I^2Rt$.

Must remember

  • High voltage power transmission minimizes $I^2R$ line losses.
  • Fuse wire requires low melting point and high resistivity.
  • Maximum power transfer theorem requires load resistance to equal internal resistance ($R = r$).
  • Tungsten filament lamps operate at high incandescence.
  • Series bulb brightness depends on resistance, not wattage rating.

Concept-specific exam traps

  • Assuming a 100W bulb always emits 100W regardless of applied voltage.
  • Confusing electrical power units (Watts) with electrical energy units (kWh).
Concept 3

Domestic Electric Circuits and Safety Devices

Definition and scope

  • Domestic circuits use parallel wiring to maintain constant voltage.
  • Color codes: Live (Red), Neutral (Black), Earth (Green).
  • Switches must be connected to the Live wire.

Must remember

  • Indian domestic supply is 220V AC at 50Hz.
  • Earthing protects users from body leakage shock.
  • MCBs provide automated trip protection against overloads and short circuits.
  • Short circuit occurs when Live and Neutral wires touch directly.
  • Overloading occurs when total connected load exceeds safe wire capacity.

Concept-specific exam traps

  • Believing appliances in homes are wired in series.
  • Placing switches in the neutral wire instead of the live wire.
Concept 4

Magnetic Fields, Biot-Savart Law, Ampere's Law, and Lorentz Force

Definition and scope

  • Magnetic field $B$ is measured in Tesla (T).
  • Biot-Savart law gives magnetic field due to current element.
  • Lorentz force accounts for combined electric and magnetic forces on moving charges.

Must remember

  • Ampere's Circuital Law: $\oint B \cdot dl = \mu_0 I$.
  • Solenoid magnetic field: $B = \mu_0 n I$.
  • Magnetic force on moving charge: $F = qvB\sin\theta$.
  • Magnetic force on current wire: $F = IlB\sin\theta$.
  • Magnetic force does zero work on moving charges.

Concept-specific exam traps

  • Confusing Fleming's Left-Hand Rule (motors) with Right-Hand Rule (generators).
  • Assuming magnetic force acts on stationary electric charges.
Concept 5

Electromagnetic Induction, Faraday's Law, Lenz's Law, and Transformers

Definition and scope

  • Faraday's law: Induced EMF $E = -N \frac{d\Phi}{dt}$.
  • Lenz's law dictates opposition to flux change (conservation of energy).
  • Transformer transfers AC energy via electromagnetic induction.

Must remember

  • Motional EMF formula: $E = Blv$.
  • Transformer ratio: $\frac{V_s}{V_p} = \frac{N_s}{N_p} = \frac{I_p}{I_s}$.
  • Transformers operate exclusively on AC supply.
  • Laminated soft iron cores minimize eddy current losses.
  • Step-up transformers increase voltage and decrease current.

Concept-specific exam traps

  • Believing transformers can step up DC voltage.
  • Forgetting the negative sign in Faraday's law when determining induced EMF direction.

Quick facts

  • Ohm's law ($V = IR$) applies only under constant physical conditions like temperature.
  • Resistivity depends strictly on material and temperature, not on length or cross-sectional area.
  • Commercial unit of electrical energy is 1 kWh ($3.6 \times 10^6$ Joules).
  • Domestic electrical circuits in India operate at 220V AC and 50Hz frequency.
  • Household appliances are connected in parallel to ensure independent operation and rated voltage.
  • Fuse wire is made of lead-tin alloy, featuring high resistivity and low melting point.
  • Magnetic force on a moving charged particle does zero work because force is perpendicular to velocity.
  • Transformers operate exclusively on alternating current (AC) through mutual induction.

Important distinctions

ResistancevsResistivity
  • Resistance depends on length, cross-sectional area, material, and temperature; resistivity depends only on material and temperature.
  • Unit of resistance is Ohm ($\Omega$); unit of resistivity is Ohm-meter ($\Omega\cdot\text{m}$).
Fleming's Left-Hand RulevsFleming's Right-Hand Rule
  • Left-Hand Rule is used for electric motors to find the direction of magnetic force on a current-carrying conductor.
  • Right-Hand Rule is used for electric generators to find the direction of induced current in a moving conductor.
Step-Up TransformervsStep-Down Transformer
  • Step-up transformer increases voltage ($N_s > N_p$) and decreases current.
  • Step-down transformer decreases voltage ($N_s < N_p$) and increases current.

Memory hooks

  • VIR: Voltage = Current $\times$ Resistance.
  • ELF: Electric field $\times$ Length = Force (Lorentz).
  • MOTORS use Left hand; GENERATORS use Right hand.

Exam traps and cautions

  • Do not assume resistivity changes when stretching a wire; volume remains constant, changing $R$ but not $\rho$.
  • Do not forget that transformers are strictly AC devices; DC causes zero secondary EMF.
  • Remember that household switches must always be wired into the live wire, never the neutral wire.
  • Recognize that magnetic force does zero work on a moving charge because force is perpendicular to displacement.

Question 1MCQ

If a copper wire is stretched to increase its length by 10%, what is the approximate percentage increase in its resistance?

An electric heater rated 1000W is connected to a 250V supply. What is the resistance of its heating element?

Using $P = V^2 / R$, we get $R = V^2 / P = (250)^2 / 1000 = 62500 / 1000 = 62.5 \Omega$.

Which wire in a domestic 3-pin plug is connected to the metallic casing of an appliance for user safety?

Question 4MCQ

A proton and an alpha particle with equal kinetic energies enter a uniform magnetic field perpendicular to the field lines. What is the ratio of their orbit radii ($r_{\text{proton}} / r_{\text{alpha}}$)?

Radius $r = \frac{\sqrt{2mK}}{qB}$. Since $K$ and $B$ are equal, $r \propto \frac{\sqrt{m}}{q}$. Proton mass $m$, charge $e$; alpha mass $4m$, charge $2e$. Ratio = $(\sqrt{m}/e) / (\sqrt{4m}/2e) = 1 / (2/2) = 1:1$ wait. Let's recalculate: $\sqrt{4m}/2e = 2\sqrt{m}/2e = \sqrt{m}/e$, so ratio is 1:1? Alpha particle has mass $4m$ and charge $2e$. $\sqrt{4m}/(2e) = 2\sqrt{m}/(2e) = \sqrt{m}/e$. Thus, radii are equal, ratio is 1:1.

Question 5MCQ

The primary coil of an ideal transformer has 200 turns and the secondary has 1000 turns. If the power input is 10 kW at 200V, what is the secondary current?
