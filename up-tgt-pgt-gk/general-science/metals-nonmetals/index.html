Concept 1 · foundation

Physical and Chemical Properties of Metals and Non-Metals

Elements are classified into metals and non-metals based on their distinctive physical characteristics and chemical bonding tendencies.

Prerequisites

  • Basic understanding of elements, atoms, and the periodic table structure.
  • Concept of valence electrons and octet rule.

Definitions and scope

  • Metals are elements that readily lose electrons to form positive ions (cations) and possess metallic luster, high thermal and electrical conductivity.
  • Non-metals are elements that tend to gain or share electrons, exhibiting poor electrical and thermal conductivity in their solid states.

Step-by-step explanation

  • Metals are generally solids at room temperature, with the notable exception of mercury, which is a liquid.
  • Malleability is the property allowing metals to be beaten into thin sheets; gold and silver are the most malleable metals.
  • Ductility is the property enabling metals to be drawn into thin wires; 1 gram of gold can be drawn into a wire approximately 2 kilometers long.
  • Metals possess high melting and boiling points due to strong metallic bonding, though cesium and gallium have very low melting points.
  • Non-metals exist in all three states of matter at room temperature: solids (carbon, sulfur), liquids (bromine), and gases (oxygen, nitrogen).
  • Non-metals are non-malleable and brittle when solid; they shatter upon hammering rather than deforming.
  • Chemical property difference: Metals form basic or amphoteric oxides when reacting with oxygen (e.g., MgO, Al2O3).
  • Chemical property difference: Non-metals form acidic or neutral oxides when reacting with oxygen (e.g., CO2, SO2, H2O).

Key points

  • Mercury is the only liquid metal at standard temperature and pressure.
  • Bromine is the only liquid non-metal at standard temperature and pressure.
  • Graphite is a non-metal that is a good conductor of electricity.
  • Diamond, an allotrope of carbon, is the hardest naturally occurring substance.
  • Sodium and potassium are soft metals that can be cut with a knife.
  • Metals are sonorous, producing a ringing sound when struck.
Worked examples
  • Example 1: Burning Magnesium ribbon in air produces a dazzling white flame and forms white basic magnesium oxide powder (2Mg + O2 -> 2MgO).
  • Example 2: Burning sulfur in air produces sulfur dioxide gas, which dissolves in water to form sulfurous acid, turning blue litmus red (S + O2 -> SO2; SO2 + H2O -> H2SO3).

Applications and connections

  • Copper and aluminum are used in electrical wiring due to high conductivity.
  • Iron and steel are used in construction due to high tensile strength.
  • Sulfur is utilized in the vulcanization of rubber and manufacture of sulfuric acid.

Advanced points, exceptions and limitations

  • Metalloids (semiconductors like silicon and germanium) exhibit intermediate properties between metals and non-metals.
  • Metallic bonding involves a 'sea of delocalized electrons' surrounding positive metal kernels, explaining electrical conductivity.
  • Exceptions to trends include diamond's exceptionally high thermal conductivity despite being a non-metal.

Additional examples

  • Metals: Iron, Copper, Gold, Silver.
  • Non-metals: Oxygen, Nitrogen, Sulfur, Phosphorus.
  • Metalloids: Silicon, Arsenic, Antimony.
Important distinctions
  • Metals have 1 to 3 valence electrons; non-metals usually have 4 to 8 valence electrons.
  • Metals act as reducing agents (electron donors); non-metals act as oxidizing agents (electron acceptors).
  • Metal oxides react with water to form bases; non-metal oxides react with water to form acids.
Common misconceptions
  • All metals are hard (False: Sodium and potassium are soft).
  • All non-metals are poor conductors (False: Graphite conducts electricity).
  • All metal oxides are basic (False: Some like Al2O3 and ZnO are amphoteric).
Exam focus
  • Identify exceptions like liquid metal (Hg) and liquid non-metal (Br).
  • Remember amphoteric oxides (Al2O3, ZnO).
  • Distinguish chemical nature of oxides formed by metals vs non-metals.
  • Recall electrical conductivity of graphite.
  • Understand malleability and ductility trends.
Concept 2 · core

Reactivity Series of Metals and Displacement Reactions

The reactivity series arranges metals in order of decreasing chemical reactivity, predicting displacement reactions and extraction methods.

Prerequisites

  • Oxidation and reduction definitions (loss and gain of electrons).
  • Concept of chemical reactivity and displacement.

Definitions and scope

  • Reactivity series (Activity series) is a vertical arrangement of metals in decreasing order of their reactivity.
  • Displacement reaction is a chemical reaction where a more reactive metal displaces a less reactive metal from its salt solution.

Step-by-step explanation

  • The standard reactivity series sequence from top to bottom: K > Na > Ca > Mg > Al > Zn > Fe > Pb > (H) > Cu > Hg > Ag > Au.
  • Potassium and sodium are extremely reactive, reacting vigorously with cold water and air, thus stored under kerosene.
  • Calcium and magnesium react less vigorously with water; magnesium floats due to hydrogen bubbles adhering to its surface.
  • Zinc, iron, and lead react with steam or dilute acids (HCl, H2SO4) to evolve hydrogen gas.
  • Hydrogen is included in the series as a reference point; metals above hydrogen displace it from dilute acids, while metals below do not.
  • Copper, mercury, silver, and gold do not liberate hydrogen from dilute acids because they are less reactive than hydrogen.
  • In a displacement reaction, a metal higher in the series loses electrons more readily and reduces the metallic ion of the lower metal.
  • Extraction technique correlation: Highly reactive metals (top) are extracted by electrolytic reduction; moderately reactive metals by carbon reduction; unreactive metals (bottom) are found native or extracted by thermal decomposition.

Key points

  • Potassium (K) is the most reactive metal in the standard series.
  • Gold (Au) is the least reactive metal, found in native uncombined state.
  • Zinc displaces copper from copper sulfate solution, turning the blue solution colorless/greenish.
  • Copper cannot displace iron from iron sulfate solution.
  • Iron displaces copper, forming a reddish-brown copper coating on an iron nail.
  • Metals above hydrogen react with dilute acids to produce hydrogen gas.
Worked examples
  • Example 1: Fe(s) + CuSO4(aq) -> FeSO4(aq) + Cu(s). Iron displaces copper because iron is more reactive than copper.
  • Example 2: Zn(s) + H2SO4(aq) -> ZnSO4(aq) + H2(g). Zinc reacts with dilute sulfuric acid to release hydrogen gas.

Applications and connections

  • Predicting whether a chemical reaction will occur when mixing metals and metal salt solutions.
  • Determining industrial metallurgical extraction routes based on ore stability.
  • Preventing galvanic corrosion by attaching sacrificial anodes (more reactive metals like zinc) to steel structures.

Advanced points, exceptions and limitations

  • Standard electrode potentials (E°) quantitatively determine the exact electrochemical reactivity series based on Gibbs free energy change.
  • Nitric acid (HNO3) is a strong oxidizing agent; when reacting with most metals (except Mg and Mn), it produces nitrogen oxides instead of hydrogen gas.
  • Anomalies in reduction potentials can occur under non-standard conditions or complexing agent presence.

Additional examples

  • Magnesium reacting with steam: Mg + H2O(g) -> MgO + H2.
  • Silver nitrate reacting with copper: Cu + 2AgNO3 -> Cu(NO3)2 + 2Ag.
  • Sodium reacting vigorously with cold water: 2Na + 2H2O -> 2NaOH + H2 + heat.
Important distinctions
  • More reactive metals have more negative standard reduction potentials; less reactive metals have positive or less negative potentials.
  • Top metals require electrolysis for extraction; middle metals require chemical reducing agents like carbon/CO.
  • Metals above hydrogen react with acids; metals below hydrogen do not react with non-oxidizing acids.
Common misconceptions
  • Copper reacts with dilute hydrochloric acid to produce hydrogen gas (False: Copper is below hydrogen).
  • Silver is more reactive than iron because silver is more precious (False: Iron is far more reactive).
  • All metals react with water at room temperature (False: Only K, Na, and Ca react with cold water; others require steam or do not react).
Exam focus
  • Memorize the exact order of K, Na, Ca, Mg, Al, Zn, Fe, Pb, H, Cu, Hg, Ag, Au.
  • Identify color changes in displacement reactions (e.g., CuSO4 blue to colorless).
  • Recall why nitric acid does not evolve H2 with most metals.
  • Relate reactivity position to extraction method.
  • Identify which metals react with cold water, steam, and dilute acids.
Concept 3 · application

Corrosion of Metals and Prevention Methods

Corrosion is the gradual degradation of metals due to chemical or electrochemical reactions with environmental moisture, oxygen, and acids.

Prerequisites

  • Oxidation-reduction processes.
  • Understanding of atmospheric gases and moisture.

Definitions and scope

  • Corrosion is an electrochemical process where a metal is oxidized by substances in the environment, forming stable chemical compounds like oxides, carbonates, or sulfides.
  • Rusting is a specific type of corrosion involving the formation of hydrated iron(III) oxide on iron surfaces in the presence of both oxygen and moisture.

Step-by-step explanation

  • Rusting of iron requires the simultaneous presence of both oxygen (O2) and moisture (H2O); dry air or oxygen-free water alone does not cause rust.
  • Chemically, rust is represented by the formula Fe2O3·xH2O (hydrated ferric oxide).
  • Corrosion of copper leads to the formation of a green coating called basic copper carbonate [CuCO3·Cu(OH)2], due to attack by moist CO2 and air.
  • Silver articles turn black over time due to tarnishing caused by the formation of silver sulfide (Ag2S) upon reacting with hydrogen sulfide (H2S) traces in air.
  • Unlike iron rust, which is flaky and porous (allowing continuous deeper rusting), aluminum forms a thin, protective aluminum oxide (Al2O3) layer that passivates the metal against further corrosion.
  • Barrier protection methods prevent contact between the metal surface and atmospheric moisture/oxygen using paint, oil, grease, or varnish.
  • Sacrificial protection involves coating a susceptible metal (like iron) with a more reactive metal (like zinc), a process known as galvanization.
  • Cathodic protection connects the metal structure to a more reactive sacrificial anode (such as magnesium or zinc blocks) that corrodes instead.

Key points

  • Rusting requires both oxygen and water vapor.
  • Galvanization is the coating of iron or steel with a protective layer of zinc.
  • Green coating on copper is a mixture of copper carbonate and copper hydroxide.
  • Blackening of silver is due to silver sulfide formation from atmospheric H2S.
  • Tinning is coating copper/brass vessels with tin to prevent toxic green verdigris formation.
  • Alloying (e.g., stainless steel) alters internal atomic composition to prevent corrosion.
Worked examples
  • Example 1: In an iron nail experiment, a nail in a test tube with anhydrous calcium chloride (moisture absorber) and oil-sealed boiled water does not rust, proving both O2 and H2O are necessary.
  • Example 2: Galvanized iron water pipes resist rusting for decades because zinc acts as a sacrificial anode even if the surface coating is scratched.

Applications and connections

  • Automobile bodies are galvanized and painted to withstand weathering.
  • Iron bridges and pillars are painted regularly and protected with anti-corrosive primers.
  • Underground oil pipelines and ship hulls utilize sacrificial zinc/magnesium anodes.

Advanced points, exceptions and limitations

  • Corrosion is fundamentally an electrochemical cell formation where anodic regions undergo oxidation (Fe -> Fe2+ + 2e-) and cathodic regions undergo oxygen reduction.
  • Passivation is the spontaneous formation of a hard, non-reactive surface film (seen in stainless steel due to chromium oxide, and aluminum due to alumina).
  • Microbial induced corrosion (MIC) involves sulfate-reducing bacteria accelerating pipeline degradation.

Additional examples

  • Iron rusting outdoors in rainy seasons.
  • Statue of Liberty turning green due to copper corrosion over decades.
  • Silver cutlery turning dark when exposed to sulfur-containing foods or polluted air.
Important distinctions
  • Rusting is specific to iron/steel; corrosion applies to all metals (copper, silver, zinc, aluminum).
  • Iron rust is flaky and accelerates destruction; aluminum oxide layer is adherent and protects the metal.
  • Galvanization uses zinc; tinning uses tin for protective coating.
Common misconceptions
  • Iron rusts in pure dry oxygen (False: Moisture/water is strictly required).
  • Corrosion only affects iron (False: Copper, silver, and lead also corrode).
  • Painting stops corrosion permanently under all conditions (False: If moisture penetrates beneath the paint, rusting continues unseen).
Exam focus
  • Chemical formula of rust: Fe2O3·xH2O.
  • Composition of green coating on copper: CuCO3·Cu(OH)2.
  • Reason for silver turning black: Ag2S (Silver sulfide).
  • Define galvanization and sacrificial protection.
  • Recall that both oxygen and water are essential for iron rusting.
Concept 4 · advanced

Alloys: Brass, Bronze, Stainless Steel, and Amalgams

Alloys are homogeneous solid solutions of two or more metals, or a metal and a non-metal, designed to enhance desirable mechanical and chemical properties.

Prerequisites

  • Concept of mixtures and solutions.
  • Basic metallic bonding and crystal lattice structures.

Definitions and scope

  • An alloy is a solid solution or intimate mixture composed of two or more elements, where at least one is a metal.
  • An amalgam is a specific alloy in which mercury is one of the constituent metals.

Step-by-step explanation

  • Alloying is performed to enhance hardness, tensile strength, corrosion resistance, and to modify melting points compared to pure metals.
  • Pure metals are often soft and malleable; introducing foreign atoms of different sizes disrupts the regular lattice layers, preventing them from sliding over one another easily (explaining increased hardness).
  • Brass is an alloy of Copper (Cu) and Zinc (Zn); typically containing 60-80% copper and 20-40% zinc. It is hard, corrosion-resistant, and golden-yellow.
  • Bronze is an alloy of Copper (Cu) and Tin (Sn); typically containing ~88% copper and ~12% tin. It is extremely tough, durable, and resistant to marine corrosion.
  • Stainless Steel is an alloy of Iron (Fe), Chromium (Cr), Nickel (Ni), and Carbon (C). Chromium (typically 10-20%) provides exceptional corrosion resistance by forming a passive surface oxide film.
  • Solder is an alloy of Lead (Pb) and Tin (Sn) with a low melting point, used extensively for soldering electrical circuits.
  • Duralumin is an alloy of Aluminum (Al), Copper (Cu), Magnesium (Mg), and Manganese (Mn), known for its lightweight strength and used in aircraft construction.
  • Amalgams: Dental amalgam is an alloy of mercury with silver, tin, and copper. Sodium amalgam is used as a reducing agent in organic synthesis.

Key points

  • Brass composition: Copper + Zinc (Cu + Zn).
  • Bronze composition: Copper + Tin (Cu + Sn).
  • Stainless steel composition: Iron + Chromium + Nickel + Carbon (Fe + Cr + Ni + C).
  • Solder composition: Lead + Tin (Pb + Sn).
  • Gold is alloyed with copper or silver (measured in carats; 24 carat is pure gold, 22 carat means 22 parts gold and 2 parts copper/silver) to increase hardness for jewelry.
  • Amalgams must contain mercury as one of the constituent elements.
Worked examples
  • Example 1: Calculating percentage composition: A sample of 100g brass containing 70g Cu and 30g Zn has a 70% copper content.
  • Example 2: Solder has a low melting point (~183°C) compared to pure lead (327°C) or pure tin (232°C), making it ideal for joining electrical wires without damaging components.

Applications and connections

  • Brass is used for manufacturing musical instruments, screws, hardware, and cartridge cases.
  • Bronze is used for making statues, medals, coins, and ship propellers.
  • Stainless steel is used for surgical instruments, cutlery, kitchen sinks, and chemical tanks.
  • Solder is used in electronics assembly for joining wires and components on PCB boards.

Advanced points, exceptions and limitations

  • Substitution vs Interstitial alloys: In substitutional alloys (like brass), solute atoms replace solvent atoms in the lattice. In interstitial alloys (like steel), smaller non-metal atoms (carbon) occupy spaces (interstices) between larger metal atoms.
  • Hume-Rothery rules govern solid solubility in binary metallic alloys based on atomic size, crystal structure, and electronegativity.
  • Shape memory alloys (e.g., Nitinol - Nickel-Titanium) can revert to their original shape when heated after deformation.

Additional examples

  • Brass door knobs and water taps.
  • Bronze Olympic medals and ancient cannons.
  • Stainless steel surgical scalpels and kitchen utensils.
Important distinctions
  • Brass contains zinc alongside copper; bronze contains tin alongside copper.
  • Pure iron rusts rapidly; stainless steel (containing chromium) resists rust entirely.
  • Alloys generally have lower electrical conductivity and lower melting points than their constituent pure metals.
Common misconceptions
  • Bronze is an alloy of copper and zinc (False: Copper and tin; copper + zinc is brass).
  • Stainless steel never stains or corrodes under any condition (False: It resists corrosion well, but can stain in harsh chemical environments like concentrated acids or prolonged saltwater exposure).
  • Alloys are chemical compounds with fixed stoichiometric formulas (False: Alloys are solid solutions and can have variable compositions within limits).
Exam focus
  • Direct recall of exact alloy compositions (Brass = Cu+Zn; Bronze = Cu+Sn; Stainless Steel = Fe+Cr+Ni+C).
  • Purpose of adding carbon to iron to make steel.
  • Definition and examples of amalgams.
  • Why gold is alloyed (carat system).
  • Properties and uses of solder.

Rapid revision: Metals, Non-Metals & Alloys

Review every concept point-wise before attempting the quiz and timed test.

Concept 1

Physical and Chemical Properties of Metals and Non-Metals

Definition and scope

  • Metals form cations and basic oxides, exhibit luster and high conductivity.
  • Non-metals form anions or covalent bonds, acidic/neutral oxides, and lack luster.
  • Metalloids exhibit intermediate properties.

Must remember

  • Mercury is liquid metal; Bromine is liquid non-metal.
  • Graphite conducts electricity.
  • Diamond is the hardest natural substance.
  • Al2O3 and ZnO are amphoteric oxides.
  • Sodium and potassium are soft metals.

Concept-specific exam traps

  • Assuming all non-metals are poor conductors (forgetting graphite).
  • Assuming all metal oxides are basic (forgetting amphoteric ones).
Concept 2

Reactivity Series of Metals and Displacement Reactions

Definition and scope

  • Reactivity series is descending order of metal activity.
  • Displacement reactions occur when a more reactive metal displaces a less reactive one.
  • Hydrogen acts as the reference boundary for acid reactivity.

Must remember

  • Order: K > Na > Ca > Mg > Al > Zn > Fe > Pb > (H) > Cu > Hg > Ag > Au.
  • Potassium and sodium react with cold water.
  • Metals above hydrogen liberate H2 gas with dilute acids.
  • Copper, silver, gold do not react with dilute acids.
  • Iron displaces copper from CuSO4 solution.

Concept-specific exam traps

  • Thinking copper reacts with HCl to release hydrogen gas.
  • Confusing the order of zinc and iron in the series.
Concept 3

Corrosion of Metals and Prevention Methods

Definition and scope

  • Corrosion is electrochemical degradation of metals.
  • Rusting requires both moisture and oxygen.
  • Galvanization protects iron with a zinc coating.

Must remember

  • Rust formula: Fe2O3·xH2O.
  • Green coating on copper: CuCO3·Cu(OH)2.
  • Blackening of silver: Ag2S.
  • Aluminum forms a protective Al2O3 layer.
  • Sacrificial protection uses more reactive metals.

Concept-specific exam traps

  • Believing iron rusts in dry oxygen alone without water.
  • Confusing silver tarnish (sulfide) with copper corrosion (carbonate).
Concept 4

Alloys: Brass, Bronze, Stainless Steel, and Amalgams

Definition and scope

  • Alloys are solid solutions of metals or metal/non-metal.
  • Amalgams contain mercury.
  • Alloying increases hardness and corrosion resistance.

Must remember

  • Brass = Copper + Zinc.
  • Bronze = Copper + Tin.
  • Stainless Steel = Iron + Chromium + Nickel + Carbon.
  • Solder = Lead + Tin.
  • Dental amalgam contains mercury.

Concept-specific exam traps

  • Swapping the components of brass and bronze (Brass has Zinc, Bronze has Tin).
  • Assuming alloys are pure chemical compounds with fixed formulas.

Quick facts

  • Mercury is the only metal liquid at room temperature.
  • Bromine is the only non-metal liquid at room temperature.
  • Gold and silver are the most ductile and malleable metals.
  • Galvanization involves coating iron with zinc.
  • Brass is an alloy of copper and zinc.
  • Bronze is an alloy of copper and tin.
  • Stainless steel contains iron, chromium, nickel, and carbon.
  • Graphite is a non-metal that conducts electricity.

Glossary

MalleabilityThe ability of a substance to be hammered or rolled into thin sheets.
DuctilityThe ability of a substance to be drawn into thin wires.
Amphoteric OxideAn oxide that reacts with both acids and bases to form salt and water.
GalvanizationThe protective coating of iron or steel with a thin layer of zinc.
AmalgamAn alloy of mercury with another metal.

Important distinctions

BrassvsBronze
  • Brass is composed of Copper and Zinc.
  • Bronze is composed of Copper and Tin.
MetalsvsNon-Metals
  • Metals form basic/amphoteric oxides; non-metals form acidic/neutral oxides.
  • Metals act as reducing agents; non-metals act as oxidizing agents.

Memory hooks

  • Memory hook for Brass vs Bronze: 'AZ' (Brass = Copper + Zinc) and 'BT' (Bronze = Copper + Tin).
  • Reactivity series mnemonic: 'Please Send Cats, Monkeys And Zebras In Lead Hydro Cages Securely Gold' (K, Na, Ca, Mg, Al, Zn, Fe, Pb, H, Cu, Ag, Au).
  • Corrosion mnemonic for copper and silver: 'CuCO3 is Green like grass, Ag2S is Black like soot'.

Exam traps and cautions

  • Do not confuse brass (Cu+Zn) with bronze (Cu+Sn).
  • Remember that iron rusting requires both oxygen AND moisture.
  • Keep in mind that graphite is a non-metal with electrical conductivity.
  • Note that nitric acid does not evolve hydrogen gas with most metals.
