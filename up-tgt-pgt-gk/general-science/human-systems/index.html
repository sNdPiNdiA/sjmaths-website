Concept 1 · foundation

Human Digestive System and Metabolism

The digestive system breaks down ingested food mechanically and chemically into absorbable nutrients to sustain cellular metabolism.

Prerequisites

  • Basic knowledge of organic macromolecules: carbohydrates, proteins, and lipids.
  • Cellular requirement for energy (ATP) and building blocks.

Definitions and scope

  • Digestion is the sequential mechanical and enzymatic breakdown of large food polymers into small, absorbable monomers.
  • Alimentary canal is the continuous muscular tube running from mouth to anus, complemented by accessory digestive glands.

Step-by-step explanation

  • Ingestion begins in the oral cavity where teeth masticate food and salivary amylase (ptyalin) initiates starch hydrolysis into maltose.
  • Swallowing (deglutition) sends the bolus down the esophagus via coordinated wave-like smooth muscle contractions known as peristalsis.
  • The stomach secretes gastric juice containing hydrochloric acid (HCl) which activates pepsinogen into pepsin for protein digestion and provides an acidic pH of 1.5 to 2.0.
  • Mucus lining in the stomach protects the gastric epithelium from autodigestion by its own acid and enzymes.
  • Chyme enters the duodenum where bile from the liver emulsifies fats and pancreatic juice supplies trypsin, lipase, and amylase.
  • The small intestine (jejunum and ileum) serves as the primary site of nutrient absorption, maximized by circular folds, villi, and microvillous brush borders.
  • Water, mineral absorption, and vitamin synthesis occur in the large intestine (colon and cecum), leading to feces formation.
  • Egestion involves the elimination of indigestible waste products through the rectum and anal sphincter.

Key points

  • Salivary amylase digests starches in the mouth at an optimal neutral to slightly acidic pH.
  • Pepsin functions in the acidic environment of the stomach to cleave proteins.
  • Bile salts lack enzymes but physically emulsify large lipid droplets into smaller micelles.
  • Pancreatic juice contains trypsinogen, chymotrypsinogen, carboxypeptidase, pancreatic lipase, and nucleases.
  • Villi contain blood capillaries for amino acids/monosaccharides and lacteals for fatty acids/glycerol.
  • The enteric nervous system regulates gastrointestinal motility and secretion independently of the CNS.
Worked examples
  • Carbohydrate digestion pathway: Polysaccharides -> Salivary amylase (mouth) -> Maltose -> Pancreatic amylase (duodenum) -> Glucose (brush border maltase) -> Hepatic portal vein.
  • Protein digestion pathway: Proteins -> Pepsin/HCl (stomach) -> Polypeptides -> Trypsin/Chymotrypsin (small intestine) -> Peptides -> Aminopeptidases/Carboxypeptidases -> Amino acids.

Applications and connections

  • Clinical diagnosis of peptic ulcers caused by Helicobacter pylori disrupting gastric mucosal barriers.
  • Understanding jaundice due to bile duct obstruction leading to bilirubin accumulation in blood.
  • Pharmacology of antacids and proton pump inhibitors (PPIs) in managing hyperacidity.

Advanced points, exceptions and limitations

  • The gut-brain axis demonstrates bidirectional communication between enteric and central nervous systems via the vagus nerve.
  • Bariatric surgery alters anatomical pathways to restrict nutrient intake or malabsorb calories.
  • The gut microbiome plays critical roles in vitamin synthesis, immune education, and short-chain fatty acid production.

Additional examples

  • Saliva production triggered by cephalic phase stimulation.
  • Peristaltic movement pushing food upward against gravity (e.g., swallowing while upside down).
  • Emulsification of dietary butter by bile salts in the duodenum.
Important distinctions
  • Intracellular digestion occurs within individual cells (protozoa) vs Extracellular digestion occurs in specialized lumens (humans).
  • Mechanical digestion alters physical size without chemical identity change vs Chemical digestion breaks covalent bonds using enzymes.
  • Hepatic portal system routes absorbed water-soluble nutrients directly to the liver vs Lacteals route dietary fats into the lymphatic system.
Common misconceptions
  • Believing that digestion of all food types occurs primarily in the stomach.
  • Thinking that bile contains digestive enzymes.
  • Assuming water is only absorbed in the small intestine.
Exam focus
  • Identify exact enzymes secreted by specific organs (e.g., pepsin in stomach, trypsin in pancreas).
  • Recall the pH requirements of different digestive compartments.
  • Understand the fate of end products (carbohydrates and proteins to hepatic portal vein; fats to lacteals).
Concept 2 · core

Human Circulatory System and Cardiovascular Dynamics

The circulatory system pumps blood through a closed vascular network to transport respiratory gases, nutrients, wastes, and hormones throughout the body.

Prerequisites

  • Basic physical laws of fluid pressure and flow resistance.
  • Elementary cellular respiration and gas exchange requirements.

Definitions and scope

  • Closed double circulation means blood passes twice through the heart in one complete circuit (pulmonary and systemic circuits).
  • Cardiovascular system comprises the four-chambered muscular heart, blood vessels (arteries, veins, capillaries), and circulating blood.

Step-by-step explanation

  • Deoxygenated blood from the body enters the right atrium via superior and inferior venae cavae.
  • Blood passes through the tricuspid valve into the right ventricle, which pumps it through the pulmonary semilunar valve into the pulmonary trunk and lungs.
  • Oxygenated blood returns from lungs via four pulmonary veins into the left atrium.
  • Blood flows through the bicuspid (mitral) valve into the left ventricle, which exerts high pressure to pump blood through the aortic semilunar valve into the aorta for systemic distribution.
  • The cardiac cycle consists of atrial systole, ventricular systole, and joint diastole, regulated by the sinoatrial (SA) node (pacemaker) and atrioventricular (AV) node.
  • Arteries carry blood away from the heart under high pressure, featuring thick elastic and muscular walls; veins carry blood toward the heart under low pressure, featuring valves to prevent backflow.
  • Capillaries consist of a single endothelial layer facilitating rapid exchange of gases, nutrients, and metabolic wastes via diffusion and filtration.
  • Blood composition includes plasma (55%) and formed elements (45%): erythrocytes (RBCs for oxygen transport), leukocytes (WBCs for defense), and thrombocytes (platelets for clotting).

Key points

  • The human heart is myogenic, meaning contraction originates within specialized cardiac muscle tissue (SA node).
  • Systolic blood pressure normal reference value is approximately 120 mmHg; diastolic is 80 mmHg.
  • Erythrocytes lack nuclei and organelles in mammals to maximize hemoglobin packing capacity.
  • Fetal circulation bypasses lungs via foramen ovale and ductus arteriosus.
  • The hepatic portal system connects capillary beds of the GI tract directly to liver sinusoids.
  • Blood clotting cascade culminates in the conversion of soluble fibrinogen into insoluble fibrin threads.
Worked examples
  • Tracing a red blood cell from toe to brain: Great saphenous vein -> Inferior vena cava -> Right atrium -> Tricuspid valve -> Right ventricle -> Pulmonary artery -> Lungs -> Pulmonary veins -> Left atrium -> Bicuspid valve -> Left ventricle -> Aorta -> Carotid artery -> Brain.
  • Cardiac output calculation: Stroke volume (70 mL/beat) multiplied by Heart rate (72 beats/min) equals Cardiac Output (~5.04 Liters/min).

Applications and connections

  • Electrocardiogram (ECG) interpretation to diagnose myocardial infarction and conduction arrhythmias.
  • Understanding hypertension pathophysiology and treatment using beta-blockers or ACE inhibitors.
  • Blood grouping (ABO and Rh systems) to ensure safe blood transfusion practices.

Advanced points, exceptions and limitations

  • Starling's Law of the Heart states that stroke volume increases in response to an increase in the volume of blood filling the heart (end-diastolic volume).
  • Atherosclerosis involves lipid plaque buildup in arterial walls, narrowing lumen diameter and predisposing to thrombus formation.
  • Lymphatic system acts as an accessory circulatory network returning interstitial fluid and absorbed fats back to subclavian veins.

Additional examples

  • Pacemaker cells in the SA node spontaneously depolarizing at ~70-80 times per minute.
  • Edema formation during protein deficiency due to decreased plasma oncotic pressure.
  • Rh incompatibility (erythroblastosis fetalis) occurring when an Rh-negative mother carries an Rh-positive fetus.
Important distinctions
  • Arteries transport blood away from heart (mostly oxygenated, except pulmonary artery) vs Veins transport blood toward heart (mostly deoxygenated, except pulmonary veins).
  • Systemic circulation provides high-pressure blood to body organs vs Pulmonary circulation provides low-pressure blood for gas exchange in lungs.
  • Erythrocytes function in oxygen transport vs Leukocytes function in immunological defense.
Common misconceptions
  • Assuming all arteries carry oxygenated blood and all veins carry deoxygenated blood.
  • Believing the heart pushes blood purely due to nervous signals rather than intrinsic myogenic rhythm.
  • Thinking blood plasma and serum are identical (serum lacks clotting factors like fibrinogen).
Exam focus
  • Trace the exact pathway of blood through heart chambers and valves.
  • Identify functions of heart valves (prevent backflow) and ECG wave components (P, QRS, T waves).
  • Distinguish between open and closed circulatory systems, and single vs double circulation.
Concept 3 · core

Human Respiratory System and Gas Exchange

The respiratory system facilitates pulmonary ventilation and external/internal respiration to supply oxygen and eliminate carbon dioxide.

Prerequisites

  • Basic gas laws (Boyle's Law relating pressure and volume).
  • Cellular respiration requiring oxygen as the final electron acceptor.

Definitions and scope

  • Pulmonary ventilation is the mechanical movement of air into and out of the lungs.
  • Respiratory membrane is the ultra-thin barrier across which O2 and CO2 diffuse between alveolar air and pulmonary capillary blood.

Step-by-step explanation

  • Air enters via nasal cavities where it is filtered, warmed, and humidified before passing through the pharynx and larynx.
  • The trachea and branching bronchi/bronchioles conduct air to millions of microscopic alveoli, which provide a massive surface area for gas exchange.
  • Inhalation (inspiration) occurs when the diaphragm contracts (flattens) and external intercostal muscles elevate the rib cage, increasing thoracic volume and decreasing alveolar pressure below atmospheric pressure.
  • Exhalation (expiration) during quiet breathing is passive, caused by elastic recoil of lungs and relaxation of inspiratory muscles, decreasing thoracic volume and raising alveolar pressure above atmospheric.
  • Oxygen binds reversibly to hemoglobin in erythrocytes within pulmonary capillaries, forming oxyhemoglobin.
  • Oxygen-hemoglobin dissociation curve shows sigmoidal kinetics, reflecting cooperative binding where high PO2 in lungs favors loading and low PO2 in tissues favors unloading.
  • Bohr effect describes how increased hydrogen ion concentration (lower pH) and higher CO2 concentration shift the oxygen-hemoglobin curve to the right, promoting oxygen release in active tissues.
  • Carbon dioxide is transported in blood primarily as bicarbonate ions (70%), bound to hemoglobin as carbaminohemoglobin (23%), and dissolved in plasma (7%).

Key points

  • The respiratory rhythm center is located in the medulla oblongata, modulated by the pons.
  • Chemoreceptors in the carotid and aortic bodies monitor blood CO2, pH, and O2 levels.
  • Residual volume is the volume of air remaining in lungs after maximal expiration, preventing alveolar collapse.
  • Vital capacity is the maximum amount of air a person can expel from the lungs after a maximum inhalation.
  • Surfactant produced by type II alveolar cells reduces surface tension, preventing alveolar collapse.
  • Emphysema destroys alveolar walls, drastically reducing surface area for gas exchange.
Worked examples
  • Inhalation mechanics: Diaphragm contraction -> Thoracic cavity volume increases -> Intrapleural/Alveolar pressure drops -> Air rushes in down pressure gradient.
  • Carbon dioxide transport conversion: CO2 + H2O <-> H2CO3 <-> H+ + HCO3- (catalyzed by carbonic anhydrase in RBCs).

Applications and connections

  • Diagnosis of restrictive and obstructive pulmonary disorders using spirometry.
  • Understanding altitude sickness (hypoxia) experienced by mountaineers traveling to high elevations.
  • Medical administration of hyperbaric oxygen therapy for carbon monoxide poisoning.

Advanced points, exceptions and limitations

  • Haldane effect explains how oxygenation of blood in the lungs promotes the dissociation of CO2 from hemoglobin.
  • Chloride shift maintains electrical neutrality as bicarbonate ions diffuse out of RBCs into plasma in exchange for chloride ions.
  • Ventilation-perfusion coupling matches blood flow to lung alveoli with air supply for optimal gas exchange efficiency.

Additional examples

  • Yawning as a deep inspiration reflex to increase alveolar ventilation.
  • Hyperventilation lowering blood CO2 levels, leading to respiratory alkalosis.
  • Asthma attacks causing bronchospasm and increased airway resistance.
Important distinctions
  • External respiration refers to gas exchange between alveoli and pulmonary blood vs Internal respiration refers to gas exchange between systemic capillaries and tissue cells.
  • Inspiratory capacity is the sum of tidal volume and inspiratory reserve volume vs Functional residual capacity is the sum of residual volume and expiratory reserve volume.
  • Obstructive disorders (asthma, COPD) impair airflow vs Restrictive disorders (fibrosis) impair lung expansion.
Common misconceptions
  • Believing that lungs actively pull air in through muscular contraction of lung tissue itself.
  • Thinking that carbon monoxide poisoning is reversible simply by breathing normal room air without intervention.
  • Assuming oxygen is transported entirely dissolved in blood plasma.
Exam focus
  • Memorize lung volumes and capacities (Tidal volume ~500 mL, Vital capacity ~3500-4500 mL).
  • Understand shifts in the oxygen-hemoglobin dissociation curve (Bohr effect: right shift due to high CO2, low pH, high temperature).
  • Recall primary locations of respiratory control centers (medulla and pons).
Concept 4 · core

Human Nervous System and Neural Coordination

The nervous system provides rapid, electrochemical communication and control across the body via neurons and supporting neuroglia.

Prerequisites

  • Basic electrical principles of ionic concentration gradients across membranes.
  • Cellular anatomy of animal cells and membrane receptors.

Definitions and scope

  • Central Nervous System (CNS) consists of the brain and spinal duct, serving as the integration and command center.
  • Peripheral Nervous System (PNS) comprises cranial and spinal nerves connecting CNS to peripheral effectors.

Step-by-step explanation

  • Neurons are structural and functional units consisting of dendrites (receive signals), a cell body (soma), and an axon (transmit impulses).
  • Resting membrane potential (~ -70 mV) is maintained by sodium-potassium pumps actively pumping 3 Na+ out and 2 K+ in.
  • Action potential is triggered when a stimulus depolarizes the membrane to threshold potential (-55 mV), opening voltage-gated Na+ channels causing rapid spike depolarization.
  • Repolarization occurs when voltage-gated Na+ channels close and K+ channels open, effluxing K+ ions to restore negativity.
  • Saltatory conduction occurs in myelinated axons where nerve impulses jump rapidly from one Node of Ranvier to the next.
  • Synaptic transmission involves action potential reaching axon terminal, triggering calcium influx, vesicle fusion, and neurotransmitter (e.g., acetylcholine) release across the synaptic cleft.
  • The brain is divided into the forebrain (cerebrum, thalamus, hypothalamus), midbrain, and hindbrain (cerebellum, pons, medulla oblongata).
  • Reflex actions are rapid, involuntary responses to stimuli mediated by a reflex arc bypassing conscious brain processing for immediate protection.

Key points

  • Acetylcholine and norepinephrine are primary neurotransmitters in the peripheral nervous system.
  • The cerebrum controls higher mental functions, sensory perception, and voluntary motor actions.
  • The cerebellum coordinates balance, posture, and precise voluntary muscle coordination.
  • The medulla oblongata controls involuntary vital functions like heart rate, respiration, and blood pressure.
  • Autonomic nervous system (ANS) splits into sympathetic ('fight or flight') and parasympathetic ('rest and digest') branches.
  • Myelin sheath in CNS is formed by oligodendrocytes; in PNS, by Schwann cells.
Worked examples
  • Reflex arc pathway: Sensory receptor -> Sensory (afferent) neuron -> Interneuron in spinal cord -> Motor (efferent) neuron -> Effector (muscle contraction).
  • Action potential ionic shift: Resting (-70mV) -> Depolarization (Na+ influx, peak +30mV) -> Repolarization (K+ efflux) -> Hyperpolarization -> Return to resting.

Applications and connections

  • Neurological assessment of deep tendon reflexes (e.g., knee-jerk reflex) to test spinal cord integrity.
  • Pharmacology of local anesthetics blocking voltage-gated sodium channels to stop pain signaling.
  • Understanding neurodegenerative disorders like Parkinson's disease involving dopamine depletion in the basal ganglia.

Advanced points, exceptions and limitations

  • Long-term potentiation (LTP) at hippocampal synapses serves as a cellular model for learning and memory formation.
  • Multiple sclerosis is an autoimmune demyelinating disease slowing nerve conduction velocity.
  • Neurotransmitters bind to ionotropic or metabotropic receptors, initiating fast or slow intracellular signaling cascades.

Additional examples

  • Knee-jerk monosynaptic stretch reflex.
  • Sympathetic nervous system dilating pupils and accelerating heartbeat during stress.
  • Parasympathetic nervous system stimulating digestion and slowing heart rate.
Important distinctions
  • Central Nervous System (brain and spinal cord) vs Peripheral Nervous System (cranial and spinal nerves).
  • Somatic nervous system controls voluntary skeletal muscles vs Autonomic nervous system controls involuntary visceral effectors.
  • Sympathetic division activates 'fight or flight' response vs Parasympathetic division activates 'rest and digest' response.
Common misconceptions
  • Believing reflex arcs require conscious thought before a motor response occurs.
  • Thinking nerve impulses travel through wires via electric current rather than electrochemical ionic waves.
  • Assuming all nerves in the peripheral system are capable of efficient regeneration.
Exam focus
  • Memorize components of a reflex arc in correct sequence.
  • Distinguish between sympathetic and parasympathetic autonomic effects on organs.
  • Identify roles of major brain regions (cerebrum, cerebellum, medulla oblongata, hypothalamus).
Concept 5 · advanced

Human Endocrine System and Hormonal Regulation

The endocrine system utilizes ductless glands releasing chemical messengers (hormones) into the bloodstream to regulate long-term metabolic homeostasis.

Prerequisites

  • General understanding of cellular membrane receptors and signal transduction pathways.
  • Concept of physiological homeostasis and negative feedback regulation.

Definitions and scope

  • Hormones are organic chemical messengers secreted by endocrine glands into blood, acting on distant target cells possessing specific receptors.
  • Negative feedback is a regulatory mechanism where a system's output counteracts the initial stimulus to maintain stability.

Step-by-step explanation

  • The hypothalamus serves as the master neuroendocrine link, secreting releasing and inhibiting hormones that control the anterior pituitary gland.
  • The pituitary gland (hypophysis) is divided into the anterior lobe (adenohypophysis, releasing GH, TSH, ACTH, FSH, LH, PRL) and posterior lobe (neurohypophysis, storing oxytocin and vasopressin/ADH).
  • The thyroid gland secretes thyroxine (T4) and triiodothyronine (T3) regulating basal metabolic rate, and calcitonin lowering blood calcium.
  • Parathyroid glands secrete parathyroid hormone (PTH), which raises blood calcium levels by acting on bone, kidney, and intestine.
  • The pancreas contains Islets of Langerhans: alpha cells secrete glucagon (raises blood glucose) and beta cells secrete insulin (lowers blood glucose).
  • Adrenal glands comprise the adrenal cortex (secreting mineralocorticoids like aldosterone, glucocorticoids like cortisol, and sex corticoids) and adrenal medulla (secreting epinephrine and norepinephrine).
  • Gonads (testes and ovaries) secrete sex steroids (testosterone, estrogen, progesterone) controlling secondary sexual characteristics and reproduction.
  • Hormone action mechanism involves peptide/protein hormones binding cell-surface receptors generating second messengers (e.g., cAMP), whereas steroid hormones pass through cell membranes to bind intracellular receptors and alter gene transcription.

Key points

  • Insulin lowers blood glucose by promoting cellular glucose uptake and glycogenesis.
  • Glucagon raises blood glucose by stimulating glycogenolysis and gluconeogenesis in the liver.
  • Diabetes mellitus results from insulin deficiency (Type 1) or insulin resistance (Type 2).
  • Goiter is caused by dietary iodine deficiency impairing thyroid hormone synthesis.
  • Oxytocin stimulates uterine contractions during childbirth and milk ejection during lactation.
  • Antidiuretic hormone (ADH / Vasopressin) promotes water reabsorption in renal collecting ducts, preventing dehydration.
Worked examples
  • Blood glucose negative feedback: High blood glucose -> Pancreatic beta cells release insulin -> Cells take up glucose / Liver stores glycogen -> Blood glucose levels drop to normal.
  • Thyroid axis regulation: Hypothalamus releases TRH -> Anterior pituitary releases TSH -> Thyroid gland releases T3/T4 -> Negative feedback inhibits TRH and TSH.

Applications and connections

  • Hormone replacement therapy (HRT) for hypothyroidism (levothyroxine) or type 1 diabetes (insulin injections).
  • Diagnosis of endocrine disorders via blood hormone assays and stimulation/suppression tests.
  • Understanding stress response pathways mediated by the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis.

Advanced points, exceptions and limitations

  • Up-regulation and down-regulation of hormone receptors allow target cells to adjust sensitivity to circulating hormone levels.
  • Synergistic, permissive, and antagonistic hormone interactions coordinate complex physiological responses.
  • Endocrine disrupting chemicals (EDCs) can mimic or block natural hormones, interfering with homeostatic development and reproduction.

Additional examples

  • Adrenaline surge increasing heart rate and blood glucose during sudden fear.
  • Prolactin stimulating milk production in mammary glands.
  • Aldosterone conserving sodium and excreting potassium in distal renal tubules.
Important distinctions
  • Endocrine system uses chemical messengers via blood stream with slow, prolonged effects vs Nervous system uses electrochemical signals via neurons with rapid, short-lived effects.
  • Peptide hormones act via cell surface receptors and second messengers vs Steroid hormones act via intracellular nuclear receptors influencing gene expression.
  • Hypoglycemic hormone (insulin) lowers blood glucose vs Hyperglycemic hormones (glucagon, cortisol, adrenaline) raise blood glucose.
Common misconceptions
  • Believing hormones are only involved in sexual reproduction rather than broad metabolic regulation.
  • Confusing exocrine glands (with ducts, e.g., sweat, salivary) with endocrine glands (ductless, e.g., thyroid, pituitary).
  • Thinking insulin enters cells directly rather than signaling glucose transporters (GLUT4) to move to the membrane.
Exam focus
  • Match every endocrine gland with its secreted hormones and target functions.
  • Identify deficiency and excess disorders (e.g., diabetes insipidus vs diabetes mellitus, dwarfism vs gigantism, cretinism).
  • Distinguish between peptide and steroid hormone action mechanisms.

Concept 1

Human Digestive System and Metabolism

Definition and scope

  • Sequential mechanical and chemical breakdown of food polymers.
  • Comprises alimentary canal and accessory digestive glands.
  • Converts complex organic nutrients into absorbable micromolecules.

Must remember

  • Salivary amylase digests starch in the mouth.
  • Pepsin works in acidic stomach environment (HCl).
  • Bile emulsifies fats without enzymes.
  • Villi maximize surface area for absorption in small intestine.
  • Hepatic portal system routes nutrients to liver first.

Concept-specific exam traps

  • Assuming bile contains digestive enzymes.
  • Forgetting that protein digestion starts in the stomach, not the mouth.
Concept 2

Human Circulatory System and Cardiovascular Dynamics

Definition and scope

  • Closed double circulation through pulmonary and systemic circuits.
  • Powered by a four-chambered myogenic heart.
  • Transports respiratory gases, nutrients, and wastes.

Must remember

  • Deoxygenated blood enters right atrium.
  • Left ventricle pumps blood through systemic aorta.
  • SA node acts as the natural pacemaker.
  • Erythrocytes lack nuclei in mammals.
  • Valves prevent backflow in veins and heart.

Concept-specific exam traps

  • Confusing pulmonary artery (deoxygenated) with systemic arteries.
  • Believing heart contraction is initiated by nervous impulses rather than myogenic tissue.
Concept 3

Human Respiratory System and Gas Exchange

Definition and scope

  • Pulmonary ventilation and gas exchange across alveolar membrane.
  • Driven by pressure gradients created by diaphragm and intercostal muscles.
  • Transports O2 bound to hemoglobin and CO2 as bicarbonate.

Must remember

  • Inhalation involves diaphragm contraction and thoracic expansion.
  • Oxygen-hemoglobin dissociation curve is sigmoidal.
  • Bohr effect shifts curve right with high CO2 and low pH.
  • Medulla oblongata houses respiratory rhythm center.
  • Residual volume prevents alveolar collapse.

Concept-specific exam traps

  • Thinking lungs expand via active muscle tissue within the lung itself.
  • Assuming carbon dioxide is transported solely dissolved in plasma.
Concept 4

Human Nervous System and Neural Coordination

Definition and scope

  • Electrochemical communication via neurons and neuroglia.
  • Divided into Central (CNS) and Peripheral (PNS) nervous systems.
  • Mediates rapid sensory integration and motor response.

Must remember

  • Resting membrane potential is maintained at approximately -70 mV.
  • Action potential involves Na+ influx and K+ efflux.
  • Cerebellum coordinates balance and posture.
  • Medulla controls autonomic vital functions.
  • Reflex arcs bypass conscious thought for rapid protection.

Concept-specific exam traps

  • Assuming all reflex arcs involve brain processing.
  • Confusing somatic and autonomic nervous system pathways.
Concept 5

Human Endocrine System and Hormonal Regulation

Definition and scope

  • Ductless glands secreting chemical messengers into blood.
  • Regulates long-term metabolic homeostasis via negative feedback.
  • Exerts actions via cell-surface or intracellular receptors.

Must remember

  • Insulin lowers blood glucose; glucagon raises it.
  • Thyroid hormones (T3/T4) regulate basal metabolic rate.
  • Hypothalamus controls pituitary gland secretions.
  • Adrenal medulla secretes adrenaline for 'fight or flight'.
  • ADH promotes water reabsorption in kidney collecting ducts.

Concept-specific exam traps

  • Confusing diabetes mellitus (insulin issue) with diabetes insipidus (ADH issue).
  • Assuming steroid hormones act via cell surface second messengers.

Quick facts

  • Normal human blood pressure is 120/80 mmHg.
  • The sinoatrial (SA) node is the natural pacemaker of the heart.
  • Bile is produced in the liver and stored in the gallbladder.
  • The respiratory control center is located in the medulla oblongata.
  • Insulin is secreted by beta cells of the pancreatic Islets of Langerhans.
  • Erythrocytes (RBCs) have an average lifespan of 120 days.

Glossary

PeristalsisWave-like smooth muscle contractions that propel food through the alimentary canal.
MyogenicMuscle contraction originating spontaneously from cardiac muscle cells rather than nerve stimulation.
Bohr EffectReduction in hemoglobin's affinity for oxygen caused by increased hydrogen ions and carbon dioxide concentration.
Saltatory ConductionRapid jumping of action potentials from one node of Ranvier to another along myelinated axons.
HomeostasisMaintenance of a stable internal physiological environment despite external fluctuations.

Important distinctions

ArteriesvsVeins
  • Arteries carry blood away from the heart; veins carry blood toward the heart.
  • Arteries generally transport oxygenated blood (except pulmonary); veins transport deoxygenated blood (except pulmonary).
  • Arteries have thick, elastic walls to withstand high pressure; veins have thinner walls with valves to prevent backflow.
Sympathetic Nervous SystemvsParasympathetic Nervous System
  • Sympathetic mediates 'fight or flight' stress responses; parasympathetic mediates 'rest and digest' restorative functions.
  • Sympathetic increases heart rate and dilates airways; parasympathetic decreases heart rate and constricts airways.
InsulinvsGlucagon
  • Insulin is a hypoglycemic hormone lowering blood glucose; glucagon is a hyperglycemic hormone raising blood glucose.
  • Insulin is secreted by pancreatic beta cells; glucagon is secreted by pancreatic alpha cells.

Memory hooks

  • Heart valves order: Tricuspid on the Right, Bicuspid (Mitral) on the Left (TRI-TRY, BI-MIT).
  • Blood flow through heart: RA -> RV -> Lungs -> LA -> LV -> Body.
  • Endocrine vs Exocrine: Endocrine enters blood directly (Endo = Inside blood); Exocrine uses ducts (Exo = Exit via tube).

Exam traps and cautions

  • Do not confuse pulmonary artery (carrying deoxygenated blood to lungs) with systemic arteries.
  • Remember that pepsin requires an acidic environment, whereas salivary amylase works in neutral/alkaline pH.
  • Distinguish clearly between diabetes mellitus (glucose regulation) and diabetes insipidus (water reabsorption).
  • Do not forget that the SA node is myogenic and does not require nervous stimulation to beat.

Question 1MCQ

Which of the following enzymes requires an acidic pH provided by hydrochloric acid to become active in the human stomach?

Pepsinogen is secreted by chief cells and activated into pepsin in the presence of gastric HCl.

Question 2MCQ

What is the correct sequence of blood flow through the human heart chambers starting from venous return?

Deoxygenated blood enters the right atrium, passes to right ventricle, goes to lungs, returns to left atrium, and is pumped out by left ventricle.

Question 3Assertion–Reason

Assertion (A): A shift in the oxygen-hemoglobin dissociation curve to the right occurs during strenuous exercise. Reason (R): Increased body temperature, high CO2 concentration, and low blood pH all reduce hemoglobin's oxygen affinity.

Both statements are true and correctly linked; metabolic byproducts of exercise shift the curve right (Bohr effect), enhancing oxygen unloading to active muscles.

Question 4MCQ

Which part of the human brain is principally responsible for maintaining balance, posture, and fine motor coordination?

The cerebellum integrates sensory input from muscles and joints to ensure smooth, coordinated voluntary movement and balance.

Question 5Match the following

Match the endocrine gland with its primary hormone secretion:

Thyroid secretes thyroxine, Adrenal cortex secretes cortisol, Pancreas secretes insulin, and Posterior pituitary stores ADH.

Question 6Case-based

A patient suffering from severe gallstones experiences blockage in the common bile duct. Which immediate digestive process will be most impaired?

Bile duct obstruction prevents bile salts from entering the duodenum, severely impairing the emulsification and digestion of dietary fats.

Question 7MCQ

Which blood vessels possess one-way valves to prevent the backflow of blood under low pressure?

Veins feature internal valves that assist venous return against gravity under low hydrostatic pressure.

Question 8MCQ

What is the primary physiological consequence of emphysema on lung tissue structure?

Emphysema destroys alveolar walls, decreasing the surface area available for gas exchange and causing shortness of breath.

Question 9Assertion–Reason

Assertion (A): The resting membrane potential of a neuron is negative inside relative to outside. Reason (R): The sodium-potassium pump actively transports 3 sodium ions out for every 2 potassium ions brought in.

Both statements are true; electrogenic pumping of 3 Na+ out and 2 K+ in maintains the inside negative relative to the outside.

Question 10MCQ

Which disorder is caused by a deficiency in antidiuretic hormone (ADH), leading to excessive urine output and severe thirst?

Diabetes insipidus results from underproduction or lack of response to ADH, impairing water reabsorption in the renal collecting ducts.
