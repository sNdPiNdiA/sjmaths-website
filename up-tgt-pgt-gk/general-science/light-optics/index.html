Concept 1 · foundation

Reflection of Light and Spherical Mirrors

Study of light bouncing off surfaces and image formation by plane and curved mirrors using standard sign conventions.

Prerequisites

  • Basic rectilinear propagation of light
  • Concept of normal, angle of incidence, and angle of reflection
  • Cartesian coordinate system basics

Definitions and scope

  • Reflection is the phenomenon of bouncing back of light into the same medium when it strikes a reflecting surface.
  • Concave mirror is a spherical mirror with an inward-curving reflecting surface.
  • Convex mirror is a spherical mirror with an outward-bulging reflecting surface.

Step-by-step explanation

  • First law of reflection states that the incident ray, the reflected ray, and the normal to the reflecting surface at the point of incidence all lie in the same plane.
  • Second law of reflection states that the angle of incidence (i) is always equal to the angle of reflection (r).
  • Plane mirrors always form virtual, erect images of the same size as the object, with lateral inversion.
  • In spherical mirrors, the pole (P) is the geometrical center of the mirror surface, and the focus (F) is the point where parallel rays converge or appear to diverge.
  • Radius of curvature (R) is twice the focal length (f), expressed as R = 2f for paraxial rays.
  • New Cartesian Sign Convention: All distances are measured from the pole; distances measured in the direction of incident light are positive, and against are negative.
  • Mirror formula relates object distance (u), image distance (v), and focal length (f) as 1/f = 1/v + 1/u.
  • Magnification (m) for a mirror is given by m = -v/u = height of image / height of object.

Key points

  • Concave mirrors have negative focal lengths; convex mirrors have positive focal lengths.
  • Real images formed by mirrors are always inverted; virtual images are always erect.
  • Concave mirrors are used in headlights, shaving mirrors, and solar furnaces.
  • Convex mirrors are used as rear-view mirrors in vehicles due to their wide field of view and upright images.
  • A ray passing through the center of curvature retraces its path after reflection.
  • Magnification greater than 1 indicates an enlarged image, while less than 1 indicates a diminished image.
Worked examples
  • An object is placed at a distance of 15 cm in front of a concave mirror of focal length 10 cm. Find the position of the image. Solution: u = -15 cm, f = -10 cm. Using 1/f = 1/v + 1/u, -1/10 = 1/v - 1/15, giving 1/v = 1/15 - 1/10 = -1/30, so v = -30 cm.
  • Find the magnification in the above problem. Solution: m = -v/u = -(-30)/(-15) = -2. The image is real, inverted, and twice the size of the object.

Applications and connections

  • Automotive rear-view mirrors for traffic monitoring
  • Reflecting telescopes used in astronomy
  • Ophthalmoscopes used by doctors to examine the retina

Advanced points, exceptions and limitations

  • Spherical aberration occurs in large aperture mirrors where marginal rays do not focus at the principal focus, which can be corrected using parabolic mirrors.
  • Caustics are formed due to the envelope of light rays reflected from a curved surface.
  • Newton's relation for mirrors: x1 * x2 = f^2, where x1 and x2 are distances of object and image from the focus.

Additional examples

  • Dentist's head mirror
  • Security mirrors in departmental stores
  • Solar cookers using large concave dishes
Important distinctions
  • Plane mirror forms virtual images of equal size; concave mirror can form real or virtual images of various sizes; convex mirror forms only diminished virtual images.
  • Focal length of a plane mirror is infinity; focal length of a spherical mirror is finite (R/2).
  • Concave mirror converges light; convex mirror diverges light.
Common misconceptions
  • Assuming convex mirrors can form real images under normal circumstances (they only form real images if a convergent beam is incident on them).
  • Confusing the signs of object distance which is almost always negative for real objects.
  • Believing magnification of -1 means a virtual image (it actually means a real, inverted image of the same size).
Exam focus
  • Memorize ray diagrams for concave mirror positions (at infinity, beyond C, at C, between C and F, at F, between F and P).
  • Apply Cartesian sign convention meticulously in numerical problems.
  • Remember relationship R = 2f.
  • Distinguish clearly between real (in front) and virtual (behind) image positions for mirrors.
  • Practice mirror formula and magnification expressions frequently.
Concept 2 · core

Refraction of Light and Snell's Law

Bending of light as it passes from one transparent medium to another due to change in speed.

Prerequisites

  • Wave nature and speed of light in vacuum versus material media
  • Plane geometry and trigonometry (sine function)
  • Concept of optical density

Definitions and scope

  • Refraction is the change in direction of a light ray when it passes obliquely from one transparent medium to another.
  • Refractive index (n) of a medium is the ratio of speed of light in vacuum to the speed of light in that medium (n = c/v).
  • Relative refractive index of medium 2 with respect to medium 1 is n21 = n2 / n1 = v1 / v2.

Step-by-step explanation

  • First law of refraction: The incident ray, the refracted ray, and the normal to the interface at the point of incidence all lie in the same plane.
  • Second law of refraction (Snell's Law): The ratio of the sine of the angle of incidence to the sine of the angle of refraction is a constant for a given pair of media (sin i / sin r = constant).
  • When light travels from an optically rarer medium to an optically denser medium, it bends towards the normal (i > r).
  • When light travels from a denser to a rarer medium, it bends away from the normal (i < r).
  • Frequency of light remains unchanged during refraction, whereas wavelength and velocity change.
  • Apparent depth of a tank filled with water is less than its real depth due to refraction (Real depth / Apparent depth = Refractive index).
  • Atmospheric refraction causes twinkling of stars and delayed sunset/early sunrise.
  • Lateral shift is the perpendicular distance between the incident ray and the emergent ray through a parallel-sided glass slab.

Key points

  • Absolute refractive index of vacuum is 1, and for air it is approximately 1.0003 (taken as 1 in calculations).
  • Refractive index of water is 4/3 (~1.33) and of glass is 3/2 (~1.5).
  • Higher refractive index implies higher optical density and lower speed of light.
  • Normal incidence (i = 0) leads to un-deviated transmission (r = 0), though speed changes.
  • Principle of reversibility of light states that if the path of a ray is reversed, it retraces its path.
  • Atmospheric layers of varying temperature and density bend starlight continuously.
Worked examples
  • Light enters from air into glass having refractive index 1.5. What is the speed of light in glass? Solution: c = 3 * 10^8 m/s, n = 1.5. v = c / n = (3 * 10^8) / 1.5 = 2 * 10^8 m/s.
  • A pond of water appears to be 12 cm deep. If refractive index of water is 4/3, what is the actual depth? Solution: Real depth = Apparent depth * n = 12 * (4/3) = 16 cm.

Applications and connections

  • Design of optical instruments and corrective eyewear
  • Explanation of twinkling of stars and mirages
  • Measurement of purity of liquid solutions using refractometers

Advanced points, exceptions and limitations

  • Fermat's Principle of Least Time states that light takes the path that requires the least time, which mathematically derives Snell's law.
  • Refractive index can be anisotropic in birefringent crystals like calcite, giving rise to ordinary and extraordinary rays.
  • Chromatic dispersion occurs because refractive index depends slightly on the wavelength of light.

Additional examples

  • A pencil submerged obliquely in water appears bent at the surface
  • Bottom of a swimming pool appearing raised
  • Reading printed text through a glass block placed on top of it
Important distinctions
  • Reflection stays within the same medium; refraction crosses boundaries into a new medium.
  • Angle of incidence equals angle of reflection; angle of incidence rarely equals angle of refraction.
  • Optical density relates to wave speed, whereas mass density relates to mass per unit volume.
Common misconceptions
  • Assuming an optically denser medium always has greater mass density (e.g., turpentine is optically denser than water but has lower mass density).
  • Believing frequency of light changes upon entering a new medium.
  • Thinking normal incidence produces bending.
Exam focus
  • Memorize standard refractive index values (water = 4/3, glass = 3/2).
  • Apply Snell's law in numerical problems involving multiple parallel layers.
  • Understand real depth versus apparent depth formula applications.
  • Explain physical reasons for atmospheric refraction phenomena.
  • Keep track of units and constants in speed calculations.
Concept 3 · advanced

Total Internal Reflection (TIR) and Optical Fibers

Complete reflection of light striking a boundary from a denser to a rarer medium beyond the critical angle.

Prerequisites

  • Laws of refraction and Snell's Law
  • Concept of critical angle
  • Optical denser and rarer media distinction

Definitions and scope

  • Critical angle (ic) is the angle of incidence in the denser medium for which the angle of refraction in the rarer medium is 90 degrees.
  • Total Internal Reflection (TIR) is the phenomenon where a light ray travelling from a denser to a rarer medium is completely reflected back into the denser medium when the angle of incidence exceeds the critical angle.

Step-by-step explanation

  • From Snell's law: n1 sin(ic) = n2 sin(90°), where n1 is denser and n2 is rarer medium. Thus, sin(ic) = n2 / n1.
  • If n2 = 1 (air), sin(ic) = 1 / n, where n is the refractive index of the denser medium.
  • When i < ic, partial reflection and refraction occur.
  • When i = ic, the refracted ray grazes the surface (r = 90°).
  • When i > ic, no refraction takes place; 100% of light energy is reflected according to laws of reflection.
  • Diamond sparkling is due to its very low critical angle (~24.4°), causing multiple internal reflections of light entering it.
  • Mirage is an optical illusion in deserts caused by total internal reflection of light from hotter air layers near the ground.
  • Optical fibers transmit data using multiple total internal reflections along a core of high refractive index surrounded by cladding of lower refractive index.

Key points

  • Two essential conditions for TIR: Light must travel from an optically denser to a rarer medium, and angle of incidence must be greater than the critical angle.
  • Critical angle for glass-air interface is approximately 42 degrees.
  • Critical angle for water-air interface is approximately 48.8 degrees.
  • Optical fibers use silica glass with core refractive index ~1.5 and cladding ~1.48.
  • Endoscopy in medicine utilizes optical fiber bundles for internal organ imaging.
  • Prisms can deflect light by 90° or 180° without energy loss using TIR, superior to silvered mirrors.
Worked examples
  • Calculate the critical angle for glass with refractive index 1.5. Solution: sin(ic) = 1 / 1.5 = 2/3 = 0.6667. Therefore, ic = sin^(-1)(0.6667) ≈ 41.8°.
  • Find the maximum angle of incidence in air for a light ray to propagate inside a water-filled pipe (n = 4/3) via TIR. Solution: Cladding index is air (1). Critical angle inside water is sin(ic) = 3/4 = 0.75, so ic ≈ 48.6°.

Applications and connections

  • Telecommunications via fiber optic cables
  • Medical endoscopes and laparoscopes
  • Prism binoculars and total reflecting prisms
  • Sparkling of cut diamonds

Advanced points, exceptions and limitations

  • Frustrated total internal reflection occurs when another dense medium is brought within a fraction of a wavelength, allowing tunneling of evanescent waves.
  • Evanescent waves decay exponentially outside the boundary during TIR.
  • Total internal reflection fluorescence microscopy (TIRFM) illuminates only fluorophores very close to the coverslip.

Additional examples

  • Mirage seen on hot asphalt roads during summer
  • Brilliant shine of an air bubble trapped inside water
  • Prism totally reflecting light by 90 degrees in periscopes
Important distinctions
  • Regular reflection loses some energy through absorption and partial refraction; TIR achieves 100% reflection without transmission loss.
  • Reflection happens at any angle; TIR happens strictly only when incidence angle exceeds critical angle.
  • Mirrors require silver coatings; TIR occurs purely due to refractive index contrast.
Common misconceptions
  • Thinking TIR can occur when light travels from air to water (rarer to denser).
  • Believing diamonds produce light of their own instead of reflecting entrapped external light via TIR.
  • Confusing total internal reflection with total absorption.
Exam focus
  • Memorize the formula sin(ic) = 1 / n.
  • Learn critical angle values for glass (42°) and water (48.8°).
  • State the two mandatory conditions for TIR clearly.
  • Explain diamond brilliance and mirage formation mechanisms.
  • Understand optical fiber structure (core and cladding refractive index roles).
Concept 4 · core

Spherical Lenses and Refraction at Spherical Surfaces

Image formation by convex and concave lenses using lens maker's formula and thin lens equation.

Prerequisites

  • Refraction principles and Snell's Law
  • Spherical mirror terminology (pole, focus, radius of curvature)
  • Sign conventions

Definitions and scope

  • A lens is a transparent optical medium bounded by at least one spherical surface.
  • Convex (converging) lens is thicker at the center and thinner at the edges.
  • Concave (diverging) lens is thinner at the center and thicker at the edges.

Step-by-step explanation

  • Optical center (C or O) is the central point of a lens through which light passes without deviation.
  • Principal focus (F) of a convex lens is where parallel rays converge; for a concave lens, it is where parallel rays appear to diverge from.
  • Lens Maker's Formula: 1/f = (n - 1) * (1/R1 - 1/R2), where n is the refractive index of lens material relative to surroundings, and R1, R2 are radii of curvature.
  • Thin Lens Formula: 1/f = 1/v - 1/u, relating focal length, image distance, and object distance.
  • Linear Magnification (m) for a lens is given by m = height of image / height of object = v / u.
  • Power of a lens (P) is the reciprocal of its focal length in meters: P = 1 / f (in meters), measured in Diopters (D).
  • Convex lenses form real/inverted or virtual/erect images depending on object position.
  • Concave lenses always form virtual, erect, and diminished images regardless of object position.

Key points

  • Power of a convex lens is positive; power of a concave lens is negative.
  • When lenses are kept in contact, equivalent focal length is given by 1/F = 1/f1 + 1/f2, and equivalent power P = P1 + P2.
  • If a lens is immersed in a liquid of higher refractive index than the lens itself, its converging nature reverses to diverging (and vice versa).
  • A convex lens behaves like a magnifying glass when the object is placed within its focal length.
  • A ray passing through the optical center of a thin lens goes straight without deviation.
  • A ray parallel to the principal axis passes through (or appears to come from) the principal focus after refraction.
Worked examples
  • Find the focal length of a convex lens made of glass (n = 1.5) with both spherical surfaces having a radius of curvature of 20 cm. Solution: R1 = +20 cm, R2 = -20 cm. 1/f = (1.5 - 1) * (1/20 - (-1/20)) = 0.5 * (2/20) = 0.5 * (1/10) = 1/20. Thus, f = +20 cm.
  • Calculate the power of a lens of focal length -25 cm. Solution: f = -0.25 m. P = 1 / (-0.25) = -4 D.

Applications and connections

  • Spectacles for correcting myopia (nearsightedness) and hypermetropia (farsightedness)
  • Compound microscopes and astronomical telescopes
  • Camera lenses and projectors
  • Magnifying glasses

Advanced points, exceptions and limitations

  • Chromatic aberration in lenses occurs due to wavelength-dependent focal lengths, corrected using achromatic doublets.
  • Spherical aberration in lenses can be minimized by using crossed lenses or stop apertures.
  • Cylindrical lenses are used to correct astigmatism.

Additional examples

  • Reading glasses used by elderly people
  • Zoom lens of a DSLR camera
  • Objective lens of a school laboratory microscope
Important distinctions
  • Convex lens converges light rays; concave lens diverges light rays.
  • Convex mirror has positive focus while convex lens has positive focus; however, mirror formula uses + (1/v + 1/u) whereas lens formula uses - (1/v - 1/u).
  • Power of convex lens is positive; power of concave lens is negative.
Common misconceptions
  • Confusing mirror formula (1/f = 1/v + 1/u) with lens formula (1/f = 1/v - 1/u).
  • Assuming a convex lens always forms a real image (it forms virtual images when object is between F and optical center).
  • Believing the power of a lens is measured in cm^-1 instead of m^-1 (Diopters).
Exam focus
  • Memorize Lens Maker's Formula and Thin Lens Formula thoroughly.
  • Understand sign conventions for lenses (convex = +f, concave = -f).
  • Practice lens combination power calculations (P = P1 + P2).
  • Know the effect on focal length when a lens is immersed in water.
  • Be ready for numericals on object-image distances and magnification.
Concept 5 · advanced

Dispersion of Light and Formation of Rainbow

Splitting of white light into constituent spectrum components and meteorological formation of primary and secondary rainbows.

Prerequisites

  • Refraction and Snell's law
  • Wavelength dependence of refractive index (Cauchy's relation)
  • Prism refraction mechanics

Definitions and scope

  • Dispersion of light is the splitting of a composite white light beam into its component colors (VIBGYOR) when passing through a dispersive medium.
  • Spectrum is the banded pattern of colors obtained after dispersion.
  • A rainbow is a meteorological optical phenomenon caused by reflection, refraction, and dispersion of sunlight in water droplets.

Step-by-step explanation

  • Refractive index of a medium depends on wavelength: shorter wavelengths (violet) experience greater refractive index and bend more than longer wavelengths (red).
  • Angle of deviation is maximum for violet light and minimum for red light through a glass prism.
  • Newton demonstrated that white light is a mixture of seven colors by recombining a spectrum using an inverted second prism.
  • Primary rainbow is formed by one total internal reflection and two refractions inside water droplets, with red on top and violet on the bottom, subtending an angle of 42° to the observer's eye.
  • Secondary rainbow is formed by two total internal reflections and two refractions, with violet on top and red on the bottom, subtending an angle of 51° and appearing fainter.
  • Sunlight must be behind the observer and water droplets in front for a rainbow to be visible.
  • Raindrops act as tiny spherical prisms refracting, dispersing, and reflecting light.
  • Angular dispersion is the difference in deviation between extreme colors (delta_v - delta_r).

Key points

  • Order of colors in spectrum from bottom to top in a prism: V-I-B-G-Y-O-R (Violet, Indigo, Blue, Green, Yellow, Orange, Red).
  • Red light travels fastest in glass; violet light travels slowest.
  • Primary rainbow has an angular radius of 40° to 42°.
  • Secondary rainbow has an angular radius of 50° to 53° and is dimmer due to extra reflection loss.
  • Artificial rainbows can be observed in garden water sprinklers on a sunny day with the sun at your back.
  • Total internal reflection inside raindrops is critical for redirecting light back toward the observer.
Worked examples
  • A ray of white light strikes a prism at an angle. Which color deviates the most? Solution: Violet light deviates the most because it has the shortest wavelength and highest refractive index in glass.
  • Explain why the sky is blue but clouds are white. Solution: Blue light undergoes intense Rayleigh scattering due to its short wavelength, whereas clouds contain water droplets larger than light wavelengths, causing non-selective Mie scattering of all colors, producing white.

Applications and connections

  • Spectroscopic chemical analysis using prisms and diffraction gratings
  • Meteorological studies of atmospheric moisture and cloud composition
  • Design of optical filters and anti-reflective coatings

Advanced points, exceptions and limitations

  • Cauchy's dispersion formula expresses refractive index as n(lambda) = A + B/lambda^2 + C/lambda^4.
  • Supernumerary rainbows are faint secondary fringes inside the primary rainbow caused by interference of light waves.
  • Rainbow formation can be rigorously analyzed using geometric optics Debye series and Airy's rainbow integral.

Additional examples

  • Sunlight passing through crystal chandeliers producing rainbow patterns on walls
  • Oil film on wet pavement exhibiting spectral colors due to thin-film interference and dispersion
  • Raindrops illuminated by low sun after a summer shower
Important distinctions
  • Primary rainbow involves 1 TIR; secondary rainbow involves 2 TIRs.
  • Primary rainbow has red on outer edge; secondary rainbow has red on inner edge.
  • Scattering depends on particle size relative to wavelength; dispersion depends on wavelength-dependent refractive index of a continuous medium.
Common misconceptions
  • Believing a rainbow is a physical object located at a specific spot in the sky (it is an optical phenomenon relative to the observer's position).
  • Thinking raindrops paint the colors rather than dispersing sunlight already present.
  • Confusing primary and secondary rainbow color order.
Exam focus
  • Remember the VIBGYOR sequence of deviation (Violet deviates most, Red deviates least).
  • Contrast primary and secondary rainbow formation mechanisms (1 TIR vs 2 TIR).
  • State the angles subtended by primary (42°) and secondary (51°) rainbows.
  • Know Newton's prism recombination experiment.
  • Understand the role of dispersion in atmospheric optics.

Concept 1

Reflection of Light and Spherical Mirrors

Definition and scope

  • Bouncing back of light into the same medium.
  • Concave mirrors converge, convex mirrors diverge.
  • Governed by mirror formula 1/f = 1/v + 1/u.

Must remember

  • Angle of incidence equals angle of reflection.
  • Radius of curvature R = 2f.
  • Concave mirror focal length is negative; convex is positive.
  • Real images are inverted; virtual images are erect.
  • Convex mirrors give a wider field of view.

Concept-specific exam traps

  • Forgetting negative sign for concave mirror focal length.
  • Confusing real image position (in front vs behind mirror).
Concept 2

Refraction of Light and Snell's Law

Definition and scope

  • Bending of light across medium boundaries.
  • Refractive index n = c/v.
  • Snell's law: sin i / sin r = constant.

Must remember

  • Rarer to denser bends towards normal.
  • Frequency remains constant during refraction.
  • Water refractive index is 4/3; glass is 3/2.
  • Real depth / apparent depth equals refractive index.
  • Stars twinkle due to continuous atmospheric refraction.

Concept-specific exam traps

  • Assuming frequency changes in a new medium.
  • Confusing optical density with mass density.
Concept 3

Total Internal Reflection (TIR) and Optical Fibers

Definition and scope

  • Complete reflection beyond critical angle.
  • Critical angle sin(ic) = 1/n.
  • Works only from denser to rarer medium.

Must remember

  • Two conditions: denser to rarer medium, i > ic.
  • Critical angle for glass is 42°, water is 48.8°.
  • Optical fibers use core and cladding index difference.
  • Diamond sparkles due to low critical angle (24.4°).
  • Mirage is caused by TIR in hot desert air.

Concept-specific exam traps

  • Applying TIR from rarer to denser medium.
  • Forgetting that critical angle formula assumes surrounding medium is air.
Concept 4

Spherical Lenses and Refraction at Spherical Surfaces

Definition and scope

  • Transparent medium bounded by curved surfaces.
  • Thin lens formula: 1/f = 1/v - 1/u.
  • Power P = 1/f in meters (Diopters).

Must remember

  • Lens maker formula: 1/f = (n - 1)(1/R1 - 1/R2).
  • Convex lens power is positive; concave lens power is negative.
  • Lens immersed in higher index liquid reverses behavior.
  • Combined power of lenses in contact: P = P1 + P2.
  • Ray through optical center goes straight.

Concept-specific exam traps

  • Using mirror plus sign in the lens formula.
  • Forgetting to convert focal length to meters when calculating power.
Concept 5

Dispersion of Light and Formation of Rainbow

Definition and scope

  • Splitting of white light into VIBGYOR spectrum.
  • Caused by wavelength-dependent refractive index.
  • Rainbow involves refraction, dispersion, and TIR.

Must remember

  • Violet deviates most; red deviates least.
  • Primary rainbow: 1 TIR, red on top, 42° angle.
  • Secondary rainbow: 2 TIRs, violet on top, 51° angle.
  • Newton proved white light composition using two prisms.
  • Scattering differs from dispersion.

Concept-specific exam traps

  • Reversing color order between primary and secondary rainbows.
  • Confusing dispersion with scattering.

Quick facts

  • Speed of light in vacuum is exactly 299,792,458 m/s (~3 * 10^8 m/s).
  • Refractive index of diamond is 2.42, highest among common gemstones.
  • Power of a plane mirror is zero (focal length is infinity).
  • Human eye lens is a converging crystalline lens.
  • Myopia is corrected using concave lenses; hypermetropia using convex lenses.
  • Total internal reflection principle is the backbone of modern telecommunication fiber optics.

Glossary

Refractive IndexRatio of speed of light in vacuum to speed of light in a medium.
Critical AngleAngle of incidence in denser medium giving 90 degree refraction angle in rarer medium.
DiopterSI unit of optical power of a lens equal to reciprocal of focal length in meters.
DispersionSeparation of white light into constituent wavelengths due to varying refractive index.
Lateral InversionApparent left-right reversal of an image formed by a plane mirror.

Important distinctions

Concave MirrorvsConvex Lens
  • Concave mirror converges light by reflection; convex lens converges light by refraction.
  • Concave mirror formula has positive sign (1/f = 1/v + 1/u); thin lens formula has negative sign (1/f = 1/v - 1/u).
Primary RainbowvsSecondary Rainbow
  • Primary rainbow involves 1 total internal reflection; secondary involves 2 total internal reflections.
  • Primary rainbow has red on the outer edge (42°); secondary rainbow has red on the inner edge (51°).
ReflectionvsRefraction
  • Reflection occurs when light bounces back into the same medium; refraction occurs when light crosses into a new medium.
  • Angle of incidence equals angle of reflection; angle of incidence rarely equals angle of refraction.

Memory hooks

  • VIBGYOR: Violet bends most (shortest wavelength), Red bends least (longest wavelength).
  • CONCAVE mirror is CAVE-in (inward curving); CONVEX bulges out like a vertex.
  • LENS formula has a MI-NUS sign (1/v - 1/u), while MIRROR formula has a PLUS sign (1/v + 1/u).

Exam traps and cautions

  • Mixing up mirror and lens sign formulas during high-pressure calculations.
  • Forgetting that power requires focal length expressed strictly in meters.
  • Assuming total internal reflection can happen from air to water instead of water to air.
  • Confusing the color arrangement order in primary versus secondary rainbows.

Question 1MCQ

An object is placed at the center of curvature of a concave mirror. Where is the image formed?

When an object is at C, the real, inverted image is also formed at C with equal size.

Question 2MCQ

The refractive index of glass is 1.5. What is the speed of light in glass?

v = c / n = (3 * 10^8) / 1.5 = 2 * 10^8 m/s.

Question 3MCQ

Which of the following conditions is mandatory for Total Internal Reflection to take place?

TIR requires transmission from an optically denser to rarer medium with angle of incidence greater than critical angle.

Question 4MCQ

If two thin lenses of power +2 D and +3 D are kept in contact, what is the focal length of the combination?

Net power P = +2 + +3 = +5 D. Focal length f = 1 / P = 1 / 5 = +0.2 meters.

Question 5MCQ

In a secondary rainbow, which color appears on the outermost boundary of the bow?

In a secondary rainbow, due to two total internal reflections, violet appears on the outer edge and red on the inner edge.

Question 6Assertion–Reason

Assertion (A): Convex mirrors are preferred over plane mirrors as rear-view mirrors in cars. Reason (R): Convex mirrors provide a wider field of view and always produce erect images.

Convex mirrors diverge light, enabling a wide view of traffic behind, making them ideal rear-view mirrors.

Question 7Case-based

A ray of light strikes a glass slab at an angle of incidence of 45° and refracts at an angle of 30°. What is the relative refractive index?

Refractive index n = sin(45°) / sin(30°) = (1 / sqrt(2)) / (1 / 2) = 2 / sqrt(2) = sqrt(2).

Question 8Match the following

Match the lens type with its power sign and focal length nature: A. Convex lens, B. Concave lens. I. Positive power, real focus; II. Negative power, virtual focus.

Convex lenses have positive focal lengths and powers; concave lenses have negative focal lengths and powers.

Question 9MCQ

Which of the following optical phenomena is directly responsible for the sparkling of diamonds?

Diamonds owe their brilliance to multiple total internal reflections resulting from a very small critical angle.

Question 10Case-based

An object is placed 10 cm in front of a convex mirror of focal length 15 cm. Find the image distance.

Using 1/f = 1/v + 1/u => 1/15 = 1/v + 1/(-10) => 1/v = 1/15 + 1/10 = 5/30 = 1/6 => v = +6 cm.
