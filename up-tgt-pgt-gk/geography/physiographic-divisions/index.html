Physiographic Divisions of India

India's major physiographic divisions reflect geological history, tectonic movements, erosion, deposition and coastal or glacial processes.

UP TGT PGT GK6 concepts42 quiz questions10-question test

Topic overview

  • The six broad divisions are the Northern Mountains, Northern Plains, Peninsular Plateau, Indian Desert, Coastal Plains and Islands.
  • Each division differs in relief, rocks, soils, drainage, climate, natural resources, settlement and economic activity.
  • The Himalayas are young fold mountains, the Northern Plains are depositional alluvial plains and the Peninsular Plateau is an ancient stable block.
  • UP examinations frequently test Himalayan divisions, Bhabar-Terai-Bhangar-Khadar, plateau ranges, coastal plains, island groups and physiographic significance.

Concept 1

Northern Mountains: Himalayas and Northeastern Hills

The Northern Mountains are young fold systems with major climatic, hydrological, ecological and strategic significance.

Step-by-step explanation

  • The Trans-Himalaya includes high ranges such as Karakoram, Ladakh and Zanskar and contains glaciers, passes and cold-desert landscapes.
  • The Greater Himalaya or Himadri is the highest and most continuous range, with major peaks, glaciers and headwaters of rivers.
  • The Lesser Himalaya or Himachal contains ranges, valleys, hill stations and more varied settlement and agriculture.
  • The Shiwalik is the southernmost and youngest range, made of unconsolidated sediments and associated with duns and foothill fans.
  • The eastern Himalaya bends southward into Purvanchal hills including Patkai, Naga, Manipur and Mizo ranges.
  • The mountains intercept monsoon winds, generate orographic rainfall, store snow and ice and feed perennial rivers.
  • They contain forests, biodiversity, tourism, hydropower, medicinal resources and strategic passes but are tectonically fragile.
  • Earthquakes, landslides, flash floods, glacier hazards and unplanned construction require risk-sensitive mountain development.

Key points

  • Trans-Himalaya north of main ranges
  • Himadri highest and glaciated
  • Himachal valleys and ranges
  • Shiwalik youngest foothill range
  • Purvanchal eastern continuation.

Concept 2

Northern Plains and Alluvial Belts

The Northern Plains are fertile depositional plains built by the Indus, Ganga and Brahmaputra river systems.

Step-by-step explanation

  • The Northern Plains were formed by alluvial deposits brought by the Indus, Ganga, Brahmaputra and their tributaries.
  • They are broad, flat and agriculturally productive, with dense population, irrigation, transport and urban settlements.
  • Bhabar is a narrow coarse-pebble belt along the foothills where streams often disappear into porous deposits.
  • Terai lies south of Bhabar, has finer deposits, re-emerging streams, high groundwater and historically dense forests or marshy conditions.
  • Bhangar is older alluvium located above the active floodplain and may contain calcareous kankar nodules.
  • Khadar is newer alluvium deposited in active floodplains and is renewed by periodic flooding, making it generally fertile but hazard-prone.
  • The plains are divided broadly into Punjab, Ganga and Brahmaputra sections according to drainage and regional setting.
  • Floods, riverbank erosion, waterlogging, groundwater stress and pollution require basin-level management despite high agricultural value.

Key points

  • Alluvial Indus-Ganga-Brahmaputra plain
  • Bhabar porous foothill belt
  • Terai marshy re-emergence zone
  • Bhangar older alluvium
  • Khadar newer floodplain alluvium.

Concept 3

Peninsular Plateau and the Ghats

The Peninsular Plateau is an ancient stable block with old rocks, mineral resources and dissected uplands.

Step-by-step explanation

  • The Peninsular Plateau is composed largely of ancient crystalline rocks and is one of the oldest landmasses of the Indian subcontinent.
  • The Central Highlands lie north of the Narmada and include the Malwa Plateau, Bundelkhand, Baghelkhand and associated uplands.
  • The Deccan Plateau lies south of the Narmada and is bounded by the Western and Eastern Ghats and slopes broadly eastward.
  • The Western Ghats are higher, more continuous and form an important watershed; they include the Sahyadri and receive heavy orographic rain on the west.
  • The Eastern Ghats are discontinuous and dissected by rivers such as the Mahanadi, Godavari, Krishna and Kaveri.
  • The plateau contains mineral resources, black-soil regions, forests, waterfalls, river valleys and major industrial locations.
  • Peninsular rivers are generally older, more graded and more dependent on seasonal rainfall than Himalayan rivers, though major exceptions and dams exist.
  • Plateau development must consider drought, soil erosion, mining, forest loss and water scarcity.

Key points

  • Ancient crystalline stable block
  • Central Highlands north of Narmada
  • Deccan Plateau south of Narmada
  • Western Ghats continuous watershed
  • Eastern Ghats dissected by rivers.

Concept 4

Indian Desert and Arid Landforms

The Indian Desert is a hot arid region shaped by low rainfall, high evaporation, wind action and seasonal drainage.

Step-by-step explanation

  • The Indian Desert or Thar lies mainly west of the Aravalli range and extends across parts of Rajasthan and adjoining areas.
  • Rainfall is low and highly variable, evaporation is high and temperatures show strong daily and seasonal ranges.
  • Wind action forms sand dunes, including barchans and longitudinal dunes, while sparse vegetation protects soil only in patches.
  • Luni is an important seasonal river that flows toward the Rann region and does not reach the sea as a perennial river.
  • Inland drainage, salt lakes and playas occur where streams terminate in depressions or evaporate.
  • Canal irrigation, groundwater, shelterbelts, solar power, livestock and tourism support desert livelihoods but create water-management challenges.
  • The Aravalli orientation and monsoon wind direction help explain rainfall scarcity in western Rajasthan.
  • Desertification results from climatic variability and human pressure such as overgrazing, vegetation loss, salinity and unsuitable irrigation.

Key points

  • Thar west of Aravalli
  • Low rainfall and high evaporation
  • Sand dunes and wind action
  • Luni seasonal drainage
  • Canal irrigation and desertification issues.

Concept 5

Coastal Plains and Island Groups

India's coastal and island divisions reflect marine processes, river deposition, coral formation and tectonic or volcanic activity.

Step-by-step explanation

  • The Western Coastal Plain is generally narrow because the Western Ghats lie close to the Arabian Sea.
  • It includes the Konkan, Kannad and Malabar coastal sections, with estuaries, lagoons, ports and varied fishing or plantation activity.
  • The Eastern Coastal Plain is broader and more deltaic because large east-flowing rivers deposit sediment along the Bay of Bengal.
  • Northern Circars and Coromandel Coast are broad eastern sections with deltas, lagoons, cyclones and important ports or agriculture.
  • Andaman and Nicobar are a hilly, forested island group associated with a submerged mountain chain and tectonic activity.
  • Barren Island is associated with India's active volcanic activity, while the islands also contain coral reefs and rich marine biodiversity.
  • Lakshadweep consists mainly of low-lying coral islands and atolls with lagoons in the Arabian Sea.
  • Coastal erosion, cyclones, storm surges, sea-level rise, coral bleaching and unplanned tourism threaten coastal and island environments.

Key points

  • West coast narrow with estuaries and lagoons
  • East coast broad and deltaic
  • Andaman-Nicobar hilly and tectonic
  • Lakshadweep coral atolls
  • Cyclones and sea-level hazards.

Concept 6

Physiography, Resources and Environmental Significance

Relief determines drainage, soil, resources, settlement, economy and natural hazards across India.

Step-by-step explanation

  • Mountains provide water towers, forests, biodiversity, hydropower, tourism and strategic passes but are vulnerable to landslides and earthquakes.
  • Plains provide fertile alluvium, dense settlement, transport corridors and irrigation but face floods, erosion, pollution and groundwater stress.
  • Plateaus contain minerals, forests, waterfalls, black soils and industrial resources but face drought, mining impacts and soil erosion.
  • Deserts support specialised pastoralism, irrigation agriculture, solar energy and tourism but are sensitive to water scarcity and desertification.
  • Coasts support ports, fisheries, agriculture, industries and tourism but face cyclones, erosion, storm surges and salinity.
  • Islands provide marine resources, biodiversity and strategic value but have limited land, freshwater and high disaster exposure.
  • River direction and basin form influence dams, irrigation, deltas, estuaries, navigation and flood management.
  • Planning must match infrastructure, settlements and resource use with carrying capacity and hazard zonation.

Key points

  • Mountains - water, forests and hazards
  • Plains - agriculture and floods
  • Plateau - minerals and drought
  • Desert - water scarcity and solar
  • Coasts/islands - marine resources and cyclones.

  • Coastal growth brings ports and markets but increases exposure to cyclones and sea-level change.

  • The same development model is suitable for every physical region.

Important distinctions

Northern Mountains: Himalayas and Northeastern HillsvsNorthern Plains and Alluvial Belts
  • Himadri is high and glaciated; Shiwalik is lower, younger and made of loose sediments.
  • Western Himalaya is generally drier in parts; eastern Himalaya receives heavier monsoon rainfall.
Northern Plains and Alluvial BeltsvsPeninsular Plateau and the Ghats
  • Bhangar is older and above active floodplain; Khadar is newer and renewed by floods.
  • Bhabar absorbs streams; Terai receives their re-emergence and has higher moisture.

Memory hooks

  • Remember Northern Mountains: Himalayas and Northeastern Hills: Trans-Himalaya north of main ranges
  • Remember Northern Plains and Alluvial Belts: Alluvial Indus-Ganga-Brahmaputra plain
  • Remember Peninsular Plateau and the Ghats: Ancient crystalline stable block

Exam traps and cautions

  • The Shiwalik is the highest Himalayan range.
  • All Himalayan regions have identical climate and vegetation.
  • Khadar is always less fertile because it floods.
  • Bhabar and Terai are identical foothill belts.
  • The Eastern Ghats are more continuous and higher than the Western Ghats.
  • All Peninsular Plateau rivers flow westward.
  • The Indian Desert has no rivers or agriculture at all.
  • Every dune is formed by running water rather than wind.

Permeability changes across the foothill belts explain stream behaviour.
