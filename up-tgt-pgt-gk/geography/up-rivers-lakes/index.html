Geography

Rivers, Canals and Dams of Uttar Pradesh

Uttar Pradesh is shaped by the Ganga-Yamuna drainage system and tributaries flowing through the Himalayan foothills, the alluvial plains and the southern plateau.

UP TGT PGT GK6 concepts42 quiz questions10-question test

Topic overview

  • Rivers provide water for irrigation, drinking, industry, fisheries, transport, religious and cultural activities, but also create floods, erosion, pollution and disputes over use.
  • Canals, tube wells, barrages, reservoirs and dams regulate or distribute water for agriculture, cities, industry, flood moderation and power where conditions permit.
  • River geography must be studied through source and direction, tributaries, confluences, basins, floodplains, projects and regional significance.
  • UP examinations frequently test Ganga and Yamuna tributaries, eastern rivers, Bundelkhand rivers, major canals, reservoirs, dams, floods and river conservation.

Concept 1

Ganga, Yamuna and Major Confluences

The Ganga and Yamuna provide the main drainage framework for the plains and connect physical, economic and cultural geography.

Step-by-step explanation

  • The Ganga enters and flows across the northern plain of UP, receiving tributaries and supporting dense agriculture, cities and cultural centres.
  • The Yamuna is a major river of western and southern UP and joins the Ganga at Prayagraj, a major confluence and cultural location.
  • The Ganga-Yamuna doab is the fertile tract between the two rivers and has long supported settlement, farming, irrigation and transport.
  • Tributaries, floodplains, levees, meanders and depositional surfaces create local differences in soil, drainage, settlement and hazard.
  • Rivers supply irrigation, drinking water, fisheries, sand and cultural services but face sewage, industrial discharge, solid waste and over-abstraction.
  • River flow changes seasonally with monsoon rainfall, snowmelt contribution, groundwater interaction, barrages and water withdrawals.
  • Floodplain encroachment and loss of wetlands increase flood damage and reduce natural storage and ecological functions.
  • Ganga-Yamuna planning requires sewage treatment, ecological flow, riparian protection, flood zoning, monitoring and participation by cities and communities.

Key points

  • Ganga principal river
  • Yamuna and Prayagraj confluence
  • Ganga-Yamuna doab
  • Floodplain and tributary processes
  • Pollution and ecological flow.

Concept 2

Ghaghara, Gomti, Rapti and Eastern Rivers

Northern and eastern rivers carry high monsoon flows and support fertile plains while creating major flood and drainage challenges.

Step-by-step explanation

  • The Ghaghara is a major Himalayan-fed river system associated with northern and eastern UP and receives tributaries before joining the Ganga system.
  • The Rapti drains Terai and eastern areas and can produce flood conditions when monsoon rainfall, upstream flow and local drainage combine.
  • The Gomti flows through central UP and the Lucknow region, supporting settlements and agriculture while facing urban pollution and encroachment.
  • Eastern river plains are fertile but low-lying areas may experience waterlogging, bank erosion, channel shifts and embankment pressure.
  • High sediment load and changing channels can alter riverbanks, bridges, farmland and settlement safety.
  • Flood management includes forecasting, warning, evacuation, raised shelters, drainage, embankment maintenance, safe land use and resilient infrastructure.
  • Wetlands, floodplain ponds and natural storage reduce peak impacts and support fish, birds, groundwater and local livelihoods.
  • Eastern river planning must combine infrastructure with basin-level coordination and attention to vulnerable households, farms and roads.

Key points

  • Himalayan-fed eastern flows
  • Ghaghara and Rapti flood risk
  • Gomti and urban pollution
  • Sediment and channel change
  • Floodplain storage and warning.

Concept 3

Chambal, Betwa, Ken, Son and Plateau Drainage

Southern UP rivers reflect plateau relief, ravines, seasonal flow, water scarcity and reservoir-based planning.

Step-by-step explanation

  • Chambal, Betwa and Ken are associated with the Yamuna drainage and flow through or along the Bundelkhand and southern upland region.
  • Plateau relief, rocky beds, ravines and seasonal rainfall produce different channel and storage conditions from the alluvial Ganga plain.
  • The Betwa supports irrigation and reservoir projects, including inter-State systems important to Bundelkhand water management.
  • The Ken basin is associated with ravine, forest and drought-prone landscapes where water, biodiversity and development decisions need careful assessment.
  • The Son drains the south-eastern upland margin and contributes to the wider Ganga system, with reservoirs and irrigation significance in the region.
  • Ravines develop through erosion and can reduce cultivable land, increase runoff and complicate roads, farming and settlement.
  • Watershed development, check dams, farm ponds, recharge, soil conservation and crop planning complement large reservoirs.
  • Plateau river projects must consider displacement, forest, wildlife, sediment, downstream flow, inter-State coordination and livelihood effects.

Key points

  • Yamuna tributaries in southern UP
  • Bundelkhand relief and water stress
  • Ravine erosion
  • Reservoir and watershed planning
  • Inter-State river coordination.

  • Large dams store water; watershed measures improve local infiltration and reduce runoff.

Concept 4

Canals, Barrages and Irrigation Networks

Canals and barrages distribute river water and support intensive agriculture, but their benefits depend on maintenance, equity and drainage.

Step-by-step explanation

  • The Upper Ganga Canal and Lower Ganga Canal systems distribute water across command areas and support agriculture in parts of the plains.
  • Yamuna-linked and Sharda or eastern canal systems add to the irrigation network where engineering, water availability and command conditions permit.
  • A barrage diverts or regulates river water and differs from a reservoir dam that primarily stores water behind a larger structure.
  • Canal irrigation can increase cropping intensity, stabilise yields and reduce monsoon dependence.
  • Poor maintenance, siltation, seepage, illegal outlets, unequal distribution and tail-end shortages reduce canal performance.
  • Excess irrigation without drainage can create waterlogging, salinity and groundwater imbalance.
  • Lining, desilting, rotational supply, field channels, micro-irrigation, drainage and participatory water management improve efficiency.
  • Irrigation planning must match crop choice with available water and protect downstream ecological flow and users.

Key points

  • Upper and Lower Ganga canals
  • Barrage diverts or regulates water
  • Command area and tail-end issue
  • Waterlogging and salinity
  • Maintenance and equitable distribution.
Worked examples

  • A barrage controls or diverts flow; a reservoir dam stores a larger volume behind an impounded structure.

  • A barrage and a dam are exactly the same structure.

Concept 5

Dams, Reservoirs and Multipurpose Projects

Reservoirs and dams store or regulate water for irrigation, supply, power and flood management, with social and ecological trade-offs.

Step-by-step explanation

  • Rihand reservoir or Govind Ballabh Pant Sagar is a major southern UP water and power-related project associated with the Rihand basin.
  • Matatila and Betwa-linked projects support storage, irrigation and regional water management across the UP-Madhya Pradesh area.
  • Bansagar is an inter-State multipurpose project in the Son basin with irrigation, water and power significance for connected regions.
  • Multipurpose projects may provide irrigation, drinking water, hydropower, industrial supply, fisheries, tourism or flood moderation.
  • Benefits depend on command-area distribution, canal completion, maintenance, sediment management and reliable operation.
  • Large projects can displace communities, submerge forests or farmland, alter downstream flows, trap sediment and affect fish and river ecology.
  • Drought-prone regions need a portfolio of reservoirs, small structures, recharge, watershed work, efficient irrigation and demand management.
  • Project evaluation should include social impact, rehabilitation, environmental flow, downstream rights, safety and climate-resilience planning.

Key points

  • Rihand-Govind Ballabh Pant Sagar
  • Betwa and Matatila systems
  • Bansagar and Son basin
  • Multipurpose benefits
  • Displacement and ecological costs.

  • A dam is a structure; a reservoir is the stored water body behind or associated with it.
  • Large storage offers regional regulation; small water harvesting offers local recharge and flexibility.

  • A multipurpose project has benefits without social or ecological costs.

Concept 6

Floods, Pollution and Integrated Water Management

Sustainable river management must address water quantity, quality, hazards, ecosystems and competing users together.

Step-by-step explanation

  • Floods result from intense monsoon rainfall, upstream flow, high river stage, sedimentation, drainage obstruction, embankment failure and floodplain occupation.
  • Drought and water scarcity arise from irregular rainfall, groundwater decline, high demand, poor storage, inefficient irrigation and land degradation.
  • River pollution comes from untreated sewage, industrial effluent, agricultural runoff, solid waste, religious waste and reduced flow or dilution.
  • Treatment plants must function, sewer networks must connect, industries need compliance and river monitoring must identify pollution sources.
  • Riparian vegetation, wetlands, ponds, floodplains and groundwater recharge areas are part of a river system and should not be treated as vacant land.
  • Early warning, evacuation routes, raised infrastructure, crop insurance, resilient bridges and community drills reduce flood losses.
  • Water allocation should balance drinking water, food, industry, ecology, fisheries, downstream users and future generations.
  • Integrated river-basin management coordinates surface water, groundwater, land use, cities, farms, dams, pollution control, ecosystems and neighbouring States.

Key points

  • Flood causes and warning
  • Drought and groundwater stress
  • Sewage and industrial pollution
  • Wetlands and riparian buffers
  • Basin-level coordination.
Worked examples
  • Design a flood-resilient village plan
  • List river-pollution controls

Additional examples

  • Design a flood-resilient village plan
  • List river-pollution controls
Important distinctions
  • Flood control manages excess water; drought management secures water through storage, recharge and demand reduction.
  • Water quantity planning is incomplete without water quality and ecosystem protection.
Common misconceptions
  • Floods can be eliminated by embankments alone.
  • A sewage treatment plant works without collection, operation and monitoring.
