Indian History

Governor-Generals, Viceroys and Constitutional Acts

British administration in India developed through a sequence of parliamentary laws, Company regulations, Governor-General policies and constitutional reforms.

UP TGT PGT GK6 concepts42 quiz questions10-question test

Topic overview

  • British administration in India developed through a sequence of parliamentary laws, Company regulations, Governor-General policies and constitutional reforms.
  • The Regulating Act, Pitt India Act, Charter Acts, Government of India Acts and Indian Councils Acts changed the relationship between the Company, Crown, executive and legislatures.
  • Governor-Generals and Viceroys are tested through their reforms, wars, annexations, economic policies, education measures and association with national events.
  • The constitutional process gradually introduced councils, representation, provincial devolution and limited responsible government without removing imperial control until independence.
  • The most reliable revision method is a chronology table connecting every major office-holder or Act with its distinctive provision and historical context.

Concept 1

Company Rule and Early Parliamentary Control

Parliament gradually intervened in Company administration as military expansion and corruption made private rule a British political concern.

Step-by-step explanation

  • The Regulating Act of 1773 was the first major parliamentary intervention in Company rule and placed the Bengal presidency in a superior position.
  • The Governor-General of Bengal received authority over certain matters of the other presidencies, though the arrangement remained incomplete and conflict-prone.
  • The Act created a Supreme Court at Calcutta, producing jurisdictional tensions between the court, Governor-General and officials.
  • Warren Hastings was the first Governor-General of Bengal under the new framework and faced wars, revenue problems and administrative conflict.
  • Pitt India Act of 1784 created dual control: the Court of Directors handled commercial management while the Board of Control supervised political affairs.
  • The Company remained a commercial body, but its territorial administration increasingly came under ministerial and parliamentary oversight.
  • Charter Acts renewed the Company charter and gradually reduced its monopoly, administrative autonomy and commercial role.
  • These Acts show the transition from private trading power to regulated imperial government.

Key points

  • Regulating Act-1773
  • Governor-General of Bengal
  • Supreme Court at Calcutta
  • Pitt India Act-1784
  • Dual control.

Concept 2

Cornwallis, Bentinck and Administrative-Social Reform

The late eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries saw changes in revenue, civil service, justice, education and social policy.

Step-by-step explanation

  • Cornwallis reorganised the civil service, judicial administration and police and is associated with the Cornwallis Code.
  • The Permanent Settlement of 1793 fixed the Company demand from zamindars in Bengal while transforming property and revenue relations.
  • Cornwallis reforms gave senior posts and higher salaries mainly to Europeans, creating a racially unequal administrative structure.
  • Charter Act 1813 ended most Company trade monopoly except tea and China trade and opened space for missionaries and education expenditure.
  • William Bentinck is associated with abolition of sati in 1829, suppression of Thuggee and measures of administrative economy.
  • The English Education Act or policy of 1835 under Bentinck and Macaulay shifted official educational emphasis toward English and Western knowledge.
  • Charter Act 1833 made the Governor-General of Bengal the Governor-General of India and centralised legislative power.
  • Reforms should be studied with their stated objectives, social effects, limits and relationship to colonial control.

Key points

  • Cornwallis Code
  • Permanent Settlement-1793
  • Charter Act-1813
  • Abolition of sati-1829
  • Charter Act-1833 and centralisation.

Concept 3

Expansion, Dalhousie and the Background to 1857

Military expansion, annexation, administrative change and infrastructure under the Company created both integration and deep resentment.

Step-by-step explanation

  • Subsidiary Alliance under Lord Wellesley expanded British influence by requiring Indian rulers to accept British troops and restrict external relations.
  • Lord Hastings continued expansion and strengthened Company supremacy after the Anglo-Maratha conflicts.
  • Dalhousie used the Doctrine of Lapse to annex states where the ruler died without a biological male heir accepted by the Company.
  • Satara, Jhansi and Nagpur became important examples associated with annexation policy, while Awadh was annexed in 1856 on grounds of alleged misgovernment.
  • Railways, telegraph, postal reforms, public works and the educational or administrative network increased connectivity and state capacity.
  • Annexation, loss of privileges, interference in succession, revenue pressure, military grievances and social fears contributed to the background of the Revolt of 1857.
  • Infrastructure had practical benefits and also served troop movement, communication, revenue collection and imperial control.
  • A balanced answer distinguishes immediate military causes of 1857 from wider political, economic, social and administrative causes.

Key points

  • Wellesley and Subsidiary Alliance
  • Dalhousie and Doctrine of Lapse
  • Awadh annexation-1856
  • Railways and telegraph
  • Background to 1857.

Concept 4

Crown Rule and the Development of Legislative Councils

After 1858 the Crown reorganised administration and slowly expanded councils without granting responsible government.

Step-by-step explanation

  • Government of India Act 1858 transferred authority from the East India Company to the British Crown after the Revolt of 1857.
  • The Secretary of State for India in London, assisted by a council, became the main imperial authority, while the Viceroy represented the Crown in India.
  • Queen Victoria Proclamation promised non-interference in religion, equal protection in principle and respect for treaties, though practice remained unequal.
  • The Indian Councils Act 1861 restored legislative councils and allowed limited non-official Indian participation through nomination.
  • The 1861 Act recognised portfolio administration and enabled legislative decentralisation to provinces, but the executive retained control.
  • The Indian Councils Act 1892 enlarged councils and allowed indirect election or recommendation, yet members had limited power over budgets and questions.
  • The reforms introduced representation without responsible government, and official majorities or executive safeguards protected imperial authority.
  • Councils became platforms for political criticism and helped nationalists develop constitutional demands and public debate.

Key points

  • Government of India Act-1858
  • Secretary of State
  • Viceroy
  • Indian Councils Act-1861
  • Indian Councils Act-1892.

Concept 5

Curzon, Morley-Minto Reforms and Communal Representation

Administrative centralisation and new representative arrangements shaped nationalism and political competition in the early twentieth century.

Step-by-step explanation

  • Lord Curzon pursued administrative centralisation, university and police measures and partitioned Bengal in 1905.
  • The Partition of Bengal triggered Swadeshi, boycott, national education and wider nationalist mobilisation and was annulled in 1911.
  • Curzon also supported archaeological conservation and administrative surveys, showing that his legacy included both institutional work and political repression.
  • The Indian Councils Act 1909 is known as the Morley-Minto Reforms after Secretary of State John Morley and Viceroy Lord Minto.
  • The 1909 reforms expanded legislative councils and allowed more discussion, but executive responsibility remained absent.
  • Separate electorates for Muslims were introduced in the 1909 reforms and shaped later constitutional and communal politics.
  • The reforms responded partly to political pressure but also categorised representation through communal and official interests.
  • Exam answers should separate Curzon policies from the later 1909 constitutional provisions and avoid treating all reforms as democratic self-rule.

Key points

  • Curzon-1899 to 1905
  • Partition of Bengal-1905
  • Annulment-1911
  • Morley-Minto-1909
  • Separate electorates.

Concept 6

Montagu-Chelmsford, Government of India Act 1935 and Transfer of Power

The late colonial constitutional process expanded provincial institutions but retained imperial safeguards until independence and partition.

Step-by-step explanation

  • The Government of India Act 1919 followed the Montagu-Chelmsford proposals and introduced dyarchy in the provinces.
  • Under dyarchy, transferred subjects were handled by Indian ministers while reserved subjects remained with the Governor and executive officials.
  • The 1919 Act expanded legislatures and introduced a limited franchise, but the central executive remained responsible to the Viceroy rather than the legislature.
  • The Simon Commission and Round Table Conferences exposed the limits of the 1919 framework and intensified demands for constitutional change.
  • Government of India Act 1935 proposed an all-India federation, introduced provincial autonomy and divided powers through federal, provincial and concurrent lists.
  • The 1935 Act expanded the franchise and enabled elected provincial ministries after the 1937 elections, although governors retained special powers.
  • Congress ministries resigned in 1939 after India was declared at war without consultation, linking constitutional questions with the national movement.
  • The Indian Independence Act 1947 ended British sovereignty, created the Dominions of India and Pakistan and accompanied partition and transfer of power.

Key points

  • Dyarchy-1919
  • Reserved and transferred subjects
  • Provincial autonomy-1935
  • 1937 ministries
  • Independence Act-1947.
Worked examples
  • Compare dyarchy and provincial autonomy
  • Arrange 1919-1935-1947

  • Compare dyarchy and provincial autonomy
  • Arrange 1919-1935-1947

  • Dyarchy divided provincial subjects between ministers and officials; provincial autonomy removed that formal division but retained gubernatorial safeguards.
  • The 1935 Act expanded institutions; the 1947 Act transferred sovereignty and created new Dominions.

  • The 1935 federation was fully implemented before independence.
