Topic overview

  • The Bahmani Sultanate was established in 1347 CE by Ala-ud-Din Bahman Shah (Hasan Gangu) in the Deccan, initiating a prolonged phase of regional conflict and cultural synthesis.
  • Krishnadevaraya of the Tuluva dynasty marked the golden age of Vijayanagara, renowned for territorial expansion, Telugu literature, and monumental temple architecture.
  • Hampi, the UNESCO World Heritage site, served as the majestic capital of Vijayanagara, showcasing unique Dravidian rock-cut and structural monuments.

Concept 1 · foundation

Origins and Four Dynasties of Vijayanagara

The Vijayanagara Empire was sustained across four successive dynasties that defended Hindu polity in South India.

Prerequisites

  • Decline of the Hoysala, Kakatiya, and Yadava kingdoms in the 14th century.
  • Expansionist policies of Sultan Muhammad bin Tughlaq in the Deccan.

Definitions and scope

  • Vijayanagara means 'City of Victory'.
  • The empire was ruled by four distinct dynasties: Sangama, Saluva, Tuluva, and Aravidu.

Step-by-step explanation

  • Harihara I and Bukka Raya I, sons of Sangama, established the empire in 1336 CE with the blessings of saint Vidyaranya.
  • The Sangama dynasty (1336–1485 CE) consolidated control over the Tungabhadra-Krishna doab and Madurai.
  • The Saluva dynasty (1485–1505 CE) was founded by Saluva Narasimha to restore order after Sangama decline.
  • The Tuluva dynasty (1505–1570 CE) reached the absolute zenith of military glory and cultural output under Krishnadevaraya.
  • The Aravidu dynasty (1570–1650s CE) maintained a truncated empire from Penukonda and Chandragiri after the Battle of Talikota.
  • Foreign travelers like Ibn Battuta (Morocco), Nicolo Conti (Italy), Abdur Razzaq (Persia), and Domingo Paes (Portugal) visited the empire.
  • Their accounts provide invaluable details on urban planning, markets, court life, and military strength.
  • The empire served as a vital bulwark protecting peninsular temple culture from northern conquests.

Key points

  • Four dynasties: Sangama, Saluva, Tuluva, Aravidu.
  • Founders: Harihara I and Bukka Raya I (1336 CE).
  • Spiritual mentor: Vidyaranya and commentator Sayana.
  • First capital: Anegondi; major capital: Vijayanagara (Hampi).
  • Final ruling house: Aravidu dynasty post-Talikota.
  • Primary foreign visitors: Nicolo Conti, Abdur Razzaq, Paes, Nuniz.
Worked examples
  • Chronological ordering test: Sangama (1336) -> Saluva (1485) -> Tuluva (1505) -> Aravidu (1570).
  • Traveler matching: Abdur Razzaq came during Deva Raya II's reign; Domingo Paes visited during Krishnadevaraya's reign.

Applications and connections

  • Answering direct chronological matching questions in UP TGT/PGT history papers.
  • Analyzing foreign traveler accounts as primary historical source material.

Advanced points, exceptions and limitations

  • Debates among historians regarding the exact Kannada or Telugu ethnic origins of the founding brothers.
  • The transition from centralized Sangama rule to decentralized feudatory governance under later Tuluva rulers.

Additional examples

  • Deva Raya II of the Sangama dynasty employing Muslim cavalrymen in his army.
  • Saluva Narasimha seizing power to save the realm from administrative paralysis.
Important distinctions
  • Sangama dynasty focused on initial consolidation; Tuluva dynasty achieved imperial expansion.
  • Aravidu dynasty operated as a decentralized regional power compared to the centralized Tuluva administration.
Common misconceptions
  • Assuming Krishnadevaraya belonged to the Sangama dynasty instead of the Tuluva dynasty.
  • Believing Vijayanagara collapsed entirely immediately after the Battle of Talikota.
Exam focus
  • Memorize the exact order of the four dynasties.
  • Identify the founders of each dynasty for matching questions.
  • Note the foreign travelers associated with specific monarchs.
Concept 2 · core

Krishnadevaraya and the Tuluva Dynasty

Krishnadevaraya, known as Andhra Bhoja, brought the Vijayanagara Empire to its greatest political and cultural pinnacle.

Prerequisites

  • Rise of the Tuluva dynasty under Vira Narasimha.
  • Geopolitical rivalry over the Raichur Doab with the Bijapur Sultanate.

Definitions and scope

  • Ashtadiggajas: The eight celebrated Telugu poets in Krishnadevaraya's court.
  • Amuktamalyada: Epic poem in Telugu composed by Krishnadevaraya.

Step-by-step explanation

  • Krishnadevaraya reigned from 1509 to 1529 CE, representing the golden age of Vijayanagara.
  • He successfully defeated Adil Shah of Bijapur in the Battle of Raichur (1520 CE) and captured Gulbarga.
  • He maintained friendly diplomatic relations with the Portuguese governor Albuquerque, securing monopoly over horse imports.
  • His domestic policy focused on agrarian welfare, constructing large irrigation tanks like Kamalapur tank with Portuguese engineering assistance.
  • He was an accomplished scholar, writing 'Amuktamalyada' in Telugu and 'Jambavati Kalyanam' in Sanskrit.
  • His court featured the 'Ashtadiggajas', prominently including Allasani Peddana (Manucharitramu), hailed as the grandfather of Telugu poetry.
  • He constructed the famous Hazara Rama temple and Vitthala temple complex at Hampi.
  • He practiced religious tolerance, patronizing Vaishnavism, Shaivism, and Jainism alike.

Key points

  • Reign period: 1509–1529 CE.
  • Title: Andhra Bhoja, Abhinava Bhoja.
  • Chief literary work: Amuktamalyada (Telugu).
  • Sanskrit drama: Jambavati Kalyanam.
  • Chief poet: Allasani Peddana (Andhra Kavitapitamaha).
  • Portuguese contact: Affonso de Albuquerque.
Worked examples
  • Identify author: Amuktamalyada was written by Krishnadevaraya, not Allasani Peddana.
  • Military campaign sequence: Siege of Udayagiri -> Capture of Raichur Doab -> Invasion of Kalinga against Prataparudra Gajapati.

Applications and connections

  • Answering questions on medieval Indian literature and royal patronage in UP PGT exams.
  • Evaluating the geopolitical balance of power in the 16th-century Deccan.

Advanced points, exceptions and limitations

  • Examining the socio-political significance of Krishnadevaraya's administrative edicts inscribed on temple walls.
  • Analyzing the impact of horse trade monopolies with Portuguese Goa on Vijayanagara military superiority.

Additional examples

  • Allasani Peddana composing 'Manucharitramu'.
  • Paes describing Krishnadevaraya's physical appearance and daily routine in glowing terms.
Important distinctions
  • Krishnadevaraya's literary output in Telugu contrasted with Sanskrit-dominated court culture of earlier dynasties.
  • Aggressive offensive strategy against Deccan Sultans contrasted with defensive postures of later Aravidu rulers.
Common misconceptions
  • Confusing Krishnadevaraya's Telugu masterpiece 'Amuktamalyada' with a Sanskrit text.
  • Believing Krishnadevaraya was the founder of the Tuluva dynasty instead of its third ruler.
Exam focus
  • Remember all eight Ashtadiggajas or at least Allasani Peddana and Tenali Ramakrishna.
  • Memorize his specific titles like Andhra Bhoja.
  • Note his diplomatic ties with the Portuguese.
Concept 3 · core

Hampi Architecture and Cultural Achievements

Hampi architecture represents the ultimate evolution of the Dravidian style, characterized by monolithic pillars, ornate mandapas, and stone chariots.

Prerequisites

  • Evolution of Dravidian temple architecture under the Pallavas, Cholas, and Hoysalas.
  • Techniques of granite quarrying and rock-cut sculpture.

Definitions and scope

  • Kalyana Mandapa: Ornate pillared halls dedicated to divine marriages within temple complexes.
  • Stone Chariot: Iconic monolithic shrine structure inside the Vittthala Temple complex.

Step-by-step explanation

  • The Vijayanagara architectural style is a synthesis of Chola, Hoysala, Pandya, and Chalukya traditions.
  • Temples are enclosed within massive compounds featuring tall gopurams (gateway towers).
  • Pillars frequently feature intricate carvings of rearing horses, yalis (mythical beasts), and charging warriors.
  • The Virupaksha Temple is the oldest shrine at Hampi, dedicated to Lord Shiva, continuously functional since the 7th century.
  • The Vittthala Temple is celebrated for its musical pillars that produce resonant metallic sounds when tapped.
  • Secular architecture includes the Lotus Mahal, Elephant Stables, and the Great Platform (Mahanavami Dibba).
  • Sculpture embraced both massive monolithic statues, such as the Lakshmi Narasimha monolith, and delicate ivory-like stone carvings.
  • Music, dance (Bharatanatyam and Yakshagana), and Carnatic music flourished under royal and temple patronage.

Key points

  • Key architectural features: Tall gopurams, Kalyan Mandapas, ornate monolithic pillars.
  • UNESCO Status: Hampi inscribed as a World Heritage Site in 1986.
  • Notable monuments: Virupaksha Temple, Vittthala Temple, Lotus Mahal, Elephant Stables.
  • Iconic symbol: Stone Chariot of Vittthala Temple.
  • Material used: Hard grey-pink archaean granite.
  • Secular structures: Mahanavami Dibba, Stepped Tank.
Worked examples
  • Monument identification: If a question mentions 'musical pillars' and a 'stone chariot', the correct answer is Vittthala Temple at Hampi.
  • Secular architecture identification: Lotus Mahal and Elephant Stables exemplify Indo-Islamic architectural synthesis in Vijayanagara.

Applications and connections

  • Answering art and culture questions in UP TGT/PGT Fine Arts and History sections.
  • Recognizing UNESCO World Heritage monuments in India.

Advanced points, exceptions and limitations

  • The architectural transition from soapstone (used by Hoysalas) to hard granite due to local geological availability.
  • Incorporate Islamic arch and dome motifs into secular royal structures like the Lotus Mahal.

Additional examples

  • The monolithic Lakshmi Narasimha statue carved out of a single boulder.
  • The grand celebrations depicted on the bas-reliefs of Mahanavami Dibba.
Important distinctions
  • Vijayanagara pillars with rearing yalis contrasted with simpler Chola pillars.
  • Granite durability of Hampi architecture contrasted with softer sandstone used in North Indian monuments.
Common misconceptions
  • Believing Hampi was built entirely during the reign of a single king instead of evolving across centuries.
  • Assuming the stone chariot is a real functional vehicle rather than a shrine.
Exam focus
  • Match monuments like Vittthala Temple and Virupaksha Temple with Hampi.
  • Identify the architectural hallmark: Kalyan Mandapa and musical pillars.
  • Note the primary building material (granite).
Concept 4 · application

Vijayanagara Administration, Society, and Economy

The Nayankara system and Ayagar system formed the administrative backbone of Vijayanagara, supported by robust maritime trade and agrarian taxation.

Prerequisites

  • Feudal administrative structures of the Chola and Kakatiya empires.
  • Concept of provincial governance and land revenue assessment.

Definitions and scope

  • Nayankara System: Feudal military tenure where chiefs (Nayaks) maintained troops in exchange for land assignments (Amaram).
  • Ayagar System: Hereditary village functionaries (12 officials) managing local administration.

Step-by-step explanation

  • The king was the supreme head of state, assisted by a council of ministers headed by the Mahapradhani.
  • The empire was divided into provinces (Mandalas), headed by Mandaleshvaras, further subdivided into Nadus, Sthalas, and Gramas.
  • The Nayankara system mirrored the North Indian Iqta system; Nayaks remitted a fixed financial tribute and provided military contingents.
  • The Ayagars were a group of twelve village officers who managed local security, land records, and judicial matters independently.
  • Land revenue was typically one-sixth of agricultural produce, assessed according to soil fertility and irrigation availability.
  • Foreign trade flourished through ports like Bhatkal, Mangalore, and Cannanore, exporting spices, textiles, and precious stones, and importing horses from Arabia.
  • Society was marked by rigid caste hierarchies, yet women held prominent roles as wrestlers, astrologers, accountants, and royal bodyguards.
  • Religious pluralism prevailed, though Sri Vaishnavism and Veerashaivism enjoyed widespread royal patronage.

Key points

  • Administrative backbone: Nayankara and Ayagar systems.
  • Provincial units: Mandalas -> Nadus -> Sthalas -> Gramas.
  • Land revenue standard: One-sixth (shadbhaga) of produce.
  • Major export items: Spices, cotton textiles, precious gems.
  • Major import items: Warhorses from Arabia and Persia, silk, copper.
  • Position of women: High social standing, participation in sports, literature, and warfare.
Worked examples
  • System matching: If asked about hereditary village functionaries, the correct term is the Ayagar system.
  • Revenue calculation: On a land yielding 60 bags of paddy, standard state share under Vijayanagara norms was 10 bags.

Applications and connections

  • Answering conceptual administrative questions in UP PGT History examinations.
  • Comparing medieval South Indian feudalism with North Indian Sultanate models.

Advanced points, exceptions and limitations

  • The gradual rise of independent Nayaks (like the Nayaks of Madurai and Tanjore) who undermined central imperial authority.
  • The socio-economic tension between 'Vangai' (left-hand) and 'Daddai' (right-hand) caste factions in rural areas.

Additional examples

  • Nayaks maintaining armed retinues of thousands of soldiers to serve the emperor.
  • Portuguese travelers marveling at the bustling diamond markets of Hampi.
Important distinctions
  • Nayankara system (hereditary military assignments) contrasted with Delhi Sultanate Iqta system (transferable revenue grants).
  • Ayagar system (village-level collective management) contrasted with centralized North Indian Kotwal administration.
Common misconceptions
  • Assuming Nayaks were mere tax collectors rather than powerful military chieftains with judicial autonomy.
  • Believing women were completely secluded in purdah in South Indian society.
Exam focus
  • Master definitions of Nayankara and Ayagar systems.
  • Recall provincial hierarchy (Mandalas to Gramas).
  • Note the role of foreign trade and horse imports.
Concept 5 · advanced

Bahmani Kingdom and the Deccan Sultanates

The Bahmani Sultanate, founded in 1347 CE, fractured into five Deccan Sultanates, engaging in perpetual power struggles with Vijayanagara.

Prerequisites

  • Tughlaq imperial overextension in the Deccan under Muhammad bin Tughlaq.
  • Geographical significance of the Raichur Doab and Krishna-Tungabhadra basin.

Definitions and scope

  • Bahmani Sultanate: First independent Islamic kingdom in South India.
  • Deccan Sultanates: Five successor states - Bijapur, Golconda, Ahmednagar, Bidar, and Berar.

Step-by-step explanation

  • Ala-ud-Din Bahman Shah (Hasan Gangu) founded the Bahmani Sultanate in 1347 CE with Gulbarga as its capital, later shifted to Bidar by Ahmad Shah.
  • Muhammad Gawan, the brilliant Persian prime minister of the Bahmani kingdom, introduced administrative reforms, dividing the realm into eight tarafs (provinces).
  • Gawan promoted higher learning by building a magnificent madrasa (college) at Bidar stocked with thousands of manuscripts.
  • Internal factionalism between 'Afaqis' (foreign immigrants from Persia/Iraq) and 'Dakhanis' (local Muslims) weakened the central authority.
  • By the early 16th century, the Bahmani kingdom fractured into five distinct sultanates: Adil Shahi of Bijapur, Qutb Shahi of Golconda, Nizam Shahi of Ahmednagar, Imad Shahi of Berar, and Barid Shahi of Bidar.
  • These sultanates formed a grand coalition that decisively defeated Vijayanagara at the Battle of Talikota in 1565 CE.
  • Architectural synthesis flourished in the Deccan, producing masterpieces like the Gol Gumbaz in Bijapur (famous for its whispering gallery) and the Charminar in Hyderabad.
  • Deccan painting (Deccani school) developed distinct Persian-indigenous color palettes and compositional styles.

Key points

  • Founder: Ala-ud-Din Bahman Shah (Hasan Gangu) in 1347 CE.
  • Capitals: Gulbarga initially, later Bidar.
  • Prime Minister reformer: Mahmud Gawan.
  • Five successor states: Bijapur, Golconda, Ahmednagar, Bidar, Berar.
  • Decisive battle: Battle of Talikota (1565 CE).
  • Architectural marvels: Gol Gumbaz (Bijapur), Charminar (Hyderabad).
Worked examples
  • Sultanate matching: Adil Shahi dynasty ruled Bijapur; Qutb Shahi dynasty ruled Golconda.
  • Reform identification: Mahmud Gawan curtailed provincial governor powers by reducing taraf sizes and placing royal garrisons in forts.

Applications and connections

  • Answering questions on medieval Deccan history in UP TGT/PGT examinations.
  • Analyzing Indo-Islamic art, architecture, and administrative evolution.

Advanced points, exceptions and limitations

  • The geopolitical interplay where Deccan Sultans sometimes allied with Vijayanagara against rival Muslim states.
  • The economic importance of Golconda diamond mines and Bijapur's maritime ports.

Additional examples

  • Muhammad Gawan executing administrative audits to eliminate corruption.
  • Construction of Gol Gumbaz featuring the second-largest dome in the world without pillars.
Important distinctions
  • Bahmani centralized administration under Mahmud Gawan contrasted with fragmented Sultanate rule post-1518.
  • Indo-Islamic Deccan architecture contrasted with Dravidian granite architecture of Vijayanagara.
Common misconceptions
  • Believing the Bahmani kingdom survived until the Mughal conquest of Aurangzeb intact without splitting.
  • Confusing the Battle of Talikota as a direct clash between only one Sultanate and Vijayanagara.
Exam focus
  • Remember the founder Hasan Gangu and reformer Mahmud Gawan.
  • Memorize the names of the five Deccan Sultanates and their capitals.
  • Note key monuments like Gol Gumbaz and Charminar.

  • Victory in Battle of Raichur (1520 CE).
  • Contact with Portuguese governor Albuquerque.
  • Construction of Hazara Rama and Vitthala temples.
  • Allasani Peddana as Andhra Kavitapitamaha.

Concept-specific exam traps

  • Attributing Amuktamalyada to Allasani Peddana instead of Krishnadevaraya.
  • Placing Krishnadevaraya in the Sangama dynasty.
Concept 3

Hampi Architecture and Cultural Achievements

Definition and scope

  • Masterpiece of Dravidian architecture using hard granite.
  • Characterized by tall gopurams, Kalyan Mandapas, and ornate monolithic pillars.
  • Inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage site in 1986.

Must remember

  • Vittthala Temple musical pillars and stone chariot.
  • Virupaksha Temple as the oldest shrine.
  • Secular monuments like Lotus Mahal and Elephant Stables.
  • Mahanavami Dibba platform.
  • Monolithic Lakshmi Narasimha statue.

Concept-specific exam traps

  • Assuming Hampi monuments are made of soapstone like Hoysala temples.
  • Mistaking secular palaces for religious temples.
Concept 4

Vijayanagara Administration, Society, and Economy

Definition and scope

  • Operated on the Nayankara (military feudalism) and Ayagar (village administration) systems.
  • Land revenue standard fixed at one-sixth of agricultural produce.
  • Active international maritime trade via Western ports.

Must remember

  • Nayaks as military chieftains holding Amaram lands.
  • Twelve hereditary village officers under the Ayagar system.
  • Provincial hierarchy: Mandalas to Gramas.
  • Arabian horse imports through Goa and Bhatkal ports.
  • High social standing and active public roles of women.

Concept-specific exam traps

  • Confusing Nayankara system with Delhi Sultanate Iqta system.
  • Viewing Ayagar officials as central revenue collectors.
Concept 5

Bahmani Kingdom and the Deccan Sultanates

Definition and scope

  • Founded in 1347 CE by Ala-ud-Din Bahman Shah (Hasan Gangu).
  • Mahmud Gawan reformed administration by dividing the realm into eight tarafs.
  • Fractured into five Deccan Sultanates that defeated Vijayanagara in 1565 CE.

Must remember

  • Founding year: 1347 CE.
  • Capitals: Gulbarga and Bidar.
  • Five sultanates: Bijapur, Golconda, Ahmednagar, Bidar, Berar.
  • Decisive Battle of Talikota (1565 CE).
  • Monuments: Gol Gumbaz and Charminar.

Concept-specific exam traps

  • Believing the Bahmani kingdom remained unified until the Mughal conquest.
  • Confusing the Battle of Talikota participants.

Quick facts

  • Vijayanagara Empire was founded in 1336 CE by Harihara and Bukka Raya.
  • Bahmani Sultanate was established in 1347 CE by Hasan Gangu.
  • Krishnadevaraya reigned from 1509 to 1529 CE and wrote Amuktamalyada.
  • The Battle of Talikota took place in 1565 CE, destroying Hampi.
  • Hampi is situated on the banks of the Tungabhadra River.
  • Mahmud Gawan was the renowned Persian prime minister of the Bahmani Sultanate.
  • Gol Gumbaz in Bijapur features the world's second-largest unreinforced dome.

Glossary

AshtadiggajasThe eight celebrated Telugu poets in the court of Krishnadevaraya.
Nayankara SystemA feudal military tenure system where chiefs maintained troops in exchange for land assignments.
Ayagar SystemA system of twelve hereditary village functionaries managing local administration and security.
Kalyana MandapaOrnate pillared hall inside Vijayanagara temples dedicated to divine marriages.
AfaqisForeign Muslim immigrants from Persia and Iraq who served in the Bahmani administration.

Important distinctions

Nayankara SystemvsIqta System
  • Nayankara grants were hereditary military feuds in Vijayanagara; Iqta grants under Delhi Sultanates were generally non-transferable revenue assignments.
  • Nayaks held extensive judicial and administrative autonomy within their territories compared to Iqtedars.
Vijayanagara ArchitecturevsHoysala Architecture
  • Vijayanagara temples utilized hard archaean granite and featured tall gopurams and massive pillared halls.
  • Hoysala temples utilized soft soapstone (chloritic schist) and featured star-shaped plans and intricate scrollwork.

Memory hooks

  • Remember Vijayanagara dynasties with the acronym SST-A (Sangama, Saluva, Tuluva, Aravidu).
  • Recall the five Deccan Sultanates through the acronym BAG-AB (Bijapur, Ahmednagar, Golconda, Bidar, Berar).
  • Associate Krishnadevaraya with 'A' for Andhra Bhoja and Amuktamalyada.

Exam traps and cautions

  • Do not mistake Allasani Peddana for the king; he was the chief poet laureate.
  • Do not confuse Hasan Gangu (Bahmani founder) with Delhi Sultans.
  • Remember that Hampi was destroyed after the Battle of Talikota in 1565, not 1526.
  • Note that the Battle of Talikota is also called Rakshasa-Tangadi.
