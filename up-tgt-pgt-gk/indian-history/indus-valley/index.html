Indus Valley Civilisation: Sites, Urban Planning and Culture

The Indus or Harappan Civilisation was a Bronze Age urban tradition spread across a large region of north-western South Asia and known for planned settlements, craft production and long-distance exchange.

UP TGT PGT GK6 concepts42 quiz questions10-question test

Topic overview

  • The Indus or Harappan Civilisation was a Bronze Age urban tradition spread across a large region of north-western South Asia and known for planned settlements, craft production and long-distance exchange.
  • Harappa, Mohenjo-daro, Dholavira, Lothal, Kalibangan, Rakhigarhi and many other sites reveal regional variation within a connected cultural world.
  • Urban features include streets, drainage, wells, brick architecture, storage or public structures, craft areas, seals, standardised weights and planned water management.
  • The civilisation had agriculture, pastoralism, craft specialisation, internal exchange and external links with regions known in Mesopotamian sources as Meluhha.
  • The script remains undeciphered, so conclusions must be based on archaeology, material patterns and cautious interpretation rather than invented readings.

Concept 1

Discovery, Extent and Chronology

The Harappan Civilisation was a wide Bronze Age cultural network with urban and rural phases rather than one city or one uniform kingdom.

Step-by-step explanation

  • Harappa was excavated in the 1920s and gave the civilisation its common archaeological name, while Mohenjo-daro revealed another major urban centre.
  • The civilisation spread across present-day Pakistan and north-western and western India, with sites from Baluchistan and Punjab to Gujarat, Haryana and Rajasthan.
  • Early, Mature and Late Harappan phases help explain the development, peak and transformation of urban settlements.
  • The Mature Harappan phase is generally placed around 2600–1900 BCE, while regional antecedents and successors have longer and varied dates.
  • Rakhigarhi, Dholavira, Lothal, Kalibangan, Banawali, Chanhudaro and many smaller sites show that the civilisation was not limited to two cities.
  • The term civilisation describes shared material patterns, exchange and urban features, not necessarily one centrally ruled empire.
  • Archaeological chronology uses layers, pottery, architecture, artefacts and scientific dating; individual dates may be revised as evidence improves.
  • The civilisation had regional variation in town plans, crops, craft emphasis, burial practices and environmental adaptation.

Key points

  • Harappa and Mohenjo-daro
  • Mature phase c. 2600-1900 BCE
  • Large north-western spread
  • Urban and rural networks
  • Regional variation.

Concept 2

Urban Planning, Drainage and Water Management

Harappan settlements are especially important for planned streets, brick construction, sanitation, wells and water systems.

Step-by-step explanation

  • Many Mature Harappan towns used planned streets, activity zones and standardised brick proportions, although plans varied by site.
  • Mohenjo-daro is known for a citadel area, lower town, Great Bath and an extensive drainage network.
  • Covered drains, soakage arrangements, household bathing spaces and wells show attention to water and waste management.
  • Dholavira developed reservoirs, channels and storage systems suited to the dry Kutch environment and demonstrates regional adaptation.
  • Fortified or elevated sectors may have served public, administrative, ritual or protective functions, but their exact social meaning is debated.
  • The Great Bath is a large water structure whose use was probably ritual or public, but its precise function cannot be proven with certainty.
  • Urban planning required labour, brick production, craft coordination, maintenance and some form of community or authority organisation.
  • The cities were not identical; local geology, rainfall, river channels and resources influenced architecture and water systems.

Key points

  • Grid-like streets
  • Baked bricks and wells
  • Covered drainage
  • Mohenjo-daro Great Bath
  • Dholavira reservoirs.

Concept 3

Agriculture, Economy, Crafts and Trade

Harappan prosperity depended on farming, pastoralism, specialist crafts, exchange networks and access to varied ecological zones.

Step-by-step explanation

  • Wheat, barley, pulses, sesame, dates, cotton and regionally varied crops supported different Harappan communities.
  • Cattle, buffalo, sheep, goat and other animals contributed to traction, food, dairy, wool, transport or ritual life, though proportions varied.
  • Bead-making, pottery, metallurgy, shell work, faience, weaving and ornament production show craft specialisation.
  • Carnelian beads from Gujarat, shell from coastal areas, metals from resource zones and stone from distant regions indicate exchange and procurement networks.
  • Standardised weights and seals could support trade, identity, storage, taxation or administrative communication, but their exact institutional use remains debated.
  • Lothal and coastal Gujarat show maritime or coastal connections, while inland routes linked settlements across river plains and semi-arid regions.
  • Mesopotamian records refer to Meluhha, often connected with the Indus region, but the nature of political and commercial relations is uncertain.
  • Economic life was not purely urban; villages, pastoral groups, farmers, craft households and transport networks supported cities.

Key points

  • Agriculture and pastoralism
  • Craft specialisation
  • Beads, shell and metallurgy
  • Standardised weights and seals
  • Meluhha and long-distance trade.

Concept 4

Script, Seals, Art and Religious Life

Harappan symbols, figurines, pottery, ornaments and burials reveal a rich material culture but leave many beliefs open to interpretation.

Step-by-step explanation

  • The Harappan script appears on seals, pottery, tablets and other objects and remains undeciphered.
  • Because the signs cannot be read securely, claims about named kings, laws or religious texts are speculative and should not be presented as facts.
  • Steatite seals often show animals, composite figures and short inscriptions and may have supported identity, exchange, authority or ritual communication.
  • Bronze, stone and terracotta objects include human and animal figures, ornaments, toys, pottery designs and tools.
  • The so-called Dancing Girl bronze and the Priest-King label are modern descriptive names, not confirmed historical identities.
  • Terracotta female figurines, sacred trees, animals, fire-related structures and water practices have prompted religious interpretations, but no single goddess system is proven.
  • Burial practices vary and include extended burials, grave goods and regional differences, providing evidence about social and ritual life.
  • Art and religious life should be studied through objects, context and comparison rather than projecting later religious categories directly onto Harappan evidence.

Key points

  • Undeciphered script
  • Steatite seals
  • Animal motifs
  • Dancing Girl and Priest-King labels
  • Burials and cautious religious interpretation.

Concept 5

Major Sites and Regional Features

Site-wise comparison is essential because Harappan settlements reveal different ecological settings, crafts, planning and cultural features.

Step-by-step explanation

  • Harappa in the Punjab region is the type-site and provides settlement, craft, burial and seal evidence.
  • Mohenjo-daro in Sindh is noted for the Great Bath, drains, citadel and lower town and is one of the best-known urban centres.
  • Dholavira in Kutch is recognised for reservoirs, fortified sectors, urban divisions and adaptation to a dry environment.
  • Lothal in Gujarat is associated with bead-making, craft production and maritime trade; the dockyard identification remains an interpretive issue.
  • Kalibangan in Rajasthan provides evidence of a ploughed field, fire-related structures and settlement planning in the Ghaggar-Hakra zone.
  • Rakhigarhi in Haryana is one of the largest Harappan sites and helps revise assumptions based only on Pakistan sites.
  • Chanhudaro is known for craft specialisation, while Banawali and Surkotada show regional variation in planning, fortification and subsistence.
  • Daimabad and other peripheral or later sites show wider interaction and the limits of a simple Mature Harappan core-periphery model.

Key points

  • Harappa-type site
  • Mohenjo-daro Great Bath
  • Dholavira reservoirs
  • Lothal craft and maritime context
  • Kalibangan ploughed field and Rakhigarhi.

Concept 6

Transformation, Decline and Legacy

After about 1900 BCE many urban centres changed or declined, but Harappan traditions continued through regional communities rather than disappearing suddenly.

Step-by-step explanation

  • Late Harappan changes included reduced urban scale, altered craft patterns, changing pottery traditions, settlement shifts and weaker long-distance networks.
  • Possible contributing factors include river-course changes, climate variability, floods, drought, ecological stress, declining trade, resource pressure and transformation of political organisation.
  • No single invasion or simple Aryan destruction theory explains all sites and regions; evidence is uneven and interpretations remain debated.
  • Some cities were abandoned while other settlements continued or adapted, showing regional transformation rather than one simultaneous collapse.
  • Agricultural and craft traditions survived in later cultures, with new settlement patterns, technologies and social formations developing over time.
  • The decline of urban centrality does not mean total population disappearance or cultural discontinuity.
  • Environmental change and social choices interacted; communities could migrate, diversify crops, alter water systems or reorganise exchange.
  • The Harappan legacy includes planning, craft traditions, water management, artistic motifs and archaeological knowledge, but later cultures should not be treated as unchanged replicas.

Key points

  • Late Harappan transformation
  • River and climate factors
  • Trade and ecological stress
  • No single proven collapse cause
  • Regional continuity and change.
