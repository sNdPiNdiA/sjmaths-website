Concept 1 · foundation

Foundations of Swarajya and Early Military Campaigns

Shivaji Maharaj laid the institutional and military bedrock of the Maratha Swarajya by uniting the Mavlas and defying regional Sultanates.

Prerequisites

  • Geographical understanding of the rugged Western Ghats (Sahyadri range) and Konkan coast.
  • Political fragmentation of the Deccan featuring the Nizam Shahi, Adil Shahi, and Qutb Shahi Sultanates alongside Mughal expansionism.

Definitions and scope

  • Swarajya: Concept of self-rule and independence for the Maratha people rooted in indigenous culture and governance.
  • Mavlas: Hardy peasant warriors from the Maval region who formed the backbone of Shivaji's infantry.

Step-by-step explanation

  • Shahaji Bhonsle, Shivaji's father, held jagirs under the Nizam Shahi and later Adil Shahi rulers, providing a launchpad for regional influence.
  • Jijabai instilled deep cultural, religious, and martial values in young Shivaji at Pune and Bangalore.
  • Dadaji Konddev managed the Pune jagir efficiently, improving agriculture and revenue collection prior to Shivaji taking direct charge.
  • The capture of Torna fort in 1645 initiated the active expansion phase of Swarajya.
  • Forts (Durgs) were recognized as the primary instruments of defense and territorial control in the hilly terrain.
  • Shivaji avoided pitched open-field battles against heavily armored cavalry, relying instead on Ganimi Kava (guerilla warfare).
  • Local intelligence networks and swift night attacks crippled larger opposing armies in unfamiliar terrain.
  • Religious tolerance towards all faiths was maintained while asserting Hindu political sovereignty against Islamic orthodox regimes.

Key points

  • Torna was the first fort captured in 1645.
  • Pratapgad fort was the site of the Afzal Khan encounter in 1659.
  • Maval infantrymen were famed for their loyalty and rugged endurance.
  • Ganimi Kava refers to Maratha guerilla tactics.
  • Pune served as the early administrative headquarters of Shivaji.
  • Shahaji Bhonsle served multiple Deccan Sultanates.
Worked examples
  • Application of Ganimi Kava: Drawing Adil Shahi or Mughal forces into narrow mountain defiles where Maratha infantry could ambush them effectively.
  • Fort Acquisition Strategy: Capturing strategic hill forts like Raigad, Rajgad, and Torna to command trade routes and valley access.

Applications and connections

  • Used in UP TGT/PGT history questions testing early military chronology.
  • Understanding regional independence movements against central imperial authority.
  • Analyzing geographical determinism in medieval Indian military tactics.

Advanced points, exceptions and limitations

  • Shivaji's strategy was not merely anti-Muslim, but anti-imperialist, as he employed Muslim soldiers and naval commanders (e.g., Daulat Khan) in his forces.
  • The reliance on forts created a decentralized defense system that required continuous maintenance and loyal garrison commanders (Havaldars).

Additional examples

  • Capture of Torna (1645)
  • Construction of Pratapgad fort
  • Defeat of Afzal Khan (1659)
Important distinctions
  • Unlike Mughal open-field cavalry charges, Maratha warfare utilized hit-and-run tactics in hilly terrains.
  • Unlike Jagirdari grants under Mughals, Shivaji discouraged hereditary alienation of fort commands.
  • Unlike centralized empires that relied on standing professional armies, early Maratha Swarajya mobilized localized militias of peasant Mavlas.
Common misconceptions
  • Misconception: Shivaji's army had no cavalry. Fact: While infantry was crucial for hills, the Maratha cavalry (Siledars and Págis) grew rapidly.
  • Misconception: Shivaji fought only the Mughals. Fact: His earliest campaigns were against the Adil Shahi Sultanate of Bijapur.
Exam focus
  • Remember Dadaji Konddev's role as Shivaji's guardian/administrator.
  • Memorize the year 1645 for Torna and 1659 for Afzal Khan.
  • Understand the term Ganimi Kava.
  • Know the significance of Maval region.
  • Identify Shahaji Bhonsle and Jijabai's historical contributions.
Concept 2 · core

Shivaji's Central Administration and the Ashta Pradhan

Shivaji organized his central government around the Ashta Pradhan, a council of eight ministers designed to assist the Chhatrapati without diluting royal authority.

Prerequisites

  • Understanding of traditional Hindu and Persian administrative titles in medieval India.
  • Concept of absolute monarchy balanced by consultative ministerial bodies.

Definitions and scope

  • Ashta Pradhan: Council of eight specialized ministers established by Shivaji Maharaj at his coronation in 1674.
  • Peshwa: The Prime Minister heading civil and military administration.

Step-by-step explanation

  • The council was purely advisory; all ministers except the Nyayadhish (Chief Justice) and Pandit Rao also held military commands.
  • 1. Peshwa (Merapant Pingle): Prime Minister, looked after general administration and state welfare.
  • 2. Amatya or Majumdar: Finance and Revenue Minister, audited accounts of income and expenditure.
  • 3. Waqia-navis: Home Minister / Intelligence / News-writer, maintained royal daily records and court proceedings.
  • 4. Sachiv or Shuranis: Royal Correspondence Minister, drafted royal edicts (Farmeens) and checked correspondence.
  • 5. Sumant or Dabir: Foreign Minister and Master of Ceremonies, dealt with foreign envoys and wars/peace.
  • 6. Senapati or Sar-i-Naubat: Commander-in-Chief, responsible for recruitment, organization, and army deployment.
  • 7. Nyayadhish: Chief Justice, administered civil and criminal justice based on Hindu jurisprudence and customs.
  • 8. Pandit Rao: Minister for Ecclesiastical Affairs, charitable grants, religious ceremonies, and moral purity.

Key points

  • Ashta Pradhan was established during the 1674 coronation.
  • All ministers except Nyayadhish and Pandit Rao had military duties.
  • Peshwa was designated as the chief minister.
  • Amatya managed revenue auditing.
  • Waqia-navis looked after intelligence and royal diaries.
  • Sumant managed foreign affairs.
Worked examples
  • Matching ministerial titles to portfolios in objective exams (e.g., Dabir = Foreign Affairs).
  • Tracing the transition of the Ashta Pradhan from an advisory council under Shivaji to a hereditary prime ministerial autocracy under later Peshwas.

Applications and connections

  • Crucial for UP PGT matching questions on administrative vocabulary.
  • Comparative study of Mughal Mansabdari vs. Maratha ministerial setup.
  • Understanding institutional evolution in medieval Indian history.

Advanced points, exceptions and limitations

  • Except for the Nyayadhish and Pandit Rao, all other ministers were required to lead military campaigns, reflecting the militarized nature of the Maratha state.
  • The titles were predominantly Sanskritized replacements for Persian administrative terms introduced by Shivaji to promote indigenous governance.

Additional examples

  • Moreshwar Pingale serving as the first Peshwa under Shivaji.
  • Pandit Rao overseeing grants to Brahmins and temples.
  • Senapati handling the mobilization of horsemen.
Important distinctions
  • Unlike the Mughal Wazir who wielded immense independent executive power, Shivaji's Peshwa was strictly subordinate to the Chhatrapati.
  • Unlike Akbar's diversified Mansabdari system, Shivaji's Ashta Pradhan was a compact council of eight specific portfolio holders.
  • Unlike British cabinet systems where decisions are collective, the Ashta Pradhan members reported individually and solely to the monarch.
Common misconceptions
  • Misconception: Ashta Pradhan was a cabinet with executive veto power. Fact: It was strictly advisory to the Chhatrapati.
  • Misconception: The Ashta Pradhan system originated with the Peshwas. Fact: It was created by Shivaji Maharaj in 1674.
Exam focus
  • Memorize all 8 ministers and their exact portfolios.
  • Note that Pandit Rao and Nyayadhish did not have military commands.
  • Remember that the terms were Sanskritized by official decree.
  • Distinguish between Amatya (finance) and Sachiv (correspondence).
  • Keep in mind the coronation year of 1674.
Concept 3 · advanced

Revenue Administration, Chauth, Sardeshmukhi, and Military Organization

Shivaji's fiscal framework combined rigorous land revenue assessment (Anno-Pant system) with external tribute extraction via Chauth and Sardeshmukhi, supported by a disciplined standing army.

Prerequisites

  • Basic understanding of feudal land revenue terms (Ryotwari, Jagirdari).
  • Concept of military recruitment in medieval India.

Definitions and scope

  • Chauth: One-fourth (25%) of the standard revenue claimed from non-Maratha territories as protection money against Maratha raids.
  • Sardeshmukhi: An additional levy of 10% claimed by Shivaji in his capacity as the hereditary Sardeshmukh over territories outside Swarajya.
  • Paga: Permanent state-maintained cavalry units.

Step-by-step explanation

  • Land revenue in Swarajya was organized by eliminating corrupt hereditary Deshmukhs and Patils through direct state engagement with cultivators.
  • Anno-Pant (Annaji Datto) conducted a systematic land survey, classifying soil fertility and fixing state share at 2/5th of the produce.
  • Cash payment was encouraged, though crop-sharing remained permissible during bad harvest years.
  • Chauth was exacted from Mughal and Sultanate territories to ensure they were not plundered further by Maratha forces.
  • Sardeshmukhi was demanded by Shivaji claiming ancestral rights over the entire Deccan plateau.
  • Military consisted of two types of cavalry: Paga (regular soldiers mounted on state-owned horses) and Silahdars (irregular cavaliers who brought their own horses and weapons).
  • Strict discipline prevailed in military camps: women, dancers, and religious plunder were forbidden during campaigns.
  • Naval fleet was established along the Konkan coast under Admirals like Kanhoji Angre to protect trade and counter European naval powers.

Key points

  • Chauth equals 25% of revenue; Sardeshmukhi equals 10%.
  • Annaji Datto reformed the land revenue system under Shivaji.
  • State share of revenue was fixed at roughly 40% (2/5th).
  • Paga soldiers rode state horses; Silahdars brought their own horses.
  • Kanhoji Angre was a prominent Maratha naval commander.
  • Plunder of sacred places and maltreatment of women were severely punished.
Worked examples
  • Calculation of Tribute: If a Mughal district yielded 1,00,000 rupees in revenue, Chauth extracted 25,000 rupees and Sardeshmukhi extracted 10,000 rupees.
  • Military Inspection: Regular branding of horses (Paga system) to prevent corruption among cavalry commanders.

Applications and connections

  • Exam questions on the exact mathematical proportions of Chauth and Sardeshmukhi.
  • Analyzing medieval fiscal policies and state formation.
  • Evaluating early Indian naval history in the western littoral.

Advanced points, exceptions and limitations

  • Chauth was not mere plunder; it functioned as a subsidiary alliance prototype where the Marathas guaranteed external defense in exchange for tribute.
  • The abolition of hereditary intermediary rights (Deshmukhs) temporarily angered local elites, though many were later co-opted.

Additional examples

  • Annaji Datto's survey of Konkan and Desh regions.
  • Naval fortifications at Sindhudurg and Vijaydurg.
  • Strict code of conduct for Maratha soldiers on expedition.
Important distinctions
  • Unlike Mughal land revenue assessed via Todar Mal's Zabti system, Maratha revenue relied heavily on Annaji Datto's survey and direct ryot contact.
  • Chauth differed from regular taxes as it was extracted from external, unconquered sovereign territories.
  • Paga cavalry differed from Silahdars in that horses and weapons were state-issued and directly maintained.
Common misconceptions
  • Misconception: Chauth was looted randomly without agreement. Fact: Chauth was a formal treaty-based tribute for protection.
  • Misconception: Marathas had no navy. Fact: Shivaji built a formidable navy to challenge Siddhis and Europeans.
Exam focus
  • Learn definitions and percentages of Chauth (25%) and Sardeshmukhi (10%).
  • Remember Annaji Datto's role in land revenue.
  • Distinguish between Paga and Silahdar cavalry.
  • Know Kanhoji Angre as a naval stalwart.
  • Note the strict moral code enforced in the Maratha army.
Concept 4 · exam-synthesis

Rise of the Peshwas and the Maratha Confederacy

Following Shivaji's successors and the release of Shahu in 1707, the Brahmin Peshwas based in Pune transformed the Maratha state into an expansive imperial confederacy.

Prerequisites

  • Post-Shivaji succession struggles including Sambhaji's execution, Rajaram's regency, and Tarabai's leadership.
  • Release of Shahu by the Mughals in 1707.

Definitions and scope

  • Peshwa Hegemony: Period where hereditary Prime Ministers wielded supreme political and military power while Chhatrapatis became titular heads.
  • Maratha Confederacy: Loose federation of dominant Maratha clans holding distinct territories.

Step-by-step explanation

  • Balaji Vishwanath became Peshwa in 1713, stabilizing Shahu's rule and securing imperial recognition from Mughal Emperor Farrukhsiyar in 1719.
  • Baji Rao I (1720–1740), the greatest military tactician among Peshwas, propounded the policy of Hindu Pad Padshahi and expanded northwards.
  • Baji Rao I defeated the Nizam of Hyderabad at Palkhed (1728) and captured Malwa and Gujarat.
  • Balaji Baji Rao (Nana Saheb) extended Maratha sway to Punjab and Odisha, reaching the zenith of territorial expansion.
  • The Maratha Confederacy emerged with prominent clans: Scindias (Gwalior), Holkars (Indore), Gaekwads (Baroda), and Bhonsles (Nagpur).
  • Third Battle of Panipat (1761) fought between Ahmad Shah Abdali and Sadashivrao Peshwa crippled Maratha military might.
  • Internal dissensions, fratricidal wars (e.g., after Madhavrao's death), and conflicts with the British East India Company led to the eventual fall of the confederacy.
  • Three Anglo-Maratha Wars (1775–1818) ended with the complete annexation of Maratha territories by the British.

Key points

  • Balaji Vishwanath was the first hereditary-influencing Peshwa.
  • Baji Rao I famously declared 'Strike at the trunk and the branches will fall off' regarding the Mughal Empire.
  • Battle of Palkhed (1728) saw Baji Rao I defeat Nizam-ul-Mulk.
  • Third Battle of Panipat occurred in 1761.
  • Peshwa headquarters shifted permanently to Pune.
  • Anglo-Maratha wars dismantled the Maratha Empire by 1818.
Worked examples
  • Confederacy Mapping: Associating Scindia with Gwalior, Holkar with Indore, Gaekwad with Baroda, and Bhonsle with Nagpur.
  • Chronological Ordering: Balaji Vishwanath -> Baji Rao I -> Balaji Baji Rao -> Madhavrao I.

Applications and connections

  • Standard UP TGT/PGT questions on Peshwa chronology and Panipat 1761.
  • Analyzing the decentralization of imperial power into regional confederacies.
  • Understanding British expansionist strategies against the Marathas.

Advanced points, exceptions and limitations

  • The shift from centralized Maratha unity under Shivaji to a decentralized confederacy of autonomous generals sowed the seeds of internal disunity.
  • The Peshwa succession disputes (e.g., Raghunath Rao seeking British help) directly invited British intervention in Maratha internal politics.

Additional examples

  • Baji Rao I's lightning campaign against the Nizam at Palkhed
  • Establishment of Scindia power in Gwalior by Mahadji Scindia
  • Treaty of Salbai (1782) during the First Anglo-Maratha War
Important distinctions
  • Unlike Shivaji's direct administrative control through appointed officials, Peshwa administration relied heavily on semi-autonomous clan chiefs.
  • Unlike the defensive guerilla warfare of early Marathas, later Peshwa armies engaged in massive open-field imperial campaigns across India.
  • Unlike the unitary executive under Shivaji, the later Maratha polity operated as a polycentric confederacy with competing regional capitals.
Common misconceptions
  • Misconception: Shivaji was the first Peshwa. Field: Shivaji appointed a Peshwa in Ashta Pradhan, but the office became supreme only under Balaji Vishwanath.
  • Misconception: The Maratha Empire ended at Panipat in 1761. Fact: Though crippled, the Marathas revived under Madhavrao I and Mahadji Scindia before falling in 1818.
Exam focus
  • Memorize the sequence of the first four Peshwas.
  • Note the year and significance of the Third Battle of Panipat (1761).
  • Remember the clan-territory pairings for Confederacy exams.
  • Understand Baji Rao I's military achievements and Palkhed battle.
  • Recall the outcome of the Third Anglo-Maratha War (1818).

SwarajyaSelf-rule and independence of the Maratha territory.
Ganimi KavaMaratha tactic of guerilla warfare in hilly terrain.
Ashta PradhanThe council of eight ministers instituted by Shivaji Maharaj.
ChauthOne-fourth tax collected from non-Maratha territories for protection.
SardeshmukhiAdditional 10% levy claimed over territories outside core Swarajya.
PagaCavalry troops maintained directly by the state.
SilahdarCavalrymen who brought their own horses and weapons.

Important distinctions

Shivaji's AdministrationvsPeshwa Administration
  • Centralized authority rested with the Chhatrapati, advised by Ashta Pradhan.
  • Real power shifted to hereditary Peshwas in Pune, with autonomous confederate chiefs.
ChauthvsSardeshmukhi
  • Chauth was 25% of standard revenue levied for protection against raids.
  • Sardeshmukhi was 10% demanded as hereditary royal overlordship.

Memory hooks

  • Ashta Pradhan ministers: Remember 'P-A-W-S S-S-N-P' (Peshwa, Amatya, Waqia-navis, Sachiv, Sumant, Senapati, Nyayadhish, Pandit Rao).
  • Chauth and Sardeshmukhi: 'Quarter and Tenth' (25% and 10%).
  • Peshwa Chronology: 'Balaji Baji Balaji Baji' -> Balaji Vishwanath, Baji Rao I, Balaji Baji Rao, Madhavrao I.

Exam traps and cautions

  • Do not confuse Sachiv (correspondence) with Amatya (finance).
  • Remember that Nyayadhish and Pandit Rao had no military duties unlike the other six Ashta Pradhan members.
  • Do not mistake the date of Shivaji's coronation (1674) with the Treaty of Purandar (1665).
  • Remember that the Third Battle of Panipat was fought in 1761, not 1757 or 1764.
