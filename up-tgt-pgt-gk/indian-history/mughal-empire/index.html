Mughal Empire and Administration: Akbar's Din-i-Ilahi, Mansabdari System, Architecture and Revenue System

The Mughal Empire established a highly centralized yet diverse administrative apparatus that shaped medieval Indian polity and society.

UP TGT PGT GK4 concepts28 quiz questions10-question test

Topic overview

  • The Mughal Empire established a highly centralized yet diverse administrative apparatus that shaped medieval Indian polity and society.
  • Akbar transformed the military and civil administration through the revolutionary Mansabdari system and streamlined agrarian revenue collection via the Zabti system.
  • Architectural synthesis under the Mughals reached its zenith, merging Persian and indigenous Indian styles into magnificent monuments.
  • Din-i-Ilahi represented Akbar's philosophical quest for religious harmony and universal peace (Sulh-i-Kul).

Concept 1 · core

The Mansabdari System

The Mansabdari system was a unique military-cum-civil administrative grading system instituted by Akbar to organize nobility and maintain the imperial army.

Prerequisites

  • Basic understanding of feudal and military organization in medieval India.
  • Familiarity with central Asian military traditions and tribal loyalty structures.

Definitions and scope

  • Mansab refers to a rank, position, or grade assigned to imperial officials.
  • Mansabdar was the holder of such a rank who performed dual civil and military duties.

Step-by-step explanation

  • Akbar introduced the Mansabdari system around 1570-1571 CE, drawing inspiration from Mongol decimal organization.
  • The system integrated all civil and military officers into a single unified hierarchy.
  • Every official was given a rank denoted by Zat and Sawar numbers.
  • Zat indicated the personal rank and salary status of the official.
  • Sawar indicated the exact number of horsemen the mansabdar was expected to maintain.
  • Later, Jahangir and Shah Jahan introduced the Du-aspa Sih-aspa (two or three horses) and conditional ranks (mashrut).
  • Salaries were paid either in cash (naqdi) or through revenue assignments (jagir), though jagirdars did not own the land.
  • Strict branding (dagh) of horses and descriptive rolls (chehra) of soldiers were maintained to prevent fraud.

Key points

  • Dual grading: Zat (personal rank) and Sawar (military contingent).
  • Unified civil and military hierarchy under direct imperial control.
  • Payment through Jagirs or cash salaries.
  • Strict horse branding (dagh) and soldier roll (chehra) systems.
  • Non-hereditary status; titles and ranks lapsed upon the death of the mansabdar.
  • Absorbed diverse ethnic groups including Turanis, Iranis, Afghans, Rajputs, and Deccanis.
Worked examples
  • A mansabdar holding a rank of 5000 Zat and 5000 Sawar (5000/5000) was required to maintain a full quota of 5000 horsemen and received salary accordingly.
  • If a mansabdar's Sawar rank exceeded his Zat rank (or specific rules of category 1, 2, 3 applied), adjustments were made in his maintenance allowance and horse quotas.

Applications and connections

  • Understanding Mughal imperial centralization in Indian history syllabus.
  • Analyzing socio-political alliances between Mughals and regional elites like Rajputs.
  • Explaining the administrative decline during the later Mughal period due to the 'Jagirdari Crisis'.

Advanced points, exceptions and limitations

  • The system suffered from severe inflation of ranks under Shah Jahan and Aurangzeb, leading to a shortage of available jagirs (Jagirdari Crisis).
  • Hereditary tendencies crept in informally despite formal rules against inheritance of ranks.
  • Discrepancies between paper strength and actual troop muster caused military vulnerabilities.

Additional examples

  • Raja Man Singh held the elite rank of 7000 Zat and 7000 Sawar.
  • Mirza Aziz Koka also attained the rare 7000/7000 rank.
  • Lower ranks started from 10 Zat.
Important distinctions
  • Unlike European feudalism, mansabs were non-hereditary and directly controlled by the Emperor.
  • Unlike the Delhi Sultanate's Iqta system, the Mansabdari system institutionalized both civil and military grades into a synchronized numerical scale.
  • Zat fixed personal status, whereas Iqta was strictly a revenue assignment for maintaining troops.
Common misconceptions
  • Mansabdars owned the land assigned to them as jagirs (False: they only collected revenue).
  • Sawar rank meant the number of horses a mansabdar rode himself (False: it was the contingent of cavalry he maintained).
Exam focus
  • Distinction between Zat and Sawar ranks.
  • Introduction of Dagh and Chehra systems by Akbar.
  • Du-aspa Sih-aspa introduced by Jahangir.
  • The concept of Jagirdari crisis linked to Mansabdari expansion.
  • Non-hereditary nature of the mansab.
Concept 2 · core

Mughal Revenue System: Zabti, Dahsala, and Land Classification

The Mughal agrarian revenue system, perfected by Raja Todar Mal, relied on systematic land measurement, classification, and cash assessment.

Prerequisites

  • Basic medieval agrarian terms like Rayat, Kankut, and Batai.
  • Understanding of medieval currency systems and silver Rupaiya.

Definitions and scope

  • Zabti system: A system of land revenue assessment based on measurement (zabt) and fixed cash rates per bigha.
  • Dahsala system: Ten-year settlement fixing average yield and prices over the preceding ten years.

Step-by-step explanation

  • Akbar abolished arbitrary revenue assessment and instituted a scientific survey of cultivated lands.
  • Standard units of measurement were established, replacing the varying gaz with the Ilahi Gaz.
  • Lands were classified into four categories based on continuous cultivation: Polaj, Parauti, Chachar, and Banjar.
  • Polaj was land cultivated every year without a break.
  • Parauti was land left fallow temporarily to recover fertility.
  • Chachar was land left fallow for three to four years.
  • Banjar was wasteland uncultivated for five or more years.
  • The Dahsala system (1580 CE) calculated the average produce and market prices of ten years to fix permanent state demand (usually one-third of the produce).

Key points

  • Raja Todar Mal's role as Diwan-i-Asraf in revenue reforms.
  • Introduction of Ilahi Gaz for land measurement.
  • Four-fold land classification: Polaj, Parauti, Chachar, Banjar.
  • Dahsala (ten-year) average pricing mechanism.
  • State demand fixed generally at one-third of average produce.
  • Collection in cash (Naqdi) preferred over grain (Galla-bakshi).
Worked examples
  • If wheat yield over 10 years averaged 15 maunds per bigha and market price averaged 1 rupee per maund, the average gross produce value was determined, and one-third was collected as revenue.
  • If a farmer held Polaj land, he paid full standard revenue every harvest without remission.

Applications and connections

  • Foundation of modern land settlement and revenue administration in British and post-independence India.
  • Historical economic analysis of Mughal surplus extraction.
  • Understanding peasant uprisings (e.g., Satnamis, Jats) due to high revenue demands.

Advanced points, exceptions and limitations

  • While Zabti was efficient in northern plains, other systems like Batai (crop sharing) and Nasaq (estimation) continued in distant provinces like Kashmir, Sindh, and parts of the Deccan.
  • Over-assessment in later reigns led to agrarian distress and flight of peasants (upari).
  • The monetization of the rural economy increased dependency on grain merchants and sahukars.

Additional examples

  • Polaj land yielded revenue every single harvest.
  • Banjar land was exempt from revenue until brought under cultivation.
  • Dahsala rates varied by crop (galla-i-jins) and local productivity zones (dastur-ul-amal).
Important distinctions
  • Zabti relied on measurement and cash rates, whereas Galla-bakshi (crop sharing) split actual harvested grain on the spot.
  • Sher Shah Sur laid the groundwork, but Akbar and Todar Mal institutionalized it into a uniform imperial framework.
  • Unlike revenue farming (Ijarah), direct or semi-direct settlement minimized middleman extortion.
Common misconceptions
  • Peasants were owners of the land (False: state claimed supreme proprietary rights over agricultural land).
  • Revenue was always collected in cash everywhere (False: crop sharing and appraisement existed concurrently in many regions).
Exam focus
  • Name Raja Todar Mal and Dahsala system.
  • Four land types: Polaj, Parauti, Chachar, Banjar.
  • Ilahi Gaz measurement unit.
  • Proportion of state demand (typically 1/3rd).
  • Alternative systems: Batai, Nasaq, Kankut.
Concept 3 · advanced

Akbar's Din-i-Ilahi and Religious Policy

Din-i-Ilahi was an elite spiritual fellowship founded by Akbar that synthesized ethical principles from diverse religions under the overarching philosophy of Sulh-i-Kul.

Prerequisites

  • Akbar's liberal intellectual environment and curiosity.
  • Establishment of the Ibadat Khana at Fatehpur Sikri in 1575 CE.

Definitions and scope

  • Din-i-Ilahi: Literally 'Divine Faith', officially termed Tauhid-i-Ilahi (Divine Monotheism).
  • Sulh-i-Kul: Universal peace and absolute tolerance toward all religious sects.

Step-by-step explanation

  • Akbar initially built the Ibadat Khana (House of Worship) in 1575 for Sunni theologians, later opening it to Shia, Sufi, Hindu, Jain, Parsi, and Christian scholars.
  • Disillusioned by narrow-minded sectarian debates and orthodoxy among religious clerics (Ulema), Akbar issued the Infallibility Decree (Mahzar) in 1579, making the Emperor the final arbiter in religious disputes.
  • In 1582, he introduced Din-i-Ilahi as a moral and spiritual order to bind the diverse nobility to the throne.
  • It had no sacred scriptures, priesthood, holy book, or compulsory rituals, but emphasized purity, truth, non-violence, and devotion to the Emperor.
  • Initiation involved disciples (murids) placing their head at the Emperor's feet and receiving a Shast (likeness of Akbar and Allah-u-Akbar).
  • Only a handful of courtiers, notably Birbal, accepted it, making it a personal imperial circle rather than a mass religion.
  • Contemporary chroniclers like Badauni criticized Akbar as an apostate, while modern historians view it as a political instrument of integration.

Key points

  • Ibadat Khana (1575) evolved into inter-faith discussions.
  • Mahzar (1579) curtailed orthodox Ulema influence.
  • Sulh-i-Kul (Universal Peace) as the cornerstone of imperial sovereignty.
  • Din-i-Ilahi (1582) as a spiritual fellowship, not a new proselytizing religion.
  • Abolition of Jizya (1564) and pilgrimage tax.
  • Patronage to scholars of all faiths (Abul Fazl, Faizi, Tansen, Todar Mal, Man Singh).
Worked examples
  • A courtier entering Din-i-Ilahi pledged to abstain from meat on certain days, practice charity, and surrender life, property, religion, and honor to the Emperor.
  • Jizya was abolished to remove discrimination against non-Muslim subjects, fostering political loyalty.

Applications and connections

  • Understanding secularism and pluralism roots in Indian historical statecraft.
  • Analyzing Akbar's strategy of imperial consolidation through cultural synthesis.
  • Answering UP TGT/PGT questions on Mughal religious policies and chronicles (Akbarnama, Muntakhab-ut-Tawarikh).

Advanced points, exceptions and limitations

  • Badauni's accounts are notoriously hostile and biased against Akbar's religious experiments.
  • Abul Fazl presents Din-i-Ilahi as a rational, enlightened philosophical path rather than an organized church.
  • Modern historians debate whether it was a genuine spiritual quest or purely political statecraft.

Additional examples

  • Jain scholar Acharya Harivijaya Suri was invited to Fatehpur Sikri and honored by Akbar.
  • Jesuit priests (Aquaviva, Monserrate) were welcomed at the imperial court.
  • Birbal was famously the only Hindu to join Din-i-Ilahi.
Important distinctions
  • Unlike Aurangzeb's orthodox Islamic policy, Akbar's policy was built on religious liberalism and pluralism.
  • Unlike state religions of contemporary Europe (e.g., Tudor England), Din-i-Ilahi was voluntary and lacked dogmatic theology.
  • Mahzar centralized political-religious authority in the Emperor, countering the autonomy of the Qazi and Ulema.
Common misconceptions
  • Akbar forced people to convert to Din-i-Ilahi (False: it had fewer than 20 initiates and was strictly voluntary).
  • Akbar renounced Islam entirely (False: he remained a Muslim, albeit deeply influenced by Sufi and Vedantic pantheism).
Exam focus
  • Year of Ibadat Khana (1575) and Mahzar (1579).
  • Definition of Sulh-i-Kul.
  • Role of Abul Fazl and Badauni.
  • Abolition of Jizya in 1564.
  • Misconception regarding Din-i-Ilahi as a mass religion.
Concept 4 · application

Mughal Architecture: Synthesis of Persian and Indian Styles

Mughal architecture represents a magnificent synthesis of Timurid-Persian structural forms with indigenous Indian trabeate traditions, characterized by red sandstone and white marble.

Prerequisites

  • Basic architectural terms: dome, arch, vault, bracket, lintel, jali, pietra dura.
  • Chronology of Mughal emperors from Babur to Aurangzeb.

Definitions and scope

  • Arcuate style: Architectural style relying on arches, domes, and vaults introduced by Islamic rulers.
  • Trabeate style: Indigenous Indian style relying on lintels, beams, and pillars.

Step-by-step explanation

  • Babur and Humayun introduced Central Asian/Persian styles, seen in Humayun's Tomb (first double dome in India).
  • Akbar's reign marked the extensive use of red sandstone and trabeate features combined with arches, visible at Agra Fort, Lahore Fort, and Fatehpur Sikri.
  • Buland Darwaza at Fatehpur Sikri commemorates Akbar's Gujarat victory and showcases towering grandeur.
  • Jahangir shifted focus toward gardens (Chahar Bagh) and delicate arts, patronizing Akbar's Mausoleum at Sikandra and Shalimar Bagh in Kashmir.
  • Shah Jahan's reign is celebrated as the Golden Age of Mughal architecture, marked by pure white marble, symmetrical balance, and pietra dura inlay work.
  • Key Shahjahanian monuments include the Taj Mahal, Red Fort (Delhi), Jama Masjid (Delhi), and Moti Masjid (Agra).
  • Aurangzeb's period saw a decline in monumental building; notable exceptions include Bibi Ka Maqbara in Aurangabad and Badshahi Mosque in Lahore.

Key points

  • Humayun's Tomb: Earliest use of Persian double dome and Chahar Bagh layout in India.
  • Akbar's red sandstone synthesis: Fatehpur Sikri, Agra Fort.
  • Jahangir's focus: Shalimar Gardens, tomb painting integration.
  • Shah Jahan's white marble zenith: Taj Mahal, Red Fort, Pietra Dura work.
  • Fusion of Islamic arcuate and Hindu trabeate (brackets, pillared halls).
Worked examples
  • The Taj Mahal utilizes symmetrical planning, white Makrana marble, minarets, a central bulbous double dome, and intricate semi-precious stone inlay (pietra dura).
  • Fatehpur Sikri's Panch Mahal uses tiered pillared pavilions reflecting indigenous Gujarati and Rajasthani wooden architecture executed in red sandstone.

Applications and connections

  • Art and culture questions in UP TGT/PGT examinations.
  • Historical site identification and architectural chronology.
  • Understanding cultural syncretism in medieval Indian history.

Advanced points, exceptions and limitations

  • Pietra dura (Parchin Kari) was heavily influenced by Italian Florentine inlay techniques introduced via European trade and artisans.
  • The shift from red sandstone under Akbar to white marble under Shah Jahan symbolized imperial opulence and purity.
  • Water channels, fountains, and flowing cascades were central to Mughal garden design (Charbagh).

Additional examples

  • Buland Darwaza at Fatehpur Sikri.
  • Itimad-ud-Daulah's Tomb (earliest structure entirely of white marble with extensive pietra dura).
  • Diwan-i-Aam and Diwan-i-Khas in Delhi Red Fort.
Important distinctions
  • Delhi Sultanate architecture used massive stone blocks with austere ornamentation; Mughal architecture emphasized aesthetic refinement, gardens, and marble elegance.
  • Akbar's buildings favored red sandstone with trabeate beams; Shah Jahan's buildings favored white marble with arcuate domes.
  • Indigenous temple architecture used corbelled roofs and flat ceilings; Mughal architecture mastered true domes and vaulted iwans.
Common misconceptions
  • Taj Mahal was built by foreign architects without Indian labor (False: thousands of Indian stone-cutters, calligraphers, and masons worked under master architects like Ustad Ahmad Lahori).
  • Mughal architecture ignored Indian styles (False: it extensively incorporated Hindu brackets, chhajjas, and floral motifs like the lotus).
Exam focus
  • Humayun's Tomb: First double dome.
  • Itimad-ud-Daulah: First pure marble structure with pietra dura.
  • Shah Jahan: Golden age of white marble.
  • Akbar: Fatehpur Sikri and red sandstone.
  • Key architects and patrons.

  • Shah Jahan predominantly used pure white marble with symmetrical arcuate domes and pietra dura.

Memory hooks

  • Mansab Ranking: 'Zat is Salary & Status, Sawar is Soldiers & Swords.'
  • Land Types: 'P-P-C-B' -> Polaj (annual), Parauti (temporary fallow), Chachar (3-4 years fallow), Banjar (5+ years wasteland).
  • Religious Milestones: Ibadat Khana (1575) -> Mahzar (1579) -> Din-i-Ilahi (1582).

Exam traps and cautions

  • Do not confuse Jagir (revenue assignment) with Jagir ownership; mansabdars were transferable officials, not feudal landlords.
  • Remember that Din-i-Ilahi had very few followers and was not a compulsory state religion.
  • Do not attribute the abolition of Jizya to Aurangzeb; it was abolished by Akbar and reinstated by Aurangzeb in 1679.
  • Check whether a question refers to Zat or Sawar when evaluating mansabdar military obligations.
