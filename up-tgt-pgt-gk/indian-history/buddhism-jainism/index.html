Indian History

Buddhism and Jainism: Teachings, Councils and Spread

Buddhism and Jainism emerged in the sixth century BCE in a period of urban growth, expanding trade, new kingdoms and debate over ritual authority.

UP TGT PGT GK6 concepts42 quiz questions10-question test

Topic overview

  • Both traditions gave importance to ethical conduct, self-discipline, karma, rebirth and liberation, while differing in their doctrines and paths.
  • Buddhism is associated with Gautama Buddha, the Four Noble Truths, the Eightfold Path and the Middle Way.
  • Jainism is associated with the Tirthankaras, especially Mahavira, and stresses non-violence, asceticism, many-sided reality and liberation of the soul.
  • UP examinations frequently test founders, doctrines, councils, sects, scriptures, symbols, patronage, stupas, caves and the comparison of Buddhist and Jain ideas.

Concept 1

Sixth Century BCE Context and Founders

Buddhism and Jainism grew in a period of social, economic and intellectual change in northern India.

Step-by-step explanation

  • The sixth century BCE saw the growth of towns, trade, coin use, mahajanapadas, stronger monarchies and new social groups.
  • Urban and mercantile communities often supported teachers and institutions that offered ethical and spiritual alternatives to expensive or exclusive ritual practices.
  • Gautama Buddha was born at Lumbini, attained enlightenment at Bodh Gaya, preached first at Sarnath and attained Mahaparinirvana at Kushinagara.
  • Mahavira, traditionally born at Kundagrama near Vaishali, is regarded as the 24th Tirthankara and a major organiser of Jain teachings.
  • Both traditions belong to the wider Shramana environment that valued renunciation, discipline, karma and liberation outside a sole dependence on Vedic ritual authority.
  • The Buddha and Mahavira taught through itinerant preaching, communities, dialogue and disciplined orders of monks, nuns and lay followers.
  • Their traditions were not identical: Buddhism rejected a permanent self, while Jainism affirmed the soul and its purification.
  • Political rulers, merchants, householders, artisans and ordinary followers contributed to the spread and institutional survival of both traditions.

Key points

  • Urbanisation and trade
  • Mahajanapadas
  • Buddha and four places
  • Mahavira and 24th Tirthankara
  • Shramana context.

Concept 2

Buddhist Doctrine: Four Truths, Eightfold Path and Nirvana

Buddha explained suffering, its cause, its cessation and a practical path of ethical and mental discipline.

Step-by-step explanation

  • The First Noble Truth recognises suffering or unsatisfactoriness in conditioned existence.
  • The Second Truth identifies craving or thirst as a major cause of suffering and continued becoming.
  • The Third Truth teaches that cessation of craving and suffering is possible, described through Nirvana.
  • The Fourth Truth presents the Noble Eightfold Path as the way to end suffering.
  • The Eightfold Path includes right view, intention, speech, action, livelihood, effort, mindfulness and concentration.
  • The Middle Way avoids both indulgence in sensual luxury and extreme self-mortification.
  • Buddhist ethics, meditation and wisdom are connected; liberation is not simply a ritual reward or a social title.
  • The doctrine of anatta or no permanent self and impermanence distinguishes Buddhism from traditions that affirm an eternal soul.

Key points

  • Dukkha
  • Craving as cause
  • Nirvana
  • Eightfold Path
  • Middle Way and anatta.

Concept 3

Sangha, Councils and Buddhist Schools

The Buddhist Sangha preserved teachings, maintained discipline and helped transmit Buddhism across regions.

Step-by-step explanation

  • The Sangha included monks and nuns who followed discipline, studied teachings, taught communities and depended partly on lay donations.
  • Lay followers supported monasteries through food, clothing, shelter, medicine, buildings and endowments.
  • The First Council is traditionally associated with Rajagriha after Buddha's death and the recitation of teachings and discipline.
  • The Second Council at Vaishali is linked with disputes over monastic rules and discipline.
  • The Third Council at Pataliputra is associated in Buddhist tradition with Ashoka and the classification or purification of teachings.
  • The Fourth Council is described differently in traditions; it is often associated with Kashmir and the development of scholastic or sectarian literature.
  • Theravada, Mahayana and later Vajrayana represent broad developments with different texts, ideals, practices and geographic histories.
  • Councils and sects should be studied with the caution that dates, locations and details vary across historical traditions.

Key points

  • Sangha and lay support
  • Rajagriha
  • Vaishali
  • Pataliputra and Ashoka
  • Theravada-Mahayana developments.

Concept 4

Jain Doctrine, Ethics and Sects

Jainism stresses purification of the soul through right faith, right knowledge, right conduct and rigorous ethical discipline.

Step-by-step explanation

  • Jainism recognises jiva or soul and ajiva or non-soul categories; karma binds the soul and liberation requires removal of karmic matter.
  • The Three Jewels or Triratna are right faith, right knowledge and right conduct.
  • The Five Great Vows are ahimsa, satya, asteya, aparigraha and brahmacharya for ascetics; lay followers observe them in modified forms.
  • Ahimsa extends beyond human beings and encourages care toward animals, plants and microscopic life as understood in Jain thought.
  • Anekantavada teaches the many-sidedness of reality, while syadvada expresses conditional or qualified ways of making statements.
  • Asceticism, fasting, non-attachment, meditation and disciplined conduct are central to the path of liberation.
  • Digambara and Svetambara traditions developed different practices, dress traditions, texts and interpretations while sharing core Jain concerns.
  • Jain communities supported trade, literacy, architecture, manuscript culture, art, charity and urban institutions across regions.

Key points

  • Jiva and ajiva
  • Three Jewels
  • Five vows and ahimsa
  • Anekantavada and syadvada
  • Digambara-Svetambara.

Concept 5

Spread, Literature, Art and Patronage

Rulers, merchants, monks, nuns and lay communities supported the spread of Buddhism and Jainism through institutions and networks.

Step-by-step explanation

  • Buddhist monasteries and stupas developed along trade routes and near towns, receiving donations from rulers, merchants, guilds and ordinary people.
  • Ashoka used inscriptions, Dhamma policy and missions to support Buddhism and wider ethical communication across his empire.
  • Buddhism spread to Sri Lanka and later to Central, East and Southeast Asia through monks, texts, trade and political patronage.
  • Jainism spread through merchant networks, monastic travel and regional patronage, with strong communities in western, central and southern India.
  • The Tripitaka or Tipitaka is a major Buddhist textual collection, while Jataka stories preserve narratives of Buddha's previous births.
  • Stupas symbolised relics and Buddhist sacred memory; railings, gateways, reliefs and circumambulation were important features.
  • Ajanta, Ellora, Sanchi, Bharhut, Nalanda and other sites show links between art, monastic life, education and patronage.
  • Jain caves, temples, images and manuscripts also reflect regional styles, donor networks and the value placed on disciplined religious communities.

Key points

  • Ashoka and missions
  • Trade routes and monasteries
  • Sri Lanka and wider Asia
  • Tripitaka and Jatakas
  • Stupas, caves and Nalanda.

Concept 6

Comparison, Decline and Legacy

Buddhism and Jainism shared an ethical Shramana background but developed distinct doctrines, institutions and historical trajectories.

Step-by-step explanation

  • Both traditions emphasised karma, rebirth, ethical conduct, renunciation, monastic communities and liberation from worldly bondage.
  • Buddhism taught the Middle Way, impermanence and no permanent self, while Jainism taught soul, severe asceticism and the purification of karmic matter.
  • Buddhist ideas spread widely outside India through missions, texts, monasteries and political patronage.
  • In India, Buddhism gradually lost many institutional centres because of changing patronage, monastic decline, absorption of practices and political disruptions.
  • Jainism maintained stronger continuity in several merchant and regional communities, although its geographical distribution was uneven.
  • Buddhist and Jain ethics, art, literature, philosophy, education, architecture and ideas of non-violence continued to influence Indian culture.
  • Decline should not be explained by one cause alone; patronage, institutions, competition, regional politics, social integration and economic networks all mattered.
  • Comparative answers should use a table or paired points instead of saying simply that both religions were identical reform movements.

Key points

  • Shared Shramana setting
  • Anatta versus jiva
  • Buddhist Asian spread
  • Different Indian trajectories
  • Ethical and cultural legacy.
