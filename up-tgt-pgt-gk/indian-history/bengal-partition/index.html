Concept 1

Partition of Bengal: Causes, Design and Annulment

The 1905 partition transformed a regional administrative decision into a major all-India nationalist issue.

Step-by-step explanation

  • Lord Curzon announced the partition of Bengal in 1905, dividing the large province into Western Bengal and Eastern Bengal and Assam.
  • The official justification referred to administrative convenience, but nationalists argued that the division weakened the politically active Bengali public and encouraged communal separation.
  • Eastern Bengal and Assam had Dacca as its capital, while Western Bengal remained centred on Calcutta within the revised arrangement.
  • 16 October 1905, the date the partition took effect, was observed as a day of mourning, fasting, unity ceremonies and public protest.
  • The anti-partition movement connected political protest with boycott, Swadeshi production, national education, songs, processions and public pledges.
  • The British response included repression, restrictions and efforts to manage political opposition and community differences.
  • The partition was annulled in 1911, but the capital was shifted from Calcutta to Delhi and Bihar and Orissa were separated from Bengal.
  • The episode demonstrated the power of organised public opinion and shaped later strategies of mass nationalism.

Key points

  • Curzon and 1905 partition
  • Eastern Bengal and Assam
  • 16 October 1905
  • Annulment in 1911
  • Capital shifted to Delhi.

Concept 2

Swadeshi, Boycott and National Education

The anti-partition movement expanded nationalism from petitions to economic action, public mobilisation and alternative education.

Step-by-step explanation

  • Swadeshi encouraged the use and production of Indian goods, while boycott targeted imported products associated with colonial economic dominance.
  • Students, women, workers, traders and urban groups participated through processions, picketing, public meetings, songs and household-level choices.
  • National education institutions sought to promote technical learning, science, patriotic instruction and greater control over curriculum.
  • Swadeshi enterprises, banks, insurance, weaving and small industries attempted to convert political emotion into constructive economic activity.
  • Rabindranath Tagore used songs and unity ceremonies such as Raksha Bandhan to emphasise social solidarity.
  • The movement also encouraged passive resistance and wider political education, although participation varied by class, region and gender.
  • Repression, economic limitations, internal differences and the absence of sustained mass organisation restricted the movement after its early phase.
  • The methods of boycott, constructive work and national education later influenced Gandhian mass politics.

Key points

  • Indian goods and production
  • Boycott of foreign goods
  • Students and public mobilisation
  • National education
  • Constructive economic nationalism.
Worked examples
  • Use of Raksha Bandhan as unity symbolism
  • National schools and technical education

Additional examples

  • Use of Raksha Bandhan as unity symbolism
  • National schools and technical education
Important distinctions
  • Boycott withdraws support from foreign goods; Swadeshi builds Indian production and institutions.
  • Petition politics appeals to authority; boycott and picketing directly alter participation and consumption.
Common misconceptions
  • Swadeshi meant refusing all foreign knowledge or technology.
  • Boycott was limited to one day of protest.

Concept 3

Revolutionary Nationalism and Bengal Politics

The Swadeshi period also saw secret societies and revolutionary action, especially in Bengal.

Step-by-step explanation

  • Anushilan Samiti and Jugantar developed physical training, secret organisation, political literature and revolutionary networks.
  • Aurobindo Ghose, Barindra Kumar Ghose, Bagha Jatin and other activists were associated with different phases of Bengal revolutionary politics.
  • The Muzaffarpur Bomb Case of 1908 involved Khudiram Bose and Prafulla Chaki in an attempt against Magistrate Kingsford.
  • The Alipore Bomb Case exposed the Manicktala network and became a major colonial prosecution of revolutionary activists.
  • Revolutionaries believed that dramatic action could inspire fearlessness and challenge the authority of the colonial state.
  • Secret activity faced surveillance, arrests, trials, deportation and difficulty in building broad, sustained public participation.
  • Revolutionary action existed alongside Swadeshi, boycott, national education, constitutional politics and later mass movements.
  • The period is best studied through organisation-leader-action-location matching and by distinguishing revolutionary methods from economic boycott.

Key points

  • Anushilan Samiti
  • Jugantar
  • Khudiram Bose and Muzaffarpur
  • Alipore Bomb Case
  • Secret action and colonial repression.

Concept 4

Muslim League, Surat Split and Morley-Minto Reforms

The period saw new political organisations, Congress tensions and constitutional changes that shaped representation.

Step-by-step explanation

  • The All-India Muslim League was formed at Dacca on 30 December 1906 by Muslim political leaders including the initiative associated with Nawab Salimullah.
  • Its early objectives included protection of Muslim political interests, representation and cooperation with the colonial government within the then constitutional framework.
  • The Surat Split of 1907 reflected conflict between Moderates and Extremists over methods, leadership and the pace of political action.
  • The split weakened Congress unity temporarily, but different nationalist currents continued to develop outside a single organisational rhythm.
  • The Indian Councils Act 1909, associated with Morley and Minto, expanded councils but introduced separate electorates for Muslims.
  • Separate electorates institutionalised a communal principle in representation and influenced later constitutional negotiations.
  • Political developments must be understood alongside the Swadeshi movement, repression, economic grievances and debates about self-government.
  • The period did not produce a simple linear division; alliances and political positions changed over time.

Key points

  • Muslim League at Dacca in 1906
  • Surat Split in 1907
  • Indian Councils Act 1909
  • Separate electorates
  • Changing political alliances.

Concept 5

Home Rule Leagues and the Demand for Self-Government

The Home Rule movement revived political mobilisation during the First World War and demanded self-government within the Empire as an immediate political goal.

Step-by-step explanation

  • Bal Gangadhar Tilak launched a Home Rule League in April 1916, with influence especially in Maharashtra and parts of western and central India.
  • Annie Besant launched her Home Rule League in September 1916 and built a wider network through branches, lectures, newspapers and political education.
  • Home Rule meant self-government for India and drew on the experience of dominion self-government and earlier nationalist demands.
  • The movement used public meetings, pamphlets, newspapers, tours and local committees to reach educated and politically active groups.
  • Tilak and Besant helped bring different nationalist currents closer after the Surat division.
  • The British government interned Annie Besant in 1917, producing protests and strengthening public sympathy for the movement.
  • Besant was released and became the first woman President of the Indian National Congress at the Calcutta session in 1917.
  • The Montagu Declaration of 20 August 1917 promised gradual development of self-governing institutions but did not satisfy immediate nationalist demands.

Key points

  • Tilak League-April 1916
  • Besant League-September 1916
  • Demand for Home Rule
  • Besant internment and release
  • Montagu Declaration-20 August 1917.

Concept 6

Lucknow Pact and Historical Significance

The 1916 Lucknow Pact temporarily brought Congress and the Muslim League together and marked the wider significance of the Home Rule phase.

Step-by-step explanation

  • The Lucknow session of 1916 witnessed an agreement between the Congress and the Muslim League on constitutional demands and representation.
  • Bal Gangadhar Tilak and Muhammad Ali Jinnah were important figures associated with the political cooperation of this phase.
  • Congress accepted separate electorates in the agreement, while both organisations pressed for greater Indian participation and self-government.
  • The pact also marked a reunion of Moderate and Extremist Congress groups after the earlier Surat conflict.
  • The Home Rule movement increased public interest in constitutional reform and helped prepare organisational networks for later mass politics.
  • The pact was a temporary political agreement; later differences over representation, constitutional safeguards and strategy re-emerged.
  • The period shows that nationalist politics involved both cooperation and disagreement among organisations and communities.
  • For examinations, connect 1905 partition, Swadeshi, 1906 League, 1907 Surat, 1909 reforms, 1916 Home Rule and Lucknow Pact in sequence.

Key points

  • Lucknow Pact-1916
  • Congress-League cooperation
  • Reunion of Congress groups
  • Separate electorates accepted in pact
  • Temporary alliance and later divergence.
