Indian History

Indian National Congress: Formation, Moderates and Early Nationalism

The Indian National Congress emerged in 1885 as an all-India political platform and gradually became the principal organisation of the freedom struggle.

UP TGT PGT GK6 concepts42 quiz questions10-question test

Topic overview

  • The Indian National Congress emerged in 1885 as an all-India political platform and gradually became the principal organisation of the freedom struggle.
  • Its early phase included constitutional agitation, economic criticism, public associations, political education and demands for representation and administrative reform.
  • Moderate leaders used petitions, speeches, resolutions, newspapers, legislative work and deputations, while later assertive nationalists promoted boycott, Swadeshi and mass mobilisation.
  • The Surat split, the anti-partition movement, the Muslim League, the Lucknow Pact and the Home Rule phase shaped the transition before Gandhian mass politics.
  • Examinations test founders, sessions, leaders, methods, economic ideas, important Acts, organisations, chronology and the relationship between unity and internal differences.

Concept 1

Formation of Congress and Early All-India Platform

The Congress created a regular forum through which political workers from different regions could discuss common demands.

Step-by-step explanation

  • The first session of the Indian National Congress met at Bombay in December 1885 with W. C. Bonnerjee as president.
  • A. O. Hume helped organise the platform, while Indian leaders and associations supplied political experience, networks and public legitimacy.
  • The annual sessions rotated among cities and built contact among lawyers, teachers, journalists, professionals, merchants and public activists.
  • Early Congress demands included expansion of legislative councils, Indianisation of services, civil liberties, financial accountability and reduction of military expenditure.
  • The Congress was initially an urban and educated organisation and did not represent every class, region or community equally.
  • Its value lay in creating an all-India political vocabulary, resolutions, press networks, leadership training and constitutional criticism.
  • Regional public associations remained important; Congress connected but did not erase provincial political identities.
  • The organisation changed over time as new leaders, methods, social groups and mass movements entered its politics.

Key points

  • Bombay session-1885
  • W. C. Bonnerjee
  • A. O. Hume
  • Annual all-India platform
  • Representation and financial accountability.

Concept 2

Moderates, Methods and Economic Nationalism

Moderate nationalists used constitutional methods and economic analysis to expose the costs of colonial rule and demand reform.

Step-by-step explanation

  • Moderate leaders included Dadabhai Naoroji, Gopal Krishna Gokhale, Pherozeshah Mehta, M. G. Ranade and Surendranath Banerjea.
  • They used petitions, resolutions, public meetings, speeches, newspapers, deputations and legislative questions to influence public opinion and British policy.
  • Their political education explained budgets, civil services, military expenditure, taxation, trade, famine and administrative inequality to a wider public.
  • Dadabhai Naoroji developed the Drain of Wealth critique, identifying home charges, salaries, pensions, profits and other unrequited transfers.
  • R. C. Dutt linked land revenue, poverty, famines, commercial policy and underdevelopment in his economic criticism.
  • Moderates demanded simultaneous civil-service examinations, greater Indian representation, separation of judiciary and executive, and financial accountability.
  • Their methods were gradual and constitutional, but their criticism prepared the intellectual ground for Swadeshi, self-government and later mass politics.
  • They faced limitations of social reach, faith in British liberal opinion, slow results and dependence on educated urban networks.

Key points

  • Petition and constitutional methods
  • Budget and public criticism
  • Drain of Wealth
  • Naoroji and R. C. Dutt
  • Indianisation and representation.

Concept 3

Assertive Nationalism, Swadeshi and Surat Split

The anti-partition movement encouraged more direct political action and produced differences over leadership, pace and method within Congress.

Step-by-step explanation

  • Bal Gangadhar Tilak, Lala Lajpat Rai, Bipin Chandra Pal and Aurobindo Ghose are associated with assertive or extremist nationalism.
  • They emphasised swaraj, self-reliance, public festivals, national education, boycott, Swadeshi and stronger participation beyond petitions.
  • The Partition of Bengal in 1905 transformed economic and cultural action into a broad anti-colonial movement.
  • Boycott targeted foreign goods while Swadeshi promoted Indian production, national education, technical learning, indigenous enterprise and public solidarity.
  • Students, women, workers, traders and urban groups participated, though the movement varied across regions and social groups.
  • Revolutionary organisations grew alongside public Swadeshi, but they were not identical to the open Congress movement.
  • The Surat Split of 1907 reflected conflict over leadership, methods and the pace of struggle and weakened united Congress action for a time.
  • Repression, internal differences and limited sustained organisation restricted the movement, but its methods influenced later nationalism.

Key points

Concept 4

Constitutional Acts and Political Representation

Colonial reforms expanded councils in stages while retaining executive control and introducing forms of representation that shaped later politics.

Step-by-step explanation

  • The Indian Councils Act 1892 enlarged councils and introduced indirect election or recommendation principles but gave limited powers over budgets and questions.
  • The Morley-Minto reforms or Indian Councils Act 1909 expanded councils further and introduced separate electorates for Muslims.
  • Separate electorates created a distinct constitutional mechanism for representation and influenced future debates over community, minority safeguards and political power.
  • The Montagu Declaration of 1917 promised gradual development of self-governing institutions but did not grant immediate responsible government.
  • The Government of India Act 1919 introduced dyarchy in provinces, dividing subjects into transferred and reserved categories.
  • Indian leaders used councils for criticism, public debate and constitutional training even though executive accountability remained limited.
  • Reforms were responses to pressure and also strategies of imperial management; representation did not equal sovereignty.
  • A good chronology distinguishes 1892, 1909, 1917 and 1919 provisions rather than merging them into one reform.

Key points

  • Councils Act-1892
  • Morley-Minto-1909
  • Separate electorates
  • Montagu Declaration-1917
  • Dyarchy-1919.

Concept 5

Muslim League, Home Rule and Lucknow Pact

Separate political organisation, wartime self-government campaigns and temporary Congress-League cooperation shaped the politics of 1906–1917.

Step-by-step explanation

  • The All-India Muslim League was founded at Dacca in 1906 by Muslim political leaders seeking a separate platform for political interests and representation.
  • The League was not politically static; its policies and relationship with Congress changed with constitutional debates and leadership shifts.
  • The Home Rule Leagues of 1916 were led by Bal Gangadhar Tilak and Annie Besant and demanded self-government through branches, meetings, pamphlets and political education.
  • The movement spread constitutional knowledge beyond annual Congress sessions and mobilised local volunteers during the First World War.
  • The Lucknow Pact of 1916 brought Congress and the Muslim League together on constitutional demands and accepted a scheme involving separate electorates.
  • The pact reflected temporary unity and negotiation, but it also carried forward the communal representation framework.
  • Tilak, Besant, Jinnah and other leaders helped create a moment of cooperation, though later politics changed significantly.
  • Home Rule and Lucknow show that constitutional agitation, mass pressure and inter-organisational negotiation could coexist.

Key points

  • Muslim League-1906

  • Home Rule-1916
  • Lucknow Pact-1916
  • Temporary Congress-League unity.

Concept 6

From Early Congress to Gandhian Mass Politics

War, repression and social mobilisation transformed Congress from a mainly constitutional platform into a wider organisation of mass struggle.

Step-by-step explanation

  • The First World War, economic hardship, political expectations and the failure of limited reforms created a tense post-war environment.
  • The Rowlatt Act of 1919 and Jallianwala Bagh weakened faith in British liberal promises and encouraged wider opposition.
  • Gandhi connected local grievances with all-India campaigns and introduced satyagraha, non-cooperation, khadi, volunteers and constructive work.
  • The Congress reorganised its structure and language to reach peasants, workers, students, women, merchants and local communities more effectively.
  • Non-Cooperation from 1920 shifted political action from petitions and council debate toward boycott and withdrawal of cooperation.
  • Civil Disobedience, Salt Satyagraha and Quit India later expanded the scale and intensity of national mobilisation.
  • Earlier moderate economic critique, assertive nationalism, Swadeshi, Home Rule and constitutional debate supplied experience and vocabulary for later movements.
  • The transition did not erase internal debates over class, caste, communal representation, methods, violence, council entry and social reform.

Key points

  • Post-war crisis
  • Rowlatt and Jallianwala
  • Gandhi method
  • Congress reorganisation
  • Non-Cooperation and later mass movements.
