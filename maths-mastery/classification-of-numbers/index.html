Classification of Numbers (Natural, Whole, Integers, Primes, Composite, Even/Odd)

Medium Difficulty 25-30 Mins Read High Exam Weightage Updated 2026

📘 1. Introduction to Classification of Numbers

In quantitative aptitude, Numbers are the fundamental building blocks—just like alphabets are to a language. Every competitive examination, including SSC CGL, Banking (IBPS/SBI), Railways (NTPC/Group D), CDS, and State PCS, tests your structural command over how numbers are classified and how they behave under mathematical operations.

Without mastering the classification of numbers, you will fall into classic examiners' traps involving terms like "non-negative integers", "coprime pairs", or "composite numbers". This microtopic forms the absolute backbone of the Number System, leading directly to high-scoring areas like Divisibility, Remainders, Unit Digit, and Algebra.

🎯 Exam Weightage: Directly 1-2 questions; indirectly applied in over 40% of the Quantitative Aptitude section.

🎯 2. Learning Objectives

  • Identify and categorize any given real number into its appropriate mathematical set (Natural, Whole, Integer, Rational, etc.).
  • Distinguish between Primes, Composites, Coprimes, and Twin Primes with complete conceptual clarity.
  • Master the parity rules of Even and Odd numbers and apply them to solve complex algebraic equations.
  • Perform rapid primality tests on numbers up to 1000 using standard mathematical shortcuts.
  • Identify and avoid common examiner traps, such as the unique properties of the numbers \( 0 \), \( 1 \), and \( 2 \).

📝 3. Prerequisites

Before diving into this topic, ensure you are comfortable with:

  • Basic Arithmetic Operations: Addition, subtraction, multiplication, and division.
  • Factors & Multiples: Knowing what it means when we say "a is divisible by b" (\( a \div b \) leaves no remainder).
  • Basic Inequality Notation: Terms like \( > 0 \), \( \ge 0 \), \( < 0 \).

💡 4. Core Concepts

Concept 1: Natural Numbers (\( \mathbb{N} \))

Definition: Counting numbers starting from 1 and extending to infinity.

$$ \mathbb{N} = \{1, 2, 3, 4, 5, \dots\} $$

Intuition: These are the physical quantities you can count with your fingers. You can count 3 apples, but you cannot physically point to "zero apples" or "negative three apples."

Key Observations:
  • The smallest natural number is \( 1 \).
  • There is no "largest" natural number, as they are infinite.
  • \( 0 \), negative numbers, and decimals/fractions are NOT natural numbers.
💡 Memory Tip: "N" stands for Natural or Nature. In nature, counting always begins with 1.
⚠️ Common Mistake: Including \( 0 \) in the set of natural numbers. Remember, \( 0 \) is NOT a counting number!
Mini Example: Is \( 2.5 \) a natural number? No, because counting is discrete. Is \( 10001 \) a natural number? Yes.

Concept 2: Whole Numbers (\( \mathbb{W} \))

Definition: The set of natural numbers combined with zero.

$$ \mathbb{W} = \{0, 1, 2, 3, 4, \dots\} $$

Intuition: Imagine a bucket of natural numbers; we drop \( 0 \) into this bucket to complete the set of whole numbers.

Key Observations:
  • The smallest whole number is \( 0 \).
  • All natural numbers are whole numbers, but not all whole numbers are natural numbers (specifically, \( 0 \)).
💡 Memory Tip: The word "Whole" contains the letter "O", which looks exactly like the number \( 0 \). This reminds you that Whole Numbers start with \( 0 \).
Mini Example: If \( x + y = 0 \) where \( x, y \in \mathbb{W} \), find \( x \) and \( y \). Since whole numbers cannot be negative, the only solution is \( x = 0 \) and \( y = 0 \).

Concept 3: Integers (\( \mathbb{Z} \))

Definition: The complete set of whole numbers along with their negative counter-parts.

$$ \mathbb{Z} = \{\dots, -3, -2, -1, 0, 1, 2, 3, \dots\} $$

Classification of Integers:

  • Positive Integers: \( \{1, 2, 3, \dots\} \) (Essentially equivalent to \( \mathbb{N} \))
  • Negative Integers: \( \{-1, -2, -3, \dots\} \)
  • Zero (\( 0 \)): Neither positive nor negative. It is neutral.
  • Non-Negative Integers: \( \{0, 1, 2, 3, \dots\} \) (Equivalent to Whole Numbers!)
  • Non-Positive Integers: \( \{\dots, -3, -2, -1, 0\} \)
⚠️ Examiner Trap: Many questions use the term "non-negative integers." Do not forget to include \( 0 \) in your cases!

Concept 4: Prime Numbers

Definition: Any natural number greater than \( 1 \) that has exactly two distinct factors: \( 1 \) and the number itself.

$$ \text{Primes } (P) = \{2, 3, 5, 7, 11, 13, 17, 19, 23, \dots\} $$
Crucial Properties of Prime Numbers:
  • Smallest Prime: \( 2 \) is the smallest prime number.
  • Even Prime: \( 2 \) is the only even prime number. All other prime numbers are odd.
  • Primes up to 100: There are exactly \( 25 \) prime numbers below \( 100 \). Learn them by heart!
💡 Memory Tip: The number \( 1 \) is neither prime nor composite because it does not have two distinct factors (its only factor is \( 1 \)).

Concept 5: Composite Numbers

Definition: Any natural number greater than \( 1 \) that has more than two factors (i.e., numbers that are not prime).

$$ \text{Composites } = \{4, 6, 8, 9, 10, 12, 14, 15, \dots\} $$
Key Observations:
  • The smallest composite number is \( 4 \).
  • The smallest odd composite number is \( 9 \).
  • Any natural number \( n > 1 \) is either prime or composite.

Concept 6: Coprime and Twin Prime Numbers

These terms cause massive confusion. Let's make them crystal clear:

  • Coprime (or Relatively Prime) Numbers: Two numbers (not necessarily prime individually) whose Highest Common Factor (HCF) is \( 1 \).
    Example: \( (8, 15) \). Neither \( 8 \) nor \( 15 \) is prime, but they are coprime because \( \text{HCF}(8, 15) = 1 \).
  • Twin Primes: A pair of prime numbers that have a difference of exactly \( 2 \).
    Example: \( (3, 5), (5, 7), (11, 13), (17, 19), (41, 43) \).

Concept 7: Even and Odd Numbers

Even Numbers: Any integer divisible by \( 2 \). They can be represented in the algebraic form:

$$ \text{Even} = 2k \quad (\text{where } k \in \mathbb{Z}) $$

Odd Numbers: Any integer not divisible by \( 2 \). They can be represented in the algebraic form:

$$ \text{Odd} = 2k \pm 1 \quad (\text{where } k \in \mathbb{Z}) $$
Is Zero (\( 0 \)) Even or Odd?

\( 0 \) is an even integer. It satisfies all properties of even numbers: \( 0 \div 2 = 0 \) (no remainder), and it fits the alternating pattern of numbers: ..., Odd (-1), Even (0), Odd (1), Even (2)...

📏 5. Rules & Properties

Rule 1: Parity Operations (Even/Odd Math)

When performing addition, subtraction, and multiplication on even and odd numbers, the output obeys strict laws:

Operation Even & Even Odd & Odd Even & Odd
Addition/Subtraction (\( \pm \)) Even (\( 4 \pm 2 = 6/2 \)) Even (\( 5 \pm 3 = 8/2 \)) Odd (\( 6 \pm 3 = 9/3 \))
Multiplication (\( \times \)) Even (\( 4 \times 2 = 8 \)) Odd (\( 5 \times 3 = 15 \)) Even (\( 4 \times 3 = 12 \))
🚀 Parity Rule of Thumb:
  • For Addition: Same sign yields Even, Different yields Odd.
  • For Multiplication: If there is even a single Even number in a multiplication series, the final product is always Even.

Rule 2: How to Test if a Number is Prime (\( \sqrt{N} \) Rule)

Method: To determine if a number \( N \) is prime:

  1. Find the approximate square root of \( N \) (let it be \( k \approx \sqrt{N} \)).
  2. List all prime numbers less than or equal to \( k \).
  3. Check if \( N \) is divisible by any of these listed prime numbers.
  4. If \( N \) is divisible by any of these primes, it is composite. Otherwise, it is prime.
Example: Is \( 137 \) prime?
1. \( \sqrt{137} \approx 11.7 \). Primes less than or equal to 11 are: \( 2, 3, 5, 7, 11 \).
2. Test divisibility of 137:
  • Not even (not div by 2)
  • Sum of digits \( = 1+3+7=11 \) (not div by 3)
  • Does not end in 0 or 5 (not div by 5)
  • \( 137 = 7 \times 19 + 4 \) (not div by 7)
  • \( 137 = 11 \times 12 + 5 \) (not div by 11)
Hence, 137 is a Prime Number!

Rule 3: General Algebraic Form of Primes

Every prime number greater than \( 3 \) can be expressed in the form:

$$ 6k \pm 1 \quad (\text{where } k \in \mathbb{N}) $$
⚠️ Crucial Exception: This is a one-way rule. While every prime \( > 3 \) is of the form \( 6k \pm 1 \), not every number written as \( 6k \pm 1 \) is a prime.
Counter-example: For \( k = 4 \), \( 6k + 1 = 25 \), which is a composite number!

📐 6. Formula Box

Formula 1: Sum of First \( n \) Natural Numbers

$$ S_n = \frac{n(n + 1)}{2} $$

Variables: \( n = \) the number of consecutive terms starting from 1.

When to use: Sum of series like \( 1 + 2 + 3 + \dots + 50 \).

Formula 2: Sum of First \( n \) Consecutive Even Numbers

$$ S_{\text{even}} = n(n + 1) $$

Variables: \( n = \) total number of terms (e.g., for sum of even numbers up to 50, \( n = 25 \)).

Formula 3: Sum of First \( n \) Consecutive Odd Numbers

$$ S_{\text{odd}} = n^2 $$
💡 Memory Trick: The sum of the first 3 odd numbers is \( 1 + 3 + 5 = 9 = 3^2 \). This holds true universally!

🧠 7. Solved Examples

Easy

Example 1: Identification of Primes

Which of the following numbers is a prime number?

(A) 221    (B) 161    (C) 143    (D) 197

View Step-by-Step Solution

Let's check each option using basic divisibility or the \( \sqrt{N} \) method:

  • 221: \( \sqrt{221} \approx 14.8 \). Primes to check: 2, 3, 5, 7, 11, 13.
    Does \( 13 \) divide 221? Yes, \( 13 \times 17 = 221 \). (Composite)
  • 161: Does \( 7 \) divide 161? Yes, \( 7 \times 23 = 161 \). (Composite)
  • 143: Does \( 11 \) divide 143? Yes, \( 11 \times 13 = 143 \). (Composite)
  • 197: \( \sqrt{197} \approx 14 \). Primes to check: 2, 3, 5, 7, 11, 13.
    Testing: Not div by 2, 3 (sum is 17), 5, 7 (\( 197 = 7 \times 28 + 1 \)), 11 (\( 197 = 11 \times 17 + 10 \)), or 13 (\( 197 = 13 \times 15 + 2 \)).
    Therefore, 197 is prime.

Correct Answer: (D)

Medium

Example 2: Prime Parity Application

If \( x \) and \( y \) are prime numbers such that \( x + y = 2003 \), find the value of the product \( x \cdot y \).

View Step-by-Step Solution

We are given that \( x \) and \( y \) are prime numbers, and:

$$ x + y = 2003 $$

Notice that \( 2003 \) is an odd number.

Recall our parity rule for addition:

$$ \text{Even} + \text{Odd} = \text{Odd} $$

This means one of the primes must be Even, and the other must be Odd.

Since the only even prime number in existence is \( 2 \), one of the variables must be \( 2 \).

Let \( x = 2 \). Then:

$$ 2 + y = 2003 \implies y = 2001 $$

But wait, is \( 2001 \) a prime? Let's check its digital sum: \( 2 + 0 + 0 + 1 = 3 \). Because the sum of its digits is divisible by 3, \( 2001 \) is divisible by 3 and is NOT prime.

Correction / Trap check: The problem assumes \( x \) and \( y \) are primes. Let's look at the equation: \( x + y = 2001 \) or a similar form. If \( x+y=2003 \) where \( x, y \) are primes, then one MUST be 2, and the other MUST be 2001. Since 2001 is not prime, no such primes exist! Let's adapt this to an actual exam-favorite question where the sum is \( 99 \):

Let's solve for \( x + y = 99 \):

Again, sum is odd, so one prime must be even (\( 2 \)).

$$ 2 + y = 99 \implies y = 97 $$

Since \( 97 \) is prime, this is a valid pair. Now calculate the product:

$$ x \cdot y = 2 \cdot 97 = 194 $$

Correct Answer: 194 (for sum = 99)

Hard

Example 3: Prime Triplets Concept

How many sets of three consecutive prime numbers exist in the form of \( (p, p+2, p+4) \)?

View Step-by-Step Solution

Let's analyze the properties of three consecutive odd numbers \( p, p+2, p+4 \).

When divided by 3, any integer leaves a remainder of \( 0, 1 \), or \( 2 \). Let's test the remainders of the three numbers:

  • If \( p \) leaves remainder 0 when divided by 3, then \( p \) must be a multiple of 3. Since \( p \) is prime, the only possibility is \( p = 3 \).
  • If \( p \) leaves remainder 1, then \( p+2 \) is a multiple of 3. For \( p+2 \) to be prime, \( p+2 = 3 \implies p=1 \) (not prime).
  • If \( p \) leaves remainder 2, then \( p+4 \) is a multiple of 3. For \( p+4 \) to be prime, \( p+4 = 3 \implies p=-1 \) (not prime).

Hence, the only possible solution is \( p = 3 \). This gives us the triplet:

$$ (3, 5, 7) $$

There are no other such triplets in the entire infinite line of numbers!

Correct Answer: Exactly 1 set.

⚡ 8. Exam Tricks & Shortcuts

1. Prime Number Unit Digit Trick

If a question asks for the unit digit of the product of all prime numbers, do not calculate!
The primes include \( 2 \) and \( 5 \).
Since \( 2 \times 5 = 10 \), the product will always end in a 0.
Similarly, the product of all odd prime numbers always ends in 5.

2. Instant Coprime Identifier

Any two consecutive natural numbers (e.g., 14 and 15, 99 and 100) are always coprime. Their HCF is guaranteed to be \( 1 \).

⚠️ 9. Common Mistakes & Pitfalls

The "1 is Prime" Trap

Mistake: Frequently listing 1 as the smallest prime number.

Correction: By absolute definition, prime numbers must have *exactly* two distinct factors. 1 has only one factor (itself), so 1 is neither prime nor composite.

Confusing "Primes" with "Coprimes"

Mistake: Assuming that a coprime pair must consist of prime numbers.

Correction: Coprimality is a relative property. A coprime pair can consist of entirely composite numbers (e.g., \( 8 \) and \( 9 \)) as long as they share no common factor other than \( 1 \).

📊 10. Exam Strategy & Weightage

SSC CGL/CHSL

Expect direct questions on prime number intervals (e.g., "Find sum of primes between 50 and 70") and statement-based structural properties.

CDS & State PCS

Tests conceptual statements: e.g., "Every prime of the form \( 6k \pm 1 \) is always odd (True/False)". Strong conceptual hold is required here.

Railways (NTPC)

Direct classifications: testing whether a large 3-digit number is prime or composite, or finding sums of even/odd series.

📌 11. Summary

Numbers are hierarchically classified. All numbers used in standard aptitude are Real Numbers, which split into Rational (terminating/recurring decimals) and Irrational (non-terminating, non-recurring). Under Rational numbers, we have Integers. Under Integers, we have Whole numbers and Natural numbers.

🚀 13. Key Takeaways

  • Never assume "integer" means only positive; check for zero and negative conditions.
  • Use parity rules (\( E/O \)) to quickly eliminate incorrect options in complex algebraic equation questions.
  • When testing for prime, only test divisibility of prime numbers up to \( \sqrt{N} \). Do not waste time dividing by composite numbers like 4, 6, or 8.
