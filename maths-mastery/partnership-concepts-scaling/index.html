Partnership Concepts & Scaling

Medium Difficulty 25-30 Mins Read High Exam Weightage Updated 2026

📘 1. Introduction

In commercial arithmetic, Partnership is an extension of ratio and proportion that deals with business associations where two or more people pool their financial resources to run an enterprise. Unlike basic ratio problems, Partnership problems introduce the critical dimension of Time alongside Investment (Capital).

For any competitive exam aspirant (SSC CGL/CHSL, Bank PO, Railways, or CDS), mastering this microtopic is indispensable. It is highly scoring, conceptually direct, and yields at least 1 to 2 direct questions in almost every preliminary and main exam phase. The beauty of this topic lies in its simplicity: a single core formula rules the entire domain, and complex situations are merely applications of smart ratio scaling.

🎯 2. Learning Objectives

  • Establish the fundamental relationship between Investment, Time, and Profit Sharing.
  • Scale and balance complex fractional, percentage, or sequential ratio investments.
  • Deconstruct multi-variable partnerships where investments fluctuate mid-way.
  • Analyze business models involving Active (Working) vs. Sleeping (Passive) partners.
  • Develop rapid computation strategies like LCM scaling and digit unit check methods to solve exam questions under 45 seconds.

📝 3. Prerequisites

Before diving into Partnership & Scaling, ensure you are comfortable with these building blocks:

  • Ratio Balancing: Combining individual ratios (e.g., if \(A:B = 2:3\) and \(B:C = 4:5\), then \(A:B:C = 8:12:15\)).
  • Fraction to Percentage Conversions: Understanding that \(12.5\% = \frac{1}{8}\), \(16\frac{2}{3}\% = \frac{1}{6}\), etc.
  • LCM Method: Using Least Common Multipliers to convert fractional ratios into clean integers.

💡 4. Core Concepts

Concept 1: The Fundamental Law of Partnership

Definition: Profit distributed in any business venture is directly proportional to both the amount invested (Capital) and the duration for which it is invested (Time).

Explanation: If person A invests capital \(I_1\) for time \(T_1\), and person B invests capital \(I_2\) for time \(T_2\), then their profits \(P_1\) and \(P_2\) are shared in the ratio:

$$P_1 : P_2 = (I_1 \times T_1) : (I_2 \times T_2)$$

Intuition: Money makes money, but the longer it works, the more it earns. A small capital invested for a long time can yield the same profit share as a large capital invested for a short time.

Important Observation: If the time periods of all investments are equal (\(T_1 = T_2 = T_3\)), the profits are shared purely in the ratio of their investments:

$$P_1 : P_2 = I_1 : I_2$$

Memory Tip: Think of profit as "value-months". Multiply Capital by Months to get your profit points!

Common Mistake: Directing profit shares solely in the ratio of investments while completely ignoring different time-frames.

Mini Example: Akhil invests ₹10,000 for 12 months, and Bharat invests ₹15,000 for 6 months. Ratio of profits:

$$P_A : P_B = (10,000 \times 12) : (15,000 \times 6) = 120,000 : 90,000 = 4:3$$

Concept 2: Ratio Scaling & Fractional Capital

Definition: Handling investments expressed as relative fractions or percentages rather than absolute values.

Explanation: Questions often state: "A invests \(\frac{1}{3}\) of the total capital, B invests \(\frac{1}{4}\) of the remaining, and C invests the rest."

Intuition: Working with absolute values or fractions slows down calculation. Scaling involves choosing a smart variable for total capital based on the denominators of the fractions involved.

Mini Example: Let Total Capital = \(\text{LCM}(3, 4) = 12\) units.

  • A's share = \(\frac{1}{3} \times 12 = 4\) units.
  • Remaining capital = \(12 - 4 = 8\) units.
  • B's share = \(\frac{1}{4} \times 8 = 2\) units.
  • C's share (rest) = \(12 - (4 + 2) = 6\) units.
Hence, the investment ratio is scaled neatly as \(4 : 2 : 6 \implies 2 : 1 : 3\).

Concept 3: Active (Working) vs. Sleeping (Passive) Partners

Definition: An active partner manages the business and receives a salary or a fixed percentage of the profit as compensation, while a sleeping partner only contributes capital and receives profits based purely on the investment-time ratio.

Explanation: Salary or commission is deducted from the *Total Profit* first. The *Remaining Profit* is then divided among partners in their respective \(I \times T\) ratios.

Common Mistake: Dividing the total profit first and then subtracting the salary from a single partner's final share without considering the pool source.

Mini Example: Total Profit is ₹10,000. Active partner A gets 10% as salary. Remaining ₹9,000 is shared in investment ratio. Do not distribute ₹10,000 directly; set aside ₹1,000 for A first, then split the remaining ₹9,000.

📏 5. Rules & Properties

Rule 1: Inverse Parameter Calculation Rules

Explanation: If we are given the Profit Ratio and any one of the other parameters (either Investment or Time Ratio), we can calculate the missing ratio parameter using division.

Reason: Since \(P = I \times T\), we can mathematically derive:

$$I = \frac{P}{T} \quad \text{and} \quad T = \frac{P}{I}$$

Shortcut Formulas:

  • Ratio of Investments: \(I_1 : I_2 : I_3 = \frac{P_1}{T_1} : \frac{P_2}{T_2} : \frac{P_3}{T_3}\)
  • Ratio of Times: \(T_1 : T_2 : T_3 = \frac{P_1}{I_1} : \frac{P_2}{I_2} : \frac{P_3}{I_3}\)

Example: The profit ratio of three partners A, B, and C is \(5:3:12\) and their investment time periods are in the ratio \(2:3:4\). Find their investment ratio.

$$I_A : I_B : I_C = \frac{5}{2} : \frac{3}{3} : \frac{12}{4} = \frac{5}{2} : 1 : 3$$

Multiply by 2 to clear denominators: \(5 : 2 : 6\).

📐 6. Formula Box

General Profit Sharing Formula

$$P_1 : P_2 : P_3 = (I_1 \times T_1) : (I_2 \times T_2) : (I_3 \times T_3)$$

Variables:

  • \(P_n\) = Profit share of partner \(n\)
  • \(I_n\) = Invested capital of partner \(n\)
  • \(T_n\) = Time duration of partner \(n\)'s investment

When to Use: Standard compound partnership where capital and time periods differ.

When NOT to Use: If the investments change mid-way, you must break the time into sections (e.g., \(I_{1}t_{1} + I_{2}t_{2}\)) instead of multiplying single values.

Active Partner Division Scheme

$$\text{Share of Active Partner } (A) = \text{Salary} + \left( \frac{I_A \times T_A}{\sum(I \times T)} \times (\text{Total Profit} - \text{Salary}) \right)$$

Memory Trick: Salary is a "pre-tax" deduction for the business. Clear the dues first, then split the loot!

🧠 7. Solved Examples

Easy

Example 1: Basic Compound Partnership

Question: A, B, and C enter into a partnership. A invests ₹40,000 for 6 months, B invests ₹50,000 for 8 months, and C invests ₹60,000 for 12 months. If the total profit at the end of the term is ₹34,000, find B's share of the profit.

View Step-by-Step Solution

Step 1: Simplify investments. Divide all capitals by ₹10,000 to work with easier values:
A's capital = \(4\), B's capital = \(5\), C's capital = \(6\).

Step 2: Calculate Profit Share Ratio (\(P = I \times T\)).
\(P_A : P_B : P_C = (4 \times 6) : (5 \times 8) : (6 \times 12)\)
\(P_A : P_B : P_C = 24 : 40 : 72\)

Step 3: Simplify the ratios by dividing by 8:
\(P_A : P_B : P_C = 3 : 5 : 9\)

Step 4: Find B's profit.
Sum of ratio units = \(3 + 5 + 9 = 17\) units.
Given: \(17 \text{ units} = ₹34,000\)
\(1 \text{ unit} = \frac{34,000}{17} = ₹2,000\)
B's share = \(5 \text{ units} = 5 \times 2,000 = ₹10,000\).

Answer: ₹10,000

Medium

Example 2: Fractional Investment & Scaling

Question: In a business, A invests \(\frac{1}{6}\) of the total capital for \(\frac{1}{6}\) of the total time. B invests \(\frac{1}{3}\) of the total capital for \(\frac{1}{3}\) of the total time. C invests the remaining capital for the entire time duration. If the total profit generated is ₹23,000, what is A's share?

View Step-by-Step Solution

Step 1: Use the LCM scaling technique.
Denominators of Capital are \(6\) and \(3\) \(\implies \text{Let Total Capital} = 6\) units.
Denominators of Time are \(6\) and \(3\) \(\implies \text{Let Total Time} = 6\) months.

Step 2: Calculate individual parameters based on assumptions.

  • Partner A:
    Capital = \(\frac{1}{6} \times 6 = 1\) unit
    Time = \(\frac{1}{6} \times 6 = 1\) month
  • Partner B:
    Capital = \(\frac{1}{3} \times 6 = 2\) units
    Time = \(\frac{1}{3} \times 6 = 2\) months
  • Partner C:
    Capital = Remaining capital = \(6 - (1 + 2) = 3\) units
    Time = Entire duration = \(6\) months

Step 3: Construct the Profit Ratio.
\(P_A : P_B : P_C = (1 \times 1) : (2 \times 2) : (3 \times 6) = 1 : 4 : 18\)

Step 4: Distribute the profit.
Total Profit Units = \(1 + 4 + 18 = 23\) units.
Given: \(23 \text{ units} = ₹23,000 \implies 1 \text{ unit} = ₹1,000\).
A's share = \(1 \text{ unit} = ₹1,000\).

Answer: ₹1,000

Hard

Example 3: Active Partner with Salary Commission

Question: A and B start a business with investments of ₹70,000 and ₹50,000 respectively. A is the active partner and is paid \(12.5\%\) of the total profit as salary. The remaining profit is distributed in the ratio of their investments. If A receives a total of ₹5,200 more than B at the end of the year, find the total profit of the business.

View Step-by-Step Solution

Step 1: Simplify parameters and rates.
Investment Ratio \(A : B = 70,000 : 50,000 = 7 : 5\). (Sum of ratio parts = \(12\)).
Salary for A = \(12.5\% = \frac{1}{8}\) of total profit.

Step 2: Assume a scaled value for Total Profit.
To make the calculations clean and divisible by both \(8\) (for the salary fraction) and \(12\) (for the remaining profit distribution):
Let Total Profit = \(\text{LCM}(8, 12) \times \text{scaling factor} = 24 \times 4 = 96\) units.

Step 3: Distribute the units.

  • A's Salary = \(\frac{1}{8} \times 96 \text{ units} = 12\) units.
  • Remaining Profit = \(96 - 12 = 84\) units.
Now, divide the remaining 84 units in the ratio of \(7 : 5\) between A and B.
Value of 1 part of remaining profit = \(\frac{84}{12} = 7\) units.
  • A's Profit Share = \(7 \text{ parts} \times 7 = 49\) units.
  • B's Profit Share = \(5 \text{ parts} \times 7 = 35\) units.

Step 4: Compute total earnings and find differences.
Total amount received by A = Salary + Profit Share = \(12 + 49 = 61\) units.
Total amount received by B = Profit Share = \(35\) units.
Difference between A's and B's total earnings = \(61 - 35 = 26\) units.

Step 5: Solve for the absolute profit.
Given: \(26 \text{ units} = ₹5,200\)
\(1 \text{ unit} = \frac{5,200}{26} = ₹200\)
Total Profit = \(96 \text{ units} = 96 \times 200 = ₹19,200\).

Answer: ₹19,200

Exam Level

Example 4: Fluctuating Capitals (Mid-Year Changes)

Question: A, B, and C enter into a partnership. A invests ₹50,000 initially. After 4 months, B joins with ₹60,000. After another 2 months (6 months from start), C joins with ₹80,000. At the end of the first year, A withdraws ₹10,000, B adds ₹20,000, and C withdraws ₹20,000. If the total profit at the end of 2 years is ₹1,55,000, find C's share of the profit.

View Step-by-Step Solution

Step 1: Simplify baseline values.
Let 1 unit of capital = ₹10,000.
Total project timeline = 24 months (2 years).

Step 2: Map out investments and active months.

  • Partner A:
    First 12 months: Capital = \(5\) units.
    Next 12 months: Capital = \(5 - 1 = 4\) units.
    Total Value-Months for A = \((5 \times 12) + (4 \times 12) = 60 + 48 = 108\)
  • Partner B:
    Joins after 4 months. In Year 1, B is active for \(12 - 4 = 8\) months with \(6\) units.
    In Year 2 (12 months), B adds 2 units. Capital = \(6 + 2 = 8\) units.
    Total Value-Months for B = \((6 \times 8) + (8 \times 12) = 48 + 96 = 144\)
  • Partner C:
    Joins after 6 months. In Year 1, C is active for \(12 - 6 = 6\) months with \(8\) units.
    In Year 2 (12 months), C withdraws 2 units. Capital = \(8 - 2 = 6\) units.
    Total Value-Months for C = \((8 \times 6) + (6 \times 12) = 48 + 72 = 120\)

Step 3: Determine the simplified Profit Ratio.
\(P_A : P_B : P_C = 108 : 144 : 120\)
Divide all terms by 12:
\(P_A : P_B : P_C = 9 : 12 : 10\)

Step 4: Distribute final profits.
Sum of ratio parts = \(9 + 12 + 10 = 31\) units.
Given: \(31 \text{ units} = ₹1,55,000\)
\(1 \text{ unit} = \frac{1,55,000}{31} = ₹5,000\)
C's Share = \(10 \text{ units} = 10 \times 5,000 = ₹50,000\).

Answer: ₹50,000

⚡ 8. Exam Tricks & Shortcuts

Trick 1: The LCM Total Assumption

Never work with fractions directly in a partnership question. If A invests \(\frac{2}{5}\) and B invests \(\frac{1}{3}\), assume total capital to be \(15\). This saves at least 15 seconds of fraction computation and prevents arithmetic errors.

Trick 2: Ratio Scaling of Multi-Zero Numbers

In exams like Bank PO or SSC, investments are given in large figures like ₹1,12,500 and ₹1,35,000. Cancel common zeros immediately and check for common divisors (like 25, 125, or 9) to simplify ratios to basic integers before attempting to multiply by time periods.

Trick 3: Digital Sum & Divisibility Verification

If you find that C's profit share ratio is 11 units, look at the options first! The correct option must be a multiple of 11. This often eliminates 3 out of 4 options instantly without completing the full calculation.

⚠️ 9. Common Mistakes & Pitfalls

1. Confusing "Time Left" with "Active Duration"

The Mistake: If A joins a business "after 4 months", students often multiply A's capital by 4.
Correction: If A joins 4 months after the start in a 12-month venture, his capital was used for the remaining period, which is \(12 - 4 = 8\) months. Always multiply by the active time of investment.

2. Mismanaging Mid-Term Adjustments

The Mistake: If A invests ₹50,000, and adds ₹10,000 after 6 months, writing the term as \(60,000 \times 12\) is wrong.
Correction: Treat it as: \((50,000 \times 6) + (60,000 \times 6)\). Break the timeline cleanly.

📊 10. Exam Strategy & Weightage

SSC CGL (Tier 1 & 2)

Pattern: Heavy emphasis on mid-year capital additions/withdrawals and active-partner setups.

Weightage: 2-3 Questions (Often featured in Data Interpretation caselets).

Railways & State Exams

Weightage: 1 Question.
Pattern: Directly proportional simple investments with basic integer values.

📌 11. Summary

  • The cornerstone of partnership is: \(\text{Profit} = \text{Investment} \times \text{Time}\).
  • For unequal time and capital, use: \(P_1:P_2:P_3 = I_1 T_1 : I_2 T_2 : I_3 T_3\).
  • When investments are given as fractions, use LCM of denominators to establish a clean integer base.
  • Active partner salaries are always deducted first before distributing the leftover profits among partners.

⚡ 1-Page Cheatsheet Summary

Scenario Key Equation / Logic Pro-Tip
Equal Time \(P_1 : P_2 = I_1 : I_2\) Ignore time values completely.
Unequal Time \(P_1 : P_2 = I_1 T_1 : I_2 T_2\) Convert years to months if periods are uneven.
Finding Time Ratio \(T_1 : T_2 = \frac{P_1}{I_1} : \frac{P_2}{I_2}\) Cross-multiply to eliminate denominators quickly.
Working Partner \(\text{Net Profit} = \text{Total} - \text{Salary}\) Distribute Net Profit in investment-time ratio.

🚀 13. Key Takeaways

  • Always look to establish the ratio of \((I \times T)\) as your first step.
  • Use option elimination based on prime factors of the profit ratio.
  • Ensure the units of time (months vs. years) are uniform across all partners before computing products.
