Square Roots, Cube Roots & Surds Basics

Medium Difficulty 25-30 Mins Read High Exam Weightage Updated 2026

📘 1. Introduction to Square Roots, Cube Roots & Surds

In competitive examinations, speed and accuracy are the two pillars of success. Among all the calculation-intensive areas in the Quantitative Aptitude section, Square Roots, Cube Roots & Surds form the bedrock of the BODMAS & Simplification topic.

Whether you are solving a complex compound interest problem, calculating heights and distances in trigonometry, or simplifying a multi-layered algebraic expression, roots and surds appear universally. This microtopic teaches you how to look past the intimidating radical signs (\( \sqrt{} \), \( \sqrt[3]{} \)) and resolve them within seconds using mental maths and structured mathematical laws.

Exam Relevance: In SSC CGL, CHSL, Railway NTPC, and Banking exams, direct questions on comparing surds, finding the square roots of binomial surds, and rationalization are highly frequent, carrying a weightage of 1 to 3 questions directly under Simplification.

🎯 2. Learning Objectives

By the end of this module, you will be able to:

  • Identify and define Surds and distinguish them from rational numbers.
  • Calculate the Square Root of perfect square numbers up to 5 digits using the Unit Digit and Range Method.
  • Compute Cube Roots of perfect cubes up to 6 digits within 3 seconds using the Unit Digit Method.
  • Apply fundamental laws of Surds to simplify complex radical expressions.
  • Rationalize binomial denominators and compare surds of different orders efficiently.

📝 3. Prerequisites

Before diving into Surds and Roots, make sure you are comfortable with the following foundational concepts:

  • Perfect Squares (1 to 30): E.g., \( 15^2 = 225 \), \( 26^2 = 676 \), \( 29^2 = 841 \).
  • Perfect Cubes (1 to 15): E.g., \( 8^3 = 512 \), \( 11^3 = 1331 \), \( 12^3 = 1728 \).
  • Basic Rules of Indices: Especially \( a^m \times a^n = a^{m+n} \) and \( (a^m)^n = a^{m \times n} \).

🧠 4. Core Concepts

Concept 1: Square Roots (\( \sqrt{x} \))

Definition: The square root of a number \( x \) is a number \( y \) such that \( y^2 = x \). It is denoted by the radical symbol \( \sqrt{x} \) or written exponentially as \( x^{1/2} \).

Intuition: Think of a square root as finding the side length of a square when its total area is given.

Important Observations:

  • Perfect squares can only end in the digits 0, 1, 4, 5, 6, or 9.
  • A perfect square never ends in 2, 3, 7, or 8.

Memory Tip: Observe the relationship of the unit digits: if a square ends in 9, its square root must end in either 3 or 7 (since \( 3^2 = 9 \) and \( 7^2 = 49 \)).

Common Mistake: Confusing \( \sqrt{x^2} = x \) with algebraic situations. Remember that \( \sqrt{x^2} = |x| \) (always yields a non-negative real value in real number systems).

Mini Example: Find \( \sqrt{576} \). Since it ends in 6, the root ends in 4 or 6. The number lies between \( 20^2 = 400 \) and \( 30^2 = 900 \). Since 576 is closer to 400 than 900, the root is \( 24 \).

Concept 2: Cube Roots (\( \sqrt[3]{x} \))

Definition: The cube root of a number \( x \) is a value \( y \) such that \( y^3 = x \). It is denoted as \( \sqrt[3]{x} \) or \( x^{1/3} \).

Intuition: Finding the edge length of a cube given its total volume.

Important Observation: Unlike square roots, cube roots can be negative (e.g., \( \sqrt[3]{-8} = -2 \)) because a negative number multiplied by itself three times remains negative.

Memory Tip: The unit digit of a cube root is unique and has a direct one-to-one mapping:

  • Unit digits that remain same: \( 1 \to 1 \), \( 4 \to 4 \), \( 5 \to 5 \), \( 6 \to 6 \), \( 9 \to 9 \), \( 0 \to 0 \)
  • Unit digits that swap: \( 2 \leftrightarrow 8 \), \( 3 \leftrightarrow 7 \)

Mini Example: Find \( \sqrt[3]{4096} \). Since the unit digit is 6, the cube root ends in 6. Striking off the last three digits leaves 4. The largest perfect cube less than or equal to 4 is 1 (whose cube root is 1). Thus, \( \sqrt[3]{4096} = 16 \).

Concept 3: What is a Surd?

Definition: When a root of a rational number cannot be simplified to a rational number, it is called a Surd. In simple terms, a surd is an irrational number expressed with a radical symbol.

For example, \( \sqrt{3} \), \( \sqrt[3]{5} \), and \( \sqrt[4]{10} \) are surds because their values are non-terminating, non-recurring decimals. However, \( \sqrt{9} \) is not a surd because \( \sqrt{9} = 3 \) (a rational number).

Types of Surds:

  • Simple Surd: Contains only a single surd term (e.g., \( \sqrt{5} \)).
  • Mixed Surd: Combination of a rational number and a surd (e.g., \( 3\sqrt{2} \)).
  • Binomial Surd: Sum or difference of two surds or a rational number and a surd (e.g., \( 5 + \sqrt{3} \)).

📏 5. Rules & Properties

Rule 1: Product Rule of Surds

Mathematical Statement: \( \sqrt[n]{a} \times \sqrt[n]{b} = \sqrt[n]{ab} \)

Explanation: You can multiply two surds with the same root index by multiplying their radicands under a single radical sign.

Shortcut/Reasoning: Based on exponential law: \( a^{1/n} \times b^{1/n} = (ab)^{1/n} \).

Exception: This rule is valid only for real numbers when at least one of \( a \) or \( b \) is non-negative. \( \sqrt{-2} \times \sqrt{-3} \neq \sqrt{6} \) (it belongs to imaginary numbers).

Example: \( \sqrt{8} \times \sqrt{2} = \sqrt{16} = 4 \).

Rule 2: Quotient Rule of Surds

Mathematical Statement: \( \frac{\sqrt[n]{a}}{\sqrt[n]{b}} = \sqrt[n]{\frac{a}{b}} \quad (b \neq 0) \)

Explanation: Division of two surds with the same index can be combined under a single root.

Example: \( \frac{\sqrt{75}}{\sqrt{3}} = \sqrt{\frac{75}{3}} = \sqrt{25} = 5 \).

Rule 3: Power of a Surd

Mathematical Statement: \( (\sqrt[n]{a})^n = a \)

Explanation: Raising a surd of index \( n \) to the power of \( n \) completely eliminates the radical sign.

Example: \( (\sqrt[5]{17})^5 = 17 \).

Rule 4: Root of a Root (Nested Indices)

Mathematical Statement: \( \sqrt[m]{\sqrt[n]{a}} = \sqrt[mn]{a} = \sqrt[n]{\sqrt[m]{a}} \)

Explanation: Consecutive root signs can be consolidated into a single root by multiplying the indices.

Example: \( \sqrt{\sqrt[3]{64}} = \sqrt[6]{64} = 2 \).

📐 6. Formula Box

Formula 1: Square Root of a Binomial Surd

$$ \sqrt{a \pm 2\sqrt{b}} = \sqrt{x} \pm \sqrt{y} $$

Where: \( x + y = a \) and \( xy = b \) (with \( x > y \)).

When to Use: When asked to simplify nested square roots of the form \( \sqrt{7 + 4\sqrt{3}} \) or \( \sqrt{12 - 2\sqrt{35}} \).

Memory Trick: It is based on the identity \( (u \pm v)^2 = u^2 + v^2 \pm 2uv \). Always convert the coefficient of the inner root to exactly 2 first.

Formula 2: Rationalization Factor

$$ \text{Rationalizing factor of } (a + \sqrt{b}) \text{ is } (a - \sqrt{b}) $$

Why: Multiplying them uses the algebraic identity \( (x+y)(x-y) = x^2 - y^2 \), which eliminates the square root: \( (a + \sqrt{b})(a - \sqrt{b}) = a^2 - b \).

When NOT to Use: Do not change both signs. Only change the middle sign linking the rational term and the surd.

💡 7. Solved Examples

Easy Example 1: Basic Simplification

Question: Simplify the expression: \( \sqrt{18} + \sqrt{50} - \sqrt{72} \).

View Step-by-Step Solution

Step 1: Factor each radicand to expose perfect squares.

  • \( \sqrt{18} = \sqrt{9 \times 2} = 3\sqrt{2} \)
  • \( \sqrt{50} = \sqrt{25 \times 2} = 5\sqrt{2} \)
  • \( \sqrt{72} = \sqrt{36 \times 2} = 6\sqrt{2} \)

Step 2: Substitute these values back into the expression.

$$ 3\sqrt{2} + 5\sqrt{2} - 6\sqrt{2} $$

Step 3: Perform operations like adding similar terms.

$$ (3 + 5 - 6)\sqrt{2} = 2\sqrt{2} $$

Answer: \( 2\sqrt{2} \)

Medium Example 2: Rationalization

Question: Rationalize the denominator of: \( \frac{5}{3 - \sqrt{7}} \).

View Step-by-Step Solution

Step 1: Identify the conjugate of the denominator. The conjugate of \( 3 - \sqrt{7} \) is \( 3 + \sqrt{7} \).

Step 2: Multiply both the numerator and the denominator by this conjugate.

$$ \frac{5}{3 - \sqrt{7}} \times \frac{3 + \sqrt{7}}{3 + \sqrt{7}} = \frac{5(3 + \sqrt{7})}{(3)^2 - (\sqrt{7})^2} $$

Step 3: Simplify the denominator using \( a^2 - b^2 \).

$$ (3)^2 - (\sqrt{7})^2 = 9 - 7 = 2 $$

Step 4: Write the simplified expression.

$$ \frac{5(3 + \sqrt{7})}{2} = \frac{15 + 5\sqrt{7}}{2} $$

Answer: \( \frac{15 + 5\sqrt{7}}{2} \)

Hard Example 3: Nested Square Roots

Question: Simplify: \( \sqrt{12 - 2\sqrt{35}} \).

View Step-by-Step Solution

Step 1: Identify the pattern. This is of the form \( \sqrt{a - 2\sqrt{b}} \), where \( a = 12 \) and \( b = 35 \).

Step 2: Find two numbers \( x \) and \( y \) such that:

  • \( x + y = 12 \)
  • \( x \times y = 35 \)

The factors of 35 that add up to 12 are \( 7 \) and \( 5 \).

Step 3: Write the solution using the formula \( \sqrt{x} - \sqrt{y} \) (since there is a minus sign).

$$ \sqrt{12 - 2\sqrt{35}} = \sqrt{7} - \sqrt{5} $$

Verification: \( (\sqrt{7} - \sqrt{5})^2 = 7 + 5 - 2\sqrt{35} = 12 - 2\sqrt{35} \). Correct!

Answer: \( \sqrt{7} - \sqrt{5} \)

Exam Level Example 4: Comparing Surds

Question: (SSC CGL Tier-1) Arrange the following surds in ascending order: \( \sqrt[3]{4} \), \( \sqrt[4]{6} \), \( \sqrt[6]{15} \).

View Step-by-Step Solution

Step 1: List the fractional exponents of the surds:

$$ 4^{1/3}, \quad 6^{1/4}, \quad 15^{1/6} $$

Step 2: Find the LCM of the denominators of the powers (3, 4, 6). The LCM of 3, 4, and 6 is 12.

Step 3: Express each exponent with the common denominator 12:

  • \( 4^{1/3} = 4^{4/12} = (4^4)^{1/12} = (256)^{1/12} \)
  • \( 6^{1/4} = 6^{3/12} = (6^3)^{1/12} = (216)^{1/12} \)
  • \( 15^{1/6} = 15^{2/12} = (15^2)^{1/12} = (225)^{1/12} \)

Step 4: Compare the base values inside the parenthesis:

Since \( 216 < 225 < 256 \), it follows that:

$$ (216)^{1/12} < (225)^{1/12} < (256)^{1/12} $$

$$ \sqrt[4]{6} < \sqrt[6]{15} < \sqrt[3]{4} $$

Answer: \( \sqrt[4]{6} < \sqrt[6]{15} < \sqrt[3]{4} \)

⚡ 8. Exam Tricks & Shortcuts

Trick 1: Visual Estimation of Binomial Roots

If you see a form like \( \sqrt{a + \sqrt{b}} \), make sure to divide the inside coefficient by 2. For example, in \( \sqrt{7 + 4\sqrt{3}} \), write it as \( \sqrt{7 + 2(2\sqrt{3})} \). Let \( 2\sqrt{3} = \sqrt{12} \). Now search for two numbers adding to 7 and multiplying to 12. They are 4 and 3. Thus, the square root is \( \sqrt{4} + \sqrt{3} = 2 + \sqrt{3} \).

Trick 2: Rapid Rationalization mentally

For fractions with unit difference in square terms, like \( \frac{1}{\sqrt{7} - \sqrt{6}} \), the simplified value is directly the conjugate \( \sqrt{7} + \sqrt{6} \) because the denominator difference \( (\sqrt{7})^2 - (\sqrt{6})^2 = 7 - 6 = 1 \).

Trick 3: Quick Square Roots of Large Perfect Squares

To find \( \sqrt{5184} \):

  • Separate the last 2 digits: \( 51 | 84 \).
  • The last digit is 4, so the root ends in either 2 or 8.
  • The remaining part is 51. The closest lower perfect square is 49 (which is \( 7^2 \)). Thus, the tens digit is 7.
  • The answer is either 72 or 78. Compare with \( 75^2 = 5625 \). Since 5184 is less than 5625, the root is 72.

⚠️ 9. Common Mistakes & Pitfalls

Mistake 1: Standard Additive Misconception

Incorrect: \( \sqrt{a + b} = \sqrt{a} + \sqrt{b} \)

Correct: \( \sqrt{9 + 16} = \sqrt{25} = 5 \), which is NOT equal to \( \sqrt{9} + \sqrt{16} = 3 + 4 = 7 \). Keep operators locked inside the root until processed!

Mistake 2: Forgetting the Conjugate Sign

When rationalizing \( \frac{1}{\sqrt{5} + 2} \), multiplying by the incorrect conjugate like \( \sqrt{5} + 2 \) instead of \( \sqrt{5} - 2 \) is common under exam stress. Always invert the operator sign!

📊 10. Exam Strategy & Weightage

SSC (CGL, CHSL, CPO)

Weightage: 1-2 questions. Usually focuses on nested binomial square root simplification or direct comparisons of multiple irrational surds.

Banking (IBPS PO/Clerk, SBI)

Weightage: 2-3 questions in simplification/approximation. Standard forms are solved using rapid value estimation (e.g., estimating \( \sqrt{399} \approx 20 \)).

Railways & State Exams

Weightage: 1-2 direct root/surd questions. Emphasizes finding square roots of large numbers and basic laws of indices/surds.

📌 11. Summary

To master roots and surds, visualize them as fractional exponents. The radical symbol \( \sqrt{} \) is just another language for writing powers. Simplifying surds means breaking down complex radicands into perfect powers, rationalizing any irrational denominators using conjugates, and comparing surds by bringing them to a common root exponent using the LCM method.

Laws of Surds \( \sqrt[n]{a} \times \sqrt[n]{b} = \sqrt[n]{ab} \) Indices must match before multiplying.

🎯 13. Key Takeaways

  • Perfect squares never end in the unit digits 2, 3, 7, or 8.
  • Cube roots have unique ending digits, making rapid estimation very reliable.
  • Always rationalize denominators by multiplying by their conjugate values.
  • To compare surds of different orders, convert them to a common exponent base using the LCM of the root indices.
