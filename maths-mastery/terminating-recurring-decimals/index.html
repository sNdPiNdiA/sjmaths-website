Terminating & Recurring Decimals

Medium Difficulty 25-30 Mins Read High Exam Weightage Updated 2026

📘 Introduction to Terminating & Recurring Decimals

In competitive examinations like SSC CGL, Bank PO, Railways, and CDS, the real number system forms the foundation of quantitative aptitude. One of its most high-yield and frequently tested subtopics is the classification and manipulation of decimal representations of rational numbers—specifically Terminating Decimals and Recurring (Repeating) Decimals.

Every rational number of the form \( \frac{p}{q} \) (where \( q \neq 0 \)) can be expressed as a decimal. Based on the behavior of the digits after the decimal point, these are classified into two categories. Understanding how to rapidly transition between fractional and decimal formats is a powerful tool for solving complex arithmetic simplification problems in under 30 seconds.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Identify whether a given fraction will yield a terminating or recurring decimal without actual division.
  • Convert pure and mixed recurring decimals into standard fractional form (\( \frac{p}{q} \)) using rapid mathematical short-cuts.
  • Perform algebraic operations (addition, subtraction, multiplication, and division) directly on recurring decimals.
  • Apply LCM-based tabular techniques to handle complex multi-term recurring decimal equations.
  • Eliminate incorrect options using unit-digit and digital-sum concepts optimized for competitive exams.

📝 Prerequisites

Before diving into this topic, ensure you are comfortable with:

  • Prime Factorization: Breaking down integers into prime components (e.g., \( 40 = 2^3 \times 5^1 \)).
  • Basic Simplification: Expressing fractions in their simplest form by cancelling out the Greatest Common Divisor (GCD).
  • Basic Division Rules: Knowing the difference between numerators (dividend) and denominators (divisor).

🧠 Core Concepts

Concept 1: Terminating Decimals

Definition: A decimal number that contains a finite number of digits after the decimal point.

Explanation & Intuition: When you divide the numerator by the denominator, the division process eventually ends (leaves a remainder of 0). This happens because the denominator can be scaled to a power of 10.

Condition: For a fraction \( \frac{p}{q} \) in its simplest form, the decimal representation will be terminating if and only if the prime factorization of the denominator \( q \) contains only the prime numbers 2 and/or 5. That is: $$q = 2^m \times 5^n$$ where \( m, n \geq 0 \) are non-negative integers.

Memory Tip: Think of 10 as the "base of our decimal system." Since \( 10 = 2 \times 5 \), any denominator made purely of 2s and 5s can easily scale up to become 10, 100, 1000, etc., making division clean.

Common Mistake: Forgetting to simplify the fraction to its lowest terms before checking the denominator. For example, in \( \frac{9}{30} \), the denominator is \( 30 = 2 \times 3 \times 5 \). One might incorrectly assume it is non-terminating due to the '3'. However, simplifying gives \( \frac{3}{10} \), which has only 2 and 5 in the denominator, hence it terminates (0.3).

Mini Example: Consider \( \frac{7}{80} \). The denominator is \( 80 = 2^4 \times 5^1 \). Since the prime factors consist only of 2 and 5, \( \frac{7}{80} \) is a terminating decimal (\( 0.0875 \)).

Concept 2: Pure Recurring Decimals

Definition: A decimal number in which all the digits after the decimal point repeat infinitely in a periodic cycle.

Explanation: No digits are left out; the repetition starts immediately after the decimal point. We represent the repeating part using a bar (overbar) or dots above the repeating block.

Mathematical Form: $$0.a_1a_2...a_k a_1a_2...a_k... = 0.\overline{a_1a_2...a_k}$$

Conversion Rule: To convert a pure recurring decimal into a fraction:

  1. Write down the repeating digits in the numerator.
  2. In the denominator, write as many '9's as there are repeating digits in the period.

Mini Example: Convert \( 0.\overline{45} \) to a fraction. The repeating block is "45" (2 digits). $$0.\overline{45} = \frac{45}{99} = \frac{5}{11}$$

Concept 3: Mixed Recurring Decimals

Definition: A decimal number in which some digits after the decimal point do not repeat, while other digits immediately following them repeat in a periodic cycle.

Explanation & Intuition: There is a "transition zone" of static digits before the repeating cycle begins. For example, in \( 0.12\overline{34} \), the digits '12' do not repeat, but '34' repeats infinitely.

Conversion Rule: To convert a mixed recurring decimal into a fraction:

  1. Numerator: Write the entire number formed after the decimal point (including non-repeating and repeating parts) and subtract the number formed by the non-repeating part.
  2. Denominator: Write as many '9's as there are repeating digits, followed by as many '0's as there are non-repeating digits after the decimal point.

Mini Example: Convert \( 0.25\overline{18} \) to a fraction.

  • Numerator = \( 2518 - 25 = 2493 \)
  • Denominator = two 9s (for '18') and two 0s (for '25') = \( 9900 \)
  • Result = \( \frac{2493}{9900} = \frac{277}{1100} \)

📏 Rules & Properties

Rule 1: The Prime Factor Rule of Rational Denominators

Explanation: Let \( x = \frac{p}{q} \) be a rational number such that the prime factorization of \( q \) is of the form \( 2^m 5^n \). Then \( x \) has a terminating decimal expansion. If the prime factorization of \( q \) contains any prime factor other than 2 or 5, then \( x \) has a non-terminating repeating (recurring) decimal expansion.

Reason: Since our base is 10, any fraction with a denominator whose prime factorization only consists of 2s and 5s can be converted to an equivalent fraction with a denominator of the form \( 10^k \) by multiplying the numerator and denominator by an appropriate power of 2 or 5.

Shortcut: If you see 3, 7, 11, 13, etc., in the denominator (in its simplest form), mark it as non-terminating immediately!

Example: \( \frac{13}{250} \). Here, \( 250 = 2^1 \times 5^3 \). Since only 2 and 5 are factors, it is terminating. \( \frac{13}{250} = \frac{13 \times 2^2}{250 \times 2^2} = \frac{52}{1000} = 0.052 \).

Rule 2: Determining Number of Digits After Decimal

Explanation: For a terminating decimal \( \frac{p}{2^m \times 5^n} \), the decimal representation will terminate after exactly \( \max(m, n) \) places of decimal.

Reason: The higher power of 2 or 5 determines how many factors of 10 must be built to eliminate the denominator.

Example: For \( \frac{11}{2^3 \times 5^1} \), the decimal will terminate after \( \max(3, 1) = 3 \) places of decimal. Indeed, \( \frac{11}{40} = 0.275 \).

Rule 3: Operations (Addition & Subtraction) Tabular Method

Explanation: When adding or subtracting recurring decimals, converting everything to fractions can sometimes be tedious if the denominators are very large. Instead, use the 3-Column Tabular Method.

The Method: Split your working space into three columns:

  1. Column 1 (Non-repeating Part): Contains the number of digits equal to the maximum number of non-repeating digits in the given numbers.
  2. Column 2 (Repeating Part): Contains the number of digits equal to the LCM of the number of repeating digits in the given numbers.
  3. Column 3 (Buffer Part): Write the next 2 to 3 digits of the decimal expansions to check for carry-overs.
Add or subtract across the columns normally from right to left. The bar is placed only over the result in Column 2.

📐 Formula Box

1. Pure Recurring Decimal to Fraction

$$0.\overline{xy...z} = \frac{xy...z}{\underbrace{99...9}_{\text{number of digits}}}$$

When to use: When every digit after the decimal point repeats infinitely.

Memory Trick: "No zeros, all nines." The repeating digits are clean, so the denominator uses only pure nines.

2. Mixed Recurring Decimal to Fraction

$$0.ab...\underbrace{xy...z}_{\text{repeating}} = \frac{abxy...z - ab}{\underbrace{99...9}_{\text{number of repeating digits}} \underbrace{00...0}_{\text{number of non-repeating digits}}}$$

When to use: When some digits right after the decimal do not repeat, but subsequent digits do.

Memory Trick: "The Whole minus the Silent, over Nines then Zeros."

💡 Solved Examples

Easy Example 1: Identification

Which of the following rational numbers will yield a terminating decimal expansion?

A) \( \frac{23}{45} \)      B) \( \frac{17}{120} \)      C) \( \frac{39}{24} \)      D) \( \frac{11}{35} \)

View Step-by-Step Solution

Step 1: Simplify each fraction to its lowest terms.

  • A) \( \frac{23}{45} \) is already in its simplest form. Denominator \( 45 = 3^2 \times 5 \). Since there is a factor of 3, it is non-terminating.
  • B) \( \frac{17}{120} \) is in simplest form. Denominator \( 120 = 2^3 \times 3 \times 5 \). Since there is a factor of 3, it is non-terminating.
  • C) \( \frac{39}{24} \): This is not simplified! Divide both numerator and denominator by 3: $$\frac{39 \div 3}{24 \div 3} = \frac{13}{8}$$ Now look at the denominator: \( 8 = 2^3 \). It contains only powers of 2. Hence, it is a terminating decimal!
  • D) \( \frac{11}{35} \) is in simplest form. Denominator \( 35 = 5 \times 7 \). Since 7 is present, it is non-terminating.

Correct Answer: C) \( \frac{39}{24} \) (Value is 1.625).

Medium Example 2: Conversion to Vulgar Fraction

Convert the mixed recurring decimal \( 2.34\overline{18} \) into an improper vulgar fraction in its simplest form.

View Step-by-Step Solution

Step 1: Separate the whole number part from the decimal part.

$$2.34\overline{18} = 2 + 0.34\overline{18}$$

Step 2: Apply the mixed recurring decimal formula to \( 0.34\overline{18} \).

  • Entire value after decimal = \( 3418 \)
  • Non-repeating part = \( 34 \)
  • Numerator = \( 3418 - 34 = 3384 \)
  • Denominator: Two repeating digits ('1' and '8') \( \rightarrow \) '99'. Two non-repeating digits ('3' and '4') \( \rightarrow \) '00'. Denominator = \( 9900 \).
$$0.34\overline{18} = \frac{3384}{9900}$$

Step 3: Simplify the fraction.

Dividing numerator and denominator by 36 (using common factor analysis):

$$\frac{3384 \div 36}{9900 \div 36} = \frac{94}{275}$$

Step 4: Add back the whole number part.

$$2.34\overline{18} = 2 + \frac{94}{275} = \frac{2 \times 275 + 94}{275} = \frac{550 + 94}{275} = \frac{644}{275}$$

Correct Answer: \( \frac{644}{275} \)

Exam Level Example 3: Operations & Simplification

Find the value of \( \sqrt{0.4\overline{4}} + \sqrt{0.1\overline{1}} \).

View Step-by-Step Solution

This is a classic SSC CGL Tier-1 question. Do not attempt to take square roots of raw decimals!

Step 1: Convert recurring decimals into fractional representations.

  • \( 0.4\overline{4} \) is a pure recurring decimal \( 0.\overline{4} \). $$0.\overline{4} = \frac{4}{9}$$
  • \( 0.1\overline{1} \) is a pure recurring decimal \( 0.\overline{1} \). $$0.\overline{1} = \frac{1}{9}$$

Step 2: Substitute values back into the expression.

$$\sqrt{\frac{4}{9}} + \sqrt{\frac{1}{9}}$$

Step 3: Solve the square roots.

$$\frac{2}{3} + \frac{1}{3} = \frac{3}{3} = 1$$

Correct Answer: 1

Hard Example 4: Complex Addition/Subtraction

Evaluate the value of \( 0.34\overline{67} + 0.13\overline{3} - 0.12\overline{5} \).

View Step-by-Step Solution

Converting everything into fractions here will result in large, painful denominators. Let us use the 3-Column Tabular Method.

Step 1: Determine Column sizes.

  • Number of non-repeating digits:
    • \( 0.34\overline{67} \rightarrow 2 \) digits ('34')
    • \( 0.13\overline{3} \rightarrow 2 \) digits ('13')
    • \( 0.12\overline{5} \rightarrow 2 \) digits ('12')
    Max non-repeating digits = 2. Thus, Column 1 size = 2 digits.
  • Number of repeating digits (LCM):
    • \( 0.34\overline{67} \rightarrow 2 \) repeating ('67')
    • \( 0.13\overline{3} \rightarrow 1 \) repeating ('3')
    • \( 0.12\overline{5} \rightarrow 1 \) repeating ('5')
    LCM(2, 1, 1) = 2. Thus, Column 2 size = 2 digits.
  • Column 3 (Buffer) size = 3 digits (to ensure accuracy in carries).

Step 2: Set up the additions and subtractions across the columns.

Number Col 1 (Non-rep) Col 2 (Repeating) Col 3 (Buffer)
A = \( 0.34\overline{67} \) 34 67 676
B = \( 0.13\overline{3} \) 13 33 333
Sum (A + B) 48 01 009
Subtract C = \( 0.12\overline{5} \) 12 55 555
Final Result 35 45 454

Step 3: Analyze the columns for final output.

  • Column 1 (non-repeating part) = 35
  • Column 2 (repeating part) = 45
  • Column 3 confirms the pattern '454' showing that '45' continues repeating infinitely.

Thus, the final value is \( 0.35\overline{45} \).

Correct Answer: \( 0.35\overline{45} \) (or in fractional form \( \frac{3545 - 35}{9900} = \frac{3510}{9900} = \frac{39}{110} \)).

⚡ Exam Tricks & Shortcuts

Trick 1: Rapid Fractional Approximations

When solving standard multiple-choice questions (MCQs), instead of standard fractional conversion, you can estimate to quickly eliminate choices. For example: $$0.\overline{7} \approx 0.777... \approx 0.78$$ If you are adding \( 0.\overline{7} + 0.\overline{2} \), you know it's extremely close to \( 0.78 + 0.22 = 1.00 \). The actual value is \( \frac{7}{9} + \frac{2}{9} = \frac{9}{9} = 1 \). This allows rapid sanity checks!

Trick 2: Denominator Analysis to Eliminate Options

If an option is in fractional form and you are converting a pure recurring decimal of 3 digits, say \( 0.\overline{abc} \), the denominator must be a divisor of 999 (which has factors \( 3^3 \times 37 \)). If you see a prime factor of 7 or 13 in the denominator of simplified options, you can eliminate them immediately because 999 does not contain 7 or 13.

⚠️ Common Mistakes & Pitfalls

1. Forgetting to Subtract Non-Repeating Parts

Mistake: Writing \( 0.12\overline{7} \) as \( \frac{127}{900} \) instead of subtracting the non-repeating '12' from the numerator.

Correction: Always apply the rule: \( \text{Numerator} = \text{Entire number} - \text{Non-repeating part} \). $$0.12\overline{7} = \frac{127 - 12}{900} = \frac{115}{900} = \frac{23}{180}$$

2. Miscounting Zeros in the Denominator

Mistake: In mixed numbers like \( 0.005\overline{3} \), writing only one or two zeros in the denominator.

Correction: Count all digits between the decimal point and the start of the repeating bar, including zeros. In \( 0.005\overline{3} \), '005' consists of 3 non-repeating digits. Hence, there must be three zeros. $$0.005\overline{3} = \frac{53 - 5}{9000} = \frac{48}{9000} = \frac{1}{187.5}$$

📊 Exam Strategy & Weightage

SSC (CGL, CHSL, CPO)

Weightage: 1-2 Questions in Tier-1 & Tier-2.

Pattern: Focuses heavily on multi-term additions/subtractions and algebraic simplifications featuring square roots of recurring fractions.

Banking (IBPS, SBI PO/Clerk)

Weightage: High-speed Simplification sets (0-1 question directly, often integrated into quantitative comparison).

Pattern: Swift approximation of fractions to speed up calculation of large equations.

Weightage: Direct conceptual questions (1-3 questions).

Pattern: Pure identification of terminating/non-terminating status or converting simple recurring values directly to fractions.

📌 Summary

  • Terminating decimals have denominators of the form \( 2^m \times 5^n \) when written in simplest form.
  • Pure recurring decimals repeat immediately after the decimal point and are converted using only nines in the denominator: \( 0.\overline{abc} = \frac{abc}{999} \).
  • Mixed recurring decimals have non-repeating digits before the repeating cycle and are converted by taking the whole value minus the non-repeating value, divided by nines (for repeating digits) followed by zeros (for non-repeating digits).
  • Complex sums are best solved using the 3-Column Tabular Method based on maximum non-repeating digits and the LCM of repeating cycles to avoid dealing with huge denominators.

🚀 Key Takeaways

  • To find the exact decimal limit, look at the highest power between 2 and 5 in the denominator of a simplified fraction.
  • Use structural estimations before doing heavy arithmetic to easily eliminate 2 out of 4 options on screen.
  • Never try to execute calculations with basic rounding approximation on recurring decimals—this usually leads to off-by-one or off-by-two errors in the last decimal digit, which are deliberately mapped to trap options!
