Decimal Notation, Operations & Conversions

Medium Difficulty 25-30 Mins Read High Exam Weightage Updated 2026

📘 1. Introduction: Decimal Notation, Operations & Conversions

In competitive examinations like SSC CGL, Banking (IBPS, SBI), Railways (RRB), and CDS, numbers are rarely presented as neat, clean integers. Real-world numerical problems, data interpretations, and simplification questions are loaded with decimals. Mastery over decimals is not just about understanding where the dot goes; it is about building the computational speed to process division, multiplication, and non-terminating patterns instantly.

A decimal representation is simply another way of writing a fraction whose denominator is a power of 10. For instance, the fraction \(\frac{3}{10}\) is written as \(0.3\), and \(\frac{7}{100}\) is written as \(0.07\). Understanding these conversions and their underlying arithmetic properties allows you to solve complex simplification expressions under intense time pressure.

🎯 Exam Weightage Note: Simplification questions involving decimals contribute 2 to 5 marks in Banking Preliminary exams and are heavily tested in SSC CGL Tier-1 & Tier-2 under the Arithmetic and Number System modules.

🎯 2. Learning Objectives

  • Understand the positional decimal place-value system and how values scale by powers of ten.
  • Execute rapid operations (Addition, Subtraction, Multiplication, Division) on decimals with absolute precision.
  • Identify the clear mathematical distinction between terminating and recurring (non-terminating repeating) decimals.
  • Convert any pure or mixed recurring decimal into its equivalent vulgar fraction using standard formulas and speed-math shortcuts.
  • Deploy smart elimination, estimation, and digital-root tricks to eliminate incorrect choices instantly during exams.

🧠 4. Core Concepts

Concept 1: Decimal Notation & Place Value Chart

A decimal number consists of a whole number part and a fractional part, separated by a decimal point. As we move to the right of the decimal point, each position's value becomes ten times smaller (divided by 10).

Hundreds (100) Tens (10) Ones (1) [ . ] Decimal Point Tenths (1/10) Hundredths (1/100) Thousandths (1/1000)
3 4 5 . 2 7 8

Mathematical Expansion: $$345.278 = (3 \times 100) + (4 \times 10) + (5 \times 1) + \left(2 \times \frac{1}{10}\right) + \left(7 \times \frac{1}{100}\right) + \left(8 \times \frac{1}{1000}\right)$$

💡 Intuition: Think of tenths as dimes (10 cents) and hundredths as pennies (1 cent). Thus, 0.7 is 7 dimes ($0.70), while 0.07 is 7 pennies ($0.07). Do not confuse their sizes!

⚠️ Common Mistake: Thinking that \(0.008\) is larger than \(0.02\) because \(8 > 2\). Remember, comparing decimals starts from the leftmost decimal place (tenths, then hundredths). Since \(2 \text{ hundredths} > 0 \text{ hundredths}\), \(0.02 > 0.008\).

Concept 2: Terminating vs. Recurring (Non-Terminating) Decimals

Decimals are broadly divided into two major behavior profiles when generated from real rational numbers (\(p/q\)):

  • Terminating Decimals: The division ends completely, leaving a remainder of zero. This happens if and only if the prime factorization of the denominator \(q\) contains only powers of \(2\) and/or \(5\).
    Examples: \(\frac{1}{4} = 0.25\) (since \(4 = 2^2\)), \(\frac{3}{20} = 0.15\) (since \(20 = 2^2 \times 5\)).
  • Non-Terminating Recurring Decimals: The division never ends, and a specific digit or group of digits repeats infinitely. This happens when the prime factorization of denominator \(q\) contains prime numbers other than \(2\) or \(5\) (like \(3, 7, 11, \text{etc.}\)).
    Examples: \(\frac{1}{3} = 0.3333... = 0.\bar{3}\), \(\frac{15}{7} = 2.142857142857... = 2.\overline{142857}\).

Memory Tip: Think of 2 and 5 as "terminators." If any other prime factor (like 3 or 7) slips into the denominator's base, it creates an "infinite loop" (bar decimal).

Concept 3: Fundamental Operations on Decimals

A. Addition & Subtraction

The most critical rule is to align the decimal points vertically before executing the operation. Fill up any vacant trailing spaces with zeros to avoid columns shifting.

Example: Calculate \(45.6 + 3.245 + 0.18\)

  45.600
   03.245
+  00.180
---------
  49.025

B. Multiplication

Multiply the numbers as if they were simple integers (ignoring the decimal points entirely). After obtaining the product, count the sum of decimal places in all the multiplying factors. Place the decimal point in the final product so that it has the same number of decimal places.

Example: \(1.25 \times 0.04\)

  • Treat as integer product: \(125 \times 4 = 500\).
  • Decimal places in factor 1 (\(1.25\)) = 2 places.
  • Decimal places in factor 2 (\(0.04\)) = 2 places.
  • Total decimal places needed = \(2 + 2 = 4\) places.
  • Applying 4 decimal places to \(500\) (moving left from the end): \(\mathbf{0.0500}\) (or simply \(0.05\)).

C. Division

When dividing a decimal by another, convert the divisor into a whole number by shifting the decimal point to the right. Concurrently, shift the decimal point of the dividend by the exact same number of places to keep the value unchanged.

Example: Divide \(0.0024\) by \(0.08\)

$$\frac{0.0024}{0.08} = \frac{0.0024 \times 100}{0.08 \times 100} = \frac{0.24}{8} = 0.03$$

📐 5. Rules & Properties

Rule 1: Shifting Rule for Multiplication & Division by Powers of 10

Explanation: Multiplying shifts the decimal point to the right; dividing shifts it to the left.

  • \(x \times 10^n\) : Move decimal point \(n\) places to the right.
  • \(x \div 10^n\) : Move decimal point \(n\) places to the left.

Example: \(45.28 \times 1000 = 45280\) ; \(45.28 \div 100 = 0.4528\)

Rule 2: Direct Conversion of Pure Recurring Decimals to Fractions

Explanation: A pure recurring decimal is one in which all figures after the decimal point repeat immediately.

The Golden Method: Write the repeating period as the numerator. In the denominator, write as many '9's as there are repeating digits in the period.

Shortcut Formulas: $$0.\bar{p} = \frac{p}{9} \quad | \quad 0.\overline{pq} = \frac{pq}{99} \quad | \quad 0.\overline{xyz} = \frac{xyz}{999}$$

Example: \(0.\overline{37} = \frac{37}{99}\)

Rule 3: Conversion of Mixed Recurring Decimals to Fractions

Explanation: A mixed recurring decimal has some non-repeating digits immediately after the decimal point, followed by repeating digits.

The Shortcut Formula: $$\text{Numerator} = (\text{Total number formed by all digits after decimal}) - (\text{Number formed by non-repeating digits})$$ $$\text{Denominator} = (\text{As many 9's as repeating digits}) \text{ followed by } (\text{as many 0's as non-repeating digits})$$

$$0.a\bar{b} = \frac{ab - a}{90} \quad | \quad 0.ab\bar{c} = \frac{abc - ab}{900} \quad | \quad 0.a\overline{bc} = \frac{abc - a}{990}$$

Example: Convert \(0.12\bar{5}\) to fraction:
Numerator = \(125 - 12 = 113\)
Denominator = One '9' (for repeating digit 5) and two '0's (for non-repeating digits 1 and 2) = \(900\).
Result = \(\frac{113}{900}\).

📏 6. Formula Box

Essential Recurring Decimal Conversion Sheet

1. Pure Recurring (Single digit repeating):

$$0.\bar{a} = \frac{a}{9}$$

2. Pure Recurring (Double digit repeating):

$$0.\overline{ab} = \frac{ab}{99}$$

3. Mixed Recurring (1 non-repeating, 1 repeating):

$$0.a\bar{b} = \frac{ab - a}{90}$$

4. Mixed Recurring (1 non-repeating, 2 repeating):

$$0.a\overline{bc} = \frac{abc - a}{990}$$

5. Mixed Recurring (2 non-repeating, 1 repeating):

$$0.ab\bar{c} = \frac{abc - ab}{900}$$
🧠 Memory Trick:
  • 9 represents "rebellion" (keeps repeating, breaks boundaries). So every digits under the bar gets a 9.
  • 0 represents "conformity" (zeros do not repeat, stay quiet). So every plain decimal digit gets a 0 in the denominator.

💡 7. Solved Examples

Example 1 Easy

Question: Simplify the given expression: $$\frac{0.0036 \times 2.8}{0.042 \times 0.08}$$

View Step-by-Step Solution

Step 1: Count the total number of decimal places in the numerator and the denominator.

  • Numerator: \(0.0036\) (4 places) and \(2.8\) (1 place) \(\rightarrow\) Total = \(4 + 1 = 5\) decimal places.
  • Denominator: \(0.042\) (3 places) and \(0.08\) (2 places) \(\rightarrow\) Total = \(3 + 2 = 5\) decimal places.

Step 2: Since the number of decimal places in both numerator and denominator is equal, we can completely remove the decimal points! $$\frac{36 \times 28}{42 \times 8}$$

Step 3: Perform the basic integer reduction: $$\frac{36}{8} = \frac{9}{2}$$ $$\frac{28}{42} = \frac{2}{3}$$ $$\text{Product} = \frac{9}{2} \times \frac{2}{3} = \frac{9 \times 2}{2 \times 3} = \frac{18}{6} = 3$$

Correct Answer: 3

Example 2 Medium

Question: Express the mixed recurring decimal \(0.25\bar{6}\) as a simplified vulgar fraction.

View Step-by-Step Solution

We apply the formula for converting a mixed recurring decimal to a fraction:

$$0.ab\bar{c} = \frac{abc - ab}{900}$$

Here, the total decimal portion is \(256\). The non-repeating part is \(25\).
The repeating digit is \(6\) (1 digit \(\rightarrow\) one 9).
The non-repeating digits are \(2\) and \(5\) (2 digits \(\rightarrow\) two 0s).

Step 1: Find the Numerator: $$\text{Numerator} = 256 - 25 = 231$$

Step 2: Find the Denominator: $$\text{Denominator} = 900$$

Step 3: Form the fraction and reduce to the lowest terms: $$\text{Fraction} = \frac{231}{900}$$ Divide both numerator and denominator by their common factor, \(3\): $$\frac{231 \div 3}{900 \div 3} = \frac{77}{300}$$

Correct Answer: \(\frac{77}{300}\)

Example 3 Hard (Algebraic Application)

Question: Find the exact value of: $$\frac{0.125 + 0.027}{0.25 - 0.15 + 0.09}$$

View Step-by-Step Solution

This expression looks intimidating, but it is a direct application of the algebraic identity: $$a^3 + b^3 = (a + b)(a^2 - ab + b^2)$$

Let's map our values:

  • \(0.125 = (0.5)^3 \implies a = 0.5\)
  • \(0.027 = (0.3)^3 \implies b = 0.3\)

Now observe the denominator:

  • \(0.25 = (0.5)^2 = a^2\)
  • \(0.15 = (0.5 \times 0.3) = ab\)
  • \(0.09 = (0.3)^2 = b^2\)

Substitute these algebraic terms back into our main fraction: $$\frac{a^3 + b^3}{a^2 - ab + b^2}$$

Substitute the expansion of \(a^3 + b^3\) in the numerator: $$\frac{(a + b)(a^2 - ab + b^2)}{(a^2 - ab + b^2)} = a + b$$

Now, substitute the values of \(a\) and \(b\): $$\text{Value} = 0.5 + 0.3 = 0.8$$

Correct Answer: 0.8

Example 4 Exam Special

Question: Simplify: \(3.\bar{36} - 2.\bar{05} + 1.\bar{33}\)

View Step-by-Step Solution

Method 1: Fractional Conversion (100% accurate)

Express each part as a mixed number/fraction: $$3.\bar{36} = 3 + \frac{36}{99}$$ $$2.\bar{05} = 2 + \frac{05}{99}$$ $$1.\bar{33} = 1 + \frac{33}{99}$$

Combine the integer parts and the fractional parts separately: $$\text{Integer parts} = 3 - 2 + 1 = 2$$ $$\text{Fractional parts} = \frac{36}{99} - \frac{5}{99} + \frac{33}{99} = \frac{36 - 5 + 33}{99} = \frac{64}{99}$$

Combine them back: $$2 + \frac{64}{99} = 2.\overline{64}$$

Method 2: Vertical Column Addition (Super-Fast Shortcut)

Write the numbers out to a few repeating periods and align them:

  3.363636...
- 2.050505...
-------------
  1.313131...
+ 1.333333...
-------------
  2.646464... = 2.\overline{64}

Correct Answer: \(2.\overline{64}\)

⚡ 8. Exam Tricks & Shortcuts

Trick 1: Checking Options Using Decimal Places

In multiplication, the number of decimal places in the product is the sum of decimal places in the factors. If options have different numbers of decimal places, you can select the correct answer without performing the multiplication.

Example: \(0.213 \times 0.04\)

  • Factor 1 has 3 decimal places. Factor 2 has 2 decimal places.
  • The answer must have exactly \(3 + 2 = 5\) decimal places (or end with trailing zeros that simplify).
  • If there's only one option with 5 decimal places, tick it instantly!

Trick 2: Digital Sum with Decimals

You can use the Digital Sum (Casting out 9s) rule to verify decimal calculations. Simply ignore the decimal point entirely when computing the digital sum.

Example: Verify if \(2.5 \times 1.4 = 3.5\)

  • Digital sum of \(2.5 \rightarrow 2 + 5 = 7\)
  • Digital sum of \(1.4 \rightarrow 1 + 4 = 5\)
  • Product of digital sums \(\rightarrow 7 \times 5 = 35 \rightarrow 3 + 5 = 8\)
  • Digital sum of answer \(3.5 \rightarrow 3 + 5 = 8\)
  • Matches perfectly!

Trick 3: The "Carry Over" Rule in Bar Decimal Addition

When adding recurring decimals directly, always write the repeating sequence out to at least 6 to 8 decimal places. This allows you to catch any "carry-over" values that come from the infinite sequence on the right.

⚠️ 9. Common Mistakes & Pitfalls

1. Misaligning Decimals in Addition/Subtraction

Adding \(5.4 + 0.54\) as \(5.58\) (treating them as \(54+54\) instead of \(5.40 + 0.54 = 5.94\)). Always balance decimal places with placeholder zeros before operating.

2. Forgetting to Subtract Non-Repeating Terms

While converting \(0.2\bar{7}\), students mistakenly write \(\frac{27}{90}\). Remember to subtract the non-repeating digit: \(\frac{27 - 2}{90} = \frac{25}{90} = \frac{5}{18}\).

3. Dividing Decimals without Adjusting Divisor

When dividing \(\frac{0.0009}{0.03}\), students write \(0.03\). Carefully shift the divisor's decimal: \(\frac{0.09}{3} = 0.03\). Track the place values systematically.

📊 10. Exam Strategy & Weightage

How different examinations test this specific microtopic:

SSC (CGL, CHSL, CPO, MTS)

Direct questions on converting complex mixed recurring decimals to fractions, or simplified calculations involving algebraic formulas (\(a^3 \pm b^3\)). Focus on pure accuracy.

Banking (SBI/IBPS PO & Clerk)

Rarely asks direct conversions. Instead, decimals are embedded heavily inside Approximation and Simplification questions. Learn to round off \(0.99\) to \(1\), \(0.49\) to \(0.5\) to speed up calculations.

Railways & State PSC Exams

Asks fundamental operations (addition, subtraction, and comparison of decimals in ascending/descending order). High accuracy yields full marks here.

📌 11. Summary

Decimals are a positional base-10 fractional system. We can seamlessly convert any terminating fraction to a decimal. For recurring decimals, we use specialized fraction conversions featuring 9s (for repeating values) and 0s (for non-repeating values) in the denominator. Performing quick operations requires strict alignment of the decimal point, while multiplication and division require calculating the total decimal places before placing the final decimal point.

🚀 1-Page Cheatsheet

  • Terminating: Denominator only contains factors of 2 and 5 (e.g. \(\frac{7}{40}\)).
  • Non-Terminating Recurring: Denominator has prime factors other than 2 or 5.
  • Pure Bar: \(0.\bar{a} = \frac{a}{9} \quad | \quad 0.\overline{abc} = \frac{abc}{999}\)
  • Mixed Bar: \(0.ab\bar{c} = \frac{abc - ab}{900}\)
  • Comparing Decimals: Equalize decimal positions using trailing zeros, then compare as integers.
  • Multiplication Shift: Shifts decimal Right.
  • Division Shift: Shifts decimal Left.

📌 12. Key Takeaways

  • Always look at the prime factorization of a simplified fraction's denominator to instantly identify if it terminates or recurs.
  • When converting mixed decimals, remember: "Subtract what does not repeat from the whole value."
  • Never start basic calculations without aligning decimal points first.
  • Use option elimination based on the count of trailing decimal places to save critical minutes in the exam hall.
