Direct & Inverse Variation

Medium Difficulty 25-30 Mins Read High Exam Weightage Updated 2026

📘 1. Introduction

In quantitative aptitude, understanding how quantities change in relation to one another is a foundational skill. Direct and Inverse Variation is the mathematical framework that describes these relationships. Whether it is calculating how fast a car must travel to reach a destination on time, or determining how the price of a diamond changes when it breaks into pieces, variation is the mathematical tool at play.

For Indian government exams (like SSC CGL, Banking, Railways, and CDS), variation is not just a standalone topic. It is the core operating engine behind major arithmetic chapters such as Time & Work, Time, Speed & Distance (TSD), Partnership, and Mensuration. Mastering this microtopic will unlock faster calculation speeds and deep conceptual clarity across the entire quantitative section.

🎯 2. Learning Objectives

By the end of this study module, you will be able to:

  • Identify and mathematically model Direct, Inverse, Joint, and Compound variations.
  • Calculate the Constant of Variation (\(k\)) and use it to solve multi-variable problems.
  • Deconstruct complex exam problems, such as diamond-breaking and engine-consumption scenarios, into basic variation equations.
  • Apply proportionality shortcuts to solve Time & Work and TSD questions without setting up traditional algebraic equations.
  • Eliminate incorrect options in the exam by performing quick scale checks (e.g., recognizing how doubling an input affects a squared output).

📝 3. Prerequisites

Before proceeding, ensure you are comfortable with the following foundational concepts:

  • Ratios and Proportions: Understanding that a ratio \(a:b\) represents the relative size of two quantities and can be written as a fraction \(\frac{a}{b}\).
  • Basic Algebraic Equations: Solving linear equations and manipulating equations containing exponents (e.g., \(y = kx^2\)).
  • Independent vs. Dependent Variables: Recognizing which quantity changes on its own (independent) and which quantity changes as a result (dependent).

🧠 4. Core Concepts

Concept 1: Direct Variation (\(y \propto x\))

Definition: Two quantities are said to vary directly if an increase in one leads to a proportional increase in the other, and a decrease in one leads to a proportional decrease in the other.

Explanation & Intuition: Think of direct variation as a "same-direction" relationship. If you double one quantity, the other quantity also doubles. The ratio between the two quantities remains constant at all times.

Mathematical Formulation:

$$y \propto x \implies y = kx \implies \frac{y}{x} = k$$

Where \(k\) is a non-zero constant called the Constant of Variation. For two different states:

$$\frac{y_1}{x_1} = \frac{y_2}{x_2}$$

Important Observation: The graph of a direct variation is always a straight line passing through the origin \((0, 0)\). The slope of this line represents the constant \(k\).

Memory Tip: Direct = Division. In direct variation, the division of the two variables is always constant (\(\frac{y}{x} = k\)).

Common Mistake: Confusing "increasing together" with direct variation. For example, if \(y = x + 5\), when \(x\) increases, \(y\) increases. However, this is not direct variation because \(\frac{y}{x}\) is not constant.

Mini Example: The cost of apples is directly proportional to their weight. If \(5\text{ kg}\) of apples cost ₹\(400\), find the cost of \(12\text{ kg}\) of apples.

Solution: Here, \(C \propto W \implies \frac{C_1}{W_1} = \frac{C_2}{W_2} \implies \frac{400}{5} = \frac{C_2}{12} \implies 80 = \frac{C_2}{12} \implies C_2 = \text{₹ }960\).

Concept 2: Inverse Variation (\(y \propto \frac{1}{x}\))

Definition: Two quantities are said to vary inversely if an increase in one leads to a proportional decrease in the other, and vice versa.

Explanation & Intuition: Think of this as an "opposite-direction" relationship. If you double one variable, the other variable is cut in half. The product of the two variables always remains constant.

Mathematical Formulation:

$$y \propto \frac{1}{x} \implies y = \frac{k}{x} \implies y \cdot x = k$$

Where \(k\) is the constant of variation. For two different states:

$$x_1 \cdot y_1 = x_2 \cdot y_2$$

Important Observation: The graph of an inverse variation is a hyperbola and never touches the axes.

Memory Tip: Inverse = Product. In inverse variation, the product of the two variables is always constant (\(x \cdot y = k\)).

Common Mistake: When setting up inverse ratios, students often write \(\frac{y_1}{y_2} = \frac{x_1}{x_2}\) by habit. Remember, for inverse relationships, the terms on the right side must swap places: \(\frac{y_1}{y_2} = \frac{x_2}{x_1}\).

Mini Example: If \(8\) workers can build a wall in \(15\) days, how many days will \(12\) workers take to build the same wall?

Solution: Number of workers (\(W\)) and days (\(D\)) are inversely proportional.

$$W_1 \cdot D_1 = W_2 \cdot D_2 \implies 8 \times 15 = 12 \times D_2 \implies 120 = 12 \times D_2 \implies D_2 = 10\text{ days}$$

Concept 3: Joint and Compound Variation

Definition: Joint variation occurs when a variable depends on two or more other variables directly. Compound variation involves a combination of direct and inverse variations with multiple variables.

Explanation & Intuition: Real-world scenarios are rarely limited to just two variables. For example, the interest earned on a bank deposit depends on both the principal amount and the time period. Similarly, the pressure of a gas depends directly on temperature and inversely on volume.

Mathematical Formulation:

  • If \(z\) varies jointly as \(x\) and \(y\): \(z \propto xy \implies z = kxy\)
  • If \(z\) varies directly as \(x\) and inversely as \(y\): \(z \propto \frac{x}{y} \implies z = \frac{kx}{y} \implies \frac{z_1 y_1}{x_1} = \frac{z_2 y_2}{x_2}\)

Memory Tip: Keep direct variables in the denominator on the opposite side, and inverse variables on the same level: \(\frac{\text{Result}}{\text{Direct Inputs}} = \text{Constant}\).

Common Mistake: Treating compound variation as multiple independent linear steps. Always write down the single, unified variation equation first before putting in the values.

Mini Example: The volume of a cylinder (\(V\)) varies jointly as its height (\(h\)) and the square of its radius (\(r\)). If volume is \(154\text{ cm}^3\) when \(r = 7\text{ cm}\) and \(h = 1\text{ cm}\), write the variation equation.

Solution: \(V = k \cdot r^2 \cdot h \implies 154 = k \cdot (7^2) \cdot 1 \implies 154 = 49k \implies k = \frac{154}{49} = \frac{22}{7}\). Thus, the equation is \(V = \frac{22}{7}r^2h\).

📏 5. Rules & Properties

The Constant of Variation Rule (\(k \neq 0\))

Explanation: The constant \(k\) can be a positive integer, fraction, or decimal, but it can never be zero. Its value depends entirely on the physical units of measurement used for the variables.

Reason: If \(k = 0\), the equation collapses to \(y = 0\), which eliminates any relationship between the variables.

Shortcut Application: Once you determine the value of \(k\) from the initial conditions, that same \(k\) must be used to solve all subsequent scenarios within that question.

The Power Variation Rule

Explanation: Variation is not limited to linear terms. If \(y \propto x^n\), then multiplying \(x\) by a factor of \(m\) will multiply \(y\) by a factor of \(m^n\).

Example: In physics, kinetic energy (\(KE\)) varies directly with the square of speed (\(v^2\)). If speed is doubled (\(2x\)), kinetic energy increases by a factor of \(2^2 = 4\) times.

Exception/Caution: Always check if the variation is direct/inverse to the *variable itself* or its *square, cube, or square root*. Missing the power is a primary cause of negative marks in competitive exams.

📐 6. Formula Box

1. Direct Variation

$$y = k \cdot x \quad \text{or} \quad \frac{y_1}{x_1} = \frac{y_2}{x_2}$$

Variables: \(y\) = Dependent variable, \(x\) = Independent variable, \(k\) = Proportionality constant.

When to use: When one quantity increases proportionally as the other increases (e.g., Distance vs. Time at a constant speed).

Memory Trick: Direct means "Fraction is constant".

2. Inverse Variation

$$y = \frac{k}{x} \quad \text{or} \quad x_1 \cdot y_1 = x_2 \cdot y_2$$

Variables: \(y\) = Dependent variable, \(x\) = Independent variable, \(k\) = Proportionality constant.

When to use: When one quantity decreases proportionally as the other increases (e.g., Speed vs. Time for a constant distance).

Memory Trick: Inverse means "Product is constant".

3. Compound Variation (The Joint Equation)

$$\frac{z_1 \cdot w_1}{x_1 \cdot y_1} = \frac{z_2 \cdot w_2}{x_2 \cdot y_2}$$

When to use: When multiple independent variables are acting on a dependent quantity at the same time. This is the underlying principle of the famous Chain Rule (MDH Formula) in Time & Work:

$$\frac{M_1 \cdot D_1 \cdot H_1}{W_1} = \frac{M_2 \cdot D_2 \cdot H_2}{W_2}$$

💡 7. Solved Examples

Easy

Example 1: Direct Proportionality in Spring Extension

Question: The extension of a spring is directly proportional to the weight hung on it. If a weight of \(15\text{ kg}\) produces an extension of \(2.4\text{ cm}\), what weight is required to produce an extension of \(4\text{ cm}\)?

View Step-by-Step Solution

Step 1: Identify the relationship and write the formula.

Let extension be \(E\) and weight be \(W\). Since \(E\) is directly proportional to \(W\):

$$E \propto W \implies \frac{E_1}{W_1} = \frac{E_2}{W_2}$$

Step 2: Substitute the given values.

\(E_1 = 2.4\text{ cm}\), \(W_1 = 15\text{ kg}\), and \(E_2 = 4\text{ cm}\). We need to find \(W_2\).

$$\frac{2.4}{15} = \frac{4}{W_2}$$

Step 3: Solve for \(W_2\).

$$W_2 = \frac{4 \times 15}{2.4}$$ $$W_2 = \frac{60}{2.4} = \frac{600}{24} = 25\text{ kg}$$

Final Answer: The weight required to produce a \(4\text{ cm}\) extension is \(25\text{ kg}\).

Medium

Example 2: Inverse Variation with Speed and Time

Question: A car covers a certain distance in \(4\text{ hours}\) at a speed of \(60\text{ km/h}\). If the car wants to cover the same distance in \(3\text{ hours}\), by how much must it increase its speed?

View Step-by-Step Solution

Step 1: Identify the relationship.

For a constant distance, speed (\(S\)) and time (\(T\)) are inversely proportional:

$$S \propto \frac{1}{T} \implies S_1 \cdot T_1 = S_2 \cdot T_2$$

Step 2: Substitute the known values.

\(S_1 = 60\text{ km/h}\), \(T_1 = 4\text{ hours}\), \(T_2 = 3\text{ hours}\).

$$60 \times 4 = S_2 \times 3$$ $$240 = 3 \cdot S_2 \implies S_2 = 80\text{ km/h}$$

Step 3: Calculate the required increase in speed.

The question asks "by how much must it increase its speed", not the final speed itself.

$$\text{Increase in speed} = S_2 - S_1 = 80 - 60 = 20\text{ km/h}$$

Final Answer: The car must increase its speed by \(20\text{ km/h}\).

Hard

Example 3: The Classic Diamond-Breaking Problem

Question: The cost of a precious diamond is directly proportional to the square of its weight. A diamond weighing \(10\text{ g}\) is worth ₹\(2,50,000\). If it accidentally falls and breaks into two pieces whose weights are in the ratio \(2:3\), find the loss incurred due to this breakage.

View Step-by-Step Solution

Step 1: Write down the variation equation.

Let the cost be \(C\) and weight be \(W\).

$$C \propto W^2 \implies C = k \cdot W^2$$

Step 2: Find the value of constant \(k\) using initial conditions.

When \(W = 10\text{ g}\), \(C = \text{₹ }2,50,000\).

$$2,50,000 = k \cdot (10)^2 \implies 2,50,000 = 100k \implies k = 2500$$

Step 3: Determine the weights of the broken pieces.

The total weight of \(10\text{ g}\) is divided into the ratio \(2:3\).

$$\text{Weight of piece 1 } (W_1) = \frac{2}{2+3} \times 10 = 4\text{ g}$$ $$\text{Weight of piece 2 } (W_2) = \frac{3}{2+3} \times 10 = 6\text{ g}$$

Step 4: Calculate the cost of each broken piece.

$$\text{Cost of piece 1 } (C_1) = k \cdot W_1^2 = 2500 \times 4^2 = 2500 \times 16 = \text{₹ }40,000$$ $$\text{Cost of piece 2 } (C_2) = k \cdot W_2^2 = 2500 \times 6^2 = 2500 \times 36 = \text{₹ }90,000$$

Step 5: Find the total value after breakage and calculate the loss.

$$\text{Total Value after breakage} = C_1 + C_2 = 40,000 + 90,000 = \text{₹ }1,30,000$$ $$\text{Loss Incurred} = \text{Original Cost} - \text{Total Broken Cost}$$ $$\text{Loss} = 2,50,000 - 1,30,000 = \text{₹ }1,20,000$$

Final Answer: The loss incurred due to breakage is ₹ \(1,20,000\).

Exam Special

Example 4: Compound Multi-Variable Variation

Question: \(A\) varies directly as \(B\) and inversely as the square root of \(C\). When \(B = 24\) and \(C = 16\), then \(A = 12\). Find the value of \(A\) when \(B = 32\) and \(C = 64\).

View Step-by-Step Solution

Step 1: Set up the compound variation equation.

$$A \propto \frac{B}{\sqrt{C}} \implies A = k \cdot \frac{B}{\sqrt{C}}$$

Step 2: Find the constant \(k\).

Substitute \(A = 12\), \(B = 24\), and \(C = 16\):

$$12 = k \cdot \frac{24}{\sqrt{16}}$$ $$12 = k \cdot \frac{24}{4} \implies 12 = 6k \implies k = 2$$

Step 3: Calculate the new value of \(A\).

Using \(k = 2\), \(B = 32\), and \(C = 64\):

$$A = 2 \cdot \frac{32}{\sqrt{64}}$$ $$A = 2 \cdot \frac{32}{8} \implies A = 2 \times 4 \implies A = 8$$

Final Answer: The value of \(A\) is \(8\).

⚡ 8. Exam Tricks & Shortcuts

The Multiplier / Scaling Shortcut

In many competitive exam questions, you do not need to calculate the actual value of \(k\). You can solve them quickly by tracking how much the variables scale.

Rule: If \(y \propto \frac{x^2}{z}\):

  • If \(x\) is doubled (multiplied by \(2\)), \(y\) is multiplied by \(2^2 = 4\).
  • If \(z\) is tripled (multiplied by \(3\)), \(y\) is multiplied by \(\frac{1}{3}\).
  • Overall change in \(y\) = \(4 \times \frac{1}{3} = \frac{4}{3}\) of its original value.

Apply to Diamond Problem: Instead of calculating with big monetary figures, write original weight as \(5\) units (\(2+3\)). Original Value \(\propto 5^2 = 25\). Broken pieces value \(\propto (2^2 + 3^2) = 4 + 9 = 13\). Loss \(= 25 - 13 = 12\) units. Since \(25\text{ units} = \text{₹ }2,50,000 \implies 1\text{ unit} = \text{₹ }10,000 \implies \text{Loss} = 12 \times 10,000 = \text{₹ }1,20,000\). This saves precious time during the exam!

Option Elimination via "Inverse Check"

If two variables are inversely proportional, when one increases, the other must decrease. If you are solving a speed-time problem where speed increases, look at the options and immediately cross off any values that are equal to or larger than the original time.

⚠️ 9. Common Mistakes & Pitfalls

Mistake 1: Ignoring Powers and Roots

The Trap: If \(y\) varies directly with the square of \(x\), and \(x\) increases from \(2\) to \(6\) (which is \(3\) times), students often mistakenly think \(y\) will also increase \(3\) times.

Correction: Since \(y \propto x^2\), if \(x\) is multiplied by \(3\), \(y\) must be multiplied by \(3^2 = 9\) times.

Mistake 2: Treating Partial Sums as Single Proportions

The Trap: In questions stating "Total cost is partly constant and partly varies directly with the number of guests," students often write \(C = k \cdot N\).

Correction: This is a linear system, not a simple direct proportion. The correct equation to write is \(C = A + bN\), where \(A\) is the fixed cost and \(b\) is the variable rate per guest. You must solve this using two simultaneous equations.

📊 10. Exam Strategy & Weightage

SSC (CGL, CHSL, CPO)

Weightage: \(1\text{ to }2\) Questions directly, with indirect application in \(3\text{ to }4\) arithmetic questions.

Focus: Diamond-breaking problems, engine fuel consumption vs. speed square, and joint variation in mensuration formulas.

Banking Exams (PO, Clerk)

Weightage: Frequently asked as part of "Data Sufficiency" or "Arithmetic Word Problems".

Focus: Multi-variable compound word problems with fractional values and percentage changes.

Railways & State Exams

Weightage: \(1\text{ to }2\) Questions.

Focus: Direct application of formula ratios (\(\frac{y_1}{x_1} = \frac{y_2}{x_2}\)) and simple speed-work problems.

📌 11. Summary

Variation describes how quantities scale relative to each other. When working with Direct Variation, the ratio remains constant (\(\frac{y}{x} = k\)). In Inverse Variation, the product remains constant (\(x \cdot y = k\)). For complex multi-variable exams questions, use the unified equation approach by solving for the constant \(k\) first, or use the scaling shortcut to find the answer directly from the given ratios.

🚀 13. Key Takeaways

  • Always write down the basic variation formula with \(k\) before inserting values.
  • Use the ratio scaling method for diamond breaking and volume change problems to bypass slow calculations.
  • Keep an eye out for keywords like "square", "cube", or "inverse square root".
  • In inverse variation questions, set up your variables as a constant product: \(x_1 y_1 = x_2 y_2\).
