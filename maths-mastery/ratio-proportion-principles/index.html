Ratio & Proportion Principles

Medium Difficulty 25-30 Mins Read High Exam Weightage Updated 2026

📘 1. Introduction

Ratio and Proportion form the absolute bedrock of arithmetic reasoning in competitive examinations. Whether you are aiming for SSC CGL, Bank PO, Railways, or CDS, mastering this topic is non-negotiable. Why? Because ratios are not just standalone chapters; they are powerful mathematical tools used to simplify complex calculations in Partnership, Mixture & Alligation, Time & Work, Speed & Distance, and Data Interpretation (DI).

In simple terms, a Ratio is a comparison of two or more quantities of the same kind by division, showing how many times one value contains another. A Proportion is an equation which states that two ratios are equal. Understanding these principles will transform how you view word problems, shifting your approach from heavy algebraic equations to elegant, mental arithmetic calculations.

🎯 2. Learning Objectives

By the end of this microtopic, you will be able to:

  • Understand the fundamental nature of ratios, including their properties, scaling, and unitless characteristics.
  • Master the art of combining multiple disjoint ratios (e.g., converting \(A:B\) and \(B:C\) into \(A:B:C\)) instantly.
  • Distinguish between different mathematical ratios like Duplicate, Triplicate, Sub-duplicate, and Reciprocal ratios.
  • Apply proportional properties—specifically Mean, Third, and Fourth Proportionals—to solve direct exam questions.
  • Wield algebraic proportion properties such as Componendo & Dividendo (C&D) to crack complex algebraic fractions within seconds.

📝 3. Prerequisites

Before diving into Ratio & Proportion Principles, ensure you have a comfortable grasp of the following foundational concepts:

  • Basic Simplification: Finding the Greatest Common Divisor (GCD/HCF) to reduce fractions to their simplest form.
  • Least Common Multiple (LCM): Finding the LCM of denominators to clear fractions in equations.
  • Basic Linear Equations: Understanding how to transpose variables and solve simple single-variable equations.

🧠 4. Core Concepts

Concept 1: Definition and Fundamental Properties of Ratios

Definition: A ratio of a number \(a\) to another number \(b\) (where \(b \neq 0\)) is written as \(a:b\) or \(\frac{a}{b}\). The first term \(a\) is called the antecedent, and the second term \(b\) is called the consequent.

Intuition: Think of a ratio as a "recipe recipe scale". If a cake recipe requires 2 cups of flour and 3 cups of sugar, the ratio of flour to sugar is \(2:3\). If you want to make a bigger cake, you scale up both ingredients by the same factor, say 5, to get 10 cups of flour and 15 cups of sugar. The ratio remains fundamentally identical: \(10:15 = 2:3\).

Important Observations:

  • Unitless: A ratio is pure comparison; hence it has no units. However, the quantities compared must be of the same unit. You cannot directly ratio \(5\text{ kg}\) to \(300\text{ grams}\) without converting both to grams or kilograms first (\(5000\text{g} : 300\text{g} = 50:3\)).
  • Invariance: If both terms of a ratio are multiplied or divided by the same non-zero quantity, the ratio remains unchanged. $$a:b = ak:bk \quad \text{and} \quad a:b = \frac{a}{k}:\frac{b}{k}$$

💡 Memory Tip: Order Matters! \(a:b\) is NOT the same as \(b:a\). The antecedent is always your starting perspective.

⚠️ Common Mistake: Never add or subtract a constant value directly to the terms of a ratio and assume the ratio remains unchanged. For example, if \(a:b = 2:3\), then \((a+1):(b+1) \neq 2:3\). Try it: \((2+1):(3+1) = 3:4 \neq 2:3\).

Mini Example: Express the ratio of \(1.5\text{ hours}\) to \(45\text{ minutes}\) in its simplest form.
Solution: Convert hours to minutes: \(1.5 \times 60 = 90\text{ minutes}\). The ratio is \(90 : 45\). Dividing both sides by \(45\), we get \(2 : 1\).

Concept 2: Combining Ratios (The Balancing Act)

Definition: The process of merging two or more distinct ratios sharing a common bridging variable into a single joint ratio.

Explanation: If you are given \(A:B = 2:3\) and \(B:C = 4:5\), you cannot write \(A:B:C = 2:3:5\) because the value of \(B\) is 3 in the first ratio and 4 in the second. You must balance the common term \(B\) by making its value equal to the LCM of its values in both ratios.

Intuition: Imagine two gears. Gear A rotates 2 times when Gear B rotates 3 times. Gear B rotates 4 times when Gear C rotates 5 times. To synchronize all three gears together, we must find a common speed for Gear B. The LCM of 3 and 4 is 12.

Important Observations:

  • To scale \(B\) to 12 in the first ratio, multiply the entire ratio \(A:B\) by 4: \(A:B = 2 \times 4 : 3 \times 4 = 8:12\).
  • To scale \(B\) to 12 in the second ratio, multiply the entire ratio \(B:C\) by 3: \(B:C = 4 \times 3 : 5 \times 3 = 12:15\).
  • Now, since \(B\) is identical in both, we can safely write: \(A:B:C = 8:12:15\).

⚡ Zig-Zag Multiplier Method: For quick scaling of \(A:B = a:b\) and \(B:C = c:d\):
\(A = a \times c\)
\(B = b \times c\)
\(C = b \times d\)
This gives the direct joint ratio: \(A:B:C = ac : bc : bd\).

Mini Example: If \(P:Q = 3:4\) and \(Q:R = 8:9\), find \(P:Q:R\).
Solution: Since the LCM of the common term \(Q\) (which has values 4 and 8) is 8, we only need to multiply the first ratio by 2. Thus, \(P:Q = 6:8\). The combined ratio is \(6:8:9\).

Concept 3: Types of Ratios

Examinations often use direct terminology for various mathematical variations of ratios. Learn these off by heart:

  • Duplicate Ratio: The ratio of squares of two quantities. The duplicate ratio of \(a:b\) is \(a^2:b^2\).
  • Sub-duplicate Ratio: The ratio of square roots of two quantities. The sub-duplicate ratio of \(a:b\) is \(\sqrt{a}:\sqrt{b}\).
  • Triplicate Ratio: The ratio of cubes of two quantities. The triplicate ratio of \(a:b\) is \(a^3:b^3\).
  • Sub-triplicate Ratio: The ratio of cube roots of two quantities. The sub-triplicate ratio of \(a:b\) is \(\sqrt[3]{a}:\sqrt[3]{b}\).
  • Reciprocal/Inverse Ratio: The inverse of the given ratio. The reciprocal ratio of \(a:b\) is \(\frac{1}{a}:\frac{1}{b}\), which simplifies to \(b:a\). For three variables \(a:b:c\), the reciprocal ratio is \(\frac{1}{a}:\frac{1}{b}:\frac{1}{c} = bcd : acd : abe\) (after multiplying by the LCM of \(a, b, c\)).

Mini Example: Find the sub-duplicate ratio of \(64:121\) and the reciprocal ratio of \(2:3:4\).
Solution:
1. Sub-duplicate ratio = \(\sqrt{64} : \sqrt{121} = 8 : 11\).
2. Reciprocal ratio of \(2:3:4\) = \(\frac{1}{2} : \frac{1}{3} : \frac{1}{4}\). The LCM of 2, 3, 4 is 12. Multiplying each term by 12 gives \(6 : 4 : 3\).

Concept 4: Proportion and Continued Proportion

Definition: When two ratios are equal, the four quantities are said to be in proportion. If \(a:b = c:d\), we write \(a:b :: c:d\).

Here, \(a\) and \(d\) are called the extremes, while \(b\) and \(c\) are called the means.

Fundamental Rule of Proportion: $$\text{Product of Extremes} = \text{Product of Means} \implies a \times d = b \times c$$

Continued Proportion: Three quantities \(a, b, c\) are in continued proportion if: $$a:b = b:c \implies b^2 = ac \implies b = \sqrt{ac}$$ In this scenario:

  • \(b\) is known as the Mean Proportional between \(a\) and \(c\).
  • \(c\) is known as the Third Proportional to \(a\) and \(b\).

Fourth Proportional: If four quantities are in proportion (\(a:b = c:d\)), then \(d\) is the Fourth Proportional to \(a, b, c\) and is calculated as \(d = \frac{b \times c}{a}\).

Mini Example: Find the Mean Proportional between 9 and 16, and the Third Proportional to 4 and 6.
Solution:
1. Mean Proportional = \(\sqrt{9 \times 16} = \sqrt{144} = 12\).
2. Third Proportional \(c = \frac{b^2}{a} = \frac{6^2}{4} = \frac{36}{4} = 9\).

📏 5. Rules & Properties of Proportions

To solve complex algebraic fractions with speed, memorize these operations that preserve the equality of proportions:

1. Invertendo

Rule: If \(\frac{a}{b} = \frac{c}{d}\), then \(\frac{b}{a} = \frac{d}{c}\).

Reason: Taking reciprocal on both sides does not alter the equality.

Example: If \(\frac{2}{3} = \frac{4}{6}\), then \(\frac{3}{2} = \frac{6}{4}\).

2. Alternendo

Rule: If \(\frac{a}{b} = \frac{c}{d}\), then \(\frac{a}{c} = \frac{b}{d}\).

Reason: Cross-multiplying gives \(ad = bc\). Dividing both sides by \(cd\) gives \(\frac{a}{c} = \frac{b}{d}\).

Example: If \(\frac{2}{3} = \frac{4}{6}\), then \(\frac{2}{4} = \frac{3}{6}\) (both equal \(\frac{1}{2}\)).

3. Componendo

Rule: If \(\frac{a}{b} = \frac{c}{d}\), then \(\frac{a+b}{b} = \frac{c+d}{d}\).

Reason: Add 1 to both sides: \(\frac{a}{b} + 1 = \frac{c}{d} + 1 \implies \frac{a+b}{b} = \frac{c+d}{d}\).

4. Dividendo

Rule: If \(\frac{a}{b} = \frac{c}{d}\), then \(\frac{a-b}{b} = \frac{c-d}{d}\).

Reason: Subtract 1 from both sides: \(\frac{a}{b} - 1 = \frac{c}{d} - 1 \implies \frac{a-b}{b} = \frac{c-d}{d}\).

5. Componendo & Dividendo (C&D) - ⭐️ High Yield

Rule: If \(\frac{a}{b} = \frac{c}{d}\), then: $$\frac{a+b}{a-b} = \frac{c+d}{c-d}$$

Shortcut Application: Conversely, if you are given \(\frac{x+y}{x-y} = \frac{p}{q}\), you can instantly apply C&D backwards to get: $$\frac{x}{y} = \frac{p+q}{p-q}$$

Exception/Caution: Ensure the terms in the numerator and denominator are identical except for the sign. Do not apply C&D if the variables differ (e.g., \(\frac{a+b}{c-d}\)).

Example: If \(\frac{x+3}{x-3} = \frac{5}{2}\), find \(x\).
Solution: Applying reverse C&D: \(\frac{x}{3} = \frac{5+2}{5-2} \implies \frac{x}{3} = \frac{7}{3} \implies x = 7\).

📐 6. Formula Box

Mean Proportional Formula

$$M = \sqrt{a \times b}$$

Variables: \(a, b\) are two positive numbers; \(M\) is the mean proportional.

When to use: When asked to find a single proportional middle term between two numbers.

💡 Memory Trick: Mean means "Multiply and root" (\(M = \sqrt{ab}\)).

Third Proportional Formula

$$c = \frac{b^2}{a}$$

Variables: \(a\) is the first term, \(b\) is the second term, \(c\) is the third proportional.

When to use: Given two numbers in continued proportion, and asked for the third.

Fourth Proportional Formula

$$d = \frac{b \times c}{a}$$

Variables: \(a, b, c\) are the three given quantities in order.

The K-Method (Universal Problem Solver)

If \(\frac{a}{b} = \frac{c}{d} = \frac{e}{f} = k\), then: $$a = bk, \quad c = dk, \quad e = fk$$ Also, the sum of numerators divided by sum of denominators is equal to the constant \(k\): $$\frac{a+c+e}{b+d+f} = k$$

When to use: Complex ratio expressions where multiple ratios are equal to each other.

⚡ 7. Solved Examples

Easy

Example 1: Balancing Three Ratios

Question: If \(A:B = 3:4\), \(B:C = 5:6\), and \(C:D = 11:9\), find \(A:B:C:D\).

View Step-by-Step Solution

Step 1: Write down the ratios sequentially.

  • \(A:B = 3:4\)
  • \(B:C = 5:6\)
  • \(C:D = 11:9\)

Step 2: Use the "Fill-in-the-Blank" empty space technique. Write the variables in a row, and write their ratios underneath. Fill the empty spaces with the neighboring values:

  A   :   B   :   C   :   D
  3   :   4   :  [4]  :  [4]   (Fill empty spaces to the right with 4)
 [5]  :   5   :   6   :  [6]   (Fill left with 5, right with 6)
[11]  :  [11] :  11   :   9    (Fill left with 11)

Step 3: Multiply vertically down each column:

  • \(A = 3 \times 5 \times 11 = 165\)
  • \(B = 4 \times 5 \times 11 = 220\)
  • \(C = 4 \times 6 \times 11 = 264\)
  • \(D = 4 \times 6 \times 9 = 216\)

Final Answer: \(A:B:C:D = 165 : 220 : 264 : 216\). (Check if they share a common divisor; they do not. This is the simplest form).

Medium

Example 2: Proportional Equations and Substitutions

Question: If \((5a + 3b) : (5a - 3b) = 9 : 4\), find the ratio \(a : b\).

View Step-by-Step Solution

Method 1: Direct Cross-Multiplication

$$\frac{5a + 3b}{5a - 3b} = \frac{9}{4}$$

$$4(5a + 3b) = 9(5a - 3b)$$

$$20a + 12b = 45a - 27b$$

$$12b + 27b = 45a - 20a$$

$$39b = 25a \implies \frac{a}{b} = \frac{39}{25}$$

Method 2: Smart C&D (Saves 15 seconds!)

Apply Componendo & Dividendo to \(\frac{5a+3b}{5a-3b} = \frac{9}{4}\):

$$\frac{(5a+3b) + (5a-3b)}{(5a+3b) - (5a-3b)} = \frac{9+4}{9-4}$$

$$\frac{10a}{6b} = \frac{13}{5}$$

$$\frac{5a}{3b} = \frac{13}{5} \implies \frac{a}{b} = \frac{13 \times 3}{5 \times 5} = \frac{39}{25}$$

Final Answer: \(a:b = 39:25\).

Hard

Example 3: System-based Constant Shifts

Question: When \(x\) is added to each of the numbers \(2, 3, 30,\) and \(35\), the numbers obtained in this order are in proportion. What is the mean proportional between \((x + 7)\) and \((x - 2)\)?

View Step-by-Step Solution

Step 1: Set up the proportion equation.

$$\frac{2+x}{3+x} = \frac{30+x}{35+x}$$

Step 2: Use the Difference Ratio Trick to find \(x\) instantly.
For any proportion \(\frac{A}{B} = \frac{C}{D}\), it is also equal to \(\frac{|A-C|}{|B-D|}\). Let's use this to avoid quadratic equations:

The difference between numerators is \((30+x) - (2+x) = 28\).
The difference between denominators is \((35+x) - (3+x) = 32\).
So: $$\frac{2+x}{3+x} = \frac{28}{32} = \frac{7}{8}$$

Step 3: Solve for \(x\).

$$8(2+x) = 7(3+x) \implies 16 + 8x = 21 + 7x \implies x = 5$$

Step 4: Find the Mean Proportional.
We need the mean proportional of \((x+7)\) and \((x-2)\).
Substituting \(x = 5\):
First number = \(5 + 7 = 12\)
Second number = \(5 - 2 = 3\)
Mean Proportional = \(\sqrt{12 \times 3} = \sqrt{36} = 6\).

Final Answer: 6.

Exam Special

Example 4: Combined Algebraic Constants (SSC CGL Tier-II)

Question: If \((a + b) : (b + c) : (c + a) = 7 : 6 : 5\) and \(a + b + c = 27\), then find the value of \(\frac{1}{a} : \frac{1}{b} : \frac{1}{c}\).

View Step-by-Step Solution

Step 1: Represent ratios using a constant \(k\).

  • \(a + b = 7k\)
  • \(b + c = 6k\)
  • \(c + a = 5k\)

Step 2: Add all three equations together.

$$2(a + b + c) = 7k + 6k + 5k = 18k$$

$$a + b + c = 9k$$

Step 3: Find individual values of \(a, b, c\) in terms of \(k\).
We are given \(a + b + c = 9k\). Let's subtract the given partial ratios:

  • \(c = (a+b+c) - (a+b) = 9k - 7k = 2k\)
  • \(a = (a+b+c) - (b+c) = 9k - 6k = 3k\)
  • \(b = (a+b+c) - (c+a) = 9k - 5k = 4k\)

Note: We don't even need the real sum \(a+b+c=27\) because we only need to find a ratio! This is a massive time-saving realization.

So, the ratio of \(a:b:c = 3k : 4k : 2k = 3:4:2\).

Step 4: Find the reciprocal ratio.

$$\frac{1}{a} : \frac{1}{b} : \frac{1}{c} = \frac{1}{3} : \frac{1}{4} : \frac{1}{2}$$

The LCM of 3, 4, 2 is 12. Multiply each term by 12:

$$\left(\frac{1}{3} \times 12\right) : \left(\frac{1}{4} \times 12\right) : \left(\frac{1}{2} \times 12\right) = 4 : 3 : 6$$

Final Answer: \(4 : 3 : 6\).

💡 8. Exam Tricks & Shortcuts

⚡ Trick 1: Option Elimination using Divisibility

In most competitive exams, you don't need to solve the entire equation. If an exam question asks for the final share of a person \(B\), and you establish from the problem that the ratio of \(B\)'s share is a multiple of 7, look at the answer options immediately. Only the option divisible by 7 will be your answer. If there are two options, eliminate the other three and then apply quick estimation.

⚡ Trick 2: Direct "Constant Difference" Proportion Trick

If you need to find what should be added to \(a, b, c, d\) to make them proportional:

$$x = \frac{ad - bc}{(a+d) - (b+c)}$$

This single formula bypasses all algebraic steps. (Keep track of absolute values if the result is negative, but generally, \(x\) is positive in standard exam patterns).

⚡ Trick 3: Balancing Scale Visualizer

If \(A:B = 2:3\) and \(B:C = 4:5\), visualize a physical balance scale where \(B\) is the pivot. Multiply the left scale elements by 4 and the right scale elements by 3 to reach a stable state where \(B\) reads 12 on both sides.

⚠️ 9. Common Mistakes & Pitfalls

🚫 Mistake 1: Directly equating Ratios to Absolute values

The Trap: If \(A:B = 2:3\) and \(C:D = 4:5\), writing \(A + C = 6\) or \(B + D = 8\) is mathematically illegal. Ratios of different units/systems cannot be added directly because they have different multipliers (i.e., \(A = 2k\) and \(C = 4m\)).

How to avoid: Keep ratio families strictly separated unless there is a common bridge linking them.

🚫 Mistake 2: Order of Mean vs. Third Proportional

The Trap: Confusing the terms for Third Proportional and Mean Proportional. For example, if asked to find the Third Proportional of 4 and 16, students mistakenly do \(\sqrt{4 \times 16} = 8\). 8 is the Mean proportional. The Third proportional is \(\frac{16^2}{4} = 64\).

How to avoid: Remember: Third Proportional is the term \(c\) in \(a:b :: b:c\). Mean proportional is the term \(b\) in \(a:b :: b:c\).

📊 10. Exam Strategy & Weightage

SSC CGL / CHSL / CPO

Weightage: 2 - 3 direct questions in Prelims; up to 4 - 5 questions in Mains (including indirect DI applications).

Trend: High focus on finding Mean/Third Proportionals of algebraic expressions (e.g., \(\sqrt{x}\) forms) and shift-based calculations.

Banking Exams (PO / Clerk)

Weightage: 1 - 2 direct word problems; but forms 40% of standard Data Interpretation (DI) calculations.

Trend: Focuses heavily on rapid division of large values into complex fractional ratios.

Railways & State PCS

Weightage: 3 - 4 direct, formula-based questions.

Trend: Direct questions on Mean Proportional, Duplicate/Sub-triplicate calculations, and simple scaling.

📌 11. Summary

To summarize, a ratio is a unitless comparison of two identical quantities of the same unit. When two ratios are equal, they are in proportion, where the product of the extremes equals the product of the means. We can manipulate ratios seamlessly via scaling, finding common denominators, or applying proportional theorems like Componendo & Dividendo to reduce algebraic work down to simple mental shifts.

🚀 12. Key Takeaways

  • Always keep the unit of both parameters identical before expressing them as a ratio.
  • When merging ratios like \(A:B\) and \(B:C\), prioritize balancing the shared element (\(B\)) using its LCM.
  • Do not apply arithmetic additions/subtractions directly onto ratios (i.e., do not assume \(2x+3\) can be calculated as \(2+3\) without the constant multiplier).
  • Use the reverse Componendo & Dividendo shortcut whenever you see symmetrical fractions with alternating signs.
