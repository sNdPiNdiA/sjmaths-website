Pipes, Cisterns & Work Sharing (Wages)

Medium Difficulty 25-30 Mins Read High Exam Weightage Updated 2026

📘 Introduction

This microtopic extends Time & Work concepts to scenarios involving filling/emptying containers (cisterns) using pipes, and also covers the principle of distributing wages based on work done. Understanding efficiency and total work remains central.

  • Core Application: Calculating time taken to fill/empty tanks, or determining individual/combined work rates of pipes.
  • Wages Application: Fair distribution of earnings among workers based on their contribution.
  • Exam Relevance: High-frequency topic across SSC, Railway, Banking, and State exams, often appearing as 1-2 questions.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Understand the analogy between workers and pipes, and work and tank capacity.
  • Differentiate between inlet pipes (filling) and outlet pipes (emptying).
  • Calculate the combined efficiency of multiple pipes, including those with leaks.
  • Solve problems involving pipes working for varying durations or in shifts.
  • Apply the principle of work-proportional wages to distribute earnings accurately.

📝 Prerequisites

  • Time & Work Basics: Understanding concepts like efficiency, total work, and their relation.
  • LCM & HCF: Proficiency in finding the Least Common Multiple (LCM) for calculating total work.
  • Fractions & Ratios: Basic operations and understanding proportional relationships.
  • Unitary Method: Ability to calculate values based on a single unit.

💡 Core Concepts

Pipes, Cisterns & Tank Capacity

In Time & Work, workers complete a task. In Pipes & Cisterns, pipes fill or empty a tank. The tank's capacity is analogous to total work.

  • Definition: A pipe is an agent performing work (filling/emptying), and a cistern/tank is the container where work is done.
  • Intuition: A larger tank takes longer to fill or empty at a constant rate. More efficient pipes fill/empty faster.
  • Key Observation: Problems are fundamentally Time & Work problems, just with different terminology.
  • Memory Tip: Think of "Total Work = Tank Capacity".
  • Mini Example: If a pipe fills a tank in 10 hours, its efficiency is \( \frac{1}{10} \) tank/hour.

Inlet Pipes vs. Outlet Pipes

Pipes can either add water (inlet) or remove water (outlet/leak). This dictates their contribution to the total work.

Feature Inlet Pipe (Filling Pipe) Outlet Pipe (Emptying Pipe / Leak)
Function Fills the tank with water. Empties water from the tank.
Work Contribution Positive Work (adds to total). Negative Work (subtracts from total).
Efficiency Sign Positive (e.g., \( +5 \) units/hour). Negative (e.g., \( -3 \) units/hour).
Effect on Tank Level Increases water level. Decreases water level.
  • Intuition: An inlet pipe is like a worker building something; an outlet pipe is like a worker demolishing it.
  • Key Observation: The net effect depends on the sum of positive and negative efficiencies.
  • Memory Tip: "In" means `+`, "Out" means `-`.
  • Mini Example: Pipe A fills 5 units/hr, Pipe B empties 2 units/hr. Net effect: \( 5 - 2 = 3 \) units/hr (filling).

Work Sharing (Wages)

Wages for completing a task are always distributed in proportion to the work done by each individual or group.

  • Definition: The amount of money earned is directly proportional to the share of the total work contributed.
  • Intuition: Someone who does more work deserves more pay.
  • Key Observation: If workers are paid for *time spent* and their *daily wages* are equal, then total wages are proportional to the time spent working effectively. However, if daily wages differ or they work for different durations, the core principle is always proportionality to *work done*.
  • Memory Tip: "More Work = More Pay."
  • Mini Example: A does 2/3 of work, B does 1/3. Total wage $900. A gets \( \frac{2}{3} \times 900 = 600 \), B gets \( \frac{1}{3} \times 900 = 300 \).

📏 Rules & Properties

Rule 1: Combined Efficiency of Multiple Pipes

When multiple pipes (inlet and outlet) work simultaneously, their combined efficiency is the algebraic sum of their individual efficiencies.

  • Explanation: Assign positive efficiency to inlet pipes and negative efficiency to outlet pipes/leaks. Sum these to get the net efficiency.
  • Reason: Positive work adds to the tank's contents, negative work removes from it. The net change is the sum.
  • Shortcut: If Pipe A fills in 'a' hours and Pipe B empties in 'b' hours, combined rate is \( \frac{1}{a} - \frac{1}{b} \). Time taken to fill: \( \frac{ab}{b-a} \).
  • Exception: If the combined efficiency is negative, the tank will never fill (it will empty if not already empty).
  • Example: Pipe A fills in 10 hrs (\( + \frac{1}{10} \)), Pipe B empties in 15 hrs (\( - \frac{1}{15} \)). Combined efficiency \( = \frac{1}{10} - \frac{1}{15} = \frac{3-2}{30} = \frac{1}{30} \). Tank fills in 30 hours.

Rule 2: Wages Proportional to Work Done

The total wage or payment for a task should be divided among individuals in the ratio of the work done by each.

  • Explanation: Calculate the fraction of total work completed by each person/group. Distribute the total wages using these fractions.
  • Reason: Fair compensation dictates that a greater contribution earns a greater share of the reward.
  • Shortcut: If Person A and Person B complete work in the ratio \( W_A : W_B \), their wages will be in the same ratio.
  • Exception: If workers are paid per unit of time and work equally efficiently but for different durations, wages will be proportional to time. However, this is still effectively proportional to work done.
  • Example: A works for 2 days, B for 3 days on a project. If their daily work rate is same, work done ratio is 2:3. For a total wage of $500, A gets \( \frac{2}{5} \times 500 = 200 \), B gets \( \frac{3}{5} \times 500 = 300 \).

📐 Formula Box

Combined Efficiency (LCM Method)

$$ \text{Total Work (Capacity)} = \text{LCM of individual times} $$ $$ \text{Efficiency of Pipe A} = \frac{\text{Total Work}}{\text{Time taken by Pipe A}} $$ $$ \text{Combined Efficiency} = \sum (\text{Efficiency of Inlet Pipes}) - \sum (\text{Efficiency of Outlet Pipes}) $$
  • Meaning: The total capacity of the tank is set as the LCM of the times taken by individual pipes. Efficiency is then calculated as units of work per unit time.
  • Variables: Total Work (units), Time (hours/minutes), Efficiency (units/hour).
  • When to use: Most common and versatile method for pipes and cisterns problems.
  • When NOT to use: If times are given in complex fractions, direct rate calculation might be faster.
  • Memory Trick: "LCM = Tank Capacity; Rate = Capacity / Time."

Time Taken to Fill/Empty

$$ \text{Time taken} = \frac{\text{Total Work (Capacity)}}{\text{Combined Efficiency}} $$
  • Meaning: Once total work and combined efficiency are known, the time taken to complete the task (fill/empty the tank) can be found.
  • Variables: Total Work (units), Combined Efficiency (units/hour), Time (hours).
  • When to use: To find the final answer for how long it takes for a task.
  • When NOT to use: Not applicable if the tank is partially filled and then different conditions apply.
  • Memory Trick: "Time = Total / Net Rate."

Wages Distribution

$$ \text{Share of Person X} = \frac{\text{Work done by X}}{\text{Total Work}} \times \text{Total Wages} $$
  • Meaning: Each person's share of the total wages is directly proportional to the fraction of total work they completed.
  • Variables: Work done by X (units/fraction), Total Work (units/1), Total Wages (currency).
  • When to use: Any problem involving distribution of earnings among workers.
  • When NOT to use: If the question explicitly states wages are proportional to time spent *at a fixed hourly rate*, but even then, that's equivalent to work done.
  • Memory Trick: "Work-Share = Wage-Share."

🧠 Solved Examples

Easy

Example 1: Basic Pipes Filling

Pipe A can fill a tank in 12 hours, and Pipe B can fill the same tank in 15 hours. If both pipes are opened together, how much time will they take to fill the tank?

View Step-by-Step Solution

Step 1: Determine Total Work (Tank Capacity).

Total Work = LCM of (12, 15) = 60 units.

Step 2: Calculate Individual Efficiencies.

  • Efficiency of Pipe A = \( \frac{60}{12} \) = 5 units/hour.
  • Efficiency of Pipe B = \( \frac{60}{15} \) = 4 units/hour.

Step 3: Calculate Combined Efficiency.

Since both are inlet pipes, Combined Efficiency = \( 5 + 4 \) = 9 units/hour.

Step 4: Calculate Time Taken.

Time = \( \frac{\text{Total Work}}{\text{Combined Efficiency}} = \frac{60}{9} = \frac{20}{3} \) hours.

Time taken = \( 6 \frac{2}{3} \) hours or 6 hours 40 minutes.

Medium

Example 2: Pipes with an Outlet

A tank can be filled by an inlet pipe in 8 hours. An outlet pipe can empty the full tank in 12 hours. If both pipes are opened simultaneously, how long will it take to fill the tank?

View Step-by-Step Solution

Step 1: Determine Total Work (Tank Capacity).

Total Work = LCM of (8, 12) = 24 units.

Step 2: Calculate Individual Efficiencies (with signs).

  • Efficiency of Inlet Pipe = \( +\frac{24}{8} \) = +3 units/hour.
  • Efficiency of Outlet Pipe = \( -\frac{24}{12} \) = -2 units/hour.

Step 3: Calculate Combined (Net) Efficiency.

Combined Efficiency = \( 3 + (-2) \) = 1 unit/hour.

Step 4: Calculate Time Taken.

Time = \( \frac{\text{Total Work}}{\text{Combined Efficiency}} = \frac{24}{1} \) = 24 hours.

Hard

Example 3: Pipes with Varying Times & Leak

Two pipes A and B can fill a tank in 20 minutes and 30 minutes respectively. Both pipes are opened, but after 8 minutes, pipe A is closed. At the same time, a leak develops which can empty the full tank in 40 minutes. What is the total time taken to fill the tank?

View Step-by-Step Solution

Step 1: Determine Total Work (Tank Capacity).

Total Work = LCM of (20, 30, 40) = 120 units.

Step 2: Calculate Individual Efficiencies.

  • Efficiency of Pipe A = \( +\frac{120}{20} \) = +6 units/min.
  • Efficiency of Pipe B = \( +\frac{120}{30} \) = +4 units/min.
  • Efficiency of Leak = \( -\frac{120}{40} \) = -3 units/min.

Step 3: Calculate Work Done in the First Phase (Both A & B open).

  • Combined Efficiency (A+B) = \( 6 + 4 \) = 10 units/min.
  • Work done in 8 minutes = \( 10 \times 8 \) = 80 units.
  • Remaining Work = \( 120 - 80 \) = 40 units.

Step 4: Calculate Net Efficiency in the Second Phase (B & Leak open).

  • Pipe A is closed. Pipe B and Leak are open.
  • Net Efficiency (B + Leak) = \( 4 + (-3) \) = 1 unit/min.

Step 5: Calculate Time Taken for Remaining Work.

Time for remaining work = \( \frac{\text{Remaining Work}}{\text{Net Efficiency}} = \frac{40}{1} \) = 40 minutes.

Step 6: Calculate Total Time Taken.

Total Time = Time in Phase 1 + Time in Phase 2 = \( 8 + 40 \) = 48 minutes.

Exam Level

Example 4: Work Sharing (Wages)

A and B together can complete a work in 15 days. B alone can complete the same work in 20 days. If they work together and receive a total of Rs. 1400, find the share of A.

View Step-by-Step Solution

Step 1: Determine Total Work.

Total Work = LCM of (15, 20) = 60 units.

Step 2: Calculate Efficiencies.

  • Combined Efficiency (A+B) = \( \frac{60}{15} \) = 4 units/day.
  • Efficiency of B alone = \( \frac{60}{20} \) = 3 units/day.

Step 3: Calculate Efficiency of A.

Efficiency of A = Combined Efficiency (A+B) - Efficiency of B

Efficiency of A = \( 4 - 3 \) = 1 unit/day.

Step 4: Determine Ratio of Work Done (or Efficiencies).

Since they work together for the entire duration, the work done ratio is proportional to their efficiencies.

  • Ratio of Efficiency A : B = 1 : 3.

Step 5: Distribute Wages based on Ratio.

Total ratio parts = \( 1 + 3 \) = 4 parts.

  • Share of A = \( \frac{\text{Efficiency of A}}{\text{Total Efficiency}} \times \text{Total Wages} = \frac{1}{4} \times 1400 \) = Rs. 350.
  • (Optional) Share of B = \( \frac{3}{4} \times 1400 \) = Rs. 1050.

⚡ Exam Tricks & Shortcuts

Trick 1: Product-Sum/Difference Method (Two Pipes)

If pipe A fills in 'x' hours and pipe B fills in 'y' hours, they together fill in \( \frac{xy}{x+y} \) hours.

If pipe A fills in 'x' hours and pipe B empties in 'y' hours, they together fill in \( \frac{xy}{y-x} \) hours (where \( y > x \)).

  • When to use: For quick calculations when only two pipes are involved.
  • Example: A fills in 10 hrs, B fills in 15 hrs. Together: \( \frac{10 \times 15}{10+15} = \frac{150}{25} = 6 \) hours.
  • Mental Maths: For A=10, B=15, think: \( (10 \times 15) / (10+15) = 150/25 = 6 \).

Trick 2: Proportionality for Wages

When multiple people work together for the *same duration*, their work done is proportional to their individual efficiencies. Thus, wages are distributed in the ratio of their efficiencies.

  • When to use: When total working time is common for all individuals, or when individual work fractions are known.
  • Example: A is twice as efficient as B. They work together for 5 days. Total wage Rs. 600.
    • Efficiency ratio A:B = 2:1.
    • Total parts = 3.
    • A's share = \( \frac{2}{3} \times 600 = 400 \). B's share = \( \frac{1}{3} \times 600 = 200 \).

⚠️ Common Mistakes & Pitfalls

  • Incorrectly Assigning Signs: Forgetting that outlet pipes/leaks perform negative work. Always use `+` for inlet and `-` for outlet.
  • Miscalculating Total Work: Not using the LCM correctly or choosing an arbitrary number that makes calculations harder. Always choose LCM of *all* times involved.
  • Ignoring Partial Work/Time Shifts: Failing to account for situations where pipes are opened/closed after certain intervals. Break the problem into phases.
  • Confusing Efficiency with Time: Remember: more efficient = less time. Efficiency is \( \frac{1}{\text{Time}} \).
  • Incorrect Wage Distribution: Distributing wages based on time taken (if efficiencies are different) instead of work done or efficiencies (when working for same time).
  • Unit Inconsistency: Mixing hours and minutes without proper conversion. Ensure all time units are consistent.

📊 Exam Strategy & Weightage

SSC CGL/CHSL/MTS/CPO/GD

  • Weightage: Moderate (1-2 questions).
  • Pattern: Mix of basic pipe questions, complex scenarios with leaks/varying times, and combined pipe & work-sharing questions.
  • Strategy: Master LCM method. Practice time-segmented problems.

Railway Exams (NTPC, Group D)

  • Weightage: Moderate (1-2 questions).
  • Pattern: Usually straightforward pipe problems, occasionally involving two pipes working alternately or one pipe and a leak.
  • Strategy: Focus on speed and accuracy with LCM method.

Banking Exams (IBPS PO/Clerk, SBI PO/Clerk)

  • Weightage: Low to Moderate (0-1 question, often in Data Interpretation sets).
  • Pattern: Sometimes integrated into DI, requiring understanding of rates and proportions. Standalone questions can be trickier, involving conditions like tank overflow or multiple stages.
  • Strategy: Practice problems with conditional logic and data interpretation.

CDS, CAPF, State PCS, Police Exams

  • Weightage: Moderate (1-2 questions).
  • Pattern: Similar to SSC, but sometimes with slightly more complex word problems or larger numbers.
  • Strategy: Strong conceptual understanding, robust calculation skills, and practice with varied problem types.

📌 Summary

  • Pipes & Cisterns are an extension of Time & Work; tank capacity is total work.
  • Inlet pipes do positive work; outlet pipes/leaks do negative work.
  • Efficiency is key: calculated as \( \frac{\text{Total Work}}{\text{Time}} \).
  • Combined Efficiency is the algebraic sum of individual efficiencies.
  • Time taken = \( \frac{\text{Total Work}}{\text{Net Efficiency}} \).
  • Wages are always distributed in proportion to the work done by each individual.
  • The LCM method is generally the most efficient for solving complex problems.

🚀 Quick Revision Box: Pipes, Cisterns & Wages

  • Analogy: Workers \(\leftrightarrow\) Pipes; Work \(\leftrightarrow\) Tank Capacity.
  • Total Work: Usually LCM of individual times.
  • Inlet Pipe: Fills, \(+\)ve Efficiency.
  • Outlet Pipe/Leak: Empties, \(-\)ve Efficiency.
  • Individual Efficiency: \( E = \frac{\text{Total Work}}{\text{Time}} \).
  • Combined Efficiency: \( E_{\text{net}} = \sum E_{\text{inlet}} - \sum E_{\text{outlet}} \).
  • Time to Fill/Empty: \( T = \frac{\text{Total Work}}{E_{\text{net}}} \).
  • Wages Distribution: \( \text{Wage Share} \propto \text{Work Done} \). If working together for same time, \( \text{Wage Share} \propto \text{Efficiency} \).
  • Shortcuts: For 2 pipes: \( \frac{xy}{x \pm y} \). Use \(+\) for both filling, \(-\) for one filling, one emptying.
  • Critical: Pay attention to signs for inlet/outlet and partial work scenarios.

Key Takeaways

  • Always identify whether a pipe is filling (+) or emptying (-).
  • The LCM method simplifies complex calculations by providing a concrete "total work" value.
  • Break down multi-stage problems into sequential phases.
  • Wages are a direct reflection of individual contributions to the work.
  • Accuracy in basic arithmetic and understanding problem statements are paramount.
