Average Word Problems (Ages & Mixtures)

Medium Difficulty 25-30 Mins Read High Exam Weightage Updated 2026

📘 Introduction: Average Word Problems (Ages & Mixtures)

This microtopic dives into practical applications of averages, specifically in scenarios involving ages of individuals or mixing different quantities/qualities. Mastering these problem types is crucial as they frequently appear in various competitive exams.

  • High Frequency: Averages, including age and mixture problems, are a staple in the quantitative aptitude section.
  • Foundational: Builds problem-solving skills applicable to other topics like Ratio & Proportion, Percentages, and Profit & Loss.
  • Scoring Potential: With the right approach, these problems are straightforward and high-scoring.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Understand and apply the concept of average to age-related problems.
  • Solve problems involving the average age of groups, including inclusion, exclusion, and replacement scenarios.
  • Master the weighted average concept for solving mixture-related problems.
  • Effectively use the Alligation method to find ratios in mixtures.
  • Develop efficient strategies and shortcuts for quick and accurate problem-solving in exam conditions.

📝 Prerequisites

  • Basic Averages: Understanding that Average = (Sum of Quantities) / (Number of Quantities).
  • Basic Algebra: Ability to form and solve linear equations.
  • Percentages: Basic understanding of percentage calculations.
  • Ratio & Proportion: Familiarity with basic ratio concepts, especially for mixture problems.

💡 Core Concepts

Average Age Problems

Definition: Involves calculating the average age of a group of individuals, or finding individual ages/sums based on given averages and changes.

Intuition: The average age represents the age each person would have if the total sum of ages were distributed equally among them.

  • Key Observation 1: The age difference between any two persons remains constant over time.
  • Key Observation 2: If a group's average age increases by 'x' years over 't' years, it means everyone's age has increased by 't' years, and the average itself by 't' years (e.g., average age of 5 people 5 years ago was X, now it's X+5).
  • Key Observation 3: The sum of ages changes by (Number of people \(\times\) Years elapsed) over time.

Memory Tip: "Average is Equal Distribution." Think of age problems as balancing the total age among members.

Mini Example:

The average age of 3 friends is 20 years. What is the sum of their ages?

Solution: Sum = Average \(\times\) Number of friends = \(20 \times 3 = 60\) years.

Average Mixture Problems

Definition: Involves finding the average value (cost, concentration, percentage, etc.) when two or more quantities with different values are combined or mixed.

Intuition: This is a weighted average situation, where the contribution of each component to the overall average is proportional to its quantity in the mixture.

  • Key Observation 1: The average value of the mixture will always lie between the individual average values of its components.
  • Key Observation 2: The total quantity of the mixture is the sum of the individual quantities. The total 'sum of values' (e.g., total cost) is the sum of individual 'sums of values'.
  • Key Observation 3: Often solvable using the Alligation Rule, which finds the ratio in which two components are mixed to achieve a desired average.

Memory Tip: "Mixtures are Weighted Averages." Heavier quantity, heavier influence on the average.

Mini Example:

Mixture A has 10 kg of sugar at ₹50/kg. Mixture B has 20 kg of sugar at ₹40/kg. What is the average cost per kg of the combined mixture?

Solution: Total Cost = \((10 \times 50) + (20 \times 40) = 500 + 800 = ₹1300\). Total Quantity = \(10 + 20 = 30\) kg. Average Cost = \(1300 / 30 = ₹43.33/kg\).

📏 Rules & Properties

Rule 1: Inclusion/Exclusion of a Member

Explanation: When a new member joins or an existing member leaves a group, the average changes based on their value relative to the existing average.

Reason: The total sum of quantities changes, and so does the number of quantities.

Shortcut:

  • New Member Added: Value of new member = Original Average + (Change in Average \(\times\) New Number of Members)
  • Member Removed: Value of removed member = Original Average - (Change in Average \(\times\) New Number of Members)

Exception: If the added/removed member has a value exactly equal to the original average, the average does not change.

Example:

The average weight of 10 students is 40 kg. If a new student joins, the average weight becomes 41 kg. What is the weight of the new student?

Solution: New student's weight = \(40 + (1 \times (10+1)) = 40 + 11 = 51\) kg.

Rule 2: Replacement of a Member

Explanation: When one member leaves and another joins, replacing the first. The number of members remains constant.

Reason: The change in sum is solely due to the difference between the incoming and outgoing member's values.

Shortcut: Value of New Member = Value of Replaced Member \(\pm\) (Change in Average \(\times\) Number of Members)

Use '+' if average increases, '-' if average decreases.

Exception: If the new member's value is the same as the replaced member, the average remains unchanged.

Example:

The average age of 5 boys is 15 years. If one boy aged 12 years is replaced by a new boy, the average age becomes 16 years. What is the age of the new boy?

Solution: New boy's age = \(12 + ((16-15) \times 5) = 12 + (1 \times 5) = 12 + 5 = 17\) years.

Rule 3: Combined Groups (Weighted Average)

Explanation: When two or more groups with different averages and numbers of members are combined.

Reason: The overall average is the sum of all individual values divided by the total number of individuals.

Shortcut: Use the weighted average formula.

Exception: If all groups have the same average, the combined average is also the same.

Example:

Group A has 20 students with an average score of 70. Group B has 30 students with an average score of 80. Find the combined average score.

Solution: Combined Avg = \(\frac{(20 \times 70) + (30 \times 80)}{20+30} = \frac{1400 + 2400}{50} = \frac{3800}{50} = 76\).

📐 Formula Box

Basic Average Formula

$$ \text{Average} = \frac{\text{Sum of Quantities}}{\text{Number of Quantities}} $$

Meaning: The central or typical value of a set of numbers.

Variables:

  • Sum of Quantities: Total value of all items/members.
  • Number of Quantities: Count of items/members.

When to use: To find the average, sum, or number of quantities when two of the three are known. Foundational for all average problems.

When NOT to use: When dealing with ratios or relative changes without absolute sums.

Memory Trick: "SAN" (Sum / Avg = Number, Sum / Number = Avg, Avg * Number = Sum). Remember the triangle: Sum at top, Avg and Number at bottom.

Weighted Average Formula (Combined Groups)

$$ \text{Weighted Average} = \frac{Q_1 A_1 + Q_2 A_2 + \dots + Q_n A_n}{Q_1 + Q_2 + \dots + Q_n} $$

Meaning: An average where some values contribute more than others to the final average.

Variables:

  • \(Q_i\): Quantity/Number of members in group \(i\).
  • \(A_i\): Average value of group \(i\).

When to use: When combining two or more groups with different sizes and averages (e.g., different batches of students, different types of mixtures).

When NOT to use: For simple average calculations where all quantities have equal weight.

Memory Trick: "Sum of (Quantity \(\times\) Average) divided by Total Quantity." Each group's "sum" contributes to the overall sum.

Alligation Rule (for Mixtures of two items)

$$ \frac{\text{Quantity of Cheaper}}{\text{Quantity of Dearer}} = \frac{\text{Dearer Rate} - \text{Mean Rate}}{\text{Mean Rate} - \text{Cheaper Rate}} $$

Meaning: A graphical method to find the ratio in which two ingredients (with different values) must be mixed to obtain a mixture of a desired mean value.

Variables:

  • Cheaper Rate: Value of the lower-priced/less concentrated component.
  • Dearer Rate: Value of the higher-priced/more concentrated component.
  • Mean Rate: Desired average value of the mixture.

When to use: Whenever two items/types are mixed to form a third type, and you need to find the ratio of quantities. E.g., two types of rice, two solutions with different concentrations.

When NOT to use: For combining more than two items directly (can be used iteratively) or for age problems.

Memory Trick: Draw a cross (X). Put Cheaper and Dearer rates at top-left and top-right. Mean rate in the middle. Subtract diagonally. The differences give the ratio of quantities.

🧠 Solved Examples

Easy

Example 1: Basic Age Problem

The average age of A, B, and C is 25 years. If A's age is 20 years and B's age is 30 years, what is C's age?

View Step-by-Step Solution

Step 1: Calculate the total sum of ages.
Average age of 3 people = 25 years.
Sum of ages = Average \(\times\) Number of people = \(25 \times 3 = 75\) years.

Step 2: Use the given individual ages to find the missing age.
Sum of ages of A, B, C = Age of A + Age of B + Age of C
\(75 = 20 + 30 + \text{Age of C}\)
\(75 = 50 + \text{Age of C}\)

Step 3: Solve for C's age.
Age of C = \(75 - 50 = 25\) years.

Answer: C's age is 25 years.

Medium

Example 2: Average Age with Replacement

The average age of 8 teachers in a school is 40 years. A teacher aged 55 years retired, and a new teacher joined. If the average age of the teachers is now 39 years, what is the age of the new teacher?

View Step-by-Step Solution

Method 1: Using Sum of Ages

Step 1: Calculate the initial total age.
Initial Sum = Average \(\times\) Number = \(40 \times 8 = 320\) years.

Step 2: Adjust for the retired teacher.
After retirement, Sum = \(320 - 55 = 265\) years. (Number of teachers is still 7 at this point).

Step 3: Calculate the new total age after the new teacher joins.
New average = 39 years, New number of teachers = 8.
New Sum = \(39 \times 8 = 312\) years.

Step 4: Find the age of the new teacher.
Age of new teacher = New Sum - Sum after retirement = \(312 - 265 = 47\) years.

Method 2: Using Shortcut Rule

Step 1: Identify given values.
Old Average = 40 years, Number of teachers = 8.
Age of replaced teacher = 55 years.
New Average = 39 years.

Step 2: Calculate the change in average.
Change in Average = New Average - Old Average = \(39 - 40 = -1\) year (decrease).

Step 3: Apply the replacement formula.
Age of New Teacher = Age of Replaced Teacher + (Change in Average \(\times\) Number of Members)
Age of New Teacher = \(55 + (-1 \times 8)\)
Age of New Teacher = \(55 - 8 = 47\) years.

Answer: The age of the new teacher is 47 years.

Hard

Example 3: Mixture Problem with Alligation (Exam Level)

In what ratio must a grocer mix two varieties of pulses costing ₹60/kg and ₹85/kg so that by selling the mixture at ₹90/kg, he gains 20%?

View Step-by-Step Solution

Step 1: Find the Cost Price (CP) of the mixture.
The selling price (SP) of the mixture is ₹90/kg, and the gain is 20%.
If SP = ₹90 and Profit = 20%, then SP = CP + 20% of CP = 120% of CP.
\(1.20 \times \text{CP} = 90\)
\(\text{CP} = \frac{90}{1.20} = \frac{900}{12} = 75\) ₹/kg.

Step 2: Apply the Alligation Rule.
We have two varieties: Cheaper (\(C_1\)) = ₹60/kg, Dearer (\(C_2\)) = ₹85/kg.
The Mean Rate (desired average CP of mixture) = ₹75/kg.

        60          85
         \         /
          \       /
           75
          /       \
         /         \
       (85-75)   (75-60)
         10        15

Step 3: Determine the ratio of quantities.
The ratio of Quantity of Cheaper : Quantity of Dearer is \(10 : 15\).

Step 4: Simplify the ratio.
\(10 : 15 = 2 : 3\).

Answer: The grocer must mix the two varieties of pulses in the ratio 2:3.

Exam Level

Example 4: Combined Average Age Problem with Variables

The average age of 40 students in a class is 15 years. If the teacher's age is included, the average age increases by 0.5 years. What is the teacher's age?

View Step-by-Step Solution

Method 1: Using Sum of Ages

Step 1: Calculate the initial total age of students.
Number of students = 40, Average age = 15 years.
Total age of students = \(40 \times 15 = 600\) years.

Step 2: Calculate the new average and number of people.
Teacher's age is included, so new number of people = \(40 + 1 = 41\).
Average age increases by 0.5 years, so new average = \(15 + 0.5 = 15.5\) years.

Step 3: Calculate the new total age (students + teacher).
New total age = \(41 \times 15.5 = 635.5\) years.

Step 4: Find the teacher's age.
Teacher's age = New total age - Total age of students = \(635.5 - 600 = 35.5\) years.

Method 2: Using Shortcut Rule (Inclusion)

Step 1: Identify given values.
Original Average = 15 years.
Change in Average = +0.5 years.
New Number of Members = \(40 + 1 = 41\).

Step 2: Apply the inclusion formula.
Value of New Member = Original Average + (Change in Average \(\times\) New Number of Members)
Teacher's Age = \(15 + (0.5 \times 41)\)
Teacher's Age = \(15 + 20.5\)
Teacher's Age = \(35.5\) years.

Answer: The teacher's age is 35.5 years.

⚡ Exam Tricks & Shortcuts

1. Deviation Method for Averages

Concept: Instead of calculating the full sum, assume a base average and calculate deviations from it. This is excellent for mental math or when numbers are large.

How to:

  1. Assume an average (preferably close to the given numbers).
  2. Calculate the deviation (difference) of each number from the assumed average.
  3. Sum these deviations.
  4. Divide the sum of deviations by the total count.
  5. Add this result to the assumed average to get the actual average.

Example: Find the average of 25, 28, 32, 27, 30.
Assume average = 28.
Deviations: \((25-28)=-3\), \((28-28)=0\), \((32-28)=+4\), \((27-28)=-1\), \((30-28)=+2\).
Sum of deviations = \(-3+0+4-1+2 = 2\).
Actual Average = Assumed Average + (Sum of deviations / Count) = \(28 + (2/5) = 28 + 0.4 = 28.4\).

2. Alligation Method for Weighted Averages

Concept: A powerful visual tool for finding ratios in mixtures, or the mean value when quantities and individual values are known.

How to:

  1. Write the individual values (e.g., C1, C2) on the top left and top right corners.
  2. Write the mean value (M) in the center.
  3. Subtract diagonally: \((C_2 - M)\) for the ratio of the first quantity, and \((M - C_1)\) for the ratio of the second quantity.
  4. The ratio of quantities \(Q_1 : Q_2\) will be \((C_2 - M) : (M - C_1)\).

Example: Two types of rice, ₹30/kg and ₹45/kg, are mixed to get ₹36/kg. Find the ratio.

      30          45
       \         /
        \       /
         36
        /       \
       /         \
     (45-36)   (36-30)
        9         6
Ratio = \(9 : 6 = 3 : 2\).

3. Constant Age Difference (Age Problems)

Concept: The difference in age between any two individuals remains constant throughout their lives.

How to: Use this directly to form equations or to quickly deduce relative ages. If A is X years older than B now, A will be X years older than B 10 years later.

Example: The average age of a husband and wife 5 years ago was 25 years. If their current average age (with a child born 2 years ago) is 20 years, what is the child's current age?
H+W total age 5 years ago = \(25 \times 2 = 50\).
H+W total current age = \(50 + (5 \times 2) = 60\).
H+W+Child total current age = \(20 \times 3 = 60\).
Since (H+W) current age is 60 and (H+W+Child) current age is 60, the child's age must be \(60-60=0\). Wait, this implies child is newborn *now*! This example is trickier. Let's re-evaluate. H+W 5 years ago = 25 avg, Sum = 50. H+W current = 50 + (5*2) = 60. Child born 2 years ago, so child's age is 2. H+W+Child current avg = 20. Total sum = \(20*3 = 60\). This example leads to 0, which is incorrect for a child born 2 years ago. Let me refine this example to make it useful. Refined Example: The average age of a husband and wife 4 years ago was 27 years. A child was born during this period. The present average age of the family (husband, wife, and child) is 21 years. What is the present age of the child?

Solution: H+W total age 4 years ago = \(27 \times 2 = 54\) years.
H+W total present age = \(54 + (4 \times 2) = 54 + 8 = 62\) years.
H+W+Child total present age = \(21 \times 3 = 63\) years.
Child's present age = \(63 - 62 = 1\) year.
This works and highlights how age differences/increments are used.

⚠️ Common Mistakes & Pitfalls

  • Confusing Sum with Average: Always be clear whether the problem refers to the sum of quantities or their average. A common error is using average where sum is needed.
  • Incorrectly Applying "Change in Average": When an item is added/removed, remember to multiply the change in average by the new total number of items, not the original.
  • Ignoring "Weighted" in Mixtures: Failing to account for different quantities (weights) of components in a mixture, leading to a simple average instead of a weighted one.
  • Misinterpreting "X years ago/hence": Remember to adjust all relevant ages (or the sum of ages) by 'X' years per person when moving across time.
  • Selling Price vs. Cost Price in Mixtures: In mixture problems involving profit/loss, always convert the selling price (SP) to the cost price (CP) of the mixture before applying alligation or weighted average.
  • Negative Values in Alligation: Ensure the Mean Rate is always between the two individual rates. If you get a negative difference, you've likely swapped "Dearer - Mean" and "Mean - Cheaper".

📌 Summary

  • Average Age Problems: Based on the concept that total sum of ages is (Average \(\times\) Number of people). Age difference is constant.
  • Inclusion/Exclusion: Value of new/removed member = Original Avg \(\pm\) (Change in Avg \(\times\) New Count).
  • Replacement: Value of New member = Value of Replaced member \(\pm\) (Change in Avg \(\times\) Constant Count).
  • Average Mixture Problems: Use Weighted Average when combining groups of different sizes and averages.
  • Alligation Rule: Efficient method for finding the ratio of two components in a mixture to achieve a desired mean value. Remember to use CP for mixture CP.
  • Practice: Consistent practice with diverse problem types is essential for mastery.

🚀 Quick Revision Cheatsheet: Average Word Problems

  • Basic Average: \(\text{Avg} = \frac{\text{Sum}}{\text{Count}}\)
  • Sum of Quantities: \(\text{Sum} = \text{Avg} \times \text{Count}\)
  • Age Problems:
    • Age difference is constant.
    • If Avg increases by \(x\) over \(t\) years, it's Avg + \(t\).
    • Inclusion: New Value = Old Avg + (\(\Delta\)Avg \(\times\) New Count)
    • Exclusion: Removed Value = Old Avg - (\(\Delta\)Avg \(\times\) New Count)
    • Replacement: New Value = Replaced Value \(\pm\) (\(\Delta\)Avg \(\times\) Old Count)
  • Mixture Problems (Weighted Average): $$ \text{Overall Avg} = \frac{Q_1 A_1 + Q_2 A_2}{Q_1 + Q_2} $$
  • Alligation Rule: For two components (\(C_1, C_2\)) and Mean (\(M\)) $$ \frac{\text{Quantity}_1}{\text{Quantity}_2} = \frac{C_2 - M}{M - C_1} $$ (Remember to use CP for mixture mean rate if profit/loss is involved).
  • Deviation Method: Assume Avg + (Sum of Deviations / Count)

🔑 Key Takeaways

  • Understand that average represents an equal distribution, useful for conceptualizing age problems.
  • Weighted average is crucial when quantities in a mixture are not equal.
  • The Alligation Rule is a powerful visual shortcut for two-component mixtures.
  • Always check if the problem provides SP or CP for mixture problems and convert if necessary.
  • Practice the inclusion/exclusion/replacement rules until they become intuitive for quick problem-solving.
