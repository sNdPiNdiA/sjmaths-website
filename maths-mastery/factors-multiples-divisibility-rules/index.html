📘 1. Introduction

Welcome to one of the most foundational and high-yield chapters in Quantitative Aptitude: Factors, Multiples & Divisibility Rules. Whether you are aiming for SSC CGL, Railways, Banking, or CDS, this topic is the absolute backbone of Number System and Arithmetic.

In competitive exams, direct questions from this topic appear in various formats, such as finding the missing digits in a large number to make it divisible by 72 or 88, calculating the number of odd/even factors of a composite number, or analyzing repeating digit patterns. Beyond direct questions, mastering these concepts will drastically speed up your calculation times, assist you in option elimination, and streamline your approach to LCM/HCF, Simplification, and Word Problems.

🧠 4. Core Concepts

Concept 1: Factors vs Multiples

Definition: If a number $A$ divides another number $B$ completely leaving a remainder of $0$, then $A$ is called a Factor (or Divisor) of $B$, and $B$ is called a Multiple of $A$.

Intuition: Think of factors as the structural building blocks of a number, and multiples as the infinite extensions of that number along the number line.

$$\text{If } \frac{B}{A} = Q \text{ (with Remainder = 0), then } A \text{ is a factor of } B, \text{ and } B \text{ is a multiple of } A.$$
  • Important Observations:
    • The smallest factor of any number is $1$, and the largest factor is the number itself.
    • The number of factors of any finite number is always finite.
    • The smallest multiple of any number is the number itself, and the number of multiples is infinite.
  • Memory Tip: Factors are Few (finite/limited); Multiples are Many (infinite/limitless).
  • Common Mistake: Confusing the two terms. Remember, $6$ is a factor of $12$, but $12$ is a multiple of $6$.
  • Mini Example: Find all the factors of $24$.
    $\text{Factors of } 24 = 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 12, 24$. (Total 8 factors).

Concept 2: Prime Factorization (The DNA of a Number)

Definition: Prime Factorization is the process of breaking down a composite number into a product of its prime numbers.

Intuition: Just as chemical elements make up molecules, prime numbers are the elements that build composite numbers. Prime factorization is the unique chemical formula of a number.

$$\text{Any composite number } N \text{ can be uniquely expressed as: } N = p^a \times q^b \times r^c \dots$$

Where $p, q, r \dots$ are distinct prime numbers and $a, b, c \dots$ are positive integer exponents.

  • Important Observations:
    • This representation is called the Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic.
    • Always ensure the bases ($p, q, r$) are prime numbers before applying any factor formulas.
  • Memory Tip: Never calculate factor properties using a base like $6^2$. Split it into $(2 \times 3)^2 = 2^2 \times 3^2$ first.
  • Common Mistake: Writing $120 = 4 \times 3 \times 10$ and using $4, 3, 10$ as bases. This is incorrect because $4$ and $10$ are composite!
  • Mini Example: Prime factorize $360$.
    $$360 = 36 \times 10 = (6^2) \times (2 \times 5) = (2 \times 3)^2 \times 2 \times 5 = 2^2 \times 3^2 \times 2 \times 5 = 2^3 \times 3^2 \times 5^1$$

Concept 3: Number of Factors (Total, Even, Odd)

Definition: Let the prime factorization of a number be $N = p^a \times q^b \times r^c$. The formulas to compute different types of factors are derived from basic permutations of prime powers.

  • Total Number of Factors $T(N)$: We can choose power of $p$ in $(a+1)$ ways (from $p^0$ to $p^a$), power of $q$ in $(b+1)$ ways, and power of $r$ in $(c+1)$ ways.
    $$T(N) = (a + 1)(b + 1)(c + 1)\dots$$
  • Number of Odd Factors $O(N)$: To get odd factors, we must completely exclude the even prime base ($2$). If $p=2$ is the only even prime:
    $$O(N) = (b + 1)(c + 1)\dots \quad \text{(ignoring the exponent of base 2)}$$
  • Number of Even Factors $E(N)$: To get an even factor, we must select at least one power of prime base $2$ (i.e., we have $a$ choices: $2^1, 2^2, \dots, 2^a$, excluding $2^0$).
    $$E(N) = a \times (b + 1)(c + 1)\dots$$

    Alternatively: $E(N) = T(N) - O(N)$

  • Common Mistake: Adding $1$ to the exponent of $2$ when calculating even factors. Remember: for even factors, we use $a$, not $(a+1)$.
  • Mini Example: Find total, odd, and even factors of $120$.
    Step 1: Prime factorization: $120 = 2^3 \times 3^1 \times 5^1$.
    Step 2: $T(120) = (3+1)(1+1)(1+1) = 4 \times 2 \times 2 = 16$.
    Step 3: $O(120) = (1+1)(1+1) = 4$ (ignoring $2^3$).
    Step 4: $E(120) = 16 - 4 = 12$ (or $3 \times (1+1)(1+1) = 12$).

Concept 4: Sum and Product of Factors

Definition: Quantitative analysis of the summation and multiplication of all positive divisors of $N = p^a \times q^b \times r^c$.

  • Sum of Factors $S(N)$:
    $$S(N) = \left(\frac{p^{a+1} - 1}{p - 1}\right) \times \left(\frac{q^{b+1} - 1}{q - 1}\right) \times \left(\frac{r^{c+1} - 1}{r - 1}\right)\dots$$
  • Product of Factors $P(N)$:
    $$P(N) = N^{\frac{T(N)}{2}}$$

    Where $T(N)$ is the total number of factors of $N$.

  • Important Observation: If $N$ is a perfect square, $T(N)$ is odd. Therefore, the exponent $\frac{T(N)}{2}$ will end in a fraction, but the final product is always an integer because the square root of $N$ is an integer. Let $N = K^2$, then $P(N) = (K^2)^{T(N)/2} = K^{T(N)}$.
  • Mini Example: Find the sum and product of factors of $12$.
    Step 1: Prime factorization: $12 = 2^2 \times 3^1$.
    Step 2: Sum $S(12) = \left(\frac{2^3-1}{2-1}\right) \times \left(\frac{3^2-1}{3-1}\right) = \left(\frac{7}{1}\right) \times \left(\frac{8}{2}\right) = 7 \times 4 = 28$.
    Verification: Factors are $1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 12 \rightarrow 1+2+3+4+6+12 = 28$ (Matches!).
    Step 3: Product $P(12) = 12^{6/2} = 12^3 = 1728$.

📏 5. Rules & Properties of Divisibility

Divisibility rules are direct tools to check if a dividend is completely divisible by a divisor without executing actual long division. Here is the ultimate compilation of essential rules:

1. Divisibility by $2, 4, 8, 16$ (Powers of 2)

Rule:

  • Divisibility by $2$ ($2^1$): Last $1$ digit of the number must be even ($0, 2, 4, 6, 8$).
  • Divisibility by $4$ ($2^2$): Last $2$ digits must be divisible by $4$.
  • Divisibility by $8$ ($2^3$): Last $3$ digits must be divisible by $8$.
  • Divisibility by $16$ ($2^4$): Last $4$ digits must be divisible by $16$.

Reason: Since $10$ is divisible by $2$, $100$ is divisible by $4$, $1000$ by $8$, and so on, any place values higher than the power's range are automatically divisible. Only the trailing digits determine divisibility.

Shortcut: For 8, if the hundreds digit is even, you only need to check if the last 2 digits are divisible by 8. If the hundreds digit is odd, add 4 to the last 2 digits and check if that sum is divisible by 8. (e.g., for $3572$, hundreds digit is 5 (odd), so check $72 + 4 = 76$, not divisible by 8, so $3572$ is not divisible by 8).

Example: Test $789432$ for divisibility by 8.
Hundreds digit is 4 (even). So check last 2 digits: $32$, which is divisible by 8. Thus, $789432$ is divisible by 8!

2. Divisibility by $3$ and $9$

Rule: The sum of all digits of the number must be divisible by $3$ (for divisibility by 3) or $9$ (for divisibility by 9).

Reason: Any decimal number can be written as $100a + 10b + c = (99a + 9b) + (a + b + c)$. Since $99a + 9b$ is always divisible by 3 and 9, divisibility completely depends on the remaining digit sum $(a + b + c)$.

Shortcut ("Digit Cancelling Method"): While adding digits, instantly ignore/cross out 9s or any group of digits that sum to 9 (or 3/6 for divisibility by 3). This is called "casting out nines".

Example: Is $478125$ divisible by 9?
Cancel digits summing to 9: $(4+5) \rightarrow$ cancel; $(8+1) \rightarrow$ cancel; remaining digits are $7$ and $2 \rightarrow 7+2 = 9 \rightarrow$ cancel. All cancelled! Yes, $478125$ is divisible by 9.

3. Divisibility by $5, 25, 125$

Rule:

  • Divisibility by $5$: Last digit must be $0$ or $5$.
  • Divisibility by $25$: Last $2$ digits must be $00, 25, 50, \text{or } 75$.
  • Divisibility by $125$: Last $3$ digits must be divisible by $125$ (e.g., $000, 125, 250, 375, 500, 625, 750, 875$).

4. Divisibility by $11$

Rule: The difference between the sum of digits at odd places and the sum of digits at even places must be either $0$ or a multiple of $11$ (i.e., $0, 11, 22, -11,$ etc.).

Reason: Powers of 10 modulo 11 alternate: $10^0 \equiv 1 \pmod{11}$, $10^1 \equiv -1 \pmod{11}$, $10^2 \equiv 1 \pmod{11}$, and so on.

Shortcut (Pairing Method): Pair adjacent numbers from right to left, subtract left digit from right digit in each pair, and sum these differences. If the total is divisible by 11, the number is too.

Example: Test $849235$ for divisibility by 11.
$\text{Sum of odd places (from right)} = 5 + 2 + 4 = 11$
$\text{Sum of even places (from right)} = 3 + 9 + 8 = 20$
$\text{Difference} = |11 - 20| = 9$. Since 9 is not divisible by 11, $849235$ is not divisible by 11.

5. Combined Rule for $7, 11,$ and $13$ (The Triplet Method)

Rule: For any large number, split the digits into groups of 3 starting from the right (units digit). Alternately add and subtract these 3-digit blocks. If the resulting value is divisible by $7, 11,$ or $13$, then the original number is also divisible by them respectively.

Reason: This works because $7 \times 11 \times 13 = 1001$. Any number of the form $abc,def$ can be written as $abc \times 1000 + def = abc \times (1001 - 1) + def = 1001(abc) - (abc - def)$. Since 1001 is divisible by 7, 11, and 13, divisibility depends entirely on the block difference $(def - abc)$.

Example: Check if $453,782$ is divisible by 13.
Create blocks: Block 1 = $782$, Block 2 = $453$.
Calculate difference: $782 - 453 = 329$.
Test 329 for 13: $\frac{329}{13} \rightarrow 13 \times 25 = 325 \rightarrow \text{Remainder } 4$.
Since 329 is not divisible by 13, $453,782$ is not divisible by 13. (Is it divisible by 7? $\frac{329}{7} = 47$. Yes! So $453,782$ is divisible by 7).

6. Composite Divisibility Rules (The Golden Rule)

Rule: To test divisibility of a composite divisor $D$, break $D$ into two co-prime factors $x$ and $y$ (where $\text{HCF}(x,y) = 1$) such that $D = x \times y$. The number must be divisible by both $x$ and $y$.

Warning Exception: Never break $D$ into non-coprime numbers. For example, to check divisibility by $12$, you must check for $(3 \text{ and } 4)$, NOT $(2 \text{ and } 6)$, because a number like 18 is divisible by both 2 and 6, but is NOT divisible by 12!

Common Composite Splits in Exams:
  • $\text{Divisibility by } 6 \rightarrow \text{Check } 2 \text{ and } 3$
  • $\text{Divisibility by } 72 \rightarrow \text{Check } 8 \text{ and } 9$
  • $\text{Divisibility by } 88 \rightarrow \text{Check } 8 \text{ and } 11$
  • $\text{Divisibility by } 99 \rightarrow \text{Check } 9 \text{ and } 11$

📐 6. Formula Box

Here is your essential formula repository. Bookmark these formulas for active revision!

Standard Form

$$N = p^a \times q^b \times r^c \dots$$

(p, q, r are distinct primes; a, b, c are non-negative exponents)

Factor Calculations

  • Total Factors: $$T(N) = (a + 1)(b + 1)(c + 1)\dots$$
  • Odd Factors (if $p=2$): $$O(N) = (b + 1)(c + 1)\dots$$
  • Even Factors (if $p=2$): $$E(N) = a(b + 1)(c + 1)\dots$$
  • Sum of Factors: $$S(N) = \frac{p^{a+1} - 1}{p - 1} \times \frac{q^{b+1} - 1}{q - 1} \times \frac{r^{c+1} - 1}{r - 1}\dots$$
  • Product of Factors: $$P(N) = N^{\frac{T(N)}{2}}$$

When NOT to use: Do not use these formulas if the base is not fully prime-factorized. For example, for $12^3$, convert it to $(2^2 \times 3)^3 = 2^6 \times 3^3$ first!

Memory Trick: Add "1" to exponents because we also consider the $0$-th power ($p^0 = 1$) for each prime base.

💡 7. Solved Examples

Example 1: Total and Prime Factors calculation

Easy

Question: Find the total number of factors, number of prime factors, and number of unique prime factors of $540$.

View Step-by-Step Solution

Step 1: Perform Prime Factorization of $540$.

$$540 = 54 \times 10 = (2 \times 3^3) \times (2 \times 5) = 2^2 \times 3^3 \times 5^1$$

Here, $p=2, q=3, r=5$ and exponents are $a=2, b=3, c=1$.

Step 2: Find Total Number of Factors.

$$T(540) = (a+1)(b+1)(c+1) = (2+1)(3+1)(1+1) = 3 \times 4 \times 2 = 24$$

Step 3: Find Number of Prime Factors (Total Prime Factors).

This is the sum of the power exponents in the prime factorized form:

$$\text{Total Prime Factors} = a + b + c = 2 + 3 + 1 = 6$$

Step 4: Find Number of Unique Prime Factors.

These are the unique prime bases: $2, 3,$ and $5$.

$$\text{Unique Prime Factors} = 3$$

Final Answer: Total Factors = 24, Prime Factors = 6, Unique Prime Factors = 3.

Example 2: Divisibility by Composite Number (72)

Exam Level

Question: If the 8-digit number $789x531y$ is completely divisible by $72$, find the value of $(5x - 3y)$ for the largest possible value of $y$.

View Step-by-Step Solution

Step 1: Understand the Composite Divisor.

Since $72 = 8 \times 9$, and $\text{HCF}(8,9) = 1$, the number $789x531y$ must be divisible by both $8$ and $9$.

Step 2: Apply Divisibility Rule of 8.

The last 3 digits ($31y$) must be divisible by 8. Let's divide $31y$ by 8:

$$\frac{31y}{8} \rightarrow \frac{310 + y}{8} \rightarrow 310 = 8 \times 38 + 6$$

So, the remainder of $310/8$ is $6$. We need $(6 + y)$ to be a multiple of 8. Since $y$ is a single digit ($0 \le y \le 9$), the only value that fits is $y = 2$ (as $6+2=8$).

Wait! Let's check if there are other values. Next multiple of 8 is 16, which requires $y = 10$ (not a single digit). So the only possible value of $y$ is $2$. Hence, the largest possible value of $y$ is $2$.

Thus, our number becomes: $789x5312$.

Step 3: Apply Divisibility Rule of 9.

The sum of the digits of $789x5312$ must be divisible by 9. Let's use the digit cancelling method:

$$\text{Sum} = 7 + 8 + 9 + x + 5 + 3 + 1 + 2$$

Cancel out 9, and combinations summing to 9:

  • Cancel $9$
  • Cancel $7 + 2 = 9$
  • Cancel $8 + 1 = 9$

Remaining: $x + 5 + 3 = x + 8$.

For $(x + 8)$ to be divisible by 9, $x$ must be $1$ (since $1+8=9$).

Step 4: Calculate the required value.

We have $x = 1$ and $y = 2$.

$$5x - 3y = 5(1) - 3(2) = 5 - 6 = -1$$

Final Answer: -1

Example 3: Sum and Product of Factors

Medium

Question: Find the sum of all even factors and the product of all factors of $360$.

View Step-by-Step Solution

Step 1: Prime Factorize $360$.

$$360 = 2^3 \times 3^2 \times 5^1$$

Step 2: Find Sum of Even Factors.

To ensure all terms are even in the expanded sum of factors, we exclude $2^0$ (which is 1) from the prime base 2 component. The sum formula becomes:

$$S_{\text{even}}(360) = (2^1 + 2^2 + 2^3) \times (3^0 + 3^1 + 3^2) \times (5^0 + 5^1)$$ $$S_{\text{even}}(360) = (2 + 4 + 8) \times (1 + 3 + 9) \times (1 + 5)$$ $$S_{\text{even}}(360) = (14) \times (13) \times (6) = 182 \times 6 = 1092$$

Step 3: Find Product of All Factors.

First, find the total number of factors $T(360)$:

$$T(360) = (3+1)(2+1)(1+1) = 4 \times 3 \times 2 = 24$$

Now, apply the product of factors formula:

$$P(360) = 360^{\frac{T(360)}{2}} = 360^{\frac{24}{2}} = 360^{12}$$

Final Answer: Sum of even factors is 1092; Product of factors is $360^{12}$.

Example 4: Combined Rule of 7, 11, 13 (Pattern-Based)

Hard

Question: Any 6-digit number formed by repeating a 3-digit number (e.g., $abc,abc$) is always divisible by which of the following? (A) 7 only, (B) 11 only, (C) 13 only, (D) 1001.

View Step-by-Step Solution

Step 1: Express the general form of the number algebraically.

Let the 6-digit number be $N = abc,abc$. We can expand this using place value notation:

$$N = a \times 100000 + b \times 10000 + c \times 1000 + a \times 100 + b \times 10 + c$$ $$N = 100000a + 10000b + 1000c + 100a + 10b + c$$

Step 2: Group the common variables.

$$N = a(100000 + 100) + b(10000 + 10) + c(1000 + 1)$$ $$N = a(100100) + b(10010) + c(1001)$$

Step 3: Factor out 1001.

$$N = 1001 \times [100a + 10b + c]$$ $$N = 1001 \times (abc)$$

Step 4: Evaluate the divisor properties of 1001.

The product $7 \times 11 \times 13 = 1001$. This means any number of the form $abc,abc$ is a multiple of $1001$, and because $1001$ is divisible by $7$, $11$, and $13$, the number is also divisible by all three individually.

Therefore, it is always divisible by 1001 (which includes 7, 11, and 13 as factors).

Final Answer: (D) 1001 (and consequently also divisible by 7, 11, and 13).

⚡ 8. Exam Tricks & Shortcuts

💡 Trick 1: Co-prime Factor Split Strategy

If you need to check divisibility of a giant number by $88, 72, 99$ or any composite number, do not use long division. Split them instantly into co-prime pairs and test the smaller digit rules first:

  • For 88, always check 8 (last 3 digits) first. This will instantly narrow down options or solve for variable $y$. Then use 11 to solve for variable $x$.
  • For 72, check 8 first, then 9.

💡 Trick 2: Repeating Digit Shortcut

Memorize these recurring patterns in competitive exams:

  • Any 6-digit repeating number like $aaaaaa$ (e.g., $666666$) is always divisible by $3, 7, 11, 13, 37$.
  • Any 4-digit repeating number like $abab$ (e.g., $2525$) can be written as $ab \times 101$. Hence, it is always divisible by $101$ (the smallest 3-digit prime number).
  • Any 6-digit repeating number like $abcabc$ (e.g., $456456$) can be written as $abc \times 1001$. Hence, it is always divisible by $1001$ (and therefore by $7, 11, 13$).

💡 Trick 3: Digital Root for Divisibility by 9

If you need to find the remainder of a massive number when divided by 9, you don't need to divide. Just find its digital root (the single-digit sum of all digits). The digital root is the remainder! If the digital root is 9, the remainder is 0.

⚠️ 9. Common Mistakes & Pitfalls

1. Using Non-Prime Bases in Factor Formulas

Mistake: Given $N = 6^3 \times 5^2$, calculating total factors as $(3+1)(2+1) = 12$.

Correction: The base $6$ is composite. You must convert it to $(2 \times 3)^3 = 2^3 \times 3^3$. Now, $N = 2^3 \times 3^3 \times 5^2$. Total factors is $(3+1)(3+1)(2+1) = 48$.

2. Incorrect Splitting of Composite Divisors

Mistake: Testing divisibility of a number by $12$ by checking if it is divisible by $2$ and $6$.

Correction: Since $2$ and $6$ are not co-primes ($\text{HCF} = 2$), this test fails (e.g., 18 is divisible by 2 and 6, but not 12). Always split into co-primes like $3$ and $4$ ($\text{HCF} = 1$).

3. Forgetting $1$ and the Number Itself

Mistake: Excluding $1$ or the number itself when counting total factors, or forgetting that $1$ is neither prime nor composite.

Correction: Standard formulas count all positive integer divisors including $1$ and $N$. If the question asks for "proper factors" or "factors other than 1 and itself", adjust the total accordingly.

📌 11. Summary

  • A factor divides a number completely; a multiple is a number multiplied by an integer.
  • Prime Factorization is the foundational step before using any factor properties formulas.
  • Total factors of $N = p^a q^b r^c$ is $(a+1)(b+1)(c+1)$. Odd factors are calculated by ignoring the base 2, and Even factors by using the exponent $a$ of base 2 without adding 1.
  • For divisibility checks of composite numbers, always split the divisor into co-prime numbers.
  • The combined triplet rule for 7, 11, and 13 uses the difference of blocks of 3 digits from right to left.

📌 13. Key Takeaways

  • Factoring Strategy: Always write the final prime factorization base with ascending order of primes: $2 \rightarrow 3 \rightarrow 5 \rightarrow 7 \dots$ to avoid mistakes.
  • Perfect Squares: Have an odd number of factors because their prime exponents are all even, leading to a product of odd terms $(even + 1)$.
  • Composite Rules: Ensure the HCF of the test factors is strictly $1$. Always keep this in mind.
