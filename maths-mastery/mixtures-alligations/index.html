Mixtures & Alligations

Medium Difficulty 25-30 Mins Read High Exam Weightage Updated 2026

📘 1. Introduction: Mixtures & Alligations

Welcome to one of the most powerful and versatile chapters in quantitative aptitude! Mixtures & Alligations is not just a standalone topic; it is an incredibly efficient mathematical tool. While "Mixture" refers to the physical combining of two or more substances, "Alligation" is a highly elegant shortcut method used to calculate the ratio in which ingredients of different values are mixed to achieve a desired average price or concentration.

By mastering the Alligation rule, you can bypass complex algebraic equations and solve multi-step problems from Simple Interest, Profit & Loss, Average, Percentage, and Time & Distance within seconds. In Indian competitive exams like SSC CGL, Bank PO, and Railways, at least 2 to 3 direct or indirect questions rely on this concept.

🎯 2. Learning Objectives

By the end of this module, you will be able to:

  • Understand the difference between weighted average and the Alligation rule.
  • Correctly set up the Alligation cross diagram for various data types (prices, percentages, speeds, interest rates).
  • Determine the mean price or final concentration when two or more mixtures are blended.
  • Solve complex, multi-stage successive dilution/replacement problems using the replacement formula.
  • Identify structural traps in exam questions, such as mixing Cost Price with Selling Price, to avoid negative marking.

📝 3. Prerequisites

To ensure a smooth learning experience, you should have a basic understanding of the following topics:

  • Ratios & Proportions: Basic operations, compounding, and dividing quantities into given ratios (e.g., dividing 100 in the ratio \(3:2\)).
  • Averages: Concept of Simple Average vs. Weighted Average.
  • Percentages: Converting percentages to fractions and vice versa (e.g., \(20\% = \frac{1}{5}\)).

💡 4. Core Concepts

Concept 1: The Principle of Weighted Average

Definition: When two groups of items with different average values are combined, the average of the combined group is called the Weighted Average.

Explanation: If we have \(n_1\) items of value \(A_1\) and \(n_2\) items of value \(A_2\), their combined average (\(A_m\)) is calculated as:

$$A_m = \frac{n_1 A_1 + n_2 A_2}{n_1 + n_2}$$

Intuition: The combined average will always lie strictly between \(A_1\) and \(A_2\). It tilts closer to the group that has a higher quantity.

Memory Tip: "The heavier side pulls the average towards itself." If you mix 9 kg of expensive rice with 1 kg of cheap rice, the final price will be very close to the expensive rice's price.

Common Mistake: Do NOT take a simple average \(\frac{A_1 + A_2}{2}\) unless the quantities mixed are exactly equal (\(n_1 = n_2\)).

Mini Example: 3 kg of sugar costing ₹40/kg is mixed with 2 kg of sugar costing ₹50/kg. Find the average cost of the mixture.

Solution: \(A_m = \frac{(3 \times 40) + (2 \times 50)}{3 + 2} = \frac{120 + 100}{5} = \frac{220}{5} = ₹44/\text{kg}\).

Concept 2: The Alligation Cross Rule

Definition: Alligation is the reverse of weighted average. Instead of finding the average price given the quantities, we use Alligation to find the ratio of quantities given the individual prices and the final mean price.

Mathematical Derivation:

From the weighted average formula:

$$A_m(n_1 + n_2) = n_1 A_1 + n_2 A_2 \implies n_1(A_m - A_1) = n_2(A_2 - A_m)$$

Assuming \(A_2\) (Dearer price, \(D\)) is greater than \(A_1\) (Cheaper price, \(C\)), then the mean price \(M\) must satisfy \(C < M < D\). Rearranging gives:

$$\frac{n_1}{n_2} = \frac{D - M}{M - C}$$

The Cross Diagram:

(Cheaper Value: C)                    (Dearer Value: D)
    \                                  /
      \                              /
         (Mean Value: M)
      /                              \
    /                                  \
(D - M)                                (M - C)

Ratio of Quantities (Cheaper : Dearer) = (D - M) : (M - C)

Memory Tip: Subtraction always goes diagonally downwards, and you must subtract the smaller number from the larger number (values are always positive).

Common Mistake: The values of \(C, D, \text{ and } M\) must be in the same unit. You cannot mix Cost Price (CP) with Selling Price (SP). All three parameters must represent the same metric (e.g., all CP, all SP at same profit%, or all concentration ratios).

Mini Example: In what ratio must wheat at ₹32/kg be mixed with wheat at ₹40/kg to get a mixture worth ₹35/kg?

Solution: Here, \(C = 32\), \(D = 40\), \(M = 35\).
\(D - M = 40 - 35 = 5\)
\(M - C = 35 - 32 = 3\)
Required Ratio = \(5 : 3\).

Concept 3: Successive Replacement (Dilution)

Definition: When a specific quantity \(x\) of pure liquid (like milk) is removed from a container of total volume \(V\) and replaced with an equal quantity of water, and this process is repeated \(n\) times, the final concentration of the original liquid decreases progressively.

The Dilution Formula:

$$\text{Remaining Quantity of Original Liquid} = \text{Initial Quantity} \times \left(1 - \frac{x}{V}\right)^n$$

Where:

  • \(V\) = Total volume of the mixture (which remains constant because we replace what we remove).
  • \(x\) = Volume of liquid removed and replaced in each cycle.
  • \(n\) = Number of times the operation is performed.

Memory Tip: Think of this as compound depreciation. The original liquid is depreciating by a factor of \(\left(1 - \frac{x}{V}\right)\) with each cycle.

Mini Example: A container contains 80 litres of pure milk. 8 litres of milk is replaced with water. This process is repeated one more time (total 2 times). Find the remaining quantity of milk.

Solution: Remaining Milk = \(80 \times \left(1 - \frac{8}{80}\right)^2 = 80 \times \left(\frac{9}{10}\right)^2 = 80 \times \frac{81}{100} = 64.8\text{ litres}\).

📏 5. Rules & Properties

Rule 1: The Unit Standardization Rule

Explanation: To apply the Alligation formula, the variables representing the cheaper source (\(C\)), dearer source (\(D\)), and mean mixture (\(M\)) must belong to the exact same dimension and unit system.

Reason: Mathematically, Alligation is a ratio of quantities. If units do not match, the dimensional homogeneity is violated, yielding incorrect ratios.

Shortcut/Verification: Always verify that \(C < M < D\). If your calculated mean \(M\) is less than \(C\) or greater than \(D\), your math or unit setup is wrong.

Exception: None. This is an absolute physical requirement.

Example: If a merchant mixes tea costing ₹120/kg with tea costing ₹180/kg and sells the mixture at ₹198/kg making a \(20\%\) profit, you cannot use 198 as \(M\). You must first calculate the Cost Price of the mixture: \(CP = \frac{198}{1.20} = ₹165/\text{kg}\). Then apply alligation with \(C = 120, D = 180, M = 165\).

Rule 2: Denominator Rule (What does the ratio represent?)

Explanation: The ratio obtained at the bottom of the Alligation cross diagram always represents the unit of the denominator of the rate/percentage we are comparing.

If you run Alligation on: The resulting ratio is for:
Price per kg (₹/kg) Weight (kg)
Speed (km/hr) Time spent (hours)
Profit/Loss Percentage (\(\frac{\text{Profit}}{\text{CP}} \times 100\)) Cost Price (₹)
Interest Rate (\(\frac{\text{Interest}}{\text{Principal}} \times 100\)) Principal invested (₹)

Shortcut: Remembering this saves you from misinterpreting the outputs in speed-time-distance and profit-loss questions.

📐 6. Formula Box

1. The Standard Alligation Ratio Formula

$$\frac{\text{Quantity of Cheaper } (q_1)}{\text{Quantity of Dearer } (q_2)} = \frac{D - M}{M - C}$$

Variables:

  • \(q_1, q_2\): Quantities of Cheaper and Dearer items respectively.
  • \(C\): Rate/concentration of Cheaper ingredient.
  • \(D\): Rate/concentration of Dearer ingredient.
  • \(M\): Mean rate/concentration of the mixture.

When to use: To find the mixing proportion of two elements when individual and target values are known.

When NOT to use: When there are more than two components being mixed, unless they can be grouped into two categories.

2. Liquid Replacement Formula

$$\frac{\text{Quantity of Pure Liquid Left}}{\text{Total Volume of Mixture}} = \left(1 - \frac{x}{V}\right)^n$$

Variables:

  • \(x\): Volume replaced per cycle.
  • \(V\): Total capacity of the container.
  • \(n\): Number of times the substitution was executed.

Memory Trick: Think of it as: \(\text{Fraction Left} = (\text{Fraction kept after 1 operation})^n\).

🧠 7. Solved Examples

Easy

Example 1: Basic Price-Based Alligation

Question: In what ratio must a grocer mix tea costing ₹60 per kg and ₹65 per kg so that the mixture is worth ₹62 per kg?

View Step-by-Step Solution

Step 1: Identify the values.

  • Cheaper price (\(C\)) = ₹60
  • Dearer price (\(D\)) = ₹65
  • Mean price (\(M\)) = ₹62

Step 2: Apply the Alligation Cross.

$$\text{Ratio} = \frac{D - M}{M - C}$$

$$\text{Ratio} = \frac{65 - 62}{62 - 60} = \frac{3}{2}$$

Answer: The grocer must mix them in the ratio \(3:2\).

Medium

Example 2: Profit & Loss Integration (The CP Trap)

Question: A merchant has 1000 kg of sugar, part of which he sells at \(8\%\) profit and the rest at \(18\%\) profit. He gains \(14\%\) on the whole. Find the quantity sold at \(18\%\) profit.

View Step-by-Step Solution

Step 1: Set up the percentages.

Since profit percentages are defined relative to the Cost Price, applying Alligation on profit % gives the ratio of the cost prices (which is directly proportional to the quantities of sugar sold in each category).

  • Cheaper profit rate (\(C\)) = \(8\%\)
  • Dearer profit rate (\(D\)) = \(18\%\)
  • Mean profit rate (\(M\)) = \(14\%\)

Step 2: Calculate the ratio.

$$\frac{\text{Quantity at } 8\%}{\text{Quantity at } 18\%} = \frac{18 - 14}{14 - 8} = \frac{4}{6} = 2 : 3$$

Step 3: Calculate the actual quantity.

The total quantity is 1000 kg. We split it in the ratio \(2:3\).

$$\text{Quantity at } 18\% = \frac{3}{2+3} \times 1000 = \frac{3}{5} \times 1000 = 600 \text{ kg}$$

Answer: 600 kg of sugar was sold at \(18\%\) profit.

Hard

Example 3: Double Vessel Mixture (Concentration)

Question: Two vessels A and B contain mixtures of milk and water in the ratio \(4:5\) and \(5:1\) respectively. In what ratio should the liquid from both vessels be mixed to get a new mixture containing milk and water in the ratio \(5:4\)?

View Step-by-Step Solution

Step 1: Choose one component to perform Alligation on. Let's pick Milk.

  • Concentration of Milk in Vessel A (\(C\)) = \(\frac{4}{4+5} = \frac{4}{9}\)
  • Concentration of Milk in Vessel B (\(D\)) = \(\frac{5}{5+1} = \frac{5}{6}\)
  • Target Concentration of Milk in Mixture (\(M\)) = \(\frac{5}{5+4} = \frac{5}{9}\)

Step 2: Clear fractions (Optional but highly recommended to avoid mistakes).

Find LCM of denominators (9, 6, 9) = 18.

Multiply each term by 18:

  • \(C' = \frac{4}{9} \times 18 = 8\)
  • \(D' = \frac{5}{6} \times 18 = 15\)
  • \(M' = \frac{5}{9} \times 18 = 10\)

Step 3: Apply Alligation on the simplified integers.

$$\frac{\text{Vessel A}}{\text{Vessel B}} = \frac{15 - 10}{10 - 8} = \frac{5}{2}$$

Answer: The two vessels should be mixed in the ratio \(5:2\).

Exam Level

Example 4: Successive Dilution

Question: A jar is filled with pure liquid containing 40% alcohol. 6 litres of this liquid is drawn off and replaced with water. This process is repeated one more time. If the concentration of alcohol in the jar now is 22.5%, find the capacity of the jar.

View Step-by-Step Solution

Step 1: Set up the successive replacement equation.

Let the total capacity of the jar be \(V\) litres.

Initial concentration of alcohol = \(40\%\)

Final concentration of alcohol = \(22.5\%\)

Number of cycles (\(n\)) = 2

$$\frac{\text{Final Concentration}}{\text{Initial Concentration}} = \left(1 - \frac{x}{V}\right)^n$$

$$\frac{22.5\%}{40\%} = \left(1 - \frac{6}{V}\right)^2$$

Step 2: Simplify the ratio.

$$\frac{22.5}{40} = \frac{225}{400} = \frac{9}{16}$$

$$\frac{9}{16} = \left(1 - \frac{6}{V}\right)^2$$

Step 3: Take the square root of both sides.

$$\frac{3}{4} = 1 - \frac{6}{V}$$

$$\frac{6}{V} = 1 - \frac{3}{4} = \frac{1}{4}$$

$$V = 24 \text{ litres}$$

Answer: The capacity of the jar is 24 litres.

⚡ 8. Exam Tricks & Shortcuts

Trick 1: Fraction LCM Method for Mixtures

When mixing two solutions with fractional concentrations (like milk/water ratios \(5:3\) and \(7:5\)), instead of doing calculations with complex fractions like \(\frac{5}{8}\) and \(\frac{7}{12}\), equalize the denominators first.

For example, if capacities are in ratio \(1:1\), find the LCM of total parts (8 and 12) = 24. Scale up the ratios: \(15:9\) and \(14:10\). Now you can easily add them: Milk = \(15+14=29\), Water = \(9+10=19\). Final ratio = \(29:19\).

Trick 2: Rapid Mean Value Estimation

The mean value \(M\) divides the distance between \(C\) and \(D\) in the inverse ratio of their quantities.

$$M = C + \left(\frac{q_2}{q_1 + q_2} \times (D - C)\right)$$

Use this formula to directly calculate the mean price without drawing the cross diagram, saving valuable seconds during online exams.

⚠️ 9. Common Mistakes & Pitfalls

1. The Selling Price Pitfall

Mistake: Directly using the Selling Price (SP) of the mixture as the Mean value (\(M\)) when ingredients are given in Cost Price (CP).

Correction: Always convert the Selling Price to Cost Price using the profit/loss percentage first before running Alligation.

$$\text{CP} = \frac{\text{SP}}{1 + \text{Profit Fraction}}$$

2. Misinterpreting the Output Ratios

Mistake: Running Alligation on speed (km/hr) and assuming the resulting ratio represents distance.

Correction: The resulting ratio is always of the denominator quantity. For speed (km/hr), the ratio is of time. For profit % (Profit/CP), the ratio is of Cost Price.

📌 11. Summary

  • Weighted Average calculates the single blended mean of multiple groups: \(A_m = \frac{n_1 A_1 + n_2 A_2}{n_1 + n_2}\).
  • Alligation is the reverse process, finding the mixing ratio \(\frac{n_1}{n_2} = \frac{D - M}{M - C}\) given individual values and a target mean.
  • To run alligation correctly, keep all quantities in the same dimensional units.
  • The ratio produced represents the denominator units of the rates mixed.
  • Successive dilution utilizes the power-reduction formula: \(\text{Final Liquid} = \text{Initial Liquid} \times \left(1 - \frac{x}{V}\right)^n\).

✨ 13. Key Takeaways

  • Never apply Alligation directly on Selling Prices if profit percentages are involved; calculate intermediate Cost Prices first.
  • Always find the LCM of denominators when solving multi-vessel fractional mixture problems to keep calculations integer-based.
  • Treat successive dilution problems as compound depreciation problems for speed and accuracy.
