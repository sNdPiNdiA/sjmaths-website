Quadrilaterals (Squares, Rectangles, Parallelograms, Rhombus, Trapezium)

Medium Difficulty 25-30 Mins Read High Exam Weightage Updated 2026

📘 Introduction to Quadrilaterals

Quadrilaterals are fundamental 2D geometric shapes in geometry, forming the basis for many real-world structures and mathematical problems. This microtopic will equip you with a mastery of their definitions, properties, and applications.

  • Definition: A quadrilateral is a polygon with four sides, four vertices, and four angles.
  • Angle Sum Property: The sum of interior angles of any quadrilateral is always \(360^\circ\).
  • Exam Relevance: Quadrilaterals frequently appear in geometry and mensuration sections of exams like SSC CGL, CHSL, Railway NTPC, Banking, CDS, and State PCS. Questions range from basic area/perimeter calculations to complex property-based problems involving diagonals and angles.

🎯 Learning Objectives

Upon completing this microtopic, you will be able to:

  • Define and Classify various types of quadrilaterals (parallelogram, rectangle, square, rhombus, trapezium, kite).
  • Identify and Apply the unique properties of sides, angles, and diagonals for each quadrilateral.
  • Calculate area, perimeter, and diagonal lengths using appropriate formulas.
  • Solve geometry problems involving quadrilaterals, including those based on their properties and theorems.
  • Differentiate between special quadrilaterals and understand their hierarchical relationships.

📝 Prerequisites

Before diving into quadrilaterals, ensure you have a solid understanding of these foundational concepts:

  • Basic Geometry: Points, lines, line segments, rays, angles (types, linear pair, vertically opposite angles).
  • Parallel Lines: Transversals, corresponding angles, alternate interior/exterior angles, consecutive interior angles.
  • Triangles: Types of triangles, angle sum property, congruence and similarity criteria, Pythagorean theorem.
  • Basic Algebra: Solving linear equations and systems of equations.
  • Perimeter and Area: Fundamental understanding of these concepts for 2D shapes.

💡 Core Concepts: Types of Quadrilaterals

General Quadrilateral

Definition: Any polygon with four sides. It has four vertices and four interior angles.

Intuition: Think of an irregularly shaped four-sided figure, like a random piece of paper with four corners.

Key Observations:

  • Sum of interior angles is \(360^\circ\).
  • Can be convex (all interior angles \( < 180^\circ \)) or concave (at least one interior angle \( > 180^\circ \)). We focus on convex.

Memory Tip: "Quad" means four, so any "four-sided" shape is a quadrilateral.

Mini Example: A figure with vertices A, B, C, D and sides AB, BC, CD, DA is a quadrilateral ABCD.

Parallelogram

Definition: A quadrilateral with both pairs of opposite sides parallel.

Intuition: Imagine pushing a rectangle from one side; it slants but keeps opposite sides parallel.

Key Observations:

  • Opposite sides are equal in length (\(AB = CD\), \(AD = BC\)).
  • Opposite angles are equal (\(\angle A = \angle C\), \(\angle B = \angle D\)).
  • Consecutive angles are supplementary (\(\angle A + \angle B = 180^\circ\)).
  • Diagonals bisect each other (they cut each other into two equal parts at their intersection point).

Memory Tip: "Parallel-o-gram" emphasizes the parallel sides.

Mini Example: If in parallelogram ABCD, \(AB = 5\) cm, then \(CD = 5\) cm. If \(\angle A = 70^\circ\), then \(\angle C = 70^\circ\) and \(\angle B = 110^\circ\).

Rhombus

Definition: A parallelogram with all four sides equal in length.

Intuition: A "diamond" shape, or a square that has been "pushed over" at an angle.

Key Observations:

  • All properties of a parallelogram apply.
  • All four sides are equal.
  • Diagonals bisect each other at right angles (\(90^\circ\)).
  • Diagonals bisect the angles at the vertices they connect.

Memory Tip: Think "R-hombus = R-ight angles for diagonals".

Mini Example: If a rhombus has a side length of \(6\) cm, its perimeter is \(24\) cm. Its diagonals will intersect at \(90^\circ\).

Rectangle

Definition: A parallelogram with all four angles equal to \(90^\circ\).

Intuition: A standard door, window, or television screen.

Key Observations:

  • All properties of a parallelogram apply.
  • All four angles are \(90^\circ\).
  • Diagonals are equal in length.

Memory Tip: "Rect" implies "right" (angles).

Mini Example: If a rectangle has length \(l=8\) cm and width \(w=6\) cm, its diagonals are equal and can be found using Pythagorean theorem: \(\sqrt{8^2 + 6^2} = 10\) cm.

Square

Definition: A rectangle with all four sides equal, OR a rhombus with all four angles equal to \(90^\circ\).

Intuition: The most "perfect" quadrilateral – perfectly balanced sides and angles.

Key Observations:

  • All properties of a parallelogram, rhombus, and rectangle apply.
  • All four sides are equal.
  • All four angles are \(90^\circ\).
  • Diagonals are equal in length.
  • Diagonals bisect each other at right angles (\(90^\circ\)).
  • Diagonals bisect the angles (creating \(45^\circ\) angles).

Memory Tip: A square is "super" because it has all the best properties!

Mini Example: A square with side \(5\) cm has a diagonal of \(5\sqrt{2}\) cm. Its diagonals intersect at \(90^\circ\) and are equal.

Trapezium (or Trapezoid)

Definition: A quadrilateral with exactly one pair of parallel sides.

Intuition: Think of a table with two parallel legs and two non-parallel slanted legs, or a truncated cone/pyramid face.

Key Observations:

  • The parallel sides are called bases, and the non-parallel sides are called legs.
  • Consecutive angles between parallel sides are supplementary.
  • Median (Midsegment) Theorem: The line segment connecting the midpoints of the non-parallel sides is parallel to the bases and its length is half the sum of the lengths of the bases.

Memory Tip: "Trap" has one "pair" of parallel sides.

Mini Example: If parallel sides are \(a=10\) and \(b=14\), the median is \((10+14)/2 = 12\).

Isosceles Trapezium

Definition: A trapezium where the non-parallel sides (legs) are equal in length.

Intuition: A symmetrical trapezium, like a bridge's cross-section.

Key Observations:

  • All properties of a general trapezium apply.
  • Non-parallel sides are equal.
  • Base angles are equal (angles on the same parallel base are equal).
  • Diagonals are equal in length.

Memory Tip: "Isosceles" always means "equal sides" (here, non-parallel ones).

Mini Example: An isosceles trapezium with bases 6cm and 10cm and legs 5cm will have equal diagonals.

Kite

Definition: A quadrilateral where two distinct pairs of adjacent sides are equal.

Intuition: A traditional flying kite shape.

Key Observations:

  • Two distinct pairs of adjacent sides are equal.
  • One pair of opposite angles are equal (the angles between the unequal sides).
  • Diagonals are perpendicular to each other.
  • One diagonal bisects the other diagonal (the one connecting the vertices where unequal sides meet).
  • One diagonal bisects the pair of opposite angles (the one that is the axis of symmetry).

Memory Tip: Kites fly, and they have "knife-like" diagonal intersection (\(90^\circ\)).

Mini Example: If a kite has sides 3, 3, 5, 5, then its diagonals intersect at \(90^\circ\).

📏 Rules & Properties

Angle Sum Property of Quadrilaterals

Explanation: The sum of the interior angles of any convex quadrilateral is always \(360^\circ\).

Reason: Any quadrilateral can be divided into two triangles by drawing one diagonal. Since each triangle's angles sum to \(180^\circ\), two triangles sum to \(180^\circ + 180^\circ = 360^\circ\).

Shortcut: If three angles are known, the fourth is \(360^\circ\) minus the sum of the three.

Exception: None for convex quadrilaterals.

Example: In quadrilateral ABCD, if \(\angle A = 80^\circ\), \(\angle B = 100^\circ\), \(\angle C = 70^\circ\), then \(\angle D = 360^\circ - (80^\circ + 100^\circ + 70^\circ) = 360^\circ - 250^\circ = 110^\circ\).

Varignon's Theorem (Midpoint Quadrilateral)

Explanation: The figure formed by joining the midpoints of the sides of any quadrilateral, in order, is a parallelogram.

Reason: This follows directly from the Midpoint Theorem for triangles (a line segment joining midpoints of two sides of a triangle is parallel to the third side and half its length). By applying this theorem to triangles formed by the diagonals of the original quadrilateral, we can show that opposite sides of the inner figure are parallel.

Shortcut: The perimeter of the Varignon parallelogram is equal to the sum of the lengths of the diagonals of the original quadrilateral. Its area is half the area of the original quadrilateral.

Exception: None.

Example: If P, Q, R, S are midpoints of sides AB, BC, CD, DA respectively of quadrilateral ABCD, then PQRS is a parallelogram. If AC = 10 and BD = 12, then perimeter of PQRS = 10 + 12 = 22.

Relationship Hierarchy of Quadrilaterals

Explanation: Special quadrilaterals inherit properties from more general types. This forms a hierarchy.

Reason: A square is a rectangle with equal sides, and it's also a rhombus with right angles. Both rectangles and rhombuses are parallelograms. Therefore, a square possesses all properties of parallelograms, rectangles, and rhombuses.

Shortcut: Memorize the flow: Quadrilateral \(\rightarrow\) Trapezium (1 pair parallel) OR Parallelogram (2 pairs parallel). Then Parallelogram \(\rightarrow\) Rhombus (equal sides) OR Rectangle (right angles). Finally, Rhombus AND Rectangle \(\rightarrow\) Square.

Exception: A trapezium is NOT a parallelogram. A parallelogram is NOT a trapezium. (Unless you define trapezium as "at least one pair of parallel sides", in which case a parallelogram *is* a trapezium. For exams, usually, "exactly one pair" is implied.)

Example: Every square is a rectangle, but not every rectangle is a square. Every rhombus is a parallelogram, but not every parallelogram is a rhombus.

📐 Formula Box

General Quadrilateral

$$ \text{Perimeter} = a + b + c + d $$
  • Meaning: The sum of the lengths of its four sides.
  • Variables: \(a, b, c, d\) are the lengths of the four sides.
  • When to use: To find the total boundary length.
  • Memory Trick: "Add all the outside edges!"

Parallelogram

$$ \text{Area} = \text{base} \times \text{height} = b \times h $$ $$ \text{Perimeter} = 2(a+b) $$
  • Meaning: Area is the product of one side (base) and the perpendicular distance to the opposite side (height). Perimeter is twice the sum of adjacent sides.
  • Variables: \(b\) = base length, \(h\) = perpendicular height, \(a, b\) = lengths of adjacent sides.
  • When to use: For calculating the space enclosed or boundary length.
  • Memory Trick: Area is like a "pushed over" rectangle; its height is still perpendicular.

Rhombus

$$ \text{Area} = \frac{1}{2} d_1 d_2 $$ $$ \text{Area} = \text{side} \times \text{height} = a \times h $$ $$ \text{Perimeter} = 4a $$
  • Meaning: Area can be half the product of its diagonals OR side times perpendicular height. Perimeter is four times the side length.
  • Variables: \(d_1, d_2\) = lengths of diagonals, \(a\) = side length, \(h\) = perpendicular height.
  • When to use: Diagonal formula is common. Side \(\times\) height is also valid.
  • Memory Trick: Rhombus diagonals cross at \(90^\circ\) - like 2 triangles with common base \(d_1\), height \(\frac{d_2}{2}\). Perimeter is like a square.

Rectangle

$$ \text{Area} = \text{length} \times \text{width} = l \times w $$ $$ \text{Perimeter} = 2(l+w) $$ $$ \text{Diagonal} = \sqrt{l^2 + w^2} $$
  • Meaning: Area is product of length and width. Perimeter is twice the sum of length and width. Diagonal is found by Pythagorean theorem.
  • Variables: \(l\) = length, \(w\) = width.
  • When to use: Basic geometric calculations.
  • Memory Trick: Common sense formulas, diagonal is hypotenuse of a right triangle.

Square

$$ \text{Area} = \text{side}^2 = a^2 $$ $$ \text{Area} = \frac{1}{2} d^2 $$ $$ \text{Perimeter} = 4a $$ $$ \text{Diagonal} = a\sqrt{2} $$
  • Meaning: Area is side squared OR half the diagonal squared. Perimeter is four times the side. Diagonal is side times \(\sqrt{2}\).
  • Variables: \(a\) = side length, \(d\) = diagonal length.
  • When to use: For squares, choose the area formula based on given information (side or diagonal).
  • Memory Trick: Square is a special rhombus/rectangle, so its formulas simplify. \(d = a\sqrt{2}\) is from Pythagoras.

Trapezium

$$ \text{Area} = \frac{1}{2} (a+b) h $$ $$ \text{Median Length} = \frac{a+b}{2} $$
  • Meaning: Area is half the sum of parallel bases times height. Median is the average of parallel bases.
  • Variables: \(a, b\) = lengths of parallel bases, \(h\) = perpendicular height between bases.
  • When to use: Area and median calculations for trapeziums.
  • Memory Trick: Area is (average base) \(\times\) height. Median is average base.

Kite

$$ \text{Area} = \frac{1}{2} d_1 d_2 $$
  • Meaning: Area is half the product of its diagonals.
  • Variables: \(d_1, d_2\) = lengths of diagonals.
  • When to use: When diagonal lengths are known.
  • Memory Trick: Same as rhombus area, as both have perpendicular diagonals.

🧠 Solved Examples

Easy

Example 1: Basic Rectangle Area

A rectangular field has a length of 15 meters and a width of 8 meters. What is its area?

View Step-by-Step Solution

Step 1: Identify the shape and given values.
The shape is a rectangle. Given: Length \((l) = 15\) m, Width \((w) = 8\) m.

Step 2: Recall the formula for the area of a rectangle.
Area \(= l \times w\)

Step 3: Substitute the values and calculate.
Area \(= 15 \text{ m} \times 8 \text{ m} = 120 \text{ m}^2\)

Final Answer: The area of the rectangular field is \(120 \text{ m}^2\).

Medium

Example 2: Rhombus Diagonals

The diagonals of a rhombus are 16 cm and 12 cm. Find its area and the length of its side.

View Step-by-Step Solution

Step 1: Identify the shape and given values.
The shape is a rhombus. Given: \(d_1 = 16\) cm, \(d_2 = 12\) cm.

Step 2: Calculate the Area.
Formula: Area \(= \frac{1}{2} d_1 d_2\)
Area \(= \frac{1}{2} \times 16 \text{ cm} \times 12 \text{ cm} = 8 \times 12 = 96 \text{ cm}^2\)

Step 3: Find the side length using diagonal properties.
Diagonals of a rhombus bisect each other at \(90^\circ\). This forms four congruent right-angled triangles.
Half of \(d_1 = 16/2 = 8\) cm.
Half of \(d_2 = 12/2 = 6\) cm.
The side of the rhombus is the hypotenuse of one of these right-angled triangles.

Step 4: Apply the Pythagorean Theorem.
\(\text{side}^2 = (\text{half } d_1)^2 + (\text{half } d_2)^2\)
\(\text{side}^2 = 8^2 + 6^2 = 64 + 36 = 100\)
\(\text{side} = \sqrt{100} = 10 \text{ cm}\)

Final Answer: The area of the rhombus is \(96 \text{ cm}^2\) and the length of its side is \(10 \text{ cm}\).

Hard

Example 3: Parallelogram Angles

In a parallelogram ABCD, \(\angle A = (3x + 10)^\circ\) and \(\angle B = (2x + 50)^\circ\). Find the measures of all angles of the parallelogram.

View Step-by-Step Solution

Step 1: Recall properties of a parallelogram.
In a parallelogram, consecutive angles are supplementary (add up to \(180^\circ\)).

Step 2: Set up the equation.
\(\angle A + \angle B = 180^\circ\)
\((3x + 10)^\circ + (2x + 50)^\circ = 180^\circ\)

Step 3: Solve for \(x\).
\(5x + 60 = 180\)
\(5x = 180 - 60\)
\(5x = 120\)
\(x = 120 / 5 = 24\)

Step 4: Calculate the measures of \(\angle A\) and \(\angle B\).
\(\angle A = (3x + 10)^\circ = (3 \times 24 + 10)^\circ = (72 + 10)^\circ = 82^\circ\)
\(\angle B = (2x + 50)^\circ = (2 \times 24 + 50)^\circ = (48 + 50)^\circ = 98^\circ\)

Step 5: Find the remaining angles using parallelogram properties.
Opposite angles are equal:
\(\angle C = \angle A = 82^\circ\)
\(\angle D = \angle B = 98^\circ\)

Step 6: Verify the angle sum.
\(82^\circ + 98^\circ + 82^\circ + 98^\circ = 360^\circ\).

Final Answer: The angles are \(\angle A = 82^\circ\), \(\angle B = 98^\circ\), \(\angle C = 82^\circ\), \(\angle D = 98^\circ\).

Exam Level (SSC CGL Tier-II)

Example 4: Trapezium Area and Height

The parallel sides of a trapezium are 25 cm and 11 cm, while the non-parallel sides are 15 cm and 13 cm. Find the area of the trapezium.

View Step-by-Step Solution

Step 1: Visualize and draw the trapezium.
Let the trapezium be ABCD, with AB parallel to CD. Let \(AB = 25\) cm and \(CD = 11\) cm. Non-parallel sides are \(AD = 13\) cm and \(BC = 15\) cm. We need to find the height \(h\).

Step 2: Construct a parallelogram to find height.
Draw a line CE parallel to AD from C to AB. This creates a parallelogram AECD. So, \(AE = CD = 11\) cm and \(CE = AD = 13\) cm.
Now, \(EB = AB - AE = 25 - 11 = 14\) cm.

Step 3: Use Heron's formula for triangle CEB to find its area.
Triangle CEB has sides \(13\) cm, \(15\) cm, and \(14\) cm.
Semi-perimeter \(s = (13 + 15 + 14) / 2 = 42 / 2 = 21\) cm.
Area of \(\triangle CEB = \sqrt{s(s-a)(s-b)(s-c)}\)
Area \(= \sqrt{21(21-13)(21-15)(21-14)}\)
Area \(= \sqrt{21 \times 8 \times 6 \times 7}\)
Area \(= \sqrt{(3 \times 7) \times (2^3) \times (2 \times 3) \times 7}\)
Area \(= \sqrt{2^4 \times 3^2 \times 7^2} = 2^2 \times 3 \times 7 = 4 \times 21 = 84 \text{ cm}^2\).

Step 4: Use triangle area to find the height \(h\).
The height of \(\triangle CEB\) with base EB is the height of the trapezium.
Area of \(\triangle CEB = \frac{1}{2} \times \text{base EB} \times h\)
\(84 = \frac{1}{2} \times 14 \times h\)
\(84 = 7h\)
\(h = 84 / 7 = 12 \text{ cm}\).

Step 5: Calculate the area of the trapezium.
Formula: Area \(= \frac{1}{2} (a+b) h\)
Area \(= \frac{1}{2} (25 + 11) \times 12\)
Area \(= \frac{1}{2} (36) \times 12\)
Area \(= 18 \times 12 = 216 \text{ cm}^2\).

Final Answer: The area of the trapezium is \(216 \text{ cm}^2\).

⚡ Exam Tricks & Shortcuts

Midpoint Quadrilateral Area

  • Trick: If P, Q, R, S are midpoints of sides of a quadrilateral ABCD, the area of parallelogram PQRS is exactly half the area of quadrilateral ABCD.
  • When to Use: Problems involving midpoints and areas.
  • Example: A quadrilateral has an area of \(60 \text{ cm}^2\). The midpoints of its sides are joined to form a new quadrilateral. The area of the new quadrilateral is \(30 \text{ cm}^2\).

Pythagorean Triplets for Diagonals

  • Trick: For rhombuses and rectangles, diagonal calculations often involve right-angled triangles. Recognize common Pythagorean triplets (3,4,5), (5,12,13), (8,15,17), (7,24,25) to quickly find side lengths or diagonals.
  • When to Use: Speeding up calculations in rhombus side length (using half diagonals) or rectangle diagonal length (using length/width).
  • Example: Rhombus diagonals 16 and 12. Half diagonals are 8 and 6. This is (6,8,10) triplet, so side is 10. (From Example 2)

Special Angle Properties for Parallelograms/Rhombus

  • Trick: If a diagonal of a parallelogram bisects an angle, then it's a rhombus. If a parallelogram has one right angle, it's a rectangle.
  • When to Use: Identifying the specific type of parallelogram from angle/diagonal information, which helps apply more specific formulas/properties.
  • Example: If a problem states "diagonal AC bisects \(\angle A\) in parallelogram ABCD", immediately infer it's a rhombus.

⚠️ Common Mistakes & Pitfalls

  • Confusing Rhombus and Square Properties: A square has all rhombus properties PLUS right angles and equal diagonals. A rhombus only guarantees equal sides and perpendicular diagonals, not necessarily equal diagonals or right angles.
  • Mixing up Trapezium and Parallelogram: A trapezium has EXACTLY one pair of parallel sides. A parallelogram has TWO pairs of parallel sides. They are distinct categories in many exam contexts.
  • Incorrectly Using Height: Always ensure the height used in area formulas (parallelogram, trapezium) is the PERPENDICULAR distance between the parallel lines or base and opposite side, not a slanted side length.
  • Forgetting to Halve Diagonals: When using Pythagorean theorem for rhombus sides, remember to use half the diagonal lengths as the legs of the right triangles, not the full diagonal lengths.
  • Ignoring the "Isosceles" Condition: For an isosceles trapezium, remember that non-parallel sides are equal, base angles are equal, and diagonals are equal. These extra properties are crucial for solving problems.

📊 Exam Strategy & Weightage

SSC CGL/CHSL/MTS/CPO/GD

  • Weightage: Moderate to High. Expect 1-2 questions directly from quadrilaterals in geometry or mensuration sections.
  • Question Pattern:
    • Direct application of area/perimeter formulas.
    • Property-based questions involving angles, diagonals, midpoints.
    • Finding unknown side lengths/angles given certain conditions.
    • Combination problems with triangles or circles.
  • Strategy: Master all properties and formulas. Practice identifying quadrilateral types quickly. Focus on properties of diagonals for squares, rhombuses, and rectangles.

Railway (NTPC/Group D)

  • Weightage: Low to Moderate. Basic questions usually.
  • Question Pattern:
    • Primarily basic area and perimeter calculations.
    • Simple questions on angle properties of parallelograms.
  • Strategy: Strong grasp of fundamental formulas and definitions is sufficient. Don't overcomplicate with advanced theorems.

Banking (PO/Clerk)

  • Weightage: Low. Primarily in Data Interpretation (DI) or Mensuration-based word problems.
  • Question Pattern:
    • Area/perimeter calculations as part of larger word problems.
    • Comparison of areas/perimeters of different shapes.
  • Strategy: Focus on quick and accurate formula application. Time management is key.

CDS/CAPF/State PCS/Police

  • Weightage: Moderate to High. More conceptual and multi-step problems.
  • Question Pattern:
    • Proof-based or reasoning-based questions (indirectly tested).
    • Application of Varignon's theorem.
    • Problems combining quadrilaterals with other geometric figures.
    • Coordinate geometry applications to quadrilaterals.
  • Strategy: Deep understanding of properties and theorems is essential. Practice a wide variety of problem types, including those requiring constructions.

📌 Summary

  • A quadrilateral is any 4-sided polygon, with interior angles summing to \(360^\circ\).
  • Parallelograms have two pairs of parallel and equal sides, equal opposite angles, and bisecting diagonals.
  • Rhombus is a parallelogram with all sides equal; diagonals are perpendicular bisectors and bisect angles.
  • Rectangle is a parallelogram with all angles \(90^\circ\); diagonals are equal.
  • Square has all properties of rhombus and rectangle (equal sides, equal \(90^\circ\) angles, equal and perpendicular bisecting diagonals).
  • Trapezium has exactly one pair of parallel sides (bases). Its median connects midpoints of non-parallel sides.
  • Isosceles Trapezium has equal non-parallel sides, equal base angles, and equal diagonals.
  • Kite has two pairs of equal adjacent sides; diagonals are perpendicular, and one diagonal bisects the other and the angles.
  • Varignon's Theorem: Midpoints of any quadrilateral form a parallelogram with half the area.

🚀 Quick Revision Cheat Sheet: Quadrilaterals

  • Quadrilateral: 4 sides, \(\sum \text{angles} = 360^\circ\).
  • Parallelogram (P): Opp. sides || & =, Opp. angles =, Diagonals bisect.
    • Area = \(b \times h\), Perimeter = \(2(a+b)\).
  • Rhombus (R): P + All sides =. Diagonals \(\perp\) bisect & angle bisectors.

  • Rectangle (Rec): P + All angles \(90^\circ\). Diagonals =.
    • Area = \(l \times w\), Perimeter = \(2(l+w)\), Diagonal = \(\sqrt{l^2+w^2}\).
  • Square (S): R + Rec. All sides =, All angles \(90^\circ\). Diagonals = & \(\perp\) bisect & angle bisectors.
    • Area = \(a^2\), Area = \(\frac{1}{2} d^2\), Perimeter = \(4a\), Diagonal = \(a\sqrt{2}\).
  • Trapezium (T): Exactly 1 pair || sides (bases \(a, b\)).
    • Area = \(\frac{1}{2}(a+b)h\), Median = \(\frac{a+b}{2}\).
    • Isosceles T: Non-|| sides =, Base angles =, Diagonals =.
  • Kite: 2 distinct pairs adjacent sides =. Diagonals \(\perp\), one bisects other. One pair opp. angles =.
    • Area = \(\frac{1}{2} d_1 d_2\).
  • Varignon's Thm: Midpoints of any quad form a parallelogram. Area(midpoint quad) = \(\frac{1}{2}\) Area(original quad). Perimeter(midpoint quad) = Sum of diagonals(original quad).

✅ Key Takeaways

  • Hierarchy Matters: Understand that squares are special rectangles and special rhombuses, inheriting all their properties.
  • Diagonals are Key: Many quadrilateral problems revolve around properties of diagonals (length, bisection, perpendicularity).
  • Visualise: Always draw the figure for complex problems. It helps in applying the correct properties and identifying hidden triangles.
  • Heron's Formula: It's a powerful tool for finding the area of triangles, especially useful in finding heights within trapeziums or general quadrilaterals.
  • Practice is Paramount: Consistent practice with diverse problem types solidifies understanding and improves speed.
