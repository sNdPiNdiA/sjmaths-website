Remainders & Unit/Last Digit Patterns

Medium Difficulty 25-30 Mins Read High Exam Weightage Updated 2026

📘 1. Introduction

In competitive exams, computing large arithmetic expressions directly is practically impossible. This is where Remainders and Unit/Last Digit Patterns come into play. These concepts allow us to analyze properties of massive numbers—like \(7^{2024}\) or \(987654 \times 12345\)—in a matter of seconds.

Whether you are calculating the last digit of a high-power exponent or finding the remainder of a complex product, these techniques bypass manual computation using modular arithmetic, cyclicity, and classical number theorems. Mastery over this microtopic is a potent tool for option-elimination and direct problem-solving in all major government exams.

🎯 Exam Relevance:
  • SSC CGL (Prelims & Mains): 1 to 2 direct questions, highly integrated with algebraic simplification.
  • CDS & CAPF: Frequent conceptual questions testing advanced remainder theorems (Fermat's, Euler's, Wilson's).
  • Banking & Insurance: Essential for speed math and Unit Digit simplification checks in Quantitative Aptitude.
  • Railways & State Police Exams: Direct, formula-based questions on finding unit digits and simple remainders.

🎯 2. Learning Objectives

  • Understand the Mathematical Division Algorithm and compute both positive and negative remainders.
  • Identify the cyclic patterns of unit digits for all digits from \(0\) to \(9\).
  • Apply advanced remainder theorems (Fermat's, Euler's, and Wilson's) to solve complex exponential remainder problems.
  • Determine the last two digits of any given large power or product using binomial expansions and modular arithmetic.
  • Employ option-elimination strategies using unit digits to maximize speed in calculation-intensive exam papers.

📝 3. Prerequisites

Before diving into this topic, ensure you are comfortable with the following foundational concepts:

Basic Division: Understanding dividend, divisor, quotient, and remainder.
Indices and Powers: Rules of exponents such as \((a^m)^n = a^{m \times n}\) and \(a^m \times a^n = a^{m+n}\).
Prime Numbers: Identifying prime numbers and calculating co-primality (numbers sharing no common factors other than \(1\)).

💡 4. Core Concepts

Concept 1: The Remainder Concept & Negative Remainders

Definition: When a positive integer \(A\) (dividend) is divided by another positive integer \(D\) (divisor), it leaves a remainder \(R\) such that:

$$A = D \times Q + R \quad \text{where } 0 \le R < D$$

Intuition: A remainder is simply the "leftover" value that cannot be grouped into equal parts of size \(D\). While mathematical remainders are strictly positive, we use negative remainders to make mental calculations faster. A negative remainder represents how much the dividend falls short of being a perfect multiple of the divisor.

Key Observations:
  • If \(17 \div 6\): The nearest lower multiple is \(12\) (leaving a positive remainder of \(+5\)). The nearest higher multiple is \(18\) (leaving a negative remainder of \(-1\)).
  • Notice that: \(|Positive\ Remainder| + |Negative\ Remainder| = Divisor\) (\(5 + 1 = 6\)).
  • Always convert a final negative remainder back to a positive remainder by adding the divisor to it: \(R_{pos} = R_{neg} + Divisor\).
🧠 Memory Tip: Choose the remainder (positive or negative) with the smaller absolute value to keep your multiplication steps minimal. For instance, write \(29 \equiv -1 \pmod{30}\) instead of \(+29\).
⚠️ Common Mistake: Leaving the final answer of a remainder question as a negative number. Options in exams will always feature positive remainders.
Mini Example: Find the remainder of \(\frac{49 \times 48 \times 51}{50}\).
Solution: Instead of multiplying, find individual remainders relative to \(50\):
\(49 \equiv -1 \pmod{50}\)
\(48 \equiv -2 \pmod{50}\)
\(51 \equiv +1 \pmod{50}\)
Multiply individual remainders: \((-1) \times (-2) \times (+1) = 2\).
Since \(2\) is positive and less than \(50\), the final remainder is \(2\).

Concept 2: Cyclicity of Unit Digits (Power Cycles)

Definition: The unit digit of a number raised to any power repeating itself in a fixed, periodic cycle is called its cyclicity.

Explanation: Every digit from \(0\) to \(9\) exhibits a specific cycle when repeatedly multiplied by itself. There are three primary cyclicity groups:

Group Type Digits Cyclicity Period Behavior / Pattern
Group 1 \(0, 1, 5, 6\) 1 Always remain unchanged. (e.g., \(5^n\) always ends in 5).
Group 2 \(4, 9\) 2 \(4^{\text{odd}} \to 4\), \(4^{\text{even}} \to 6\)
\(9^{\text{odd}} \to 9\), \(9^{\text{even}} \to 1\)
Group 3 \(2, 3, 7, 8\) 4 Divide power by 4. Use the remainder as the new exponent.
Key Observations:
  • For Group 3 digits, if the exponent is completely divisible by \(4\) (i.e., remainder is \(0\)), replace the exponent with \(4\) instead of \(0\). For example, the unit digit of \(2^{40}\) is the unit digit of \(2^4 = 16 \to 6\).
🧠 Memory Tip: Because the maximum cyclicity period is \(4\), you can safely apply "Divide Exponent by 4" for all digits. It is a universal rule!
Mini Example: Find the unit digit of \(7^{143}\).
Solution: Unit digit of base is \(7\) (cyclicity is \(4\)).
Divide power by 4: \(\frac{143}{4}\) leaves a remainder of \(3\).
Compute \(7^3 = 343\).
The unit digit is \(3\).

Concept 3: Determining the Last Two Digits

Definition: Finding the last two digits of a number is equivalent to finding its remainder when divided by \(100\).

Intuition: Unlike unit digits, last two digits require looking at properties relative to \(100\). We break these down into specific rules based on the ending digit of the base:

  • Numbers ending in 1: To find the last two digits of \((...X1)^{...Y}\):
    • Unit digit of the answer is always 1.
    • Tens digit of the answer is the units digit of \((X \times Y)\).
  • Numbers ending in 5: Any number ending in 5 raised to any power will always end in either 25 or 75.
    • If the tens digit of the base is even, or the power is even: it ends in 25.
    • If the tens digit of the base is odd AND the power is odd: it ends in 75.
  • Numbers ending in 3, 7, 9: Convert them to end in 1 using small powers (e.g., \(3^4 = 81\), \(7^4 = 2401\), \(9^2 = 81\)), then apply the Rule of 1.
  • Even Numbers: Leverage the standard pattern of powers of 2:
    • \(2^{10} = 1024 \to\) ends in \(24\).
    • \(24^{\text{even}} \to\) ends in \(76\).
    • \(24^{\text{odd}} \to\) ends in \(24\).
    • \(76^{\text{any power}} \to\) ends in \(76\).
Mini Example: Find the last two digits of \(31^{43}\).
Solution: The base ends in 1. \(X = 3\) (tens digit of base), \(Y = 3\) (unit digit of power).
Unit digit of answer = \(1\).
Tens digit of answer = Unit digit of \((3 \times 3) = 9\).
So, the last two digits are \(91\).

📏 5. Rules & Properties

Rule 1: Fermat’s Little Theorem

Explanation: If \(p\) is a prime number, and \(a\) is an integer not divisible by \(p\) (i.e., \(a\) and \(p\) are co-prime), then:

$$a^{p-1} \equiv 1 \pmod p$$

Reasoning: In modular arithmetic, multiplying any sequence of integers by a co-prime scale factor under a prime modulo shifts their order but keeps the set of remainders identical, leading to this elegant simplification.

Exception: This theorem fails if \(a\) is a multiple of \(p\). In that case, \(a^{p-1} \equiv 0 \pmod p\).

Example: Find the remainder when \(2^{100}\) is divided by \(101\). Since \(101\) is prime and \(\gcd(2, 101) = 1\), by Fermat's Little Theorem: \(2^{100} \equiv 1 \pmod{101}\). Remainder is \(1\).

Rule 2: Euler’s Totient Theorem (Generalization of Fermat's)

Explanation: For any modulus \(n\) and base \(a\) such that they are co-prime (\(\gcd(a, n) = 1\)):

$$a^{\phi(n)} \equiv 1 \pmod n$$

Where \(\phi(n)\) is Euler’s totient function, indicating the count of positive integers up to \(n\) that are co-prime to \(n\). For a prime factorization \(n = p^a q^b ...\), \(\phi(n) = n(1 - \frac{1}{p})(1 - \frac{1}{q})...\).

Shortcut: For any prime number \(p\), \(\phi(p) = p - 1\). For a composite number like \(10\), \(\phi(10) = 10 \times (1 - \frac{1}{2}) \times (1 - \frac{1}{5}) = 4\).

Example: Find the remainder of \(\frac{7^{42}}{10}\). Since \(\gcd(7, 10) = 1\), and \(\phi(10) = 4\), we know \(7^4 \equiv 1 \pmod{10}\). Divide power by 4: \(42 = 10 \times 4 + 2\). Therefore, \(7^{42} = (7^4)^{10} \times 7^2 \equiv 1^{10} \times 49 \equiv 9 \pmod{10}\). Remainder is \(9\).

Rule 3: Wilson's Theorem

Explanation: If \(p\) is a prime number, then:

$$(p - 1)! + 1 \text{ is divisible by } p \implies (p-1)! \equiv -1 \equiv (p-1) \pmod p$$

Example: Find the remainder when \(28!\) is divided by \(29\). Since \(29\) is prime, according to Wilson's Theorem, \(28! \equiv -1 \pmod{29}\). Converting to positive remainder: \(-1 + 29 = 28\). The remainder is \(28\).

Rule 4: Binomial Remainder Expansion

Explanation: To find the remainder of \(\frac{(ax + 1)^n}{a}\), expand using the Binomial Theorem. Every term except the last will contain \(a\) as a factor. Hence, the remainder simplifies directly to:

$$\frac{(ax + 1)^n}{a} \implies \text{Remainder} = 1^n = 1$$

Similarly, for \(\frac{(ax - 1)^n}{a}\), the remainder is \((-1)^n\). If \(n\) is even, it is \(+1\); if \(n\) is odd, it is \(-1\) (which translates to \(a - 1\)).

Example: Find the remainder when \(31^{200}\) is divided by \(8\). Rewrite \(31\) as \((4 \times 8 - 1)\). Thus, \(\frac{(32 - 1)^{200}}{8} \equiv \frac{(-1)^{200}}{8} \equiv \frac{1}{8}\). Remainder is \(1\).

📐 6. Formula Box

Euler's Totient Calculation Form

$$\phi(N) = N \left(1 - \frac{1}{p_1}\right) \left(1 - \frac{1}{p_2}\right) \dots \left(1 - \frac{1}{p_k}\right)$$

Meaning: Counts the numbers less than or equal to \(N\) that are co-prime to \(N\).

Variables:

  • \(N\): Integer whose totient value is being computed.
  • \(p_1, p_2, \dots, p_k\): Unique prime factors of \(N\).

When to use: When simplifying powers modulo \(N\) (especially when \(N\) is a composite number like 100, 15, or 12).

Memory Trick: Think of it as stripping away fractions of numbers that share factors with \(N\). For any prime \(p\), there are no other factors, so it simply scales down by \((1 - 1/p)\), leaving \(p-1\).

🧠 7. Solved Examples

Easy

Example 1: Find the unit digit of \(237^{143} \times 123^{98}\).

View Step-by-Step Solution

Step 1: Focus only on the unit digits of the bases.
The expression simplifies to finding the unit digit of \(7^{143} \times 3^{98}\).

Step 2: Apply the Cyclicity of 4 to both exponents.
For \(7^{143}\):
Divide \(143\) by \(4\). The remainder is \(3\) (since \(140\) is divisible by \(4\)).
So, \(7^{143} \to 7^3 = 343\). The unit digit is \(3\).

For \(3^{98}\):
Divide \(98\) by \(4\). The remainder is \(2\) (since \(96\) is divisible by \(4\)).
So, \(3^{98} \to 3^2 = 9\). The unit digit is \(9\).

Step 3: Multiply the individual unit digits.
\(3 \times 9 = 27\).
The final unit digit of the expression is \(7\).

Medium

Example 2: Find the remainder when \(2^{99}\) is divided by \(10\).

View Step-by-Step Solution

Method 1: Direct Unit Digit Approach (Mod 10)
Finding a remainder when divided by 10 is mathematically identical to finding the unit digit of the dividend.
The base is \(2\). The exponent is \(99\).
Divide \(99\) by \(4\) to find the cycle position: \(\frac{99}{4}\) leaves remainder \(3\).
Compute \(2^3 = 8\).
So the remainder when divided by 10 is \(8\).

Method 2: Using Euler's Totient Theorem
Can we use Euler's directly? No, because \(\gcd(2, 10) \neq 1\). We must simplify first:
Let \(R = 2^{99} \pmod{10}\).
Divide both the dividend and divisor by their common factor, \(2\):
\(\frac{2^{99}}{2} = 2^{98}\), and \(\frac{10}{2} = 5\).
Now, find \(2^{98} \pmod 5\). Since \(5\) is prime, by Fermat's theorem: \(2^4 \equiv 1 \pmod 5\).
Divide \(98\) by \(4\): remainder is \(2\).
So, \(2^{98} \equiv 2^2 \equiv 4 \pmod 5\).
To get the final remainder relative to \(10\), we must multiply back the factor of \(2\) that we canceled earlier:
\(4 \times 2 = 8\).
The remainder is \(8\).

Hard

Example 3: Find the remainder when \(3^{102}\) is divided by \(101\).

View Step-by-Step Solution

Step 1: Check conditions for Fermat's Little Theorem.
The divisor \(p = 101\) is a prime number.
The base \(a = 3\) is co-prime to \(101\).
Therefore, we can apply Fermat's Little Theorem:

$$3^{100} \equiv 1 \pmod{101}$$

Step 2: Break down the expression using exponents.
We need to evaluate \(3^{102}\). Rewrite this as:

$$3^{102} = 3^2 \times 3^{100}$$

Step 3: Substitute the modulo values.
Substitute \(3^{100} \equiv 1 \pmod{101}\) into the equation:
\(3^{102} \equiv 3^2 \times 1 \pmod{101}\)
\(3^{102} \equiv 9 \pmod{101}\).

The remainder is \(9\).

Exam Level

Example 4: Find the last two digits of \(19^{402}\).

View Step-by-Step Solution

Finding the last two digits is equivalent to finding the remainder of \(19^{402} \div 100\).

Step 1: Simplify the base using exponent properties.
Since \(19\) doesn't end in 1, let's transform it. We know \(19^2 = 361\), which ends in 1.
Rewrite the power: \(19^{402} = (19^2)^{201} = 361^{201}\).

Step 2: Find the last two digits of \(361^{201}\).
We can use the Rule for Numbers Ending in 1 on \((...X1)^{...Y}\):
Here, our base is \(361\), so \(X = 6\) (the tens digit).
Our power is \(201\), so \(Y = 1\) (the unit digit of the power).
The unit digit of our answer is always \(1\).
The tens digit of our answer is the unit digit of \(X \times Y = 6 \times 1 = 6\).
So, the last two digits are \(61\).

The final answer is \(61\).

⚡ 8. Exam Tricks & Shortcuts

Trick 1: Fast Division by 4 for Huge Exponents

To find the cycle of any massive exponent, you do not need to divide the entire number by 4. Recall the divisibility rule of 4: only divide the last two digits. For example, for \(2^{987654326}\), simply calculate \(26 \div 4 \implies\) Remainder = 2. Thus, the unit digit is identical to \(2^2 = 4\).

Trick 2: Factor-Out Remainder Simplification

If the divisor and dividend share a common factor, simplify them first. Do not forget to multiply the cancelled factor back into the final remainder!
Example: Find \(\text{rem}\left(\frac{14^{102}}{12}\right)\).
Both share factor \(2\). Write as \(\frac{14 \times 14^{101}}{12} = \frac{7 \times 14^{101}}{6}\).
Now, find remainder modulo 6: \(7 \equiv 1 \pmod 6\) and \(14 \equiv 2 \pmod 6\).
So, \(1 \times 2^{101} \pmod 6\). Since \(2^{\text{any power}} \pmod 6\) always leaves remainder \(4\) (check: \(2^1=2, 2^2=4, 2^3=8\equiv 2, 2^4=16\equiv 4\), so odd power gives \(2\), even power gives \(4\)), and \(101\) is odd, we get \(2 \pmod 6\).
Multiply back the common factor \(2\) we removed at the start: \(2 \times 2 = 4\). Remainder is \(4\).

⚠️ 9. Common Mistakes & Pitfalls

Mistake 1: Treating Remainder "0" as Exponent "0" in Cyclicity

The Mistake: Students dividing a power like \(7^{40}\) by \(4\) get a remainder of \(0\), and write \(7^0 = 1\) as the unit digit.

How to avoid: A remainder of \(0\) indicates the number falls perfectly on the 4th position of the cycle. Always replace remainder 0 with 4 as the exponent (\(7^4 = 2401 \to\) Unit digit is 1).

Mistake 2: Forgetting to Multiply back the Simplified Factor

The Mistake: Solving \(\frac{15}{9}\) by dividing by 3 to get \(\frac{5}{3}\), and leaving the remainder as \(2\).

How to avoid: The actual remainder of \(\frac{15}{9}\) is \(6\). Since you divided both parts by 3 to simplify, you must multiply the intermediary remainder (\(2\)) by \(3\) to get the true remainder: \(2 \times 3 = 6\).

📌 11. Summary

Remainders and unit digits are core components of Number Sense that test your ability to look at properties of numbers structurally rather than computationally. By converting complex division into simpler modular expressions, using negative remainders, and recognizing the cyclicity pattern of digits, you can evaluate numbers of astronomical proportions effortlessly. Keep the rules of Fermat's and Euler's handy for handling prime and composite denominators respectively.

⚡ Quick Revision Cheatsheet

  • Negative Remainders: Convert to positive by adding divisor (e.g., \(-2 \pmod 7 \equiv 5\)).
  • Universal Unit Digit Rule: Divide power by 4, replace with remainder. If remainder is 0, use exponent 4.
  • Last Two Digits: Modulo \(100\) calculation.
    • Ending in 1: \((...X1)^{...Y} \to\) Tens digit is units digit of \(X \times Y\), Unit digit is 1.
  • Fermat's Theorem: \(\frac{a^{p-1}}{p} \implies \text{Remainder} = 1\) (where \(p\) is prime, \(\gcd(a,p)=1\)).
  • Wilson's Theorem: \(\frac{(p-1)!}{p} \implies \text{Remainder} = p-1\).

🚀 12. Key Takeaways

  • Unit digit calculation has a maximum cycle of 4; hence, dividing any exponent by 4 works universally.
  • Fermat’s Little Theorem is your fastest route when dealing with prime denominators.
  • For composite denominators, Euler's Totient Theorem generalizes remainder solutions.
  • Always check options using unit digits before committing to tedious calculations.
