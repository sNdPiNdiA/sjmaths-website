Percentage-based Word Problems & Comparisons

Medium Difficulty 25-30 Mins Read High Exam Weightage Updated 2026

📘 1. Introduction: Percentage-based Word Problems & Comparisons

In competitive exams like SSC CGL, Banking, and Railways, Percentage-based Word Problems and Comparisons form the bedrock of Arithmetic. Whether it is calculating salary comparisons, price-consumption-expenditure adjustments, or multi-step population shifts, mastering this microtopic is crucial for clearing both Prelims and Mains.

At its core, this topic is about identifying the "Base Value" (the denominator) and understanding how change propagates when multiple quantities are compared. Instead of solving with tedious equations, we will learn how to parse word problems into high-speed fractional multipliers, saving you 30 to 45 seconds per question.

🎯 2. Learning Objectives

  • Identify the correct reference frame (Base) in complex word comparison scenarios.
  • Apply the Product Constancy Rule (Price × Consumption = Expenditure) instantly using fraction conversions.
  • Deconstruct multi-variable comparison chains (e.g., A to B, B to C) into unified ratios.
  • Master the "AB-Formula" and the Fraction Scale Method for successive and reciprocal percentage changes.
  • Solve high-weightage exam word problems in under 30 seconds with 100% accuracy.

📝 3. Prerequisites

Before proceeding, ensure you have a strong grasp of the following concepts:

  • Fraction to Percentage Conversions: E.g., \( \frac{1}{6} = 16.67\% \), \( \frac{1}{8} = 12.5\% \), \( \frac{1}{12} = 8.33\% \).
  • The concept of "Of" vs. "Than": The value following "of" or "than" always goes to the denominator.
  • Basic Ratio Proportions: Scaling and combining ratios (e.g., if \( A:B = 2:3 \) and \( B:C = 4:5 \)).

🧠 4. Core Concepts

Concept 1: The Principle of the Base (Reference Value)

Definition: In any comparison, the percentage change is calculated relative to a reference value known as the Base.

Explanation: "A is what % of B?" means B is the base. "By what % is A greater than B?" also means B is the base. The base is the absolute benchmark of comparison.

Mathematical Intuition:

$$\text{Percentage Change} = \frac{\text{Absolute Change}}{\text{Base Value}} \times 100$$

Memory Tip: Look for words like "than" or "of". The entity immediately succeeding these words is your denominator.

Common Mistake: Confusing the base. For example, if A is 20% more than B, students often mistakenly assume B is 20% less than A.

Mini Example: If Ajay's salary is ₹60 and Vijay's is ₹80. Vijay's salary is how much % more than Ajay's? Here, base is Ajay (₹60). $$\Delta\% = \frac{80 - 60}{60} \times 100 = \frac{20}{60} \times 100 = 33.33\%$$

Concept 2: Direct Fractional Multipliers

Definition: Instead of working with large numbers like \(100 + x\), express percentage changes as decimal/fraction multipliers.

Explanation: An increase of \(25\%\) is equivalent to multiplying the base by \(1.25\) (or \(\frac{5}{4}\)). A decrease of \(12.5\%\) is equivalent to multiplying by \(0.875\) (or \(\frac{7}{8}\)).

Intuition: Multipliers turn complex word chains into simple, single-line equations.

Mini Example: A number is increased by 20% and then decreased by 10%. Initial value = \(X\). $$\text{Final Value} = X \times \frac{6}{5} \times \frac{9}{10} = X \times \frac{54}{50} = 1.08X \implies 8\% \text{ net increase.}$$

Concept 3: Multi-Variable Comparison Chains

Definition: Word problems comparing three or more variables (e.g., A, B, and C) relative to each other.

Explanation: A is \(x\%\) more than B, B is \(y\%\) less than C. To find the direct relationship between A and C, we chain their fractional ratios together.

Memory Tip: Start solving from the variable that has no dependency on others (usually the last variable mentioned, e.g., C).

Mini Example: A is 20% more than B, B is 25% more than C. Let C = 100. Then B = 125. A = 120% of 125 = 150. Thus, A is 50% more than C.

📏 5. Rules & Properties

Rule 1: Product Constancy (The Inverse Relationship Rule)

Explanation: When the product of two variables is constant (\(X \times Y = K\)), an increase in \(X\) must be balanced by a corresponding decrease in \(Y\).

The Mathematical Reason: If \(X\) increases by a fraction \(\frac{a}{b}\), to keep the product constant, \(Y\) must decrease by the fraction:

$$\text{Decrease Fraction} = \frac{a}{b + a}$$

Shortcut Table (The Product Constancy Scale):

  • Increase of \(\frac{1}{2}\) (\(50\%\)) \(\implies\) Decrease of \(\frac{1}{3}\) (\(33.33\%\))
  • Increase of \(\frac{1}{3}\) (\(33.33\%\)) \(\implies\) Decrease of \(\frac{1}{4}\) (\(25\%\))
  • Increase of \(\frac{1}{4}\) (\(25\%\)) \(\implies\) Decrease of \(\frac{1}{5}\) (\(20\%\))
  • Increase of \(\frac{1}{5}\) (\(20\%\)) \(\implies\) Decrease of \(\frac{1}{6}\) (\(16.67\%\))

Exception/Constraint: This rule works only when the product remains completely unchanged. If the expenditure/product also changes, use the Modified Ratio Method (shown in the Solved Examples section).

Example: If the price of petrol increases by \(25\%\), by how much must a motorist reduce consumption to keep his expenditure constant?
Since \(25\% = \frac{1}{4}\) increase, consumption must decrease by \(\frac{1}{4 + 1} = \frac{1}{5} = 20\%\).

📐 6. Formula Box

1. Direct Comparison Formulas

If \(A\) is \(P\%\) more than \(B\), then \(B\) is less than \(A\) by:

$$\text{Percentage Less} = \left( \frac{P}{100 + P} \right) \times 100\%$$

If \(A\) is \(P\%\) less than \(B\), then \(B\) is more than \(A\) by:

$$\text{Percentage More} = \left( \frac{P}{100 - P} \right) \times 100\%$$

Variables: \(P\) = given percentage difference.

Memory Trick: If you want a smaller final percentage, make the denominator larger (\(100 + P\)). If you want a larger final percentage, make the denominator smaller (\(100 - P\)).

2. Successive Change Formula (AB Formula)

If a quantity undergoes successive percentage changes of \(x\%\) and \(y\%\), the net percentage change is given by:

$$\text{Net Change \%} = x + y + \frac{xy}{100}$$

Usage Guidelines: Use positive values for increases/markups and negative values for decreases/discounts. Do NOT use this formula for non-integer percentages (like \(12.5\%\), \(16.67\%\)); use the ratio method instead.

🧠 7. Solved Examples

Example 1: Basic Comparison (Direct Application)

Easy

Question: If Radhika's height is \(20\%\) less than Shivani's height, by what percent is Shivani's height more than Radhika's height?

View Step-by-Step Solution

Method 1: Formula Approach

Here, \(P = 20\%\). Since Radhika has less height, we calculate the 'more' percentage:

$$\text{Percentage More} = \left( \frac{20}{100 - 20} \right) \times 100 = \frac{20}{80} \times 100 = 25\%$$

Method 2: Ratio/Fraction Method (Fastest)

\(20\% = \frac{1}{5}\). This means if Shivani's height is \(5\) units, Radhika's height is \(5 - 1 = 4\) units.

Now, we compare Shivani's height relative to Radhika's height (base is Radhika = 4 units):

$$\text{Difference} = 5 - 4 = 1 \text{ unit}$$

$$\text{Required Percentage} = \frac{1}{4} \times 100 = 25\%$$

Correct Answer: 25%

Example 2: Price-Consumption-Expenditure (Product Constancy)

Medium

Question: The price of sugar rises by \(33\frac{1}{3}\%\). If a family wants to keep their monthly expenditure on sugar the same as before, by what percentage must they reduce their sugar consumption?

View Step-by-Step Solution

Step 1: Convert percentage to fraction

$$33\frac{1}{3}\% = \frac{1}{3}$$

An increase of \(\frac{1}{3}\) means the price ratio changes from \(3\) to \(4\) (since \(3 + 1 = 4\)).

Step 2: Apply the Product Constancy Concept

Since \(\text{Price} \times \text{Consumption} = \text{Expenditure}\) (Constant), consumption must change inversely to price.

$$\text{Price Ratio} = 3 : 4 \implies \text{Consumption Ratio} = 4 : 3$$

Step 3: Calculate percentage reduction in consumption

$$\text{Reduction} = \frac{4 - 3}{4} \times 100 = \frac{1}{4} \times 100 = 25\%$$

Shortcut Technique: If price increases by fraction \(\frac{a}{b} = \frac{1}{3}\), consumption must decrease by \(\frac{a}{b + a} = \frac{1}{3 + 1} = \frac{1}{4} = 25\%\).

Correct Answer: 25%

Example 3: Multi-Variable Comparison Chain

Hard

Question: In an exam, A scored \(10\%\) less marks than B. B scored \(25\%\) more marks than C, and C scored \(20\%\) less marks than D. If A scored 360 marks, how many marks did D score?

View Step-by-Step Solution

Let's convert all comparison steps into clean fractional multipliers:

  • A is \(10\%\) less than B \(\implies A = B \times \frac{9}{10}\)
  • B is \(25\%\) more than C \(\implies B = C \times \frac{5}{4}\)
  • C is \(20\%\) less than D \(\implies C = D \times \frac{4}{5}\)

Now, let's chain these expressions together to express A in terms of D:

$$A = \left( C \times \frac{5}{4} \right) \times \frac{9}{10}$$

$$A = \left( D \times \frac{4}{5} \times \frac{5}{4} \right) \times \frac{9}{10}$$

Notice how the middle factors beautifully cancel out:

$$A = D \times \left( \frac{4}{5} \times \frac{5}{4} \right) \times \frac{9}{10} \implies A = D \times \frac{9}{10}$$

Solve for D:

Since we are given \(A = 360\):

$$360 = D \times \frac{9}{10} \implies D = \frac{360 \times 10}{9} = 400$$

Correct Answer: 400

Example 4: Real Exam Trend (Modified Expenditure)

CGL Mains

Question: The price of petrol increased by \(20\%\). By what percentage should a consumer reduce his consumption so that his net expenditure increases by only \(8\%\)?

View Step-by-Step Solution

The Initial-Intermediate-Final Method:

Let initial Price = \(100\) and initial Consumption = \(100\).
Hence, Initial Expenditure = \(100 \times 100 = 10,000\).

  • New Price = \(120\) (due to 20% increase)
  • Target Expenditure = \(10,000 \times 1.08 = 10,800\) (due to capped 8% increase)

We know that: \(\text{New Price} \times \text{New Consumption} = \text{Target Expenditure}\)

$$120 \times C_{\text{new}} = 10,800 \implies C_{\text{new}} = \frac{10,800}{120} = 90$$

Comparing initial consumption (\(100\)) to new consumption (\(90\)):

$$\text{Reduction} = 100 - 90 = 10\%$$

Lightning Shortcut Method:

$$\text{Consumption change multiplier} = \frac{100 \pm \text{Net Expenditure Change \%}}{100 \pm \text{Price Change \%}}$$

$$\text{Consumption multiplier} = \frac{100 + 8}{100 + 20} = \frac{108}{120} = \frac{9}{10}$$

A multiplier of \(\frac{9}{10}\) represents a direct drop of \(\frac{1}{10} = 10\%\).

Correct Answer: 10%

⚡ 8. Exam Tricks & Shortcuts

The "Product Constancy Scale" Mental Math Hack

In exams, standard fractional values show up repeatedly. Memorize this unidirectional jump rule to write down answers instantly:

  • If a value increases by \(x\), to pull it back to the original level, we must decrease it. The reciprocal relationship follows the series:
    $$\frac{1}{1} \xrightarrow{\text{down}} \frac{1}{2} \xrightarrow{\text{down}} \frac{1}{3} \xrightarrow{\text{down}} \frac{1}{4} \xrightarrow{\text{down}} \frac{1}{5} \xrightarrow{\text{down}} \frac{1}{6}$$
  • Application: If B's salary is \(25\%\) (\(\frac{1}{4}\)) more than A's, then A's salary is one step down on the scale: \(\frac{1}{5} = 20\%\) less than B's.

The Multiplier Chain Equation

Do not write separate steps for long verbal comparison problems. Setup a continuous chain equation.
"A is 10% more than B, B is 20% less than C, C is 30% more than D..." is simply modeled as:

$$A = D \times \left(\frac{13}{10}\right) \times \left(\frac{4}{5}\right) \times \left(\frac{11}{10}\right)$$

This allows direct cancellation and avoids any intermediate decimal steps.

⚠️ 9. Common Mistakes & Pitfalls

1. Denominator Fallacy (Choosing the Wrong Base)

The Mistake: Treating "A is 25% more than B" as "B is 25% less than A".

How to avoid: Always trace back to the reference point. If A is 25% more than B, B is the base (100%). If we compare B to A, A becomes the base (125%). Use fractions to keep this transition explicit: \(\frac{5}{4} \to \text{base is } 4\), reverse is \(\frac{4}{5} \to \text{base is } 5\).

2. Blindly Applying the "AB-Formula"

The Mistake: Applying \(x + y + \frac{xy}{100}\) to non-integer values, which leads to messy fractions and wasted time. For example, trying to compute successive updates of \(14.28\%\) and \(12.5\%\) using decimals.

How to avoid: Convert non-integers directly to fractions: \(14.28\% = \frac{1}{7}\) (multiplier \(\frac{8}{7}\)) and \(12.5\% = \frac{1}{8}\) (multiplier \(\frac{9}{8}\)). The net multiplier is \(\frac{8}{7} \times \frac{9}{8} = \frac{9}{7}\), which is an easy \(28.57\%\) increase!

📌 11. Summary

To master percentage comparisons and word problems, remember that every calculation hinges on the benchmark you are measuring against. Fractional multipliers make complex, multi-stage equations clean and manageable. For constant product equations, any change in one factor produces an opposite shift in the other, governed by a simple scale relation. Memorizing the key percentage-fraction mappings is your ultimate shortcut to raw exam speed.

🚀 12. Key Takeaways

  • The entity coming after "than" or "as compared to" is always placed in the denominator.
  • Use the product-constancy scale for instant mental math on price-consumption adjustments.
  • Convert all non-integer percentages to fractions immediately to keep your calculations simple and clean.
  • When dealing with multi-step percentage changes, use fractional multipliers to build a continuous calculation chain.
