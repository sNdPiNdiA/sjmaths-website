Median, Mode & Average Speed Basics

Medium Difficulty 25-30 Mins Read High Exam Weightage Updated 2026

📘 Introduction: Median, Mode & Average Speed Basics

Namaste, future officers! In your journey to conquer government exams, understanding data and motion is paramount. This microtopic lays the foundational stones for two crucial areas: Statistics (Median & Mode) and Time, Speed & Distance (Average Speed).

While Median and Mode help us understand the "center" or "most frequent" aspects of a dataset, Average Speed provides insight into the overall pace of a journey. These concepts are not just abstract math; they are real-world tools that appear frequently in various exam sections.

Why is this important?

  • Direct Questions: You'll encounter direct questions on calculating Median, Mode, and Average Speed.
  • Data Interpretation (DI): Median and Mode are often required to analyze data presented in tables, charts, and graphs.
  • Problem Solving: Average Speed is a cornerstone for all Time, Speed & Distance problems.
  • Logical Reasoning: Sometimes, these concepts might be subtly tested in logical puzzles or data sufficiency.

Mastering these basics will not only fetch you easy marks but also build a strong conceptual base for more advanced topics!

🎯 Learning Objectives

By the end of this microtopic, you will be able to:

  • Define and understand the concept of Median and Mode for any given dataset.
  • Accurately calculate the Median for both odd and even numbers of observations.
  • Identify and determine the Mode(s) of a dataset, including cases with no mode or multiple modes.
  • Clearly define and intuitively grasp the concept of Average Speed.
  • Calculate Average Speed for various scenarios involving different distances and times.

📝 Prerequisites

Before diving into Median, Mode, and Average Speed, ensure you are comfortable with the following foundational concepts:

  • Basic Arithmetic Operations: Addition, subtraction, multiplication, and division of whole numbers, decimals, and fractions.
  • Ordering Numbers: Ability to arrange a given set of numbers in ascending or descending order.
  • Fractions & Decimals: Basic operations and conversions.
  • Understanding of "Data" and "Observation": What constitutes a dataset and its individual elements.
  • Basic understanding of "Distance," "Time," and "Speed": The fundamental relationship between them ($$ \text{Speed} = \frac{\text{Distance}}{\text{Time}} $$).

💡 Core Concepts

Median

Definition: The Median is the middle value in a dataset when the data is arranged in order (ascending or descending).

Explanation: Imagine lining up all the data points from smallest to largest. The Median is the value exactly in the middle, dividing the dataset into two equal halves. Half the values are below the Median, and half are above it.

Intuition: Think of it as the "middle ground" or the "typical" value that isn't swayed by extremely high or low outliers. If you're looking for the central tendency that represents the 'average' person's income in a highly unequal society, Median is often more representative than the arithmetic mean.

Important observations:

  • Always, always, ALWAYS arrange the data first!
  • If the number of observations (n) is odd, the Median is a single value.
  • If the number of observations (n) is even, the Median is the average of the two middle values.
  • It is less affected by extreme values (outliers) compared to the mean.

Memory Tip: "MED-ian" sounds like "MID-dle". Just remember to put things in "ORDER" first!

Common Mistake: Calculating the middle value without arranging the data first.

Mini Example:

  • Dataset: {5, 2, 8, 1, 6}
  • Arranged: {1, 2, 5, 6, 8}
  • Median: 5

Mode

Definition: The Mode is the value that appears most frequently in a dataset.

Explanation: It's simply the data point that has the highest count or recurrence. If you poll people about their favorite color, the color chosen by the most people would be the Mode.

Intuition: Think of it as the "most popular" or "most common" item in a list. It tells you which value has the highest probability of occurring again if you were to pick randomly from the dataset.

Important observations:

  • A dataset can have one Mode (unimodal), two Modes (bimodal), more than two Modes (multimodal), or no Mode at all (if all values appear with the same frequency).
  • Unlike Median, Mode does not require the data to be ordered.
  • Mode can be used for both numerical and categorical data (e.g., favorite colors, types of cars).

Memory Tip: "M-O-D-E" for "M-O-st O-ccurring D-ata E-lement."

Common Mistake: Confusing the mode with the frequency of the mode (e.g., saying the mode is '3' when the value '5' appears 3 times, instead of saying the mode is '5').

Mini Example:

  • Dataset: {7, 3, 5, 7, 2, 7, 9}
  • Value 7 appears 3 times, which is more than any other value.
  • Mode: 7

Average Speed

Definition: Average Speed is the total distance covered by an object divided by the total time taken to cover that distance.

Explanation: When an object moves, its speed might vary throughout the journey. Average speed gives us a single value that represents the overall rate of motion. It answers the question: "If the object had traveled at a constant speed, what speed would allow it to cover the same total distance in the same total time?"

Intuition: Don't confuse it with the simple average of different speeds. If you travel 10 km at 10 km/h and then 100 km at 100 km/h, your average speed isn't (10+100)/2 because you spent much longer at 100 km/h. It's about the entire journey as a whole.

Important observations:

  • It's always Total Distance / Total Time. This is the golden rule!
  • Average speed is NOT necessarily the arithmetic mean of individual speeds, especially when the time intervals or distances covered at different speeds are unequal.
  • Units are crucial: km/h, m/s, miles/hour, etc.

Memory Tip: "A.S. = Total D / Total T" – always think "Total over Total!"

Common Mistake: Simply averaging the given speeds (e.g., if a car travels at 40 km/h and then at 60 km/h, the average speed is not necessarily 50 km/h).

Mini Example:

  • A car travels 100 km in 2 hours.
  • Average Speed = \( \frac{100 \text{ km}}{2 \text{ hours}} = 50 \text{ km/h} \)

📏 Rules & Properties

Rule for Median (Odd Number of Observations)

Explanation: When you have an odd number of data points (n) in your dataset, there's exactly one middle value after arranging the data. Its position can be found using a simple formula.

Reason: For an odd number, if you divide (n-1) by 2, you get the number of elements on either side of the middle one. Adding 1 to this gives the position of the middle element.

Shortcut: Arrange the data. Count (n+1)/2 from either end. The value at that position is the median.

Exception: None. This rule always applies for an odd number of observations.

Example: Find the Median of {12, 18, 9, 21, 15}.

1. Arrange in ascending order: {9, 12, 15, 18, 21}

2. Number of observations (n) = 5 (odd)

3. Median position = \( \left(\frac{5+1}{2}\right)^{\text{th}} = 3^{\text{rd}} \) term.

4. The 3rd term is 15. So, Median = 15.

Rule for Median (Even Number of Observations)

Explanation: When you have an even number of data points (n) in your dataset, there isn't a single "middle" value. Instead, there are two central values. The Median is calculated by taking the average of these two central values.

Reason: Since there's no single exact middle, we find the point equidistant from the two central values to represent the dataset's midpoint.

Shortcut: Arrange the data. Identify the two middle terms: the \( \left(\frac{n}{2}\right)^{\text{th}} \) term and the \( \left(\frac{n}{2}+1\right)^{\text{th}} \) term. Add them and divide by 2.

Exception: None. This rule always applies for an even number of observations.

Example: Find the Median of {6, 10, 4, 12, 8, 14}.

1. Arrange in ascending order: {4, 6, 8, 10, 12, 14}

2. Number of observations (n) = 6 (even)

3. The two middle terms are the \( \left(\frac{6}{2}\right)^{\text{th}} = 3^{\text{rd}} \) term and the \( \left(\frac{6}{2}+1\right)^{\text{th}} = 4^{\text{th}} \) term.

4. The 3rd term is 8, and the 4th term is 10.

5. Median = \( \frac{8+10}{2} = \frac{18}{2} = 9 \).

Rule for Mode

Explanation: To find the Mode, simply count the frequency of each distinct value in the dataset. The value (or values) that appear most frequently is the Mode.

Reason: The Mode represents the peak(s) in the data distribution, indicating the most common outcome or observation.

Shortcut: Scan the list and mentally (or physically by tallying) count occurrences. The one with the highest count wins!

Exception:

  • No Mode: If all values appear with the same frequency (e.g., {1, 2, 3, 4}).
  • Multiple Modes: If two or more values share the highest frequency (e.g., {1, 1, 2, 3, 3, 4} has modes 1 and 3 – it's bimodal).

Example: Find the Mode of {A, B, C, B, A, D, B, C, B}.

1. Count frequencies:

  • A: 2 times
  • B: 4 times
  • C: 2 times
  • D: 1 time

2. The highest frequency is 4, corresponding to value B.

3. Mode = B.

📐 Formula Box

Median (Odd number of observations, n)

$$ \text{Median} = \left(\frac{n+1}{2}\right)^{\text{th}} \text{term after arranging the data} $$

Meaning: This formula gives you the position of the Median in an ordered list when the total count of numbers is odd.

Variables: \( n \) = total number of observations in the dataset.

When to use: Exclusively when your dataset has an odd number of values.

When NOT to use: When your dataset has an even number of values.

Memory Trick: Odd number of items? Just add 1 to 'n' to make it even, then halve it. That's your middle index.

Median (Even number of observations, n)

$$ \text{Median} = \frac{\left(\frac{n}{2}\right)^{\text{th}} \text{term} + \left(\frac{n}{2}+1\right)^{\text{th}} \text{term}}{2} \text{after arranging the data} $$

Meaning: This formula instructs you to find the two central values in an ordered dataset (when 'n' is even) and then calculate their arithmetic mean.

Variables: \( n \) = total number of observations in the dataset.

When to use: Exclusively when your dataset has an even number of values.

When NOT to use: When your dataset has an odd number of values.

Memory Trick: Even number of items? You need TWO middle terms. Divide 'n' by 2 for the first position, then add 1 for the next. Average them out!

Average Speed (General Formula)

$$ \text{Average Speed} = \frac{\text{Total Distance}}{\text{Total Time}} $$

Meaning: The fundamental definition of average speed, representing the overall rate of travel for an entire journey.

Variables: Total Distance = sum of all distances covered; Total Time = sum of all time intervals taken.

When to use: Always! This is the most versatile and always correct formula for average speed.

When NOT to use: While always correct, sometimes specialized formulas might be faster for specific scenarios (but this formula remains the base).

Memory Trick: "Total D over Total T" - sum everything up before dividing.

Average Speed (Special Case: Equal Distances)

$$ \text{Average Speed} = \frac{2S_1 S_2}{S_1 + S_2} \quad \text{(for 2 speeds, } S_1 \text{ and } S_2 \text{, covering equal distances)} $$

Meaning: This is a shortcut formula derived from the general average speed formula, specifically applicable when an object covers the same distance at two different speeds. It is also known as the Harmonic Mean of the two speeds.

Variables: \( S_1 \) = speed during the first part of the journey; \( S_2 \) = speed during the second part of the journey. Both parts must cover the same distance.

When to use: Only when an object travels the SAME distance at two different speeds (e.g., A to B at \( S_1 \) and B to A at \( S_2 \)).

When NOT to use: When distances are unequal, or when time intervals are equal (use arithmetic mean then).

Memory Trick: "Twice the Product over the Sum." The '2' is key! This is for equal DISTANCE.

Average Speed (Special Case: Equal Times)

$$ \text{Average Speed} = \frac{S_1 + S_2}{2} \quad \text{(for 2 speeds, } S_1 \text{ and } S_2 \text{, covering for equal times)} $$

Meaning: When an object travels for the same amount of time at two different speeds, its average speed is simply the arithmetic mean of those speeds.

Variables: \( S_1 \) = speed during the first time interval; \( S_2 \) = speed during the second time interval. Both intervals must be of the same duration.

When to use: Only when an object travels for the SAME time duration at two different speeds.

When NOT to use: When time intervals are unequal, or when distances are equal (use harmonic mean then).

Memory Trick: "Sum over Two." Simplest average. This is for equal TIME.

🧠 Solved Examples

Easy

Example 1: Median (Odd 'n')

Find the Median of the following scores: 23, 15, 30, 28, 18.

View Step-by-Step Solution

Step 1: Arrange the data in ascending order.

15, 18, 23, 28, 30

Step 2: Count the number of observations (n).

n = 5

Step 3: Since n is odd, use the formula for median position.

Median position = \( \left(\frac{n+1}{2}\right)^{\text{th}} \text{term} = \left(\frac{5+1}{2}\right)^{\text{th}} = 3^{\text{rd}} \text{term} \)

Step 4: Identify the value at the calculated position.

The 3rd term in the ordered list is 23.

Answer: The Median is 23.

Easy

Example 2: Mode

Identify the Mode(s) in the following dataset representing fruit preferences: Apple, Banana, Orange, Apple, Mango, Banana, Apple.

View Step-by-Step Solution

Step 1: Count the frequency of each distinct value.

  • Apple: 3 times
  • Banana: 2 times
  • Orange: 1 time
  • Mango: 1 time

Step 2: Identify the value(s) with the highest frequency.

The highest frequency is 3, and it corresponds to 'Apple'.

Answer: The Mode is Apple.

Medium

Example 3: Median (Even 'n')

A student scored the following marks in 6 subjects: 75, 80, 65, 90, 70, 85. Find the Median mark.

View Step-by-Step Solution

Step 1: Arrange the data in ascending order.

65, 70, 75, 80, 85, 90

Step 2: Count the number of observations (n).

n = 6

Step 3: Since n is even, identify the two middle terms.

The two middle terms are the \( \left(\frac{n}{2}\right)^{\text{th}} \) term and the \( \left(\frac{n}{2}+1\right)^{\text{th}} \) term.

  • \( \left(\frac{6}{2}\right)^{\text{th}} = 3^{\text{rd}} \) term = 75
  • \( \left(\frac{6}{2}+1\right)^{\text{th}} = 4^{\text{th}} \) term = 80

Step 4: Calculate the average of these two middle terms.

Median = \( \frac{75 + 80}{2} = \frac{155}{2} = 77.5 \)

Answer: The Median mark is 77.5.

Medium

Example 4: Average Speed (General Application)

A bus travels 150 km in 3 hours and then another 100 km in 2 hours. What is the average speed of the bus for the entire journey?

View Step-by-Step Solution

Step 1: Calculate the Total Distance covered.

Total Distance = Distance 1 + Distance 2 = 150 km + 100 km = 250 km

Step 2: Calculate the Total Time taken.

Total Time = Time 1 + Time 2 = 3 hours + 2 hours = 5 hours

Step 3: Use the Average Speed formula (Total Distance / Total Time).

Average Speed = \( \frac{250 \text{ km}}{5 \text{ hours}} = 50 \text{ km/h} \)

Answer: The average speed of the bus is 50 km/h.

Exam Level

Example 5: Average Speed (Equal Distances)

A man travels from point A to point B at a speed of 40 km/h and returns from point B to point A at a speed of 60 km/h. What is his average speed for the entire journey?

View Step-by-Step Solution

Method 1: Using the specialized formula (Equal Distances)

Step 1: Identify that the distance covered in both directions is the same.

Distance from A to B = Distance from B to A. This means we can use the special formula.

Step 2: Identify the speeds.

\( S_1 = 40 \text{ km/h} \)

\( S_2 = 60 \text{ km/h} \)

Step 3: Apply the formula for average speed with equal distances.

Average Speed = \( \frac{2S_1 S_2}{S_1 + S_2} = \frac{2 \times 40 \times 60}{40 + 60} \)

Average Speed = \( \frac{4800}{100} = 48 \text{ km/h} \)

Method 2: Using the general formula (Total Distance / Total Time) - for conceptual understanding

Step 1: Assume a convenient distance for AB.

Since the distance is the same and speeds are 40 and 60, a good distance would be their LCM. LCM(40, 60) = 120 km.

Let Distance AB = 120 km.

Step 2: Calculate time for the first leg (A to B).

Time 1 = \( \frac{\text{Distance}}{\text{Speed}} = \frac{120 \text{ km}}{40 \text{ km/h}} = 3 \text{ hours} \)

Step 3: Calculate time for the second leg (B to A).

Time 2 = \( \frac{\text{Distance}}{\text{Speed}} = \frac{120 \text{ km}}{60 \text{ km/h}} = 2 \text{ hours} \)

Step 4: Calculate Total Distance and Total Time.

Total Distance = Distance A to B + Distance B to A = 120 km + 120 km = 240 km

Total Time = Time 1 + Time 2 = 3 hours + 2 hours = 5 hours

Step 5: Apply the general Average Speed formula.

Average Speed = \( \frac{\text{Total Distance}}{\text{Total Time}} = \frac{240 \text{ km}}{5 \text{ hours}} = 48 \text{ km/h} \)

Answer: The average speed for the entire journey is 48 km/h.

⚡ Exam Tricks & Shortcuts

Median Quick Scan

Trick: For small datasets (up to 10-12 elements), you can often arrange them mentally or quickly cross off numbers from both ends of the sorted list until you reach the middle. This avoids writing down the formula.

Example: Dataset {10, 3, 7, 12, 5}. Mental sort: 3, 5, 7, 10, 12. Cross 3 & 12, then 5 & 10. Middle is 7. Median = 7.

Mode Visual Count

Trick: For finding the mode, especially in data with categorical values, just scan the list and make mental tallies. The one that "looks" most frequent usually is.

Example: {Red, Blue, Green, Red, Yellow, Blue, Red}. Scan: Red (3), Blue (2), Green (1), Yellow (1). Red is the mode.

Average Speed (Equal Distances - \( \frac{2S_1 S_2}{S_1 + S_2} \))

Trick: Memorize this formula for scenarios like "going to a place and coming back," or "traveling half the journey at speed \( S_1 \) and the other half at speed \( S_2 \)." It saves time over calculating total distance and total time.

When to use: Any time two distinct speeds cover identical distances.

Example: Car travels at 30 km/h for the first 100 km and 70 km/h for the next 100 km. Average Speed = \( \frac{2 \times 30 \times 70}{30 + 70} = \frac{4200}{100} = 42 \text{ km/h} \).

Average Speed (Equal Times - \( \frac{S_1 + S_2}{2} \))

Trick: If an object travels for the exact same duration at two different speeds, its average speed is simply the arithmetic mean of those speeds. This is much faster than going through total distance/total time.

When to use: Any time two distinct speeds are maintained for identical time durations.

Example: A runner runs for 1 hour at 10 km/h and for another 1 hour at 12 km/h. Average Speed = \( \frac{10 + 12}{2} = 11 \text{ km/h} \).

⚠️ Common Mistakes & Pitfalls

1. Not Arranging Data for Median

Mistake: Picking the middle element from an unordered list. For example, in {10, 5, 20}, picking 5 as the median.

How to Avoid: Develop a habit: the very first step for Median is always "SORT THE DATA!" Don't proceed until your data is in ascending or descending order.

2. Incorrectly Calculating Median for Even 'n'

Mistake: For an even number of observations, picking only one of the two middle terms instead of averaging them. For example, in {1, 2, 3, 4}, picking 2 or 3 as the median.

How to Avoid: If 'n' is even, remember "TWO terms, AVERAGE them." The median must be a single number. The average provides that single representative value between the two central numbers.

3. Confusing Mode with its Frequency

Mistake: Stating the frequency of the most occurring value as the mode, instead of the value itself. For example, if '5' appears 3 times, saying the mode is '3'.

How to Avoid: The mode IS the data point. It's '5', not '3'. The frequency (3) just tells you *how often* the mode occurs.

4. Always Averaging Speeds Directly

Mistake: Assuming that Average Speed is always the arithmetic mean of the given speeds. For example, if a car travels at 30 km/h and then at 60 km/h, the average speed is NOT (30+60)/2 = 45 km/h, unless the time travelled at each speed is equal.

How to Avoid: Stick to the golden rule: $$ \text{Average Speed} = \frac{\text{Total Distance}}{\text{Total Time}} $$. Only use the special formulas ($$ \frac{2S_1 S_2}{S_1 + S_2} $$ or $$ \frac{S_1 + S_2}{2} $$) when their specific conditions (equal distance or equal time, respectively) are met.

5. Unit Inconsistency in Average Speed

Mistake: Mixing units without conversion (e.g., distance in km and time in minutes, or speed in m/s and distance in km).

How to Avoid: Always check units before calculations. Convert all quantities to a consistent set of units (e.g., km and hours, or meters and seconds) before applying any formulas.

📊 Exam Strategy & Weightage

Understanding the exam landscape is vital for smart preparation. Here's how Median, Mode & Average Speed typically feature across major government exams:

SSC CGL/CHSL/MTS/CPO/GD

  • Weightage: Moderate (1-2 questions per paper).
  • Question Type:
    • Median/Mode: Often appear in Data Interpretation (DI) sets, where you'll be given a table/graph and asked to find the median/mode of certain data points. Direct questions on small datasets are also common in the Quantitative Aptitude section.
    • Average Speed: A staple topic in the Time, Speed & Distance section. Expect direct problems, sometimes involving two or three parts of a journey, or scenarios like "up and down" journeys (equal distances).
  • Strategy: Master the definitions and formulas. Practice calculating Median/Mode from raw data and from simple frequency distributions. For Average Speed, understand when to use the general formula versus the special cases. Time management is key here.

Banking Exams (SBI PO/Clerk, IBPS PO/Clerk, RRB)

  • Weightage: High (especially Median/Mode in DI).
  • Question Type:
    • Median/Mode: Very frequently asked in Data Interpretation sets. You might need to find the median/mode of profits, sales, number of employees from given data.
    • Average Speed: Regular feature in the Quantitative Aptitude section, often appearing as word problems that might be slightly trickier or multi-step than SSC questions.
  • Strategy: Focus heavily on DI practice for Median and Mode. For Average Speed, ensure you can handle varying distances and times, and apply the correct formula swiftly. Mental calculation and estimation skills are crucial.

Railway Exams (NTPC, Group D, ALP)

  • Weightage: Moderate (1-2 questions).
  • Question Type:
    • Median/Mode: Generally direct and basic calculations from small datasets.
    • Average Speed: Direct word problems from Time, Speed & Distance. Questions might be straightforward applications of the formulas.
  • Strategy: Focus on accuracy and speed for fundamental calculations. Memorize the basic formulas and practice their direct application.

CDS, CAPF, State PCS, Police Exams

  • Weightage: Low to Moderate (1-3 questions, sometimes integrated).
  • Question Type:
    • Median/Mode: Can be direct, or part of a larger statistics question. Sometimes involves slightly larger datasets.
    • Average Speed: Standard Time, Speed & Distance problems. In CDS/CAPF, they might involve a slightly more conceptual understanding or be part of a multi-topic problem.
  • Strategy: Ensure solid conceptual understanding. For state-level exams, direct formula application is more common. For CDS/CAPF, be prepared for slightly more complex reasoning around these basics.

Overall Strategy: For this microtopic, precision in calculation and careful reading of the question are your best friends. Don't fall for common traps, especially with Average Speed!

📌 Summary

  • Median: The middle value of a dataset when arranged in order.
    • Odd 'n': \( \left(\frac{n+1}{2}\right)^{\text{th}} \) term.
    • Even 'n': Average of \( \left(\frac{n}{2}\right)^{\text{th}} \) and \( \left(\frac{n}{2}+1\right)^{\text{th}} \) terms.
  • Mode: The value that appears most frequently in a dataset. Can be single, multiple, or no mode.
  • Average Speed: Total Distance covered divided by Total Time taken.
    • General: $$ \frac{\text{Total Distance}}{\text{Total Time}} $$
    • Special Case (Equal Distances, 2 speeds): $$ \frac{2S_1 S_2}{S_1 + S_2} $$
    • Special Case (Equal Times, 2 speeds): $$ \frac{S_1 + S_2}{2} $$

Always remember to sort data for Median and distinguish between the various average speed scenarios.

🚀 Quick Revision Box: Median, Mode & Average Speed Basics

1. Median: (Middle Value after Ordering)

  • Data Order: ALWAYS arrange data (ascending/descending) first!
  • Odd 'n': \( \text{Median} = \left(\frac{n+1}{2}\right)^{\text{th}} \text{term} \)
  • Even 'n': \( \text{Median} = \frac{\left(\frac{n}{2}\right)^{\text{th}} \text{term} + \left(\frac{n}{2}+1\right)^{\text{th}} \text{term}}{2} \)

2. Mode: (Most Frequent Value)

  • Value(s) with highest frequency.
  • Can be unimodal, bimodal, multimodal, or no mode.
  • No ordering required.

3. Average Speed: (Total Distance / Total Time)

  • General Formula: $$ \text{Avg Speed} = \frac{\sum \text{Distance}}{\sum \text{Time}} $$
  • Special Case (Equal Distances, 2 Speeds): $$ \text{Avg Speed} = \frac{2S_1 S_2}{S_1 + S_2} $$ (e.g., A to B at \( S_1 \), B to A at \( S_2 \))
  • Special Case (Equal Times, 2 Speeds): $$ \text{Avg Speed} = \frac{S_1 + S_2}{2} $$ (e.g., travels for 't' hrs at \( S_1 \), then 't' hrs at \( S_2 \))
  • Common Mistake: Do NOT simply average speeds unless time intervals are equal.

✅ Key Takeaways

  • For Median, sorting your data is non-negotiable. It's the first step every time!
  • The Mode can be flexible – sometimes there's none, sometimes there are many. It's about frequency, not position.
  • The golden rule for Average Speed is \( \frac{\text{Total Distance}}{\text{Total Time}} \). Derive everything from this.
  • Understand the conditions for using the special Average Speed formulas (equal distance vs. equal time) to save precious exam minutes.
  • Be vigilant about units (km/h, m/s) and ensure consistency throughout your calculations.
