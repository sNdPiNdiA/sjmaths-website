Prime Factorization & HCF/LCM Applications

Medium Difficulty 25-30 Mins Read High Exam Weightage Updated 2026

📘 1. Introduction: Prime Factorization & HCF/LCM Applications

Welcome to one of the most fundamental and high-yielding topics in the quantitative aptitude section of government exams. At its core, Prime Factorization is the process of breaking down any composite number into its ultimate, indivisible building blocks—prime numbers. This is governed by the Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic, which states that every integer greater than 1 is either a prime itself or can be represented as a unique product of prime numbers.

Why is this important? Because Prime Factorization acts as the master key to unlocking Highest Common Factor (HCF) and Least Common Multiple (LCM). In competitive exams like SSC CGL, Railways, and Banking, questions are rarely direct. Instead, they are wrapped in real-world scenarios: synchronized traffic lights, circular racing tracks, maximum tile sizing, and distribution of assets. Mastering this topic allows you to decode these complex word problems instantly and solve them with high precision and speed.

🎯 2. Learning Objectives

  • Understand and perform rapid Prime Factorization of large composite numbers.
  • Master the conceptual differences and mathematical relations between HCF and LCM.
  • Apply HCF/LCM properties to find solutions for fractions, decimals, and algebraic terms.
  • Solve complex, remainder-based application problems frequently asked in Tier-1 and Tier-2 exams.
  • Deploy speed-math techniques and option elimination methods to solve questions in under 30 seconds.

📝 3. Prerequisites

Before diving into this microtopic, ensure you are comfortable with the following foundational concepts:

  • Prime Numbers: Numbers greater than 1 that have exactly two factors: 1 and themselves (e.g., 2, 3, 5, 7, 11, 13, 17, 19, ...). Note that 2 is the only even prime number.
  • Divisibility Rules: Quick mental checks for divisibility by 2, 3, 4, 5, 8, 9, and 11.
  • Basic Division and Multiples: Understanding factors (divisors) versus multiples.

💡 4. Core Concepts

Concept 1: Prime Factorization (The Building Blocks)

Definition: Expressing a composite number as a product of powers of prime numbers.

Explanation & Intuition: Every composite number \( N \) can be uniquely written in the standard form: $$N = p_1^{a} \times p_2^{b} \times p_3^{c} \times \dots$$ where \( p_1, p_2, p_3, \dots \) are distinct prime numbers, and \( a, b, c, \dots \) are positive integers representing their powers. Think of prime numbers as "atoms" and composite numbers as "molecules." Factorization is the chemical analysis that breaks the molecule down into its constituent atoms.

Important Observations:

  • The prime factorization of any number is completely unique, disregarding the order of factors.
  • It helps in finding the total number of factors: \( \text{Total Factors} = (a+1)(b+1)(c+1)\dots \)

Memory Tip: When factorizing large numbers, look for obvious factors like \( 10 \) (\( 2 \times 5 \)), \( 4 \) (\( 2^2 \)), or \( 9 \) (\( 3^2 \)) to break the number down rapidly before writing it in final prime power form.

Common Mistake: Forgetting that bases in the final expression must be *primes*. For example, writing \( 48 = 4^2 \times 3^1 \) is NOT prime factorization because 4 is composite. It must be written as \( (2^2)^2 \times 3 = 2^4 \times 3^1 \).

Mini Example: Prime factorize \( 360 \).
\( 360 = 36 \times 10 = (6^2) \times (2 \times 5) = (2 \times 3)^2 \times 2 \times 5 = 2^2 \times 3^2 \times 2^1 \times 5^1 = 2^3 \times 3^2 \times 5^1 \).

Concept 2: Highest Common Factor (HCF / GCD)

Definition: The greatest positive integer that divides two or more integers without leaving a remainder.

How to find using Prime Factorization: Write the prime factorization of all numbers. Identify the common prime bases, and for each common base, take the lowest (minimum) power. Multiply them together. $$\text{HCF} = p_{\text{common}}^{\min(\text{powers})}$$

Intuition: Think of HCF as the "greatest common sharing capability." If you are preparing identical gift baskets from two piles of items, the HCF tells you the maximum number of uniform baskets you can create.

Mini Example: Find HCF of \( 72 \) and \( 120 \).
\( 72 = 2^3 \times 3^2 \)
\( 120 = 2^3 \times 3^1 \times 5^1 \)
Common bases are 2 and 3. Minimum power of 2 is 3, minimum power of 3 is 1 (5 is not common).
\( \text{HCF} = 2^3 \times 3^1 = 8 \times 3 = 24 \).

Concept 3: Least Common Multiple (LCM)

Definition: The smallest positive integer that is perfectly divisible by each of the given numbers.

How to find using Prime Factorization: Write the prime factorization of all numbers. List all prime bases present in any of the factorizations. For each base, take the highest (maximum) power. Multiply them together. $$\text{LCM} = p_{\text{all}}^{\max(\text{powers})}$$

Intuition: LCM is the "first common meeting point" of different repetitive cycles. If runner A laps in 72 seconds and runner B in 120 seconds, they will meet at the starting point after their LCM seconds.

Mini Example: Find LCM of \( 72 \) and \( 120 \).
Using the factorization above: All unique bases involved are 2, 3, and 5.
Maximum power of 2 is 3, of 3 is 2, of 5 is 1.
\( \text{LCM} = 2^3 \times 3^2 \times 5^1 = 8 \times 9 \times 5 = 360 \).

📏 5. Rules & Properties

Rule 1: Product of Two Numbers Property

Explanation: For any two positive integers \( a \) and \( b \):

$$\text{HCF}(a, b) \times \text{LCM}(a, b) = a \times b$$

Reasoning: When finding HCF we take the minimum powers of prime factors, and when finding LCM we take the maximum powers. For any prime factor, the sum of its minimum power and maximum power in the two numbers is exactly equal to the sum of its powers in the product of the two numbers.

Shortcut/Application: If three out of these four values (HCF, LCM, and the two numbers) are known, the fourth can be calculated instantly using division.

⚠️ Exception: This property is ONLY valid for exactly two numbers. For three numbers, \( \text{HCF}(a,b,c) \times \text{LCM}(a,b,c) \neq a \times b \times c \).

Example: If HCF of two numbers is 6 and LCM is 36. If one number is 12, find the other.
\( 6 \times 36 = 12 \times x \implies x = \frac{216}{12} = 18 \).

Rule 2: HCF and LCM of Fractions

Explanation: To find HCF and LCM of a set of fractions:

$$\text{HCF of Fractions} = \frac{\text{HCF of Numerators}}{\text{LCM of Denominators}}$$ $$\text{LCM of Fractions} = \frac{\text{LCM of Numerators}}{\text{HCF of Denominators}}$$

⚠️ Critical Pre-condition: Before applying these formulas, all fractions MUST be reduced to their lowest/simplest forms. Failing to simplify fractions will lead to incorrect answers.

Example: Find LCM of \( \frac{2}{3} \) and \( \frac{8}{9} \). (Both are simplified).
\( \text{LCM} = \frac{\text{LCM}(2, 8)}{\text{HCF}(3, 9)} = \frac{8}{3} \).

Rule 3: HCF and LCM of Decimals

Explanation: To find the HCF/LCM of decimal numbers:

  1. Identify the number with the maximum decimal places.
  2. Convert all other numbers so they have the same number of decimal places by suffixing zeroes.
  3. Remove decimal points and treat them as integers.
  4. Find the HCF/LCM of these integers.
  5. Place the decimal point back in the final answer at the same relative position.

Example: Find LCM of \( 0.6 \), \( 9.6 \), and \( 0.12 \).
Convert to equal decimal places: \( 0.60 \), \( 9.60 \), and \( 0.12 \).
Treat as integers: \( 60, 960, 12 \).
LCM of \( (60, 960, 12) = 960 \).
Place decimal back (2 places): \( 9.60 \) or \( 9.6 \).

📐 6. Formula Box

1. Total Number of Factors

$$\text{If } N = p_1^{a} \times p_2^{b} \times p_3^{c} \dots \implies \text{Number of Factors} = (a+1)(b+1)(c+1)\dots$$

Usage: Finding the count of all divisors of a number.

Memory Trick: Every prime power \( p^a \) offers \( (a+1) \) options for selection (including power 0).

2. HCF Application Remainder Theorems

  • Case A: Find the greatest number that divides \( x, y, z \) leaving remainders \( a, b, c \) respectively.
    Formula: \( \text{Required Number} = \text{HCF}(x-a, y-b, z-c) \)
  • Case B: Find the greatest number that divides \( x, y, z \) leaving the same remainder \( r \) in each case (where \( r \) is unknown).
    Formula: \( \text{Required Number} = \text{HCF}(|x-y|, |y-z|, |z-x|) \)

3. LCM Application Remainder Theorems

  • Case A: Find the least number which when divided by \( x, y, z \) leaves the same remainder \( r \) in each case.
    Formula: \( \text{Required Number} = \text{LCM}(x, y, z) + r \)
  • Case B: Find the least number which when divided by \( x, y, z \) leaves remainders \( a, b, c \) respectively, such that the difference between divisor and remainder is constant (\( x-a = y-b = z-c = K \)).
    Formula: \( \text{Required Number} = \text{LCM}(x, y, z) - K \)

🧠 7. Solved Examples (Easy to Exam Level)

Easy

Example 1: Core Calculation

Question: Find the HCF and LCM of \( \frac{2}{3}, \frac{8}{9}, \text{ and } \frac{16}{81} \).

View Step-by-Step Solution

Step 1: Check Simplicity
All given fractions—\( \frac{2}{3}, \frac{8}{9}, \frac{16}{81} \)—are already in their simplest reduced forms.

Step 2: Calculate HCF of Fractions
$$\text{HCF} = \frac{\text{HCF of Numerators (2, 8, 16)}}{\text{LCM of Denominators (3, 9, 81)}}$$ - Numerators: \( 2 = 2^1 \), \( 8 = 2^3 \), \( 16 = 2^4 \). HCF\( (2, 8, 16) = 2^1 = 2 \).
- Denominators: \( 3 = 3^1 \), \( 9 = 3^2 \), \( 81 = 3^4 \). LCM\( (3, 9, 81) = 3^4 = 81 \).
$$\text{HCF of Fractions} = \frac{2}{81}$$

Step 3: Calculate LCM of Fractions
$$\text{LCM} = \frac{\text{LCM of Numerators (2, 8, 16)}}{\text{HCF of Denominators (3, 9, 81)}}$$ - LCM of Numerators\( (2, 8, 16) = 16 \).
- HCF of Denominators\( (3, 9, 81) = 3 \).
$$\text{LCM of Fractions} = \frac{16}{3}$$

Final Answer: HCF = \( \frac{2}{81} \), LCM = \( \frac{16}{3} \)

Medium

Example 2: Cyclical Word Problem

Question: Four electronic bells toll together at intervals of 6, 8, 12, and 18 seconds respectively. If they toll together now, how many times will they toll together in the next 30 minutes (excluding the initial toll)?

View Step-by-Step Solution

Step 1: Identify key operation
Since we need to find the next point when all intervals meet, this is an LCM problem.

Step 2: Find the LCM of 6, 8, 12, and 18
Let's prime factorize each number: - \( 6 = 2^1 \times 3^1 \)
- \( 8 = 2^3 \)
- \( 12 = 2^2 \times 3^1 \)
- \( 18 = 2^1 \times 3^2 \)
All unique prime bases are 2 and 3. Taking highest powers:
\( \text{LCM} = 2^3 \times 3^2 = 8 \times 9 = 72 \text{ seconds} \).

Step 3: Calculate occurrences in 30 minutes
Convert 30 minutes to seconds: $$\text{Total Seconds} = 30 \times 60 = 1800 \text{ seconds}$$ Number of times they toll together: $$\text{Number of tolls} = \frac{1800}{72} = 25 \text{ times}$$ Note: The question specifies "excluding the initial toll", so we do not add 1. If it said "including the first toll", the answer would be 26.

Final Answer: 25 times

Hard

Example 3: Remainder-Based Number Search

Question: Find the greatest 4-digit number which when divided by 12, 15, 20, and 35 leaves remainders 8, 11, 16, and 31 respectively.

View Step-by-Step Solution

Step 1: Identify the remainder pattern
Let's check the differences between divisors and their respective remainders: - \( 12 - 8 = 4 \)
- \( 15 - 11 = 4 \)
- \( 20 - 16 = 4 \)
- \( 35 - 31 = 4 \)
Since the difference is constant (\( K = 4 \)), we must use the formula: $$\text{Number} = \text{LCM}(12, 15, 20, 35) \times M - K$$

Step 2: Find LCM of divisors
- \( 12 = 2^2 \times 3 \)
- \( 15 = 3 \times 5 \)
- \( 20 = 2^2 \times 5 \)
- \( 35 = 5 \times 7 \)
\( \text{LCM} = 2^2 \times 3 \times 5 \times 7 = 420 \).

Step 3: Target the greatest 4-digit number
The greatest 4-digit number is 9999.
Divide 9999 by the LCM (420) to find the closest multiple: $$9999 \div 420 = 23 \text{ remainder } 339$$ The largest multiple of 420 below 9999 is: $$420 \times 23 = 9660$$

Step 4: Subtract the constant difference \( K \)
$$\text{Required Number} = 9660 - 4 = 9656$$

Final Answer: 9656

Exam Level (SSC CGL Mains)

Example 4: Algebraic Factor Application

Question: The HCF of two three-digit numbers is 17 and their LCM is 714. Find the sum of these two numbers.

View Step-by-Step Solution

Step 1: Set up the algebraic representation
Since HCF is 17, the two numbers can be written as \( 17x \) and \( 17y \), where \( x \) and \( y \) are co-prime integers (they share no common factors other than 1).

Step 2: Use LCM relation
$$\text{LCM}(17x, 17y) = 17xy$$ Given LCM = 714: $$17xy = 714 \implies xy = \frac{714}{17} = 42$$

Step 3: Find co-prime pairs for \( xy = 42 \)
The possible co-prime pairs whose product is 42 are: - Case A: \( (1, 42) \)
- Case B: \( (2, 21) \)
- Case C: \( (3, 14) \)
- Case D: \( (6, 7) \)

Step 4: Filter using the "three-digit" condition
Let's calculate the actual numbers for each case using \( 17x \) and \( 17y \): - For \( (1, 42) \): Numbers are \( 17 \times 1 = 17 \) (not 3-digit) and \( 17 \times 42 = 714 \). (Discard)
- For \( (2, 21) \): Numbers are \( 17 \times 2 = 34 \) (not 3-digit) and \( 17 \times 21 = 357 \). (Discard)
- For \( (3, 14) \): Numbers are \( 17 \times 3 = 51 \) (not 3-digit) and \( 17 \times 14 = 238 \). (Discard)
- For \( (6, 7) \): Numbers are \( 17 \times 6 = 102 \) (3-digit!) and \( 17 \times 7 = 119 \) (3-digit!). (Valid!)

Step 5: Find the sum of the numbers
$$\text{Sum} = 102 + 119 = 221$$

Final Answer: 221

⚡ 8. Exam Tricks & Shortcuts

Trick 1: Difference Method for Finding HCF

The Trick: The HCF of any group of numbers is always either the difference between any two of those numbers or a factor of that difference.

How to Apply: Pick the two closest numbers in the given set. Calculate their difference. The HCF must be a divisor of this difference. Check options instantly!

Example: Find HCF of \( 306, 391, 561 \).
Difference between 306 and 391 is \( 85 \).
Factors of 85 are 1, 5, 17, 85.
Check which ones divide 306. Since 306 is not divisible by 5 or 85, we test 17. \( 306 \div 17 = 18 \). Both 391 and 561 are also divisible by 17. Thus, the HCF is 17! This avoids tedious long division.

Trick 2: Divisibility Check (Option Elimination)

The Trick: Instead of executing the full LCM workflow, test given options for divisibility using simple rules (especially of 9, 3, 5, 11).

How to Apply: If a question asks for a number divisible by 12, 15, and 18: the number must be divisible by 9 (since 18 contains \( 9 \)) and by 5 (since 15 contains \( 5 \)). Sum the digits of options to check divisibility by 9, and ensure the last digit is 0 or 5. This eliminates 3 out of 4 options in seconds!

⚠️ 9. Common Mistakes & Pitfalls

1. Forgetting to Simplify Fractions first

Mistake: Directly applying the fraction formula without simplifying fractions.
Example: Finding HCF of \( \frac{2}{4} \) and \( \frac{3}{6} \). If calculated directly, HCF of numerators is 1, LCM of denominators is 12, giving \( \frac{1}{12} \).
Correct Method: Simplify them first to \( \frac{1}{2} \) and \( \frac{1}{2} \). The real HCF is obviously \( \frac{1}{2} \). Note that \( \frac{1}{12} \neq \frac{1}{2} \).

2. Extrapolating the Product Rule to 3 Numbers

Mistake: Writing \( \text{LCM}(a,b,c) \times \text{HCF}(a,b,c) = a \times b \times c \). This is mathematically incorrect and a common trap in MCQ options.

3. Choosing HCF when LCM is required (and vice-versa)

How to avoid:

  • Choose LCM when the question demands a "future meeting point", "intervals repeating", or a number "divisible BY" other numbers.
  • Choose HCF when the question demands "maximal division", "equal scaling", "equal splits", or a number "dividing" other numbers.

Focus Area: Direct questions on HCF/LCM of fractions, decimals, and direct bell-ringing or track-running applications.

📌 11. Summary

Prime Factorization is the core engine for calculating common structures. HCF represents the maximum value that divides all entities, built from the lowest powers of shared prime components. LCM represents the smallest value divisible by all entities, built using the highest powers of all prime components. For any two numbers, their product equals the product of their HCF and LCM.

🚀 Quick Revision Cheatsheet

Concept/Type Formula / Core Operation Key Tip
Prime Factor Form \( N = p_1^a \times p_2^b \times p_3^c \dots \) All bases must be prime.
HCF (Factorization) Product of minimum powers of common factors If no common factors, HCF = 1 (Co-primes).
LCM (Factorization) Product of maximum powers of all factors Includes terms even if they aren't shared.
Fractions Formula \( \text{HCF} = \frac{\text{HCF(Num)}}{\text{LCM(Den)}} \), \( \text{LCM} = \frac{\text{LCM(Num)}}{\text{HCF(Den)}} \) Simplify fractions before calculations!
Diff Remainder (LCM) \( \text{LCM}(x, y, z) - K \) (where \( K = \text{divisor} - \text{rem} \)) Used when divisor-remainder differences are equal.

🎯 13. Key Takeaways

  • The HCF of co-prime numbers is always 1. Their LCM is equal to their product.
  • Always look at the options first! A simple check for divisibility (especially by 9 or 5) can bypass the entire calculation process.
  • For decimal HCF/LCM, ensure you add trailing zeros to normalize the decimal places before performing calculations.
  • The difference method is the fastest way to compute HCF of large numbers on paper.
