Nuclear Chemistry and Radioactivity

Discovery of Radioactivity

Radioactivity was discovered in 1896 by Henri Becquerel while studying uranium salts that spontaneously emitted penetrating radiation capable of fogging photographic plates. Marie and Pierre Curie subsequently isolated polonium and radium from pitchblende, establishing radioactivity as an atomic property.

UP PGT Chemistry Subject Code 02 B.Sc. / M.Sc. Level

Historical Discovery and Uranium Rays

  • Henri Becquerel observed in 1896 that potassium uranyl sulfate salts emitted radiation that penetrated black paper and exposed wrapped photographic plates.
  • The phenomenon was initially termed Becquerel rays and was proved to be independent of external phosphorescence or sunlight exposure.
  • Marie Curie coined the term radioactivity in 1898 after discovering that thorium compounds also exhibited identical spontaneous radiation properties.
  • Pitchblende ore was found to possess higher specific activity than pure uranium, leading to the chemical separation of polonium (atomic number 84) and radium (atomic number 88).

Characteristics of Radioactive Emissions

  • Rutherford utilized electric and magnetic fields to separate radioactive emissions into three distinct components: alpha (α), beta (β), and gamma (γ) rays.
  • Alpha particles are doubly ionized helium nuclei (He2+) with low penetration and high ionization power, deflected toward the negative plate.
  • Beta particles are high-speed electrons (e-) or positrons (e+) exhibiting intermediate penetration and deflection toward the positive plate.
  • Gamma rays are high-frequency electromagnetic radiation (photons) with zero mass and charge, showing no deflection in electromagnetic fields.

Nuclear Kinetics and Atomic Transmutation

  • Radioactive decay is a strictly first-order kinetic process whose rate depends only on the number of undecayed radioactive atoms present.
  • Soddy and Fajans' Group Displacement Law dictates that alpha emission yields a daughter nuclide with mass number decreased by 4 and atomic number decreased by 2.
  • Beta-minus emission converts a neutron into a proton within the nucleus, increasing the atomic number by 1 while keeping the mass number constant.
  • Radioactive disintegration is completely unaltered by external thermodynamic variables such as temperature, pressure, or chemical combination.
📐 Key Formulas & Equations
Half-Life Expression
t1/2 = ln(2) / λ = 0.693 / λ
Conditions: Applies to any radioactive nuclide undergoing first-order decay
Units: t1/2 = seconds, minutes, years; λ = s-1, min-1, yr-1
Mean Life Expression
τ = 1 / λ
Conditions: Average lifetime of a radioactive nucleus
Units: τ = seconds or applicable time unit
Activity of a Radioactive Sample
A = -λN = A0e-λt
Conditions: Rate of nuclear disintegration per unit time
Units: A = Becquerel (Bq) or Curies (Ci), where 1 Ci = 3.7 × 1010 disintegrations/sec
Decay Fraction Remaining
N(t) / N0 = (1/2)n
Conditions: Where n is the number of half-lives elapsed (n = t / t1/2)
Units: Dimensionless ratio
Radioactive Decay Law
N(t) = N0e-λt
Conditions: For independent, random first-order nuclear disintegrations
Units: N(t) = nuclei at time t, N0 = initial nuclei, λ = decay constant (s-1)
⚠️ Important Exceptions & Anomalies
Exception Radioactive decay rates are generally independent of external environmental conditions.

Observation: Electron capture (K-capture) decay rates can be marginally altered. — Changes in extreme chemical environments or high pressure alter inner-shell electron density slightly, affecting the capture probability.

Exception Alpha decay decreases atomic number by 2 and mass number by 4.

Observation: Cluster decay involves emission of heavier nuclei such as 14C instead of standard α particles. — Occurs in very heavy actinides due to favorable binding energy release for specific heavy fragments.

Exception Gamma emission occurs immediately following alpha or beta decay.

Observation: Nuclear isomers exhibit metastable states (m) with measurable, long half-lives before gamma de-excitation. — High spin changes or parity forbidden transitions trap the nucleus in an excited state for extended periods.

💡 Shortcuts & Memory Aids
Half-Life Fraction Rule
N = N0 / (2n)

Application: Use this directly when the elapsed time is an exact multiple of the half-life (τ = n × t1/2) to avoid logarithms.

Alpha-Beta Shift Trick
ΔZ = 2nα - nβ

Application: Quickly compute atomic number changes in a decay series by tracking net α and β emissions.

Curie to Becquerel Conversion
1 Ci = 3.7 × 1010 Bq

Application: Multiply given activities in Curies by 3.7 × 1010 to instantly convert to SI units (disintegrations per second).

🎯 Key Exam Points
  • Radioactivity was discovered by Henri Becquerel in 1896 using uranium salts and photographic plates.
  • Marie and Pierre Curie discovered polonium and radium in 1898 from pitchblende ore.
  • Radioactive decay follows first-order kinetics with decay rate -dN/dt = λN.
  • The unit of radioactivity in SI is Becquerel (Bq), equal to one disintegration per second.
  • Alpha emission reduces atomic number Z by 2 and mass number A by 4.
  • Beta-minus emission increases atomic number Z by 1 with no change in mass number A.
🚫 Common Errors & Confusions
  • Assuming radioactive decay rate is affected by temperature or pressure changes; it is purely a nuclear phenomenon.
  • Confusing mean life (τ) with half-life (t1/2); remember τ = 1/λ while t1/2 = 0.693/λ.
  • Forgetting that gamma rays carry no mass or charge, thus altering neither Z nor A upon emission.
  • Using natural logarithms incorrectly when calculating time elapsed from exponential decay equations.

⚡ Quick Revision: Discovery of Radioactivity

Core formulas, rapid recall points, and distinguishing criteria at a glance.

Discovery and Fundamental Nature

Radioactivity is the spontaneous disintegration of unstable atomic nuclei accompanied by the emission of energetic particles or electromagnetic radiation, discovered by Henri Becquerel.

Radiation Types and Properties

Emissions are categorized into alpha particles (helium nuclei), beta particles (high-speed electrons), and gamma rays (high-energy photons) differing in charge, mass, and penetration.

Displacement Laws

Soddy-Fajans displacement laws govern atomic number and mass number alterations during alpha and beta decay pathways.

Decay Kinetics

Nuclear disintegration is a statistical, first-order process characterized by half-life, decay constant, and activity equations independent of chemical state.

📚 Terms & Units Glossary

Radioactivity (Spontaneous Nuclear Decay)
Spontaneous emission of radiation from unstable atomic nuclei. SI unit: Bq.
Decay Constant (λ)
Fraction of nuclei decaying per unit time. SI unit: s-1.
Half-Life (t1/2)
Time required for half of the radioactive nuclei to disintegrate. SI unit: s.
Alpha Particle (42He)
Helium nucleus emitted during heavy nuclide decay with +2 charge.
Beta Particle (0-1e)
High-velocity electron originating from nuclear neutron conversion.
Gamma Radiation (hν)
Short-wavelength electromagnetic photon emitted from excited nuclear states.

🎯 Key Takeaways

  • t1/2 = 0.693 / λ
  • N(t) = N0e-λt
  • 1 Curie (Ci) = 3.7 × 1010 disintegrations/second
  • Alpha decay: Z → Z - 2, A → A - 4
  • Beta-minus decay: Z → Z + 1, A remains unchanged

📌 Quick Recall

  • Radioactivity was discovered in 1896 by Henri Becquerel using uranium minerals.
  • The Curies isolated polonium and radium, establishing that radioactivity is an atomic property.
  • Three distinct rays (α, β, γ) are emitted, possessing varying charge, mass, and penetrating power.
  • Decay kinetics follow first-order rate laws completely independent of external physical conditions.

⚖️ Key Differences

Atomic Number (Z) VS Mass Number (A)

Z changes in both alpha and beta decays, whereas A changes only in alpha decay.

Explanation: Mean life is defined mathematically as the reciprocal of the decay constant (τ = 1 / λ).

What is emitted during electron capture (K-capture)?

Explanation: When a nucleus captures an inner-shell electron, outer electrons drop down to fill the vacancy, emitting characteristic X-rays.

Question 16 of 18

If 75% of a radioactive substance decays in 20 minutes, what is its half-life?

Explanation: 75% decay means 25% remains, which is (1/2)2. Thus, 2 half-lives have elapsed in 20 minutes, giving t1/2 = 10 minutes.

Explanation: Ernest Rutherford characterized radioactive emissions by studying their deflection in magnetic and electric fields.

Question 18 of 18

What is the electrical charge of a beta-plus (positron) particle?

Explanation: A positron is the antiparticle of an electron, possessing a positive elementary charge (+1).

Question 4 of 10
The emission of a beta particle results in:

Solution: Beta-minus decay involves a neutron converting into a proton, emitting an electron.

Question 5 of 10
Which of the following is true for radioactive decay law?

Solution: The rate of disintegration is directly proportional to the number of radioactive atoms present at that instant (-dN/dt = λN).

Question 6 of 10
Who isolated radium for the first time?

Solution: Marie and Pierre Curie successfully isolated radium from pitchblende.

Question 7 of 10
What happens to the mass number when a nuclide emits a gamma photon?

Solution: Gamma rays are high-energy photons; their emission does not alter mass or atomic number.

Question 8 of 10
If the activity of a radioactive sample drops to 1/8th of its initial value in 15 days, what is its half-life?

Solution: 1/8 = (1/2)3, so 3 half-lives equal 15 days, giving t1/2 = 5 days.

Question 9 of 10

Solution: Gamma rays are electromagnetic quanta with no rest mass and neutral charge.
