Nuclear Chemistry and Radioactivity

Laws of Radioactive Decay

Radioactive decay is a first-order kinetic process where unstable atomic nuclei spontaneously disintegrate into more stable configurations. The rate of disintegration is directly proportional to the number of undecayed radioactive nuclei present at any given time.

UP PGT Chemistry Subject Code 02 B.Sc. / M.Sc. Level

First-Order Kinetics of Radioactive Disintegration

  • Radioactive decay is strictly a first-order kinetic process independent of external physical and chemical conditions like temperature and pressure.
  • The decay constant (λ) is a unique characteristic property of each radioactive isotope.
  • The logarithmic form of the decay law is given by ln(Nt/N0) = -λt or 2.303 log(N0/Nt) = λt.

Half-Life and Mean Life Relationships

  • The half-life (t1/2) is defined as the time required for half of the initial radioactive nuclei to decay.
  • The mean life (τ) represents the average lifetime of all radioactive nuclei, equal to the sum of lives of all atoms divided by the total number of atoms.
  • Radioactive decay follows statistical probability; it is impossible to predict the exact moment a specific nucleus will decay.

Activity and Specific Activity

  • Activity (A) of a radioactive sample is the rate of decay, expressed as A = -λN = A0e-λt.
  • The SI unit of radioactivity is the Becquerel (Bq), defined as one disintegration per second (dps).
  • Curie (Ci) is a traditional unit of radioactivity, where 1 Ci = 3.7 × 1010 disintegrations per second.
📐 Key Formulas & Equations
Integrated Rate Law
Nt = N0e-λt
Conditions: For calculating remaining nuclei after time t.
Units: Nt = remaining nuclei at time t, N0 = initial nuclei
Half-Life Formula
t1/2 = ln(2) / λ = 0.693 / λ
Conditions: Constant for a given radioactive isotope regardless of initial concentration.
Units: t1/2 = time units (years, days, seconds)
Mean Life Formula
τ = 1 / λ = 1.443 × t1/2
Conditions: Reciprocal of the radioactive decay constant.
Units: τ = time units
Fraction Remaining After n Half-Lives
Nt / N0 = (1 / 2)n
Conditions: Where n = t / t1/2
Units: Dimensionless ratio
Rate Law of Radioactive Decay
-dN/dt = λN
Conditions: Applies to spontaneous nuclear disintegration of a single radionuclide.
Units: N = number of nuclei, t = time in seconds, λ = decay constant in s-1
⚠️ Important Exceptions & Anomalies
Exception Radioactive decay rates are completely independent of temperature and pressure.

Observation: Electron Capture and Internal Conversion — Electron capture decay rates can be slightly altered (by fractions of a percent) if the electron density around the nucleus changes due to extreme chemical environments or high pressures.

Exception Half-life of a radioactive isotope is strictly constant.

Observation: Mössbauer nuclides and isomeric transitions — Chemical binding states can marginally alter the internal conversion coefficient and hence the observed decay rate for low-energy nuclear transitions.

💡 Shortcuts & Memory Aids
Fraction Remaining Shortcut
Nt = N0 / (2t / t1/2)

Application: Use this exponent formula directly when time elapsed is an exact multiple of the half-life to bypass logarithms.

Activity Percentage Rule
Remaining % = 100 × (0.5)n

Application: Quickly calculate remaining percentage after n half-lives (e.g., 3 half-lives = 100 × (1/8) = 12.5%).

Decay Constant to Half-Life Conversion
t1/2 × λ = 0.693

Application: Remember 0.693 as ln(2) to instantly cross-calculate decay constant from half-life and vice versa in numerical problems.

🎯 Key Exam Points
  • Radioactive disintegration obeys first-order chemical kinetics.
  • The decay constant depends only on the nuclear species and is unaffected by external variables.
  • The unit of radioactivity in the SI system is Becquerel (Bq).
  • 1 Curie (Ci) equals 3.7 × 1010 disintegrations per second.
  • Mean life is 1.443 times the half-life of the radioactive substance.
  • After 10 half-lives, the fraction of remaining radioactive material is approximately (1/2)10 or 0.098%.
🚫 Common Errors & Confusions
  • Confusing half-life with mean life formulas; remember mean life is greater than half-life.
  • Assuming radioactive decay is a zero-order or second-order reaction.
  • Forgetting to convert units of time when calculating the decay constant (λ).
  • Neglecting base-10 to natural log conversion (2.303) when using common logarithms.

⚡ Quick Revision: Laws of Radioactive Decay

Core formulas, rapid recall points, and distinguishing criteria at a glance.

Rate Law Fundamentals

Radioactivity is governed by statistical laws where the rate of disintegration is proportional to the number of atoms present: -dN/dt = λN.

Integrated Decay Equations

Integration yields Nt = N0e-λt, allowing precise calculation of remaining radioactive substance over elapsed time.

Half-Life Independence

The half-life t1/2 = 0.693/λ is characteristic of each radionuclide and remains constant throughout the decay process.

Units of Radioactivity

Activity is measured in Becquerel (Bq) or Curie (Ci), quantifying total nuclear disintegrations per unit time.

📚 Terms & Units Glossary

Decay Constant (λ)
Proportionality constant in radioactive decay rate law, measured in s-1.
Half-Life (t1/2)
Time required for activity or nuclei count to reduce to half its initial value.
Mean Life (τ)
Average lifespan of radioactive atoms, equal to 1/λ.
Activity (A)
Number of disintegrations per unit time, SI unit Bq.
Becquerel (Bq)
SI unit of radioactivity equal to one disintegration per second.
Curie (Ci)
Traditional unit equal to 3.7 × 1010 disintegrations per second.

🎯 Key Takeaways

  • Rate law: -dN/dt = λN
  • Integrated law: Nt = N0e-λt
  • Half-life: t1/2 = 0.693 / λ
  • Mean life: τ = 1 / λ
  • Activity relation: A = λN

📌 Quick Recall

  • Radioactive decay follows first-order kinetics.
  • Decay rate is independent of temperature, pressure, and chemical state.
  • Half-life is inversely proportional to the decay constant.
  • Remaining nuclei decrease exponentially with time.

⚖️ Key Differences

Half-life (t1/2) VS Mean life (τ)

Half-life is 0.693 times the mean life; mean life is the inverse of the decay constant.

Question 14 of 18

If 75% of a radioactive substance decays in 20 minutes, what is its half-life?

Question 1 of 10
What is the decay constant of a radioactive isotope whose half-life is 69.3 seconds?

Solution: λ = 0.693 / t1/2 = 0.693 / 69.3 = 0.01 s-1.

Question 2 of 10
The half-life of Carbon-14 is 5730 years. What fraction of 14C remains after 17190 years?

Solution: 17190 / 5730 = 3 half-lives. Remaining fraction = (1/2)3 = 1/8.

Question 3 of 10
Which parameter remains constant throughout a radioactive decay process?

Solution: The decay constant (λ) is a fixed physical property of a specific radioactive nuclide.

Question 4 of 10
If 80% of a radioactive element undergoes decay in 40 minutes, what is its half-life?

Solution: Remaining = 20% (0.2). Using t = (2.303 / λ) log(100/20), t1/2 = 40 × 0.3010 / log(5) ≈ 17.2 minutes.

Question 5 of 10
What does the slope of the plot of ln(Nt) versus time (t) represent in radioactive decay?

Solution: From ln(Nt) = ln(N0) - λt, the slope of ln(Nt) vs t is -λ.

Question 6 of 10
A radioactive isotope has a decay constant of 0.05 day-1. What is its mean life?

Solution: Mean life τ = 1 / λ = 1 / 0.05 = 20 days.

Question 7 of 10
The unit disintegrations per minute (dpm) is a measure of:

Solution: Disintegrations per minute measure the rate of decay, which is radioactivity activity.

Question 8 of 10
How many half-lives have passed when a radioactive sample is 93.75% decayed?

Solution: If 93.75% is decayed, 6.25% remains. 6.25% = (1/2)4 × 100, so 4 half-lives have passed.

Question 9 of 10
Why is radioactive decay independent of chemical bonding?

Solution: Radioactive decay is a nuclear process governed by strong and weak nuclear forces within the nucleus, far removed from valence electrons.

Question 10 of 10
If initial activity is A0, what is the activity after 2 half-lives?

Solution: Activity decays exponentially just like the number of nuclei: At = A0 / (2)2 = A0 / 4.
