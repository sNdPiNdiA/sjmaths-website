Half-Life of Radioactive Decay

Radioactive decay follows first-order kinetics where the rate of disintegration is directly proportional to the number of undecayed nuclei present. The half-life of a radioactive isotope is defined as the time required for exactly one-half of the initial quantity of radioactive nuclei to undergo decay.

UP PGT Chemistry Subject Code 02 B.Sc. / M.Sc. Level

Kinetics of Radioactive Disintegration

  • Radioactive decay is strictly a first-order kinetic process governed by the differential rate law -dN/dt = λN.
  • The decay constant (λ) represents the probability of decay of a single nucleus per unit time.
  • The integrated rate expression uses natural logarithms: ln(N/N0) = -λt.
  • Radioactive half-life is independent of temperature, pressure, and chemical combination state.

Mathematical Derivation of Half-Life

  • Setting N = N0/2 at t = t1/2 in the integrated rate equation yields λt1/2 = ln(2).
  • Substituting the numerical value ln(2) = 0.6930 makes t1/2 = 0.693/λ.
  • The fraction of a radioactive substance remaining after n half-lives is calculated as (1/2)n.
  • The fraction decayed after n half-lives is expressed mathematically as 1 - (1/2)n.

Activity and Mean Life Relations

  • Radioactive activity (A) decreases exponentially with time according to the relation A = A0e-λt.
  • One Curie (Ci) equals exactly 3.7 × 1010 disintegrations per second (dps).
  • The average life (τ) is the sum of the lives of all atoms divided by the total number of atoms initially present.
  • Seventy-five percent (75%) of a radioactive sample decays completely after exactly two half-lives.

⚠️ Important Exceptions & Anomalies
Exception Radioactive decay rates are generally independent of external physical conditions.

Observation: Electron capture (K-capture) decay rates — Electron capture depends on the electron density near the nucleus, which can be slightly altered by extreme chemical environments or high pressures.

Exception Half-life of a given isotope is completely constant.

Observation: Apparent half-life in geochemical dating systems — Open geological systems experience loss or gain of parent/daughter nuclides, leading to altered isotopic ratios.

🎯 Key Exam Points
  • Radioactive decay follows strictly first-order kinetics with molecularity not applicable.
  • The half-life of Carbon-14 used in radiocarbon dating is approximately 5730 years.
  • Uranium-238 decays through a series of alpha and beta emissions to form stable Lead-206.
  • The SI unit of radioactivity is Becquerel (Bq), where 1 Bq = 1 disintegration per second.
  • After 10 half-lives, the fraction of the remaining radioactive substance is (1/2)10 = 1/1024.
  • Artificial radioactivity was first discovered by Irène Joliot-Curie and Frédéric Joliot.
🚫 Common Errors & Confusions
  • Confusing average life (τ) with half-life (t1/2); remember τ is 1.44 times larger.
  • Assuming radioactive decay rate depends on initial mass; it is a first-order rate process.
  • Forgetting to convert time units when calculating decay constants (λ) and half-lives.
  • Confusing activity units: 1 Curie = 3.7 × 1010 Bq, not per minute.

⚡ Quick Revision: Half-Life of Radioactive Decay

Core formulas, rapid recall points, and distinguishing criteria at a glance.

First-Order Nature

Radioactive disintegration is a spontaneous first-order process where the rate depends solely on the number of active nuclei.

Half-Life Independence

The half-life t1/2 = 0.693/λ remains completely constant throughout the decay and is independent of initial concentration.

Average Life Relational Constant

The average life (τ) represents the reciprocal of the decay constant and equals 1.443 times the half-life.

Radioactive Equilibrium

In a decay series, secular equilibrium occurs when the half-life of the parent nuclide is much greater than that of the daughter nuclide.

🎯 Key Takeaways

  • t1/2 = 0.693 / λ
  • N = N0(1/2)t/t1/2
  • τ = 1.443 × t1/2
  • A = λN
  • Radioactive decay is independent of temperature and pressure.

📌 Quick Recall

  • Half-life is constant for a given radioactive isotope.
  • Decay kinetics follow first-order integrated rate laws.
  • Activity decreases exponentially with time.
  • Remaining fraction after n half-lives is (1/2)n.

⚖️ Key Differences

Half-life (t1/2) VS Average life (τ)

Half-life is the time for 50% decay, whereas average life is the statistical average lifetime of all nuclei (τ = 1.443 t1/2).

The radioactive decay follows which order of reaction?

Question 4 of 19

The relationship between average life (τ) and half-life (t1/2) is:

Units of the radioactive decay constant (λ) are:

Explanation: Since rate = λN, λ has dimensions of time-1 (e.g., s-1, min-1, year-1).

Question 6 of 19

What percentage of a radioactive substance is left after 4 half-lives?

Explanation: After 4 half-lives, remaining fraction = (1/2)4 = 1/16 = 0.0625 or 6.25%.

Question 7 of 19

Which factor affects the half-life of a radioactive isotope?

Explanation: Radioactive decay is a nuclear phenomenon completely unaffected by external physical or chemical conditions.

Question 8 of 19

If 10g of a radioactive isotope decays to 1.25g in 30 days, what is its half-life?

Explanation: 10g → 5g (1 t1/2) → 2.5g (2 t1/2) → 1.25g (3 t1/2). Total time = 3 t1/2 = 30 days, so t1/2 = 10 days.

Question 9 of 19

One Curie of radioactivity is equal to:

Explanation: One Curie (Ci) is defined as exactly 3.7 × 1010 dps.

Question 10 of 19

The decay constant of a radioactive substance is 0.0231 day-1. Its half-life is:

Explanation: t1/2 = 0.693 / 0.0231 = 30 days.

Question 11 of 19

What is the integrated rate equation for radioactive decay?

Explanation: The first-order rate expression in terms of natural logarithm is k = (1/t) ln(N0/N).

Question 12 of 19

After how many half-lives does a radioactive substance decay to nearly 99.9% completion?

Explanation: After 10 half-lives, 1 - (1/2)10 = 1 - 1/1024 = 99.902% has decayed.

Question 13 of 19

Which of the following represents radioactive activity?

Explanation: Activity is defined as the rate of decay of radioactive nuclei, given by A = -dN/dt = λN.

Question 14 of 19

If the half-life of a radioactive element is 14 days, what fraction decays in 42 days?

Explanation: Number of half-lives n = 42 / 14 = 3. Remaining fraction = 1/8. Decayed fraction = 1 - 1/8 = 7/8.

Question 15 of 19

The slope of the straight line obtained by plotting ln(N) versus time (t) for radioactive decay is:

Explanation: From ln(N) = ln(N0) - λt, the slope is -λ.

Question 16 of 19

Explanation: Radiocarbon dating measures the changing ratio of radioactive C-14 to stable C-12 in organic remains.

Question 17 of 19

Which particle emission does not alter the mass number of a radionuclide?

Explanation: Beta emission (electron or positron) changes atomic number by ±1 but leaves mass number unchanged.

Question 18 of 19

If half-life is independent of initial concentration, the reaction order is:

Explanation: Only first-order reactions possess a half-life that is entirely independent of initial concentration.

Question 19 of 19

Explanation: The SI derived unit for radioactivity activity is the Becquerel (Bq).

Question 1 of 10
If 75% of a first-order radioactive substance decays in 60 minutes, what is its half-life?

Solution: 75% decay means 2 half-lives have passed (50% then 75%). Therefore, 2 t1/2 = 60 min, so t1/2 = 30 minutes.

Question 2 of 10
The half-life of Radium-226 is 1620 years. After how many years will 1 gram of radium be reduced to 0.125 gram?

Solution: 1g → 0.5g (1) → 0.25g (2) → 0.125g (3 half-lives). Time = 3 × 1620 = 4860 years.

Question 3 of 10
Which radioactive isotope is commonly used in dating archaeological wood and charcoal specimens?

Solution: Carbon-14 (radiocarbon dating) is used for dating organic carbon remains up to 50,000 years old.

Question 4 of 10
If the decay constant λ of a nuclide is 6.93 × 10-4 s-1, what is its half-life?

Solution: t1/2 = 0.693 / (6.93 × 10-4) = 1000 seconds? Wait, 0.693 / 6.93e-4 = 1000 s.

Question 5 of 10
The activity of a radioactive sample drops to 1/16th of its initial value in 40 hours. The half-life is:

Solution: 1/16th corresponds to 4 half-lives ((1/2)4). Total time = 40 hours, so t1/2 = 40 / 4 = 10 hours.

Question 6 of 10
What is the unit of the decay constant λ?

Solution: Decay constant has dimensions of inverse time, such as s-1 or year-1.

Question 7 of 10
In radioactive disintegration, the number of undecayed nuclei N at time t is given by:

Solution: The exponential decay law is N = N0e-λt.

Question 8 of 10
A radioactive nuclide has a half-life of 20 days. What fraction of the sample will remain undecayed after 60 days?

Solution: n = 60 / 20 = 3 half-lives. Remaining fraction = (1/2)3 = 1/8.

Question 9 of 10
Which of the following statements about radioactive half-life is incorrect?

Solution: Radioactive decay half-life is independent of external temperature and pressure.

Question 10 of 10
If the average life of a radioactive element is 200 days, its half-life is approximately:

Solution: t1/2 = τ / 1.443 = 200 / 1.443 ≈ 138.6 days.
