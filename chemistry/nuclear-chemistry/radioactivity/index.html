Nuclear Chemistry and Radioactivity

Radioactivity

Radioactivity is the spontaneous disintegration of unstable atomic nuclei accompanied by the emission of ionizing radiation such as alpha, beta, and gamma rays. The process follows first-order kinetics and is independent of external physical and chemical conditions such as temperature and pressure.

UP PGT Chemistry Subject Code 02 B.Sc. / M.Sc. Level

Types of Radioactive Emissions and Properties

  • Alpha particles consist of doubly ionized helium nuclei (2He4) with high ionizing power and low penetration range.
  • Beta particles are high-speed electrons (-1e0) emitted from the nucleus during neutron-to-proton conversion, possessing moderate penetration.
  • Gamma rays are high-frequency electromagnetic radiation emitted to dissipate excess nuclear energy, possessing extremely high penetration power.

Nuclear Stability and N/Z Ratio

  • Nuclei lying within the Zone of Stability are stable, while those outside undergo spontaneous radioactive decay.
  • For light nuclei (Z < 20), the stable neutron-to-proton ratio (N/Z) is approximately 1:1.
  • Heavy nuclei with Z > 83 fall above the belt of stability and predominantly undergo alpha decay to reduce both proton and neutron numbers.

Radioactive Disintegration Series

  • Naturally occurring radioactive elements decay through successive emissions until forming stable non-radioactive lead isotopes.
  • The Thorium series (4n), Uranium series (4n+2), and Actinium series (4n+3) represent major natural decay chains.
  • The Neptunium series (4n+1) is a synthetic decay chain terminating at stable bismuth-209.

Integrated Radioactive Decay Law
N_t = N_0 e-λ t or t = 2.303λ \log(N_0N_t)
Conditions: For calculating remaining nuclei or mass after elapsed time t
Units: N_t and N_0 in moles or number of atoms, t in seconds or years
Half-Life Period
t1/2 = 0.693λ
Conditions: Time required for half of the initial radioactive nuclei to decay
Units: t1/2 in time units, λ in inverse time units
Average Life Period
τ = 1λ = 1.443 × t1/2
Conditions: Reciprocal of the radioactive decay constant
Units: τ in time units
Group Displacement Law (Soddy-Fajans)
ZXA \xrightarrow{α} Z-2YA-4 + 2He4
Conditions: Alpha decay resulting in loss of 2 protons and 2 neutrons
Units: Mass number A, Atomic number Z
Rate Law of Radioactive Decay
- dNdt = λ N
Conditions: Applies to any radioactive nuclide undergoing spontaneous decay
Units: dNdt in disintegrations per second, N is number of nuclei, λ in s-1
⚠️ Important Exceptions & Anomalies
Exception Radioactive decay rates are completely independent of external conditions.

Observation: Electron capture decay rates show extremely slight variations. — Electron capture depends on the electron density of inner shell orbitals, which can be marginally altered under immense chemical pressures or ionization states.

Exception All isotopes of elements beyond bismuth (Z > 83) are radioactive.

Observation: Technetium (Z = 43) and Promethium (Z = 61) are radioactive despite low atomic numbers. — Absence of stable isotopes due to unfavorable nuclear packing fraction and binding energy constraints in odd-odd nuclear configurations.

💡 Shortcuts & Memory Aids
Fraction Remaining Shortcut
N_tN_0 = (12)^n

Application: Where n is the number of half-lives elapsed (n = t / t1/2). Instantly calculate remaining mass without logarithms.

Alpha-Beta Count Mnemonic
Number of α = Ainitial - Afinal4

Application: Use mass number change solely for alpha determination, then use atomic number change to find beta emissions: Zfinal = Zinitial - 2(α) + β.

Radioactive Equilibrium Relation
λ_1 N_1 = λ_2 N_2

Application: In secular radioactive equilibrium, the decay rate of parent equals the decay rate of daughter nuclide.

🎯 Key Exam Points
  • Radioactivity is strictly a first-order kinetic process obeying the exponential decay law.
  • The half-life of a radioactive isotope is independent of its initial concentration and temperature.
  • Carbon-14 dating is utilized for organic archaeological specimens up to 50,000 years old.
  • Nuclear binding energy per nucleon reaches a maximum around mass number A = 56 (Iron).
  • Positron emission occurs in proton-rich nuclei to convert a proton into a neutron.
  • Geiger-Muller counter and scintillation counters are standard instruments for detecting radioactivity.
🚫 Common Errors & Confusions
  • Assuming radioactive decay rates depend on temperature or pressure like ordinary chemical reactions.
  • Confusing average life (τ) with half-life (t1/2); remember τ = 1.443 × t1/2.
  • Forgetting that beta emission increases the atomic number by one unit while mass number remains unchanged.
  • Applying incorrect units for decay constant λ when calculating time in years versus seconds.

⚡ Quick Revision: Radioactivity

Core formulas, rapid recall points, and distinguishing criteria at a glance.

Radioactive Decay Kinetics

Radioactive decay is unimolecular and follows first-order rate laws where the rate of disintegration is directly proportional to the number of active nuclei present.

Soddy-Fajans Displacement Laws

Emission of an alpha particle reduces mass number by 4 and atomic number by 2, while beta emission leaves mass number unchanged and increases atomic number by 1.

Nuclear Stability Rules

Even numbers of protons and neutrons confer extra nuclear stability, while odd-odd combinations are generally unstable and radioactive.

Artificial Transmutation and Fission

Bombardment of stable nuclei with high-energy particles induces artificial radioactivity and nuclear fission reactions releasing tremendous energy.

📚 Terms & Units Glossary

Radioactivity (- dNdt = λ N)
Spontaneous disintegration of unstable atomic nuclei with SI unit Becquerel (Bq).
Decay Constant (λ)
Proportionality constant representing fractional decay per unit time.
Half-life (t1/2)
Time interval required for activity or nuclei count to reduce to half.
Average Life (τ)
Sum of life spans of all nuclei divided by initial number of nuclei.
Binding Energy (BE = Δ m c2)
Energy released when nucleons assemble to form a stable nucleus.
Curie (Ci)
Unit of radioactivity equal to 3.7 × 1010 disintegrations per second.

🎯 Key Takeaways

  • t1/2 = 0.693 / λ
  • N_t = N_0 (1/2)^n
  • Magic numbers for nuclear stability: 2, 8, 20, 28, 50, 82, 126
  • Alpha particle = 2He4
  • Beta particle = -1e0

📌 Quick Recall

  • Radioactivity is a nuclear property unaffected by chemical bonding or physical state.
  • First-order decay equations allow determination of geological and archaeological ages.
  • Alpha, beta, and gamma emissions alter nuclear composition to attain stability.
  • Nuclear binding energy per nucleon dictates overall stability trends.

⚖️ Key Differences

Atomic Number (Z) VS Mass Number (A)

Z represents the number of protons and changes during alpha/beta decay, whereas A represents total nucleons and remains unchanged during beta decay.

Question 1 of 18

Which of the following radiations has the highest ionizing power?

Explanation: Alpha particles possess the highest mass and charge among natural radiations, leading to maximum ionization of matter along their short path.

Question 2 of 18

The half-life of a radioactive element is 10 days. What fraction remains after 30 days?

Explanation: Number of half-lives n = 30 / 10 = 3. Remaining fraction = (1/2)3 = 1/8.

Question 3 of 18

What is the change in mass number and atomic number when an alpha particle is emitted?

Explanation: Alpha emission (2He4) results in loss of 2 protons and 2 neutrons, decreasing mass number by 4 and atomic number by 2.

Question 4 of 18

Which decay process results in an increase in atomic number by 1?

Explanation: In beta-minus decay, a neutron converts to a proton (0n1 → 1p1 + -1e0), increasing atomic number by 1.

Question 5 of 18

Radioactive disintegration is an example of:

Explanation: Radioactive decay is strictly a first-order kinetic process where the rate depends linearly on the concentration of undecayed nuclei.

Question 6 of 18

The unit of radioactivity 'Curie' is defined as:

Explanation: One curie (Ci) equals 3.7 x 1010 disintegrations per second, equivalent to the activity of 1 gram of radium-226.

Question 7 of 18

Which of the following nuclides is most likely to undergo positron emission?

Explanation: Proton-rich nuclei lie below the zone of stability and decay via positron emission or electron capture to increase the neutron-to-proton ratio.

Question 8 of 18

What is the relationship between decay constant (λ) and half-life (t1/2)?

Explanation: Derived from integrated first-order rate law, t1/2 = \ln(2) / λ = 0.693 / λ.

Question 9 of 18

Gamma rays are:

Explanation: Gamma rays are short-wavelength electromagnetic photons emitted from excited nuclear energy states.

Question 10 of 18

Which series of natural radioactive decay ends at stable lead-206?

Explanation: The Uranium series (4n+2) originates from 92U238 and terminates at stable 82Pb206.

Question 11 of 18

Carbon-14 dating is based on the ratio of carbon-14 to what isotope?

Explanation: Radiocarbon dating measures the decreasing ratio of radioactive carbon-14 to stable carbon-12 in dead organic matter.

Question 12 of 18

Which of the following statements about radioactive decay is incorrect?

Explanation: Radioactive decay rate constants are entirely independent of external temperature, pressure, or chemical environment.

Question 13 of 18

What is the average life (τ) of a radioactive substance in terms of its decay constant (λ)?

Explanation: Average life is defined as the reciprocal of the decay constant, τ = 1 / λ.

Question 14 of 18

A nuclide ZXA emits 2 alpha particles and 2 beta particles. The resulting nuclide is:

Explanation: Two alpha emissions decrease Z by 4 and A by 8. Two beta emissions increase Z by 2 while leaving A unchanged. Net change: Z becomes Z - 4 + 2 = Z - 2, and A becomes A - 8.

Question 15 of 18

Which instrument measures ionizing radiation by gas ionization and gas amplification?

Explanation: The Geiger-Muller counter utilizes an electrical discharge triggered by primary gas ionization events caused by radiation.

Question 16 of 18

Nuclear binding energy is maximum for nuclides around mass number:

Explanation: Iron-56 exhibits one of the highest binding energies per nucleon, representing the peak of nuclear stability.

Question 17 of 18

Electron capture results in:

Explanation: During K-electron capture, an inner shell electron is captured by the nucleus, converting a proton into a neutron (1p1 + -1e0 → 0n1), decreasing atomic number Z by 1.

Question 18 of 18

Magic numbers in nuclear chemistry correspond to stability associated with:

Explanation: Magic numbers (2, 8, 20, 28, 50, 82, 126) represent closed shells of protons or neutrons, conferring exceptional nuclear stability.

Model Question
The radioactive nuclide 92U235 undergoes successive disintegrations to form 82Pb207. How many alpha and beta particles are emitted?
Answer: Option A (7 α and 4 β) Change in mass = 235 - 207 = 28. Number of α = 28 / 4 = 7. Expected Z = 92 - 7(2) = 78. Actual Z = 82. Difference = 82 - 78 = 4, so 4 β particles are emitted.

Question 1 of 10
The rate of radioactive decay obeys which order of reaction kinetics?

Solution: Radioactivity is a spontaneous nuclear phenomenon obeying first-order kinetics.

Question 2 of 10
What is the ratio of half-life (t1/2) to average life (τ) of a radioactive element?

Solution: t1/2 = 0.693 / λ and τ = 1 / λ, hence t1/2 / τ = 0.693.

Question 3 of 10
Which particle is emitted when a proton in the nucleus converts into a neutron?

Solution: Proton conversion to neutron (1p1 → 0n1 + +1e0) releases a positron.

Question 4 of 10
How many disintegrations per second are present in 1 microcurie (μCi)?

Solution: 1 Curie = 3.7 x 1010 dps, so 1 μCi = 10-6 x 3.7 x 1010 = 3.7 x 104 dps.

Question 5 of 10
Which nuclear series is also known as the 4n series?

Solution: The Thorium series begins with 90Th232, and all mass numbers in the chain are exactly divisible by 4 (4n).

Question 6 of 10
What happens to the neutron-to-proton ratio when a nucleus emits an alpha particle?

Solution: Alpha emission removes 2 protons and 2 neutrons. For heavy nuclei, removing 2 protons and 2 neutrons increases the N/Z ratio, bringing it closer to stability.

Question 7 of 10
Which of the following instruments is best suited for measuring high-intensity alpha radiation?

Solution: Scintillation counters using zinc sulfide screens efficiently detect alpha particles with high resolution.

Question 8 of 10
If initial amount of a radioactive substance is 16g and its half-life is 5 hours, amount left after 20 hours is:

Solution: n = 20 / 5 = 4 half-lives. Remaining amount = 16 / (2)4 = 16 / 16 = 1 g.

Question 9 of 10
Nuclear fission was discovered by:

Solution: Otto Hahn and Fritz Strassmann discovered nuclear fission of uranium by neutron bombardment in 1938.

Question 10 of 10
Which of the following nuclides is radioactive and used in cancer radiotherapy?

Solution: Cobalt-60 emits high-energy gamma rays utilized extensively in teletherapy for cancer treatment.
