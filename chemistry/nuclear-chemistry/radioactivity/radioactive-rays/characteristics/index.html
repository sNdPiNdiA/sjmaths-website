Nuclear Chemistry and Radioactivity

Characteristics of Radioactive Rays

Radioactive decay involves the spontaneous emission of alpha, beta, and gamma radiation from unstable atomic nuclei to achieve a lower energy state. Each type of ray possesses distinct mass, charge, penetrating power, and ionizing capability determined by nuclear conservation laws.

UP PGT Chemistry Subject Code 02 B.Sc. / M.Sc. Level

Nature and Deflection of Alpha Particles

  • Alpha particles are doubly ionized helium nuclei denoted as 42He2+ with a mass of 4 amu and charge of +2e.
  • They are deflected by both electric and magnetic fields in a direction opposite to cathode rays, showing lower deflection due to large mass.
  • Alpha particles have a high ionizing power due to their massive charge and slow velocity (~0.05 to 0.1c), but possess very low penetrating power.
  • They can be easily stopped by a thick sheet of paper or a few centimeters of air.

Properties and Types of Beta Radiations

  • Beta particles consist of high-speed electrons (β-) or positrons (β+) originating from nuclear transitions.
  • Due to their very small rest mass (0.00055 amu), beta rays exhibit high deflection in electric and magnetic fields.
  • Their ionizing power is about 1/100th of alpha particles, but their penetrating power is approximately 100 times greater.
  • Beta particles can penetrate a thin aluminum foil or a few millimeters of metal before being completely absorbed.

Characteristics of Gamma Photons

  • Gamma rays are high-energy electromagnetic radiation (photons) emitted during de-excitation of daughter nuclei.
  • They possess zero rest mass and zero charge, resulting in absolute no deflection in electric and magnetic fields.
  • Gamma rays have the lowest ionizing power among the three types of radiation but exhibit the highest penetrating power.
  • They require thick blocks of lead or concrete to attenuate their intensity significantly.
📐 Key Formulas & Equations
Beta-Minus Decay Process
AZX → AZ+1Y + 0-1e + ν̄
Conditions: Neutron-rich nuclei (high n/p ratio)
Units: Mass number A, Atomic number Z
Beta-Plus Decay Process
AZX → AZ-1Y + 0+1e + ν
Conditions: Proton-rich nuclei (low n/p ratio)
Units: Mass number A, Atomic number Z
Radioactive Decay Law
N(t) = N0e-λt
Conditions: First-order nuclear decay kinetics
Units: N in nuclei count, λ in s-1, t in seconds
Disintegration Energy (Q-value)
Q = (Δm)c2
Conditions: Mass defect conversion during emission
Units: Q in Joules or MeV, Δm in kg
Alpha Decay Process
AZX → A-4Z-2Y + 42α
Conditions: Heavy unstable nuclides with Z > 83
Units: Mass number A, Atomic number Z
⚠️ Important Exceptions & Anomalies
Exception Gamma emission changes the mass number of the nucleus

Observation: Gamma decay leaves both mass number (A) and atomic number (Z) completely unchanged — Gamma rays are merely photons emitted when a nucleus transitions from an excited state to a lower energy state.

Exception Beta particles are emitted from the electron shell of an atom

Observation: Beta-minus and beta-plus particles originate exclusively from inside the atomic nucleus — Neutrons decay into protons, electrons, and antineutrinos within the unstable nucleus, not from orbital shells.

Exception All particles emitted in a single decay mode possess identical kinetic energy

Observation: Beta particles exhibit a continuous energy spectrum ranging from zero to a maximum value (Emax) — The total disintegration energy is shared dynamically between the beta particle and an elusive neutrino or antineutrino.

💡 Shortcuts & Memory Aids
Deflection Direction Mnemonic
Alpha & Beta(-) deflect toward the positive plate; Beta(+) toward negative plate.

Application: Use the right-hand rule or charge attraction principle to quickly determine track curvature in magnetic/electric fields.

Penetrating vs Ionizing Power Rule
Penetrating Power ∝ 1 / (Ionizing Power)

Application: Remember that Alpha has Max Ionization & Min Penetration, while Gamma has Min Ionization & Max Penetration.

Nuclear Change Quick Check
α(ΔA=-4, ΔZ=-2), β-(ΔA=0, ΔZ=+1), γ(ΔA=0, ΔZ=0)

Application: Instantly balance nuclear reaction equations by summing atomic and mass numbers.

🎯 Key Exam Points
  • Alpha particles produce dense tracks in a Wilson cloud chamber due to their high ionizing capability.
  • Beta-minus decay involves the conversion of a nuclear neutron into a proton, electron, and antineutrino.
  • Gamma rays travel with the speed of light (3 × 108 m/s) in a vacuum.
  • Internal conversion is a process where an excited nucleus transfers its energy directly to an orbital electron, ejecting it.
  • The range of alpha particles in air is directly proportional to the 3/2 power of their initial velocity (Geiger's Law).
  • Positron emission (beta-plus decay) is strictly accompanied by orbital electron capture as a competing or alternative mode for proton-rich nuclei.
🚫 Common Errors & Confusions
  • Confusing the rest mass of beta particles with photons; beta particles possess rest mass while gamma photons do not.
  • Assuming gamma rays are deflected by magnetic fields because they are high-energy rays; remember they are neutral photons.
  • Forgetting that beta emission spectrum is continuous, unlike alpha emission which displays discrete energy lines.
  • Neglecting the emission of neutrinos/antineutrinos in beta decay energy conservation calculations.

⚡ Quick Revision: Characteristics of Radioactive Rays

Core formulas, rapid recall points, and distinguishing criteria at a glance.

Corpuscular vs Electromagnetic Radiation

Alpha and beta rays are corpuscular particle beams possessing mass and charge, whereas gamma rays are wave-particle dual electromagnetic quanta.

Conservation Laws in Radioactive Transitions

All radioactive emissions strictly conserve mass number, atomic number, total momentum, and total energy across nuclear transformations.

Ionization and Penetration Trade-Off

High interaction cross-section with matter leads to rapid energy loss, causing high ionization power to correlate inversely with penetration depth.

Field Behavior and Spectrometry

Charged emissions (α, β) experience Lorentz force in electromagnetic fields, allowing precise mass-to-charge ratio determination.

High-velocity electron originating from nuclear neutron decay.
Gamma Ray (hν)
High-frequency electromagnetic photon from nuclear de-excitation.

Capacity to strip electrons from atoms along radiation path.
Penetrating Power (Range in matter)
Distance radiation travels through absorbing material before stoppage.
Specific Charge (q/m)
Charge-to-mass ratio governing particle trajectory in fields.

🎯 Key Takeaways

  • Alpha particles have 4 times the mass of a proton and a charge of +2.
  • Beta rays travel at velocities up to 99% the speed of light.
  • Gamma rays have no mass, no charge, and travel at the speed of light.
  • Ionizing power order: Alpha > Beta > Gamma.
  • Penetrating power order: Gamma > Beta > Alpha.

📌 Quick Recall

  • Radioactive rays originate from unstable atomic nuclei seeking energetic stability.
  • Alpha decay reduces mass number by 4 and atomic number by 2.
  • Beta decay converts internal nucleons without altering the overall mass number.
  • Gamma emission releases surplus nuclear energy without changing element identity.

⚖️ Key Differences

Beta particle VS Cathode ray

Beta particles originate from the nucleus, whereas cathode rays consist of extranuclear orbital electrons.

Which of the following radioactive radiations has the highest ionizing power?

Explanation: Alpha particles possess a double positive charge and high mass, causing intense electrostatic interaction with orbital electrons, leading to maximum ionization.

Question 2 of 18

What is the nature of an alpha particle?

Explanation: An alpha particle is identical to a helium nucleus 42He2+ lacking its two orbital electrons.

Question 3 of 18

Gamma rays are deflected by:

Explanation: Gamma rays are uncharged electromagnetic photons and do not experience any Lorentz force in electric or magnetic fields.

Question 4 of 18

Which radiation is easily stopped by a ordinary sheet of paper?

Explanation: Due to their very low penetrating power, alpha particles are blocked by a single sheet of paper or a few centimeters of air.

Question 5 of 18

In beta-minus decay, which nuclear transformation occurs?

Explanation: Beta-minus decay involves the transformation of a neutron into a proton, an electron (beta particle), and an antineutrino.

Question 6 of 18

The specific charge (q/m) is maximum for which of the following?

Explanation: Beta particles have an extremely small mass (0.00055 amu) compared to their unit charge, resulting in the highest specific charge.

Question 7 of 18

Which rays possess the maximum penetrating power?

Explanation: Gamma rays have zero mass and charge with high frequency, allowing them to penetrate thick layers of dense metals like lead.

Question 8 of 18

What happens to the atomic number of a nuclide undergoing alpha decay?

Explanation: Emission of an alpha particle (42α) causes the atomic number to decrease by 2 and mass number by 4.

Question 9 of 18

The continuous energy spectrum of beta particles is attributed to the emission of:

Explanation: The total decay energy is shared variably between the beta particle and a neutrino/antineutrino, creating a continuous spectrum.

Question 10 of 18

Which radioactive ray travels with the speed of light?

Explanation: Gamma rays are electromagnetic radiation and travel at the speed of light in a vacuum (c).

Question 11 of 18

Which of the following is correct regarding the velocity of alpha particles?

Explanation: Due to their large mass, alpha particles travel at relatively slower velocities, typically between 5% and 10% of the speed of light.

Question 12 of 18

Internal conversion is a process competing with:

Explanation: Internal conversion occurs when an excited nucleus transfers its excitation energy directly to an orbital electron instead of emitting a gamma photon.

Question 13 of 18

Positron emission results in:

Explanation: During positron (β+) emission, a proton converts into a neutron, decreasing the atomic number by 1.

Question 14 of 18

Which of the following particles leaves the densest track in a cloud chamber?

Explanation: Alpha particles cause intense ionization along their short path, leaving thick and straight ion tracks in a cloud chamber.

Question 15 of 18

The penetrating power ratio of Alpha : Beta : Gamma is approximately:

Explanation: Penetrating power scales approximately as 1 : 100 : 10,000 for alpha, beta, and gamma radiations respectively.

Question 16 of 18

Which particle has a rest mass equal to zero?

Explanation: Gamma rays are massless photons with zero rest mass.

Question 17 of 18

When a radioactive nucleus emits a gamma photon, its mass number:

Explanation: Gamma emission involves only a change in nuclear energy state, leaving mass number and atomic number completely unchanged.

Question 18 of 18

Which of the following rays are deflected towards the negative plate in an electric field?

Explanation: Alpha rays carry a positive charge (+2e) and are deflected towards the negative electrode.

Question 1 of 10
Which of the following particles has a mass closest to that of a positron?

Solution: A positron is the antiparticle of an electron and has exactly the same mass (0.00055 amu).

Question 2 of 10
What is the change in mass number when a nuclide undergoes successive one alpha and two beta-minus decays?

Solution: Alpha decay decreases A by 4; each beta-minus decay leaves A unchanged. Net change = -4 + 0 + 0 = -4.

Question 3 of 10
Which radiation produces maximum fluorescence on a zinc sulfide screen?

Solution: Alpha particles cause intense scintillation and fluorescence on ZnS screens due to high localized energy deposition.

Question 4 of 10
The velocity of gamma rays in vacuum is equal to:

Solution: Gamma rays are electromagnetic radiation and propagate at the universal speed of light (3 × 108 m/s).

Question 5 of 10
Which of the following is true for beta-plus emission?

Solution: Beta-plus decay involves a nuclear proton converting into a neutron, positron, and neutrino, decreasing Z by 1.

Question 6 of 10
The ratio of specific charges (q/m) of an alpha particle to a proton is approximately:

Solution: Alpha charge = +2, mass = 4 amu (q/m = 2/4 = 0.5). Proton charge = +1, mass = 1 amu (q/m = 1/1 = 1). Ratio is 0.5 : 1 or 1 : 2.

Question 7 of 10
Which rays are electromagnetic waves of nuclear origin?

Solution: Gamma rays are high-energy photons emitted directly from atomic nuclei during radioactive transitions.

Question 8 of 10
Why do alpha particles have a short range in matter compared to beta particles?

Solution: High charge and mass cause intense interaction with matter, exhausting their kinetic energy over a very short distance.

Question 9 of 10
Which of the following nuclear emissions does not alter either the atomic number or the mass number?

Solution: Gamma emission involves photon release from an excited nucleus without changing Z or A.

Question 10 of 10
The existence of neutrinos was postulated to account for the conservation of:

Solution: Neutrinos/antineutrinos carry away the missing energy and momentum in continuous beta decay spectra to satisfy conservation laws.
