Nuclear Chemistry and Radioactivity

Artificial Radioactivity

Artificial radioactivity is the induced transformation of stable atomic nuclei into unstable radioactive isotopes by bombardment with high-energy subatomic particles or photons. First demonstrated by Irène Joliot-Curie and Frédéric Joliot-Curie in 1934, it proceeds via compound nucleus formation followed by particle emission.

UP PGT Chemistry Subject Code 02 B.Sc. / M.Sc. Level

Mechanisms of Induced Transmutation

  • Artificial radioactivity is initiated by bombarding stable target nuclei with projectiles such as protons, deuterons, alpha particles, or neutrons.
  • Charged projectiles require high kinetic energy to overcome the Coulombic repulsion barrier of the positively charged target nucleus.
  • Uncharged neutrons possess zero electrostatic charge, allowing them to penetrate target nuclei easily even at thermal energies without a potential barrier.
  • The reaction proceeds through an intermediate compound nucleus state possessing high excitation energy before ejecting a secondary particle.

Types of Projectiles and Nuclear Reactions

  • (α, p) reactions involve alpha particle capture and proton emission, transforming light elements such as 2713Al into 3014Si.
  • (d, p) reactions use deuterons where the neutron is absorbed and the proton is stripped, yielding heavier isotopes.
  • Neutron capture (n, γ) reactions result in mass number increment by one while preserving the atomic number, frequently producing beta-minus active isotopes.
  • Photonuclear reactions (γ, n) use high-energy gamma photons from linear accelerators to knock out neutrons from stable targets.

Decay Characteristics of Artificial Radionuclides

  • Artificially prepared radioactive isotopes predominantly lie outside the zone of stability on the proton-rich or neutron-rich sides.
  • Proton-rich artificial isotopes typically decay via positron emission (β+) or K-electron capture to achieve nuclear stability.
  • Neutron-rich artificial isotopes produced via nuclear fission or neutron activation decay predominantly via negatron emission (β-).
  • The half-lives of artificial radioisotopes range from fractions of a microsecond to thousands of years, following standard first-order radioactive decay kinetics.

⚠️ Important Exceptions & Anomalies
Exception Charged projectiles must possess high kinetic energy to penetrate target nuclei.

Observation: Thermal neutrons do not require high kinetic energy. — Neutrons lack an electrical charge and therefore experience zero electrostatic repulsion from the positively charged atomic nucleus.

Exception Heavier nuclei undergo spontaneous alpha or beta decay naturally.

Observation: Stable light elements can be made radioactive by particle bombardment. — Artificial input of energy and nucleons forces the nucleus into an excited, unstable configuration outside the valley of beta stability.

🎯 Key Exam Points
  • Artificial radioactivity was discovered by Irène Curie and Frédéric Joliot in 1934 by bombarding aluminum with alpha particles.
  • The first artificially radioactive isotope produced was phosphorus-30: 2713Al + 42He → 3015P + 10n.
  • Phosphorus-30 decays with the emission of a positron: 3015P → 3014Si + e+.
  • Enrico Fermi pioneered the use of slow (thermal) neutrons for producing artificial radioactive isotopes via neutron capture.
  • Cyclotrons and linear accelerators are particle accelerators used to impart high kinetic energy to charged projectiles like protons and deuterons.
  • Transuranic elements (elements with atomic numbers Z > 92) are exclusively produced artificially through neutron bombardment and heavy-ion reactions.
🚫 Common Errors & Confusions
  • Confusing artificial radioactivity with nuclear fission; artificial radioactivity encompasses any induced nuclear transmutation, whereas fission specifically involves heavy nucleus splitting.
  • Omitting neutrinos (ν or ν̄) from positron emission and electron capture nuclear balancing equations.
  • Failing to balance both mass numbers (superscripts) and atomic numbers (subscripts) across nuclear reaction arrows.
  • Assuming thermal neutrons cannot cause nuclear reactions because they lack kinetic energy; in reality, thermal neutrons have extremely high capture cross-sections.

⚡ Quick Revision: Artificial Radioactivity

Core formulas, rapid recall points, and distinguishing criteria at a glance.

Principle of Induced Transmutation

Stable nuclei are converted into radioactive species by bombarding them with high-velocity subatomic projectiles, forming an excited compound nucleus.

Projectile Types and Energetics

Charged particles require accelerators to overcome Coulomb barriers, while uncharged neutrons induce transmutations efficiently at thermal energies.

Decay Modes of Synthetic Nuclides

Proton-rich artificial isotopes decay via positron emission or electron capture, while neutron-rich isotopes undergo beta-minus decay.

Historical Milestones and Applications

Discovered by the Joliot-Curies in 1934, artificial radioactivity is essential for producing medical radioisotopes and transuranic elements.

📚 Terms & Units Glossary

Artificial Radioactivity (Induced Radioactivity)
The property of induced radioactivity exhibited by stable nuclides transformed into unstable radioactive isotopes via particle bombardment.
Compound Nucleus ([C]*)
An intermediate, highly excited short-lived nuclear state formed immediately after projectile capture.
Cross Section (σ)
A measure of the probability of a specific nuclear reaction occurring, expressed in barns (1 barn = 10-24 cm2).
Positron (e+)
The antiparticle of the electron having identical mass but positive elementary charge.
Transuranic Elements (Z > 92)
Synthetic chemical elements with atomic numbers greater than uranium, produced via artificial nuclear reactions.
Neutron Activation Analysis (NAA)
An analytical technique used to determine trace element concentrations by bombarding samples with neutrons.

🎯 Key Takeaways

  • Joliot-Curie discovery reaction: 2713Al + 42He → 3015P + 10n
  • Positron emission decreases atomic number by 1 while keeping mass number constant.
  • Thermal neutrons are slowed down using moderators like heavy water or graphite to maximize capture cross-section.
  • Q-value = Δmc2; exothermic reactions have positive Q values, endothermic have negative Q values.
  • Cyclotrons accelerate charged particles in a spiral path using a magnetic field and alternating electric field.

📌 Quick Recall

  • Artificial radioactivity forces stable nuclei into unstable configurations by particle bombardment.
  • Reactions follow strict conservation laws for mass number, atomic number, linear momentum, and total energy.
  • Decay of artificial isotopes follows standard exponential radioactive decay laws.
  • Applications span nuclear medicine, radiotracer studies, and synthesis of superheavy elements.

⚖️ Key Differences

Positron Emission VS Electron Capture

Positron emission ejects a positive beta particle from the nucleus; electron capture absorbs an inner-orbital electron into the nucleus.

Question 1 of 18

Who discovered artificial radioactivity?

Explanation: Irène Joliot-Curie and Frédéric Joliot-Curie discovered artificial radioactivity in 1934 by bombarding aluminum foil with alpha particles.

Question 2 of 18

In the nuclear reaction 2713Al + 42He → X + 10n, what is the isotope X?

Explanation: Balancing mass numbers: 27 + 4 = A + 1 → A = 30. Balancing atomic numbers: 13 + 2 = Z + 0 → Z = 15. The isotope is phosphorus-30.

Question 3 of 18

Which of the following projectiles requires no kinetic energy barrier to enter a target nucleus?

Explanation: Neutrons are electrically neutral, allowing them to enter atomic nuclei without experiencing Coulombic repulsion.

Question 4 of 18

Proton-rich artificial radionuclides typically decay by:

Explanation: Proton-rich nuclei have an excess of protons relative to neutrons and reduce this ratio via positron emission or K-electron capture.

Question 5 of 18

The unit of nuclear reaction cross-section 'barn' is equivalent to:

Explanation: One barn equals 10-24 cm2, which is equal to 10-28 m2.

Question 6 of 18

Which device is used to accelerate uncharged particles?

Explanation: Particle accelerators rely on electric and magnetic fields to accelerate charged particles; uncharged neutrons cannot be accelerated by these means.

Question 7 of 18

What is the product of neutron capture by 5927Co followed by gamma emission?

Explanation: Neutron capture (n, γ) increases the mass number by 1 while keeping the atomic number unchanged: 5927Co + 10n → 6027Co + γ.

Question 8 of 18

The half-life of artificially radioactive phosphorus-30 is approximately:

Explanation: Phosphorus-30 is a short-lived positron emitter with a half-life of approximately 2.5 to 3.2 minutes.

Question 9 of 18

Transuranic elements are synthesized primarily by:

Explanation: Elements heavier than uranium (Z > 92) are produced artificially through neutron capture and heavy-ion bombardment in reactors and accelerators.

Question 10 of 18

In the shorthand notation 14N(d, p)15N, the projectile and ejected particle are respectively:

Explanation: In 14N(d, p)15N, 'd' represents the deuteron projectile and 'p' represents the ejected proton.

Question 11 of 18

Which type of nuclear reaction is represented by (γ, n)?

Explanation: The (γ, n) process is a photonuclear reaction where a high-energy gamma photon ejects a neutron from the target nucleus.

Question 12 of 18

What happens to the atomic number when a nucleus undergoes positron emission?

Explanation: Positron emission converts a proton into a neutron plus a positron (p → n + e+), decreasing the atomic number by 1.

Question 13 of 18

Who first produced artificial radioactivity using alpha particles from polonium?

Explanation: The Joliot-Curie couple used alpha particles from polonium to bombard aluminum, boron, and magnesium, creating artificial radioisotopes.

Question 14 of 18

The Q-value of a nuclear reaction is positive when:

Explanation: A positive Q-value indicates an exothermic nuclear reaction where mass is converted into kinetic or radiant energy.

Question 15 of 18

Thermal neutrons have kinetic energies of approximately:

Explanation: Thermalized neutrons in equilibrium with their surroundings at room temperature possess an average kinetic energy of about 0.025 eV.

Question 16 of 18

Which artificially produced isotope is widely used in medicine for thyroid cancer therapy and diagnostics?

Explanation: Iodine-131 is an artificial radionuclide produced in nuclear reactors, extensively utilized in nuclear medicine for diagnosing and treating thyroid disorders.

Question 17 of 18

In electron capture, which atomic shell electron is typically captured by the nucleus?

Explanation: Due to closest proximity to the nucleus, K-shell (1s) electrons are most frequently captured in K-capture decay.

Question 18 of 18

What is the primary function of a moderator in producing artificial radioisotopes with neutrons?

Explanation: Moderators such as graphite or heavy water slow down fast fission neutrons to thermal velocities to enhance neutron capture cross-sections.

Question 1 of 10
Which nuclear reaction represents the discovery of artificial radioactivity?

Solution: The bombardment of aluminum-27 with alpha particles producing radioactive phosphorus-30 and a neutron marks the discovery of artificial radioactivity.

Question 2 of 10
The decay of phosphorus-30 proceeds via the emission of:

Solution: Phosphorus-30 is proton-rich and decays by emitting a positron (β+) to form stable silicon-30.

Question 3 of 10
What is the nuclear shorthand notation for bombarding nitrogen-14 with alpha particles and emitting a proton?

Solution: Target is 14N, projectile is alpha particle (α), ejected particle is proton (p), and product is oxygen-17.

Question 4 of 10
Thermal neutron capture by a nuclide generally results in which type of primary decay?

Solution: Adding a neutron increases the neutron-to-proton ratio, shifting the nuclide to the neutron-rich side, which subsequently undergoes beta-minus decay.

Question 5 of 10
Which of the following statements concerning artificial radioactivity is correct?

Solution: Artificial radioactivity can be induced across almost the entire periodic table, including light elements like boron, carbon, and aluminum.

Question 6 of 10
If the Q-value of an artificial nuclear reaction is negative, the reaction is:

Solution: A negative Q-value signifies that energy must be supplied (endothermic reaction) for the nuclear transformation to proceed.

Question 7 of 10
Which particle is emitted during K-electron capture along with characteristic X-rays?

Solution: Electron capture results in the emission of an electron neutrino (νe) and orbital electron rearrangement emitting X-rays.

Question 8 of 10
Which of the following units is appropriately used to express microscopic nuclear reaction cross-sections?

Solution: The cross-section for nuclear reactions is expressed in barns, where 1 barn = 10-24 cm2.

Question 9 of 10
In artificial transmutations, high-energy protons are typically accelerated using:

Solution: Cyclotrons and synchrotrons are particle accelerators designed to impart high kinetic energy to charged particles like protons and deuterons.

Question 10 of 10
What is the primary difference between natural and artificial radioactivity regarding reaction rates?

Solution: Natural radioactivity is an unalterable spontaneous decay property, whereas the rate of artificial radioactive isotope production depends directly on projectile flux and cross-section.
