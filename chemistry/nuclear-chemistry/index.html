Nuclear Chemistry and Radioactivity

Nuclear Chemistry and Radioactivity

Nuclear chemistry investigates reactions involving atomic nuclei, encompassing radioactive decay, nuclear fission, and nuclear fusion processes. It relies on the conservation of mass-energy and nucleon number rather than valence electron configurations.

UP PGT Chemistry Subject Code 02 B.Sc. / M.Sc. Level

Radioactive Decay Kinetics and Equilibrium

  • Radioactive disintegration follows first-order kinetics where the rate of decay is directly proportional to the number of undecayed nuclei present.
  • The fraction of nuclei remaining after n half-lives is given by (1/2)n, where n = t / t1/2.
  • Secular equilibrium occurs in a decay series when the half-life of the parent nuclide is much greater than that of the daughter nuclide (λA << λB).
  • Transient equilibrium is established when the parent's half-life is moderately longer than the daughter's half-life, leading to constant activity ratios.

Nuclear Stability and Binding Energy

  • The neutron-to-proton (n/p) ratio dictates nuclear stability; nuclides falling outside the zone of stability undergo spontaneous radioactive decay.
  • Nuclei with magic numbers of protons or neutrons (2, 8, 20, 28, 50, 82, 126) exhibit exceptional nuclear stability due to filled nuclear shells.
  • Mass defect (Δm) is the difference between the rest mass of a nucleus and the sum of the masses of its constituent isolated nucleons.
  • Higher binding energy per nucleon corresponds to enhanced nuclear stability, reaching a maximum around iron (56Fe).

Nuclear Reactions and Artificial Transmutation

  • Nuclear reactions conserve mass number (A), atomic number (Z), and total relativistic energy and momentum.
  • Nuclear fission involves the splitting of a heavy nucleus into smaller fragments, releasing vast amounts of energy and excess neutrons.
  • Nuclear fusion combines light nuclei at extremely high temperatures, powering stellar processes and thermonuclear weapons.
  • Artificial radioactivity is induced by bombarding stable targets with projectiles such as alpha particles, protons, neutrons, or deuterons.
📐 Key Formulas & Equations
Half-Life Formula
t1/2 = ln(2) / λ = 0.693 / λ
Conditions: First-order nuclear decay
Units: t1/2 in time units, λ in time-1
Mean Life Formula
τ = 1 / λ = t1/2 / 0.693
Conditions: Average lifetime of a radioactive nucleus
Units: τ in time units
Mass-Energy Equivalence
ΔE = Δm c2
Conditions: Calculation of nuclear binding energy from mass defect
Units: ΔE in Joules or MeV, Δm in kg or amu, c in m s-1
Binding Energy per Nucleon
BE/A = [(Δm) c2] / A
Conditions: Measure of nuclear stability for a nuclide of mass number A
Units: BE/A in MeV per nucleon
Radioactive Decay Law
N(t) = N0e-λt
Conditions: First-order radioactive disintegration kinetics
Units: N in number of nuclei, λ in s-1 or year-1, t in time
⚠️ Important Exceptions & Anomalies
Exception All heavy elements beyond bismuth (Z = 83) are radioactive.

Observation: Technetium (Z = 43) and Promethium (Z = 61) are light elements with no stable isotopes. — Absence of stable nucleon combinations according to the liquid drop model and pairing energy effects.

Exception Beta-minus decay increases the atomic number by one unit.

Observation: Orbital electron capture decreases the atomic number by one unit while emitting characteristic X-rays. — Instead of emitting a beta particle, the nucleus captures an inner-shell electron (usually K-shell) to convert a proton into a neutron.

💡 Shortcuts & Memory Aids
Decay Product Prediction Rule
n/p too high → β- decay; n/p too low → β+ decay or EC; Z > 83 → α decay

Application: Quickly identify the type of radioactive decay required to move a nuclide into the belt of stability.

Half-Life Fraction Shortcut
Remaining Fraction = (1/2)(t / t1/2)

Application: Instantly calculate remaining mass or activity without solving complex exponential equations.

Mass Defect to Energy Quick Factor
1 amu ≈ 931.5 MeV

Application: Multiply the calculated mass defect in amu directly by 931.5 to obtain total binding energy in MeV.

🚫 Common Errors & Confusions
  • Confusing the half-life formula with second-order chemical reaction kinetics; radioactive decay is strictly first-order.
  • Assuming nuclear reactions violate mass conservation without accounting for mass defect and mass-energy equivalence.
  • Forgetting that electron capture produces characteristic X-rays when outer shell electrons drop to fill the vacant K-shell.
  • Mistaking atomic mass unit (amu) conversion factors when calculating binding energy in Joules versus MeV.

⚡ Quick Revision: Nuclear Chemistry and Radioactivity

Core formulas, rapid recall points, and distinguishing criteria at a glance.

Radioactivity Fundamentals

Spontaneous emission of ionizing radiation from unstable nuclei follows first-order exponential decay laws characterized by decay constants and half-lives.

Nuclear Stability Rules

The neutron-to-proton ratio and magic numbers determine nuclear stability, directing nuclides toward decay pathways that reach the belt of stability.

Types of Radiations

Alpha, beta, positron, gamma emissions, and electron capture alter atomic and mass numbers according to strict conservation laws.

Energy Transformations

Mass defect converts directly into binding energy via Einstein's equation, quantifying the immense energy changes in fission and fusion.

📚 Terms & Units Glossary

Radioactivity (Spontaneous Decay)
Spontaneous disintegration of unstable atomic nuclei accompanied by radiation emission.
Decay Constant (λ)
Proportionality constant relating rate of decay to number of active nuclei; units s-1.
Half-Life (t1/2)
Time required for half of the radioactive nuclei in a sample to decay.
Mass Defect (Δm)
Difference between total nucleon mass and nuclear rest mass.
Nuclear Fission (Neutron-induced Splitting)
Splitting of a heavy nucleus into intermediate-mass fragments.
Nuclear Fusion (Thermonuclear Combination)
Fusion of light nuclei into a heavier nucleus at extreme temperatures.

🎯 Key Takeaways

  • Radioactive decay is independent of external physical conditions such as temperature, pressure, and catalysts.
  • t1/2 = 0.693 / λ
  • Energy equivalent of 1 amu is 931.5 MeV.
  • Nuclides with even numbers of protons and neutrons are exceptionally stable.
  • Geiger-Muller counter and scintillation counter detect and measure radioactivity.

📌 Quick Recall

  • Nuclear stability is governed by the n/p ratio and nuclear shell structure.
  • Radioactive decay kinetics are strictly first-order.
  • Mass defect accounts for nuclear binding energy.
  • Fission and fusion release energy via mass-to-energy conversion.

⚖️ Key Differences

Nuclear Fission VS Nuclear Fusion

Fission breaks heavy nuclei apart using neutrons, whereas fusion combines light nuclei at very high temperatures.

Question 1 of 18

What is the order of radioactive decay kinetics?

Explanation: Radioactive decay is a stochastic process where the rate of disintegration is directly proportional to the number of undecayed nuclei, which defines first-order kinetics.

Question 2 of 18

If the half-life of a radioactive isotope is 10 days, what fraction remains after 30 days?

Explanation: Number of half-lives n = 30 / 10 = 3. Remaining fraction = (1/2)3 = 1/8.

Question 3 of 18

Which of the following particles is emitted during alpha decay?

Explanation: An alpha particle is identical to a helium nucleus containing two protons and two neutrons (2He4).

Question 4 of 18

What happens to the atomic number during beta-minus (e-) decay?

Explanation: In beta-minus decay, a neutron transforms into a proton, increasing the atomic number by 1 while mass number remains constant.

Question 5 of 18

Which nuclide possesses exceptional stability due to magic numbers?

Explanation: Oxygen-16 has 8 protons and 8 neutrons, both of which are magic numbers, conferring high nuclear stability.

Question 6 of 18

What is the relationship between decay constant (λ) and mean life (τ)?

Explanation: Mean life (τ) is the reciprocal of the decay constant (λ).

Question 7 of 18

Which process decreases the atomic number by one unit without alpha emission?

Explanation: Electron capture involves the capture of an inner shell electron by a nucleus, converting a proton into a neutron and decreasing atomic number by 1.

Question 8 of 18

The energy equivalent of 1 atomic mass unit (amu) in MeV is approximately:

Explanation: Using E = Δm c2, 1 amu converts to approximately 931.5 MeV of energy.

Question 9 of 18

Which type of radioactive decay does not change the atomic number or mass number?

Explanation: Gamma emission involves the transition of a nucleus from an excited state to a ground state, releasing electromagnetic radiation without altering Z or A.

Question 10 of 18

Which instrument is commonly used to measure ionizing radiation via gas ionization?

Explanation: A Geiger-Muller counter detects radiation by measuring gas ionization produced within a sealed tube.

Question 11 of 18

For a radioactive element with decay constant 0.1 day-1, what is its half-life?

Explanation: t1/2 = 0.693 / λ = 0.693 / 0.1 = 6.93 days.

Question 12 of 18

Which nuclear reaction powers the sun and hydrogen bombs?

Explanation: Nuclear fusion combines light hydrogen isotopes into helium at extreme stellar temperatures, releasing tremendous energy.

Question 13 of 18

Neutron-rich nuclei typically undergo which mode of decay?

Explanation: Neutron-rich nuclei have an n/p ratio above the zone of stability and convert excess neutrons to protons via beta-minus emission.

Question 14 of 18

What is the mass defect of a nucleus?

Explanation: Mass defect is the missing mass converted into binding energy when individual nucleons bind together to form a nucleus.

Question 15 of 18

Which isotope is commonly used as a reference for atomic mass units (amu)?

Explanation: Carbon-12 (6C12) serves as the international standard for defining the atomic mass unit.

Question 16 of 18

Secular equilibrium applies when:

Explanation: Secular equilibrium occurs when the parent nuclide decays extremely slowly compared to the daughter nuclide (λA << λB).

Question 17 of 18

What is formed when a radioactive nuclide emits a positron?

Explanation: Positron emission converts a proton into a neutron, decreasing the atomic number by 1.

Question 18 of 18

Which of the following is considered a fissile material?

Explanation: Uranium-235 (92U235) undergoes fission upon absorbing thermal neutrons and is classified as a primary fissile material.

Question 1 of 10
Calculate the decay constant for a radioactive isotope whose half-life is 13.86 seconds.

Solution: λ = 0.693 / 13.86 = 0.05 s-1.

Question 2 of 10
What percentage of a radioactive substance remains after 4 half-lives?

Solution: Remaining fraction = (1/2)4 = 1/16 = 0.0625 or 6.25%.

Question 3 of 10
Which particle is emitted when a neutron-deficient nucleus decays by positron emission?

Solution: A positron is represented as +1e0 or β+.

Question 4 of 10
Which nuclear property is additive and directly proportional to mass number?

Solution: Nuclear mass and total volume scale directly with mass number A, whereas nuclear density remains constant across all nuclides.

Question 5 of 10
In the nuclear reaction 13Al27 (d, p) 13Al28, what does 'd' represent?

Solution: The symbol 'd' stands for a deuteron (1H2).

Question 6 of 10
Which radioactive series starts with 90Th232 and ends with stable 82Pb208?

Solution: The thorium series originates from 90Th232 and terminates at 82Pb208.

Question 7 of 10
What is the primary reason why heavy nuclei undergo fission?

Solution: Coulomb repulsion between numerous protons in heavy nuclei destabilizes the nucleus, making fission energetically favorable.

Question 8 of 10
If 1.0 gram of a radioactive substance decays to 0.125 grams in 15 days, what is its half-life?

Solution: 0.125 / 1.0 = 1/8 = (1/2)3, meaning 3 half-lives have elapsed in 15 days. Half-life = 15 / 3 = 5 days.

Question 9 of 10
Which type of radiation has the highest penetrating power?

Solution: Gamma rays are high-energy photons with high penetrating power, requiring thick lead or concrete shielding to block.

Question 10 of 10
What is the value of mean life (τ) for a nuclide with a decay constant of 0.02 year-1?

Solution: Mean life τ = 1 / λ = 1 / 0.02 = 50 years.
