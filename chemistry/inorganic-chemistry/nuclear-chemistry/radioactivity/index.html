Inorganic Chemistry

Radioactivity

Radioactivity is a spontaneous nuclear phenomenon in which unstable nuclides disintegrate by emitting particulate or electromagnetic radiation to attain a stable energy state. The rate of radioactive decay follows first-order kinetics and is independent of external physical and chemical conditions.

UP PGT Chemistry Subject Code 02 B.Sc. / M.Sc. Level

Types of Radioactive Emissions and Nuclear Changes

  • Alpha emission (α) decreases mass number by 4 and atomic number by 2, corresponding to a helium nucleus 2He4 emission.
  • Beta-minus emission (β-) converts a neutron into a proton inside the nucleus, increasing atomic number by 1 while mass number remains constant.
  • Positron emission (β+) or electron capture converts a proton into a neutron, decreasing atomic number by 1 without altering the mass number.
  • Gamma radiation (γ) involves the emission of high-energy photons from an excited nuclear state to a lower energy state with no change in mass or atomic number.

Kinetics of Radioactive Decay

  • Radioactivity is strictly a first-order kinetic process where the rate of disintegration is directly proportional to the number of active nuclei present.
  • The differential rate expression is given by -dN/dt = λN, where λ is the characteristic decay constant.
  • The integrated rate expression can be expressed logarithmically as λt = 2.303 log(N0 / Nt).
  • Radioactive decay rate is completely independent of temperature, pressure, concentration, and chemical combination state.

Radioactive Disintegration Series and Stability

  • Naturally occurring heavy elements decay through successive emissions until forming stable lead isotopes, constituting four distinct series: Thorium, Neptunium, Uranium, and Actinium.
  • The stability of nuclei is governed by the neutron-to-proton ratio (n/p ratio), with stable nuclei lying within the 'Zone of Stability'.
  • Nuclei with an n/p ratio above the belt undergo β- decay, whereas those below the belt undergo positron emission or electron capture.
  • Nuclei with atomic numbers greater than 83 are universally radioactive due to electrostatic repulsion overcoming strong nuclear forces.
📐 Key Formulas & Equations
Decay Constant and Half-Life Relation
t1/2 = 0.693 / λ
Conditions: First-order nuclear disintegration process.
Units: t1/2 in time units; λ in time-1.
Average Life
τ = 1 / λ = 1.443 × t1/2
Conditions: Statistical mean lifetime of a radioactive nucleus.
Units: τ in time units.
Radioactive Disintegration Fraction
Nt / N0 = (1 / 2)n
Conditions: Where n = t / t1/2 represents the number of half-lives elapsed.
Units: Dimensionless ratio.
Radioactive Decay Law
Nt = N0e-λt
Conditions: Applicable to any radioactive isotope undergoing unimolecular nuclear decay.
Units: Nt, N0 in atoms or moles; λ in s-1 or year-1; t in time units.
⚠️ Important Exceptions & Anomalies
Exception Radioactive decay rates are independent of external conditions.

Observation: Electron capture decay rates can be marginally altered. — Electron capture depends on the electron density at the nucleus, which can be slightly changed under extreme pressure or in different chemical environments.

Exception All elements heavier than bismuth (Z > 83) are radioactive.

Observation: Bismuth-209 is technically radioactive. — It undergoes extremely slow alpha decay with a half-life of 2.01 × 1019 years, though it was long considered the heaviest stable nuclide.

💡 Shortcuts & Memory Aids
Half-Life Fraction Shortcut
Remaining amount = Initial × (1/2)n

Application: Quickly calculate remaining mass or activity without logarithms when time elapsed is an exact multiple of half-life (t = n × t1/2).

Average Life Shortcut
τ = 1.44 × t1/2

Application: Instantly convert half-life to average life or vice-versa in numerical problems without deriving the reciprocal relationship.

Decay Constant from Logarithms
λ = (2.303 / t) log(N0 / Nt)

Application: Fast substitution for time and concentration ratios in first-order nuclear decay problems.

🎯 Key Exam Points
  • Radioactivity is measured in units of Becquerel (Bq) or Curie (Ci), where 1 Ci = 3.7 × 1010 disintegrations per second.
  • The half-life of a radioactive substance is independent of its initial concentration.
  • Group displacement law (Fajans and Soddy): Emission of an α-particle shifts the element two groups to the left in the periodic table.
  • Group displacement law: Emission of a β-particle shifts the element one group to the right in the periodic table.
  • Isobars are formed when a radioactive nuclide emits a β-particle (e.g., mass number remains constant, atomic number changes by +1).
  • Carbon-14 dating utilizes the constant ratio of 14C to 12C in living organisms to determine the age of archaeological specimens.
🚫 Common Errors & Confusions
  • Confusing average life (τ) with half-life (t1/2). Remember τ is greater than t1/2 by a factor of 1.44.
  • Applying Arrhenius temperature dependence formulas to radioactive decay rates, ignoring that nuclear reactions are temperature-independent.
  • Forgetting that electron capture decreases atomic number by 1, identically to positron emission.
  • Mixing up mass number and atomic number changes during radioactive series transformations.

⚡ Quick Revision: Radioactivity

Core formulas, rapid recall points, and distinguishing criteria at a glance.

First-Order Nuclear Kinetics

Radioactivity obeys first-order kinetics where the disintegration rate depends solely on the number of undecayed nuclei, expressed mathematically as Nt = N0e-λt.

Half-Life and Average Life

Half-life (t1/2) is inversely proportional to the decay constant via t1/2 = 0.693 / λ, while average life (τ) is the reciprocal of the decay constant (τ = 1 / λ).

Nuclear Stability and n/p Ratio

Nuclides outside the zone of stability undergo specific decay modes (α, β-, β+, or electron capture) to attain a stable neutron-to-proton ratio.

Radioactive Disintegration Series

Naturally occurring unstable heavy nuclides undergo sequential radioactive transformations through four distinct series until terminating at stable isotopes of lead.

📚 Terms & Units Glossary

Decay Constant (λ)
Fraction of radioactive nuclei decaying per unit time; unit is s-1.
Half-Life (t1/2)
Time required for half of the radioactive nuclei to disintegrate; unit is time.
Average Life (τ)
Sum of lives of all atoms divided by total number of atoms; equal to 1 / λ.
Curie (Ci)
Unit of radioactivity equivalent to 3.7 × 1010 disintegrations per second.
Becquerel (Bq)
SI unit of radioactivity equal to one disintegration per second.
Isobars (Same Mass Number)
Nuclides having the same mass number but different atomic numbers.

🎯 Key Takeaways

  • t1/2 = 0.693 / λ
  • τ = 1.443 × t1/2
  • Nt = N0(1/2)n
  • 1 Ci = 3.7 × 1010 dps
  • Rate of decay is independent of temperature and pressure.

📌 Quick Recall

  • Radioactivity is a nuclear phenomenon independent of chemical bonding.
  • Alpha decay reduces mass number by 4 and atomic number by 2.
  • Beta-minus decay increases atomic number by 1 with no change in mass number.
  • First-order rate law governs all radioactive disintegration processes.

⚖️ Key Differences

Nuclear Fission VS Radioactive Decay

Radioactive decay is a spontaneous disintegration of an unstable nucleus, whereas nuclear fission is the splitting of a heavy nucleus induced by neutron bombardment.

Question 1 of 19

What is the relationship between the half-life (t1/2) and the decay constant (λ) for a radioactive substance?

Explanation: From the first-order rate equation, t1/2 = ln(2) / λ = 0.693 / λ.

Question 2 of 19

The emission of an alpha particle from a radioactive nuclide results in:

Explanation: An alpha particle is a helium nucleus (2He4), so its emission decreases the mass number by 4 and atomic number by 2.

Question 3 of 19

Radioactive decay follows which order of reaction kinetics?

Explanation: The rate of radioactive disintegration is directly proportional to the number of radioactive nuclei present, which is the definition of first-order kinetics.

Question 4 of 19

What is the average life (τ) of a radioactive element in terms of its decay constant (λ)?

Explanation: Average life is defined as the reciprocal of the decay constant, τ = 1 / λ.

Question 5 of 19

Which of the following particles has the highest ionizing power?

Explanation: Alpha particles have the largest mass and charge among the options, making them the most strongly ionizing.

Question 6 of 19

In a radioactive series, the initial unstable parent nucleus eventually transforms into a stable isotope of:

Explanation: All natural radioactive series (Thorium, Uranium, Actinium) ultimately decay into stable isotopes of lead (Pb).

Question 7 of 19

What happens to the atomic number when a nuclide undergoes beta-minus (β-) decay?

Explanation: In beta-minus decay, a neutron converts into a proton, thereby increasing the atomic number by 1 while keeping the mass number constant.

Question 8 of 19

Which of the following units is equivalent to 1 Curie (Ci)?

Explanation: By definition, 1 Curie equals 3.7 × 1010 disintegrations per second, based on the activity of 1 gram of radium-226.

Question 9 of 19

The half-life of a radioactive isotope is 10 days. What fraction of the initial amount will remain after 30 days?

Explanation: Number of half-lives n = 30 / 10 = 3. Remaining fraction = (1 / 2)3 = 1 / 8.

Question 10 of 19

Which radioactive emission is electromagnetic in nature?

Explanation: Gamma rays are high-frequency electromagnetic radiation (photons) emitted from nuclear excited states.

Question 11 of 19

Electron capture results in which of the following nuclear changes?

Explanation: In electron capture, an inner-orbital electron is captured by the nucleus, combining with a proton to form a neutron (p + e- → n).

Question 12 of 19

Which of the following is true regarding radioactive decay?

Explanation: Nuclear reactions involve core nuclear changes and are entirely unaffected by external thermodynamic conditions.

Question 13 of 19

What is the ratio of average life (τ) to half-life (t1/2)?

Explanation: τ / t1/2 = (1 / λ) / (0.693 / λ) = 1 / 0.693 = 1.443.

Question 14 of 19

Positron emission (β+) causes the atomic number to:

Explanation: In positron emission, a proton converts into a neutron, decreasing the atomic number by 1.

Question 15 of 19

Which type of radiation has the highest penetrating power?

Explanation: Gamma rays have no mass and zero charge, giving them high penetrating power requiring thick lead shields to stop.

Question 16 of 19

If 75% of a radioactive substance decays in 20 minutes, what is its half-life?

Explanation: 75% decay means 25% remains, which is (1/2)2. Thus, 2 half-lives have elapsed in 20 minutes, so t1/2 = 10 minutes.

Question 17 of 19

Which nuclear property primarily determines whether a nucleus is stable or radioactive?

Explanation: The n/p ratio relative to the stability belt determines whether a nucleus is stable or undergoes radioactive decay.

Question 18 of 19

What is the SI unit of radioactivity?

Explanation: The SI unit of radioactivity is the Becquerel (Bq), defined as one disintegration per second.

Question 19 of 19

Which series of natural radioactivity starts with 92U238 and ends with 82Pb206?

Explanation: The Uranium series originates from 92U238 and terminates at stable 82Pb206.

Question 1 of 10
What fraction of a radioactive isotope remains undecayed after 4 half-lives?

Solution: Remaining fraction = (1 / 2)4 = 1 / 16.

Question 2 of 10
Which of the following represents the correct unit for the decay constant (λ)?

Solution: Since Rate = λN, λ has the dimensions of time-1 (e.g., s-1, min-1, year-1).

Question 3 of 10
The decay constant of a radioactive substance is 0.0231 day-1. Its half-life is:

Solution: t1/2 = 0.693 / 0.0231 = 30 days.

Question 4 of 10
During beta-minus decay, a neutron inside the nucleus converts into:

Solution: Neutron decay produces a proton, an electron (β-), and an electron antineutrino.

Question 5 of 10
Which nuclear reaction involves the absorption of an inner-orbital electron?

Solution: Electron capture involves the nucleus capturing an inner-shell electron (K-capture).

Question 6 of 10
The age of fossils and archaeological specimens is commonly determined using isotopes of:

Solution: Radiocarbon dating uses radioactive Carbon-14 (14C) with a half-life of 5730 years.

Question 7 of 10
If the initial activity of a radioactive sample is 800 Bq and it drops to 100 Bq after 36 hours, what is the half-life?

Solution: 800 → 400 → 200 → 100 means 3 half-lives have elapsed in 36 hours. Thus, t1/2 = 36 / 3 = 12 hours.

Question 8 of 10
Which of the following statements about radioactive isotopes is incorrect?

Solution: The half-life of a radioactive substance is independent of its initial amount.

Question 9 of 10
What is formed when 92U238 emits one alpha particle?

Solution: Subtracting 4 from mass number (238 - 4 = 234) and 2 from atomic number (92 - 2 = 90) yields 90Th234.

Question 10 of 10
What is the probability of decay per unit time for a radioactive nucleus known as?

Solution: The decay constant (λ) represents the probability of decay of a single nucleus per unit time.
