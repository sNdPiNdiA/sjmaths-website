Discovery of Radioactivity

Radioactivity is the spontaneous emission of ionizing radiation from unstable atomic nuclei seeking energetic stabilization. Its discovery marked the transition from classical physics to nuclear science, proving that the atom is not indivisible.

UP PGT Chemistry Subject Code 02 B.Sc. / M.Sc. Level

Historical Discovery and Uranium Salts

  • Henri Becquerel discovered radioactivity in 1896 while investigating phosphorescence in uranium compounds such as potassium uranyl sulfate, K2UO2(SO4)2.
  • Becquerel observed that photographic plates wrapped in black paper were fogged by uranium salts even in complete darkness, proving the emission of penetrating radiation independent of external sunlight.
  • Initial experiments showed that the radiation ionized air and discharged electroscopes, establishing its physical nature as distinct from ordinary light.

Contributions of Marie and Pierre Curie

  • Marie Curie coined the term radioactivity to describe the atomic property of spontaneous radiation emission observed in uranium and its ores.
  • By systematically processing pitchblende ore, Marie and Pierre Curie discovered two new radioactive elements: polonium (named after Poland) and radium.
  • Quantitative measurements by the Curies proved that radioactivity is an atomic property proportional to the concentration of uranium or thorium, independent of chemical combination or physical state.

Characterization of Radiations (α, β, γ)

  • Ernest Rutherford and Paul Villard classified the emissions into three types based on their behavior in electric and magnetic fields: alpha (α), beta (β), and gamma (γ) rays.
  • Alpha particles were identified as doubly ionized helium nuclei (He2+), beta particles as high-speed electrons (e-), and gamma rays as high-frequency electromagnetic radiation.
  • The discovery of these distinct rays proved that nuclear transformations involve the alteration of subatomic structure accompanied by tremendous energy release.
📐 Key Formulas & Equations
Decay Constant and Half-Life
t1/2 = ln(2) / λ ≈ 0.693 / λ
Conditions: For any radioactive nuclide undergoing monomolecular decay
Units: t1/2 in seconds, λ in s-1
Mean Life
τ = 1 / λ
Conditions: Average lifetime of a radioactive nucleus
Units: τ in seconds, λ in s-1
Activity of a Sample
A = -dNdt = λN
Conditions: Rate of nuclear disintegrations
Units: A in Becquerel (Bq) or Curie (Ci), N dimensionless
Radioactive Decay Law
N(t) = N0 e-λt
Conditions: First-order nuclear decay kinetics
Units: N in number of nuclei, t in seconds, λ in s-1
⚠️ Important Exceptions & Anomalies
Exception Radioactive decay rate is independent of external thermodynamic conditions.

Observation: Electron capture decay rate can vary slightly under extreme pressure. — Electron capture depends on the inner-shell electron density near the nucleus, which can be marginally compressed in high-pressure metallic lattices.

Exception Radioactive elements are exclusively found among heavy elements with Z > 83.

Observation: Technetium (Z = 43) and Promethium (Z = 61) are radioactive light elements. — All isotopes of Tc and Pm possess unstable neutron-to-proton ratios due to nuclear shell closure limitations.

💡 Shortcuts & Memory Aids
Activity Fraction Rule
N / N0 = (1/2)n

Application: Where n is the number of half-lives elapsed (t / t1/2), allowing rapid calculation of remaining active nuclei without exponentials.

Decay Constant Memory
λ × t1/2 = 0.693

Application: Use this relation to instantly convert half-life to decay constant or vice versa in numerical calculations.

Curie to Becquerel Conversion
1 Ci = 3.7 × 1010 Bq

Application: Standard conversion factor for radioactivity units frequently tested in nuclear chemistry problems.

🎯 Key Exam Points
  • Henri Becquerel discovered radioactivity in 1896 using uranium salts and photographic plates.
  • Marie Curie isolated radium and polonium from pitchblende, establishing radioactivity as an atomic property.
  • Radioactivity is unaffected by temperature, pressure, catalysts, or chemical bonding state.
  • Alpha particles are identical to helium nuclei with a mass of 4 amu and charge +2.
  • The SI unit of radioactivity is the Becquerel (Bq), defined as one disintegration per second.
  • The older unit of radioactivity, the Curie (Ci), equals 3.7 × 1010 disintegrations per second.
🚫 Common Errors & Confusions
  • Confusing the discovery of X-rays by Röntgen (1895) with the discovery of radioactivity by Becquerel (1896).
  • Assuming radioactivity is a chemical reaction; it is strictly a nuclear phenomenon.
  • Forgetting that gamma rays are electromagnetic waves and carry no mass or electrical charge.
  • Using incorrect units for activity by confusing Curies with Becquerels.

⚡ Quick Revision: Discovery of Radioactivity

Core formulas, rapid recall points, and distinguishing criteria at a glance.

Spontaneous Nuclear Disintegration

Radioactivity is the spontaneous decay of unstable atomic nuclei accompanied by particle emission. It proceeds via first-order kinetics governed by the decay constant λ.

Nature of Radiations

Emissions are categorized into alpha particles, beta particles, and gamma radiation, each possessing distinct mass, charge, and interaction characteristics with matter.

Independence from External Factors

Because radioactivity originates deep within the atomic nucleus, external parameters such as temperature, pressure, and chemical environment have no effect on decay rates.

Units of Measurement

Activity is measured in Becquerels (Bq) in the SI system, representing disintegrations per second, alongside the traditional unit Curie (Ci).

📚 Terms & Units Glossary

Radioactivity (Spontaneous decay)
Spontaneous emission of radiation from unstable nuclei. SI unit: Bq.
Decay Constant (λ)
Probability of decay of a nucleus per unit time. Unit: s-1.
Half-Life (t1/2)
Time required for half of the radioactive nuclei to decay. Unit: s.
Activity (A)
Rate of nuclear disintegration. Unit: Becquerel (Bq).
Pitchblende (Uranium ore)
Complex mineral containing uranium, radium, and polonium.
Curie (Ci)
Traditional activity unit equal to 3.7 × 1010 Bq.

🎯 Key Takeaways

  • Discovery year: 1896 by Henri Becquerel.
  • Term coined by: Marie Curie.
  • Half-life formula: t1/2 = 0.693 / λ.
  • 1 Curie = 3.7 × 1010 Bq.
  • Alpha particles = 2He4 nuclei.

📌 Quick Recall

  • Radioactivity originates from nuclear instability.
  • Decay follows first-order exponential kinetics.
  • Radiations comprise α, β, and γ rays.
  • Process is entirely independent of temperature and pressure.

⚖️ Key Differences

X-rays VS Gamma rays

X-rays originate from extra-nuclear electronic transitions, whereas gamma rays originate from nuclear transitions.

Question 1 of 10
Which of the following properties distinguishes gamma rays from X-rays?

Solution: Gamma rays originate from atomic nuclei, whereas X-rays originate from electronic transitions in inner shells.

Question 2 of 10
If 75% of a radioactive substance decays in 2 half-lives, what fraction remains after 4 half-lives?

Solution: After 4 half-lives, the remaining fraction is (1/2)4 = 1/16.

Question 3 of 10
The decay constant of a radioactive element is 0.0231 min-1. Its half-life is:

Solution: t1/2 = 0.693 / 0.0231 = 30 minutes.

Question 4 of 10
Which radioactive emission is deflected toward the negative electrode in an electric field?

Solution: Alpha rays carry a positive charge (+2) and are deflected toward the negative electrode.

Question 5 of 10
Marie Curie received her second Nobel Prize in which field?

Solution: Marie Curie received the 1911 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for the isolation of pure radium.

Question 6 of 10
Which subatomic particle is emitted during beta-minus decay?

Solution: Beta-minus decay involves the emission of a high-speed electron from the nucleus.

Question 7 of 10
What is the mass number and atomic number change when a gamma photon is emitted?

Solution: Gamma emission is purely energetic de-excitation; mass number and atomic number remain unchanged.

Question 8 of 10
The average life (τ) and half-life (t1/2) are related by:

Solution: τ = 1 / λ and t1/2 = 0.693 / λ, hence τ = t1/2 / 0.693 = 1.44 t1/2.

Question 9 of 10
Which of the following instruments is commonly used to detect radioactivity?

Solution: The Geiger-Müller counter is standard equipment for detecting ionizing radiation via gas ionization.

Question 10 of 10
Pitchblende is an ore primarily rich in which metal?

Solution: Pitchblende is a uraninite-rich mineral containing uranium oxides.
