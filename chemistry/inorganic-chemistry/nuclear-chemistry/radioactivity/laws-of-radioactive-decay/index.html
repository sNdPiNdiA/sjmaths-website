Laws of Radioactive Decay

Radioactive decay is a first-order kinetic process where unstable atomic nuclei spontaneously transform into more stable configurations by emitting radiation. The rate of disintegration is directly proportional to the number of undecayed radioactive atoms present at any given time.

UP PGT Chemistry Subject Code 02 B.Sc. / M.Sc. Level

First-Order Kinetics of Radioactive Disintegration

  • Radioactive decay strictly follows first-order kinetics, meaning the rate depends solely on the concentration of undecayed nuclei.
  • The decay constant λ is characteristic of a specific radioactive nuclide and is independent of temperature, pressure, and chemical state.
  • The probability of decay for any single nucleus remains constant over time and is unaffected by its past history.

Quantitative Relations: Half-Life and Average Life

  • The half-life (t1/2) is the time required for exactly half of the initial radioactive nuclei to undergo disintegration.
  • The average life (τ) represents the sum of the lives of all atoms divided by the total number of atoms, equal to 1/λ.
  • Radioactive decay can also be expressed using base-10 logarithms: t = (2.303 / λ) log (N0 / Nt).

Radioactive Equilibrium and Successive Disintegration

  • In a decay chain where parent (A) forms daughter (B) which decays to (C), secular equilibrium occurs when the half-life of the parent is much greater than the daughter.
  • At secular equilibrium, the rates of decay of parent and daughter become equal: λANA = λBNB.
  • Transient equilibrium occurs when the parent's half-life is greater than the daughter's, but both are of comparable magnitude.

⚠️ Important Exceptions & Anomalies
Exception Radioactive decay rates are completely independent of external physicochemical conditions.

Observation: Electron capture and internal conversion processes — Electron capture depends on the electron density of the inner s-orbitals, which can be altered slightly by extreme chemical environments or high pressure.

Exception Radioactive elements decay at a strictly constant rate.

Observation: Apparent fluctuations in low count rates — Radioactive decay is a statistical, random process governed by Poisson statistics, leading to statistical fluctuations in measured activity at low sample sizes.

💡 Shortcuts & Memory Aids
Fraction Remaining Shortcut
Nt / N0 = (1 / 2)n

Application: Use this directly when the given time t is an exact multiple of the half-life (t = n × t1/2).

Activity Calculation Mnemonic
A = λN

Application: Remember that activity (disintegrations per second) is directly proportional to the number of active nuclei present at that instant.

Percentage Decayed vs Remaining
% Decayed = 100 - % Remaining

Application: Always convert percentage decayed to percentage remaining before applying the logarithmic first-order decay equation.

🎯 Key Exam Points
  • Radioactive decay is a unimolecular first-order process.
  • The half-life of a radioactive isotope is inversely proportional to its decay constant.
  • About 99.9% of a radioactive substance decays after approximately 10 half-lives.
  • Curie (Ci) and Becquerel (Bq) are the standard units of radioactivity, where 1 Ci = 3.7 × 1010 disintegrations per second.
  • The decay constant λ has the dimensions of time inverse (T-1).
  • Radioactive equilibrium is achieved in serial transformations when rate of formation equals rate of decay.
🚫 Common Errors & Confusions
  • Confusing half-life with average life; remember average life is 1.44 times the half-life.
  • Applying natural logarithm formulas with base-10 log without multiplying by 2.303.
  • Assuming temperature changes alter radioactive decay rates just like ordinary chemical reactions.
  • Using mass instead of moles or number of atoms when calculating decay fractions.

⚡ Quick Revision: Laws of Radioactive Decay

Core formulas, rapid recall points, and distinguishing criteria at a glance.

Exponential Decay Law

The number of active nuclei decreases exponentially with time according to Nt = N0 e-λt.

Decay Constant and Half-Life

Half-life is a fixed characteristic of every radioisotope given by t1/2 = 0.693 / λ.

Radioactivity Measurement

Activity measures the disintegration rate A = -dN/dt = λN, expressed in Becquerels or Curies.

Radioactive Series

Heavy unstable nuclides decay through a sequence of alpha and beta emissions until stable lead isotopes are formed.

🎯 Key Takeaways

  • t1/2 = 0.693 / λ
  • Nt = N0(1/2)n
  • τ = 1.44 × t1/2
  • At = A0 e-λt
  • Radioactive decay is independent of external conditions.

📌 Quick Recall

  • Decay kinetics follow first-order rate laws.
  • Half-life is independent of initial concentration.
  • Radioactivity units include Becquerel and Curie.
  • Secular equilibrium occurs when parent half-life exceeds daughter half-life.

⚖️ Key Differences

Half-life VS Average life

Half-life is time for 50% decay (0.693/λ), whereas average life is the mean lifetime of all nuclei (1/λ).

Question 1 of 18

What is the order of a radioactive decay reaction?

Explanation: Radioactive decay is a spontaneous process that strictly follows first-order kinetics where the rate is proportional to the first power of the number of active nuclei.

Question 2 of 18

The half-life of a radioactive element is 10 days. What is its decay constant (λ)?

Explanation: λ = 0.693 / t1/2 = 0.693 / 10 = 0.0693 day-1.

Question 3 of 18

If 75% of a radioactive substance decays in 2 hours, what is its half-life?

Explanation: 75% decay means 25% remains, which is (1/2)2. Thus 2 hours equals 2 half-lives, making each half-life 1 hour (60 minutes).

Question 4 of 18

Which of the following factors affects the rate of radioactive decay?

Explanation: Radioactive decay is a nuclear process completely unaffected by external physical conditions such as temperature, pressure, or catalysts.

Question 5 of 18

What is the relationship between average life (τ) and half-life (t1/2)?

Explanation: τ = 1 / λ and t1/2 = 0.693 / λ, therefore τ = t1/2 / 0.693 = 1.44 t1/2.

Question 6 of 18

The unit Curie (Ci) is equivalent to how many disintegrations per second?

Explanation: 1 Curie is defined as the activity of 1 gram of radium-226, which equals 3.7 × 1010 disintegrations per second.

Question 7 of 18

What fraction of a radioactive sample remains after 4 half-lives?

Explanation: Remaining fraction = (1/2)n = (1/2)4 = 1/16.

Question 8 of 18

The decay constant of a radioactive element depends on:

Explanation: The decay constant (λ) is a characteristic property specific to each individual radioactive nuclide.

Question 9 of 18

In secular equilibrium between parent (A) and daughter (B):

Explanation: At secular equilibrium, the activity of the parent equals the activity of the daughter, hence λANA = λBNB.

Question 10 of 18

What is the dimension of the radioactive decay constant (λ)?

Explanation: Since rate = λN and N is dimensionless, λ has dimensions of time inverse (T-1).

Question 11 of 18

After 3 half-lives, the percentage of radioactive substance decayed is:

Explanation: Remaining fraction = (1/2)3 = 1/8 = 12.5%. Therefore, decayed percentage = 100% - 12.5% = 87.5%.

Question 12 of 18

The rate of radioactive disintegration is measured by:

Explanation: Geiger-Muller counters and scintillation counters are standard instruments used to measure radiation and disintegration rates.

Question 13 of 18

If the half-life of a substance is infinity, its decay constant is:

Explanation: λ = 0.693 / t1/2. If t1/2 = ∞, λ = 0, meaning the nuclide is stable.

Question 14 of 18

Which radioactive emission does not alter the mass number of a nucleus?

Explanation: Beta particles (electrons/positrons) and gamma photons have negligible mass numbers compared to nucleons, leaving the mass number unchanged.

Question 15 of 18

The time required for 99.9% decay of a first-order radioactive substance is approximately equal to:

Explanation: (1/2)10 = 1/1024 ≈ 0.000976, which means 99.9% has decayed after about 10 half-lives.

Question 16 of 18

What is the SI unit of radioactivity?

Explanation: The SI unit of radioactivity is the Becquerel (Bq), defined as one disintegration per second.

Question 17 of 18

In radioactive disintegration, the emission of an alpha particle results in a daughter nucleus with:

Explanation: An alpha particle is a helium nucleus (2He4), so its emission decreases mass number by 4 and atomic number by 2.

Question 18 of 18

For a radioactive isotope, a plot of ln(Nt) versus time (t) is a straight line with a slope equal to:

Explanation: From ln(Nt) = ln(N0) - λt, the slope of the linear plot is -λ.

Question 1 of 10
The half-life of carbon-14 is 5730 years. What fraction of carbon-14 will remain after 17190 years?

Solution: Number of half-lives n = 17190 / 5730 = 3. Remaining fraction = (1/2)3 = 1/8.

Question 2 of 10
What is the decay constant of a radioactive substance whose half-life is 6.93 seconds?

Solution: λ = 0.693 / 6.93 = 0.1 s-1.

Question 3 of 10
If the activity of a radioactive sample drops to 1/16th of its initial value in 40 hours, the half-life is:

Solution: 1/16 = (1/2)4, meaning 4 half-lives passed in 40 hours. Thus, half-life = 40 / 4 = 10 hours.

Question 4 of 10
Which expression correctly relates half-life (t1/2) to the decay constant (λ)?

Solution: Since t1/2 = 0.693 / λ, rearranging gives t1/2 × λ = 0.693.

Question 5 of 10
Radioactive disintegration rate is independent of:

Solution: Temperature and pressure do not influence nuclear decay rates.

Question 6 of 10
The disintegration constant of a nuclide is 2.31 × 10-3 hour-1. Its half-life is:

Solution: t1/2 = 0.693 / (2.31 × 10-3) = 300 hours.

Question 7 of 10
What is the percentage of a radioactive substance decayed after 2 average lives?

Solution: Fraction remaining after time t is e-λt. For t = 2τ = 2/λ, fraction remaining = e-2 ≈ 0.135. Decayed fraction = 1 - 0.135 = 0.865 or 86.5%.

Question 8 of 10
Secular equilibrium is attained when:

Solution: Secular equilibrium requires the parent isotope to be extremely long-lived compared to the daughter (λA << λB).

Question 9 of 10
If 1.0g of a radioactive isotope reduces to 0.125g in 15 days, its half-life is:

Solution: 0.125g / 1.0g = 1/8 = (1/2)3. Three half-lives equal 15 days, so t1/2 = 5 days.

Question 10 of 10
Which radiation emitted during radioactive decay carries no electrical charge?

Solution: Gamma rays are high-energy electromagnetic radiation and carry zero charge and zero mass.
