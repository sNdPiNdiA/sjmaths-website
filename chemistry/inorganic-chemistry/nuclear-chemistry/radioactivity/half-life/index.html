Half-Life of Radioactive Decay

Radioactive decay follows first-order kinetics where half-life is independent of the initial concentration of the radionuclide. The decay constant (λ) directly determines the rate at which a radioactive isotope disintegrates over time.

UP PGT Chemistry Subject Code 02 B.Sc. / M.Sc. Level

Mathematical Derivation and Characteristics of First-Order Decay

  • Radioactive disintegration is a first-order kinetic process dependent solely on the number of undecayed nuclei present.
  • The differential rate law is expressed as -dN/dt = λN, where λ is the decay constant.
  • Integration yields ln(N0/N) = λt, establishing a linear relationship between natural log of remaining activity and time.
  • The half-life (t1/2) is strictly independent of initial concentration or mass of the radioactive substance.

Multi-Half-Life Calculations and Radioactive Activity

  • After n half-lives, the fraction of the original substance remaining is given by (1/2)n.
  • Radioactive activity (A) is defined as the rate of decay (-dN/dt) and is proportional to the number of active nuclei: A = λN.
  • Units of radioactivity include Becquerel (Bq), where 1 Bq = 1 disintegration per second, and Curie (Ci), where 1 Ci = 3.7 × 1010 disintegrations per second.
  • Specific activity represents the activity per unit mass of a radioactive sample, critical in radiochemical dating.

Radioactive Equilibria and Secular Variations

  • In a parent-daughter decay series (A → B → C), secular equilibrium occurs when the half-life of the parent is vastly greater than that of the daughter (t1/2(A) >> t1/2(B)).
  • Under secular equilibrium, the rates of formation and decay of the daughter isotope become equal, leading to a constant activity ratio: λANA = λBNB.
  • Transient equilibrium occurs when the parent half-life is moderately greater than the daughter half-life.
  • Carbon dating utilizes the constant atmospheric ratio of 14C to 12C and the known half-life of 14C (5730 years) to determine the age of organic artifacts.
📐 Key Formulas & Equations
Integrated Rate Law for Radioactive Decay
N(t) = N0 e-λt
Conditions: Determining remaining nuclei after time t
Units: N0 and N(t) in number of nuclei or moles
Amount Remaining after n Half-Lives
N = N0 (1 / 2)n
Conditions: When time t is an exact multiple of half-life (t = n × t1/2)
Units: n is dimensionless; N and N0 in mass or activity units
Average Life
τ = 1 / λ = 1.443 × t1/2
Conditions: Reciprocal of the radioactive decay constant
Units: τ in units of time
Radioactive Decay Half-Life
t1/2 = ln(2) / λ = 0.693 / λ
Conditions: First-order nuclear disintegration processes
Units: t1/2 in seconds (s), years (y); λ in s-1, y-1
⚠️ Important Exceptions & Anomalies
Exception Radioactive decay rates are generally independent of external physical and chemical conditions.

Observation: Electron capture and internal conversion decay rates can show slight variations. — Electron density near the nucleus can be marginally altered by extreme high pressures or chemical bonding states for isotopes decaying via electron capture.

Exception Half-life of a given isotope is strictly constant under normal terrestrial conditions.

Observation: Apparent alterations in decay rates in geological time scales or artificial nuclear environments. — Cosmic ray fluxes and neutron bombardment can alter isotope populations, though the intrinsic decay constant of an isolated nuclide remains invariant.

💡 Shortcuts & Memory Aids
Fraction Remaining Shortcut
N / N0 = (1/2)(t / t1/2)

Application: Use this exponential form when the given time t is not an integer multiple of t1/2 to bypass logarithms.

Decay Constant to Half-Life Memory Anchor
0.693 ≈ ln(2)

Application: Quickly estimate t1/2 as 70% of the reciprocal of λ (or 10/14 of λ-1).

Percentage Remaining Rapid Calculation

Application: Mentally track successive half-lives (1, 2, 3, 4 t1/2) to solve percentage remaining problems without paper.

🎯 Key Exam Points
  • Radioactive decay strictly follows first-order chemical kinetics.
  • The half-life of 14C used in carbon dating is approximately 5730 years.
  • Average life (τ) is equal to 1.443 times the half-life (t1/2).
  • Activity of a radioactive sample decreases exponentially with time.
  • Secular equilibrium requires the parent half-life to be much greater than the daughter half-life.
  • One Curie (Ci) equals 3.7 × 1010 disintegrations per second.
🚫 Common Errors & Confusions
  • Assuming half-life depends on initial mass or concentration; remember it is constant for a given radionuclide.
  • Confusing average life (τ) with half-life (t1/2); τ is always longer.
  • Forgetting to convert units when time is given in years but decay constant is in seconds-1.
  • Incorrectly calculating remaining fractions when time elapsed is not an exact multiple of half-life.

⚡ Quick Revision: Half-Life of Radioactive Decay

Core formulas, rapid recall points, and distinguishing criteria at a glance.

Decay Constant and Half-Life Relation

The half-life is inversely proportional to the decay constant λ, linked by the natural logarithm of 2 (0.693).

Exponential Decay Law

The number of active nuclei decreases exponentially over time according to N(t) = N0e-λt.

Radioactive Equilibrium

Secular and transient equilibria describe parent-daughter nuclide relationships where decay rates balance formation rates.

Applications in Dating

Half-life values enable absolute age determination of geological formations and archaeological artifacts using isotopic ratios.

📚 Terms & Units Glossary

Decay Constant (λ)
Proportionality constant between decay rate and number of nuclei, measured in s-1.
Half-Life (t1/2)
Time required for half of the radioactive nuclei to disintegrate, measured in seconds or years.
Average Life (τ)
Statistical average lifetime of radioactive nuclei, equal to 1/λ.
Activity (A)
Number of disintegrations per unit time (-dN/dt), measured in Becquerel.
Secular Equilibrium (Parent-Daughter Balance)
State where parent half-life greatly exceeds daughter half-life, creating equal activities.
Specific Activity (As)
Radioactivity per unit mass of a sample, measured in Bq/g.

🎯 Key Takeaways

  • t1/2 = 0.693 / λ
  • N = N0(1/2)n
  • τ = 1.443 × t1/2
  • 1 Curie = 3.7 × 1010 Bq
  • Radioactive decay is independent of temperature and pressure.

📌 Quick Recall

  • Radioactive decay is a unimolecular first-order process.
  • Half-life remains constant regardless of external conditions or initial sample size.
  • Exponential decay dictates that complete exhaustion takes infinite theoretical time.
  • Isotopic dating relies on known half-lives of long-lived nuclides like 14C and 238U.

⚖️ Key Differences

Half-life (t1/2) VS Average life (τ)

Half-life is the time for 50% decay (0.693/λ), whereas average life is the sum of lifetimes of all nuclei divided by initial number (1/λ).

Question 1 of 19

What is the relationship between the half-life (t_1/2) and the decay constant (lambda) for a radioactive isotope?

Explanation: Derived from first-order integrated rate law, t_1/2 = ln(2) / lambda = 0.693 / lambda.

Question 2 of 19

If the half-life of a radioactive element is 10 days, what fraction of the original sample remains after 30 days?

Explanation: 30 days represents 3 half-lives (30/10 = 3). The remaining fraction is (1/2)^3 = 1/8.

Question 3 of 19

Radioactive decay kinetics corresponds to which order of reaction?

Explanation: Radioactive decay is independent of concentration and follows first-order rate kinetics.

Question 4 of 19

What is the average life (tau) of a radioactive substance in terms of its decay constant (lambda)?

Explanation: Average life is the reciprocal of the decay constant, tau = 1 / lambda.

Question 5 of 19

Which of the following units is used to measure radioactivity?

Explanation: Becquerel (Bq) is the SI unit of radioactivity, representing one disintegration per second.

Question 6 of 19

How does the half-life of a radioactive isotope change when the initial mass of the sample is doubled?

Explanation: Half-life of a first-order radioactive decay is independent of the initial concentration or mass.

Question 7 of 19

What condition defines secular equilibrium in a radioactive decay series (A -> B -> C)?

Explanation: Secular equilibrium occurs when the parent half-life is vastly greater than the daughter half-life.

Question 8 of 19

If 75% of a radioactive substance has decayed in 20 hours, what is its half-life?

Explanation: If 75% has decayed, 25% remains, which corresponds to 2 half-lives (100% -> 50% -> 25%). Thus, 2 * t_1/2 = 20 hours, so t_1/2 = 10 hours.

Question 9 of 19

What is the value of 1 Curie (Ci) in disintegrations per second?

Explanation: One Curie is defined as exactly 3.7 * 10^10 disintegrations per second (Bq).

Question 10 of 19

Which isotope is commonly used in carbon dating of archaeological organic samples?

Explanation: Carbon-14 (14C) with a half-life of 5730 years is standard for radiocarbon dating.

Question 11 of 19

The rate of radioactive decay is affected by which of the following external parameters?

Explanation: Radioactive decay is a nuclear process independent of external temperature, pressure, or chemical state.

Question 12 of 19

What is the relationship between average life (tau) and half-life (t_1/2)?

Explanation: Since tau = 1/lambda and t_1/2 = 0.693/lambda, tau = (1/0.693) t_1/2 = 1.443 t_1/2.

Question 13 of 19

What fraction of a radioactive isotope remains after 4 half-lives?

Explanation: Remaining fraction after n half-lives is (1/2)^n. For n = 4, (1/2)^4 = 1/16.

Question 14 of 19

If the decay constant lambda is 0.0693 year^-1, what is the half-life of the isotope?

Explanation: t_1/2 = 0.693 / 0.0693 = 10 years.

Question 15 of 19

What does the term 'activity' refer to in nuclear chemistry?

Explanation: Activity is defined as the number of radioactive disintegrations occurring per unit time (-dN/dt).

Question 16 of 19

Which decay mode can exhibit slight rate changes due to extreme pressure variations affecting electron density?

Explanation: Electron capture involves inner-shell orbital electrons, which can be marginally affected by high pressure altering chemical electron densities.

Question 17 of 19

In a first-order decay plot of ln(N) versus time (t), what does the slope of the straight line represent?

Explanation: From ln(N) = ln(N_0) - lambda*t, the slope of the line is -lambda.

Question 18 of 19

What is the percentage of a radioactive substance decayed after 3 half-lives?

Explanation: After 3 half-lives, 12.5% remains, meaning 100% - 12.5% = 87.5% has decayed.

Question 19 of 19

Which particle is emitted during beta-minus decay?

Explanation: Beta-minus decay involves the emission of a high-speed electron (beta particle) formed when a neutron converts to a proton.

Question 5 of 10
