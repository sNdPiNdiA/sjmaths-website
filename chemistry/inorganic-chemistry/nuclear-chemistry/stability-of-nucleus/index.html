Inorganic Chemistry

Stability of Nucleus

Nuclear stability is governed by the delicate balance between the attractive short-range strong nuclear forces and the repulsive electrostatic forces among protons. Nuclei whose neutron-to-proton ratios fall within the zone of stability exhibit permanent existence against radioactive decay.

UP PGT Chemistry Subject Code 02 B.Sc. / M.Sc. Level

Neutron-Proton Ratio and the Belt of Stability

  • Light stable nuclei (Z ≤ 20) typically exhibit an optimal neutron-to-proton ratio of approximately 1:1.
  • As atomic number Z increases, the repulsive electrostatic force increases faster than the strong nuclear force, shifting the stable N/Z ratio up to 1.5:1 for heavy elements.
  • Nuclei lying above the belt of stability possess an excess of neutrons and undergo β- decay to decrease N and increase Z.
  • Nuclei lying below the belt of stability possess a deficit of neutrons and undergo positron emission or electron capture to increase N and decrease Z.

Nuclear Binding Energy and Packing Fraction

  • Mass defect arises because the mass of a assembled nucleus is always less than the sum of the rest masses of its constituent nucleons.
  • The curve of binding energy per nucleon peaks sharply around mass number A = 50 to 80, with 56Fe representing one of the most stable configurations.
  • Nuclear fission of very heavy nuclei (A > 200) and nuclear fusion of very light nuclei (A < 20) both proceed in the direction of increased binding energy per nucleon.
  • Packing fraction, defined as (M - A)/A, relates directly to nuclear stability where negative packing fraction indicates high stability.

Magic Numbers and Nuclear Shell Model

  • Nuclei containing specific numbers of protons or neutrons, known as magic numbers (2, 8, 20, 28, 50, 82, 126), exhibit exceptional stability.
  • Magic numbers correspond to completely filled nuclear energy shells, analogous to noble gas electron configurations in atomic physics.
  • Doubly magic nuclei, such as 4He, 16O, 40Ca, and 208Pb, possess maximum binding energy and resistance to decay.
  • Even numbers of both protons and neutrons (even-even nuclei) are overwhelmingly more stable than odd-odd nuclei due to nucleon pairing effects.

⚠️ Important Exceptions & Anomalies
Exception Odd-Odd nuclei are generally unstable and rare in nature.

Observation: 2H, 6Li, 10B, and 14N are stable odd-odd light nuclei. — In very light systems, pairing energy terms can sometimes offset the inherent odd-odd instability.

Exception Elements with atomic number Z > 83 are exclusively radioactive.

Observation: Bismuth (Z = 83) possesses a primordial isotope 209Bi that behaves as virtually stable with an extremely long half-life. — Extremely large shell-stabilization effects postpone rapid radioactive decay despite high Z.

🎯 Key Exam Points
  • The nuclear force is charge-independent, highly attractive at short range (~1-2 fm), and repulsive at ultra-short distances.
  • Iron isotope 56Fe possesses one of the highest binding energies per nucleon (~8.8 MeV/nucleon).
  • Electron capture is a competitive process with positron emission, favored in heavier atoms where inner-shell electrons penetrate the nucleus.
  • The total binding energy of a nucleus is directly proportional to its mass defect multiplied by 931.5 MeV/u.
  • All isotopes of elements with atomic numbers greater than 83 are radioactive and undergo spontaneous decay series.
  • Isotopes having magic numbers of neutrons or protons have an abnormally high natural abundance.
🚫 Common Errors & Confusions
  • Confusing mass defect (Δm) with the total mass of individual nucleons; Δm is always the sum of masses minus the actual nuclear mass.
  • Assuming all odd-odd nuclei are radioactive; light odd-odd nuclei like 14N are stable.
  • Forgetting that binding energy per nucleon, not total binding energy, is the true measure of nuclear stability.
  • Confusing electron capture with internal conversion; electron capture involves nuclear capture of an orbital electron.

⚡ Quick Revision: Stability of Nucleus

Core formulas, rapid recall points, and distinguishing criteria at a glance.

Nuclear Forces and Stability

Strong nuclear forces bind protons and neutrons together, overcoming electrostatic repulsion between protons within the nuclear radius of ~10-15 m.

The Stability Belt

Stable nuclides cluster along a narrow curving band on the N versus Z plot, deviating upwards from the N = Z line as Z increases.

Binding Energy Mechanics

Mass defect represents the mass converted into binding energy holding the nucleons together, quantified via Einstein's mass-energy equivalence relation.

Shell Structure and Magic Numbers

Discrete quantum energy levels exist inside nuclei, where filled shells corresponding to magic numbers yield exceptional nuclear stability.

📚 Terms & Units Glossary

Mass Defect (Δm)
Difference between combined mass of nucleons and actual nuclear mass, in u.
Binding Energy (BE)
Energy required to break a nucleus into individual isolated nucleons, in MeV.
Magic Numbers (N, Z values)
Nucleon counts representing closed nuclear shells.
Packing Fraction (PF)
Difference between isotopic mass and mass number divided by mass number.
Belt of Stability (N/Z Curve)
Region on N vs Z plot where stable nuclides reside.
Disintegration Constant (λ)
Probability of radioactive decay per unit time, in s-1.

🎯 Key Takeaways

  • 1 atomic mass unit (u) equivalent energy = 931.5 MeV
  • Most stable nucleus with respect to binding energy per nucleon = 56Fe
  • Magic numbers for protons/neutrons = 2, 8, 20, 28, 50, 82, 126
  • Upper limit of stability without radioactive decay = Bismuth (Z = 83)
  • Even-even nuclei stability rule governs maximum natural abundance.

📌 Quick Recall

  • Nuclear stability depends on N/Z ratio, binding energy per nucleon, and nucleon pairing.
  • Deviations from the stability belt trigger radioactive decay modes like beta emission or electron capture.
  • Heavy nuclei decay via alpha emission to shed excessive mass and charge.
  • Closed nuclear shells analogous to noble gases provide extraordinary stability via magic numbers.

⚖️ Key Differences

Total Binding Energy VS Binding Energy per Nucleon

Total BE increases with mass number, whereas BE per nucleon peaks at intermediate mass numbers (A = 50-80) and indicates true stability.

Which of the following nuclei is expected to be most stable?

Explanation: 56Fe lies at the peak of the binding energy per nucleon curve, making it exceptionally stable.

Question 2 of 18

A nuclide with a high N/Z ratio (lying above the stability belt) typically undergoes which type of decay?

Explanation: Nuclei with excess neutrons (β- decay emitters) convert a neutron into a proton to lower their N/Z ratio.

Question 3 of 18

What is the energy equivalent of 1 atomic mass unit (u) in MeV?

Explanation: Multiplying 1 u by c2 yields approximately 931.5 MeV.

Question 4 of 18

Which of the following represents a doubly magic nucleus?

Explanation: 16O has Z = 8 and N = 8, both of which are magic numbers, making it doubly magic.

Question 5 of 18

In terms of nuclear stability, which pairing combination is generally the least stable?

Explanation: Odd-Odd nuclei have unpaired protons and neutrons, making them energetically least stable and rarest in nature.

Question 6 of 18

What is the primary mode of decay for heavy elements with Z > 83?

Explanation: Heavy nuclei emit alpha particles (α decay) to reduce both mass number and atomic number towards the stability zone.

Question 7 of 18

Which of the following is NOT a magic number?

Explanation: The established nuclear magic numbers are 2, 8, 20, 28, 50, 82, and 126. 74 is not a magic number.

Question 8 of 18

What does mass defect represent?

Explanation: Mass defect is the difference between the mass of constituent nucleons and the actual mass of the assembled nucleus.

Question 9 of 18

A nuclide with a low N/Z ratio (lying below the stability belt) can increase its stability by:

Explanation: Low N/Z ratios indicate a proton excess, which is corrected by converting protons into neutrons via positron emission or electron capture.

Question 10 of 18

The strong nuclear force operates effectively over which approximate distance scale?

Explanation: The strong nuclear force is a short-range interaction operating effectively at distances of approximately 1 to 2 femtometers (10-15 m).

Question 11 of 18

Which isotope is considered the threshold beyond which naturally occurring nuclides are radioactive?

Explanation: Bismuth (Z = 83) is the heaviest element with a primordial isotope (209Bi) that is virtually stable.

Question 12 of 18

What is the packing fraction of a nucleus defined as?

Explanation: Packing fraction is defined as the difference between isotopic mass (M) and mass number (A), divided by the mass number (A).

Question 13 of 18

In nuclear fusion reactions, why does energy get released?

Explanation: Fusion of light nuclei creates a heavier nucleus with a higher binding energy per nucleon, releasing the mass difference as energy.

Question 14 of 18

Which of the following forces opposes the strong nuclear force inside the nucleus?

Explanation: Protons experience mutual electrostatic repulsion that tends to disrupt the nucleus, opposed only by the strong nuclear force.

Question 15 of 18

What is the value of N/Z for most light stable elements up to atomic number 20?

Explanation: Light stable elements (Z ≤ 20) generally exhibit an equal number of protons and neutrons, giving an N/Z ratio of 1.0.

Question 16 of 18

Electron capture results in which of the following changes inside the nucleus?

Explanation: During electron capture, an inner orbital electron is captured by the nucleus, converting a proton into a neutron (p + e- → n + ν).

Question 17 of 18

Why are elements with even atomic numbers more abundant in the universe than adjacent odd atomic number elements?

Explanation: Pairing of nucleons in even-even nuclei lowers their energy state, making them significantly more stable and abundant.

Question 18 of 18

Which scientist proposed the liquid drop model of the nucleus?

Explanation: The liquid drop model of nuclear structure was developed by Niels Bohr and refined by John Wheeler.
