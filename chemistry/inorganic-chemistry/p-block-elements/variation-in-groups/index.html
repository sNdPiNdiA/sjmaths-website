Inorganic Chemistry

Variation in Groups

Periodic trends within groups of the p-block elements are dictated by nuclear charge, shielding effect, and principal quantum number expansion. These atomic parameters govern steady gradients in physical properties and distinct oxidation state stability across vertical columns.

UP PGT Chemistry Subject Code 02 B.Sc. / M.Sc. Level

Atomic and Ionic Radius Trends

  • Atomic and ionic radii increase monotonically down a group as principal quantum number (n) increases.
  • The addition of inner electron shells significantly increases shielding, outweighing the increase in nuclear charge.
  • The lanthanide contraction in Group 13 (Tl) and Group 14 (Pb, Bi) results in atomic radii of 6th-period elements being nearly identical to their 5th-period congeners.
  • Anion sizes expand sharply in the first transition series compared to subsequent periods due to diffuse electron charge clouds.

Ionization Enthalpy and Electronegativity

  • Ionization enthalpy decreases down a group due to increased atomic radius and greater distance of valence electrons from the nucleus.
  • Electronegativity values drop sharply from the top to the bottom of p-block groups as metallic character increases.
  • Elements at the bottom of groups exhibit metallic luster and form basic oxides, whereas top elements are typically non-metallic and acidic.
  • Electron gain enthalpies become less negative down a group, though first-row elements often show anomalies due to interelectronic repulsions.

Oxidation States and Inert Pair Effect

  • The group oxidation state is equal to the total number of valence electrons for lighter p-block elements.
  • Heavier elements in groups 13 to 17 exhibit the inert pair effect, favoring oxidation states two units lower than the group maximum.
  • Lower oxidation states become thermodynamically more stable down the group due to poor shielding by intervening d- and f-orbitals.
  • Thallium(I), Lead(II), and Bismuth(III) dominate their respective chemistries owing to the reluctance of ns2 electrons to participate in bonding.

⚠️ Important Exceptions & Anomalies
Exception Ionization enthalpy should decrease monotonically down every group.

Observation: Ga (Group 13) has a higher ionization enthalpy than Al. — Poor shielding of the nucleus by 3d electrons in Gallium increases effective nuclear charge, holding valence electrons more tightly.

Exception Electron gain enthalpy should become less negative down a group.

Observation: Fluorine has a less negative electron gain enthalpy than Chlorine. — Compact 2p orbitals in fluorine cause high interelectronic repulsion, making the addition of an incoming electron less energetically favorable than in 3p orbitals of chlorine.

Exception Catenation ability should decrease down a group.

Observation: Carbon exhibits extensive catenation, while silicon shows significantly reduced catenation and heavier congeners show virtually none. — High C-C bond energy (348 kJ/mol) compared to weaker single bonds of heavier Group 14 elements destabilizes extended chains.

Exception Hydride thermal stability should decrease down a group.

Observation: Water (H2O) exhibits anomalous high thermal stability and extensive intermolecular hydrogen bonding compared to H2S, H2Se, and H2Te. — High electronegativity and small size of oxygen result in strong O-H covalent bonds and robust hydrogen-bonded networks.

🎯 Key Exam Points
  • The maximum covalency of boron is 4 ([BF4]-) due to lack of d-orbitals, whereas aluminum expands its coordination number to 6 ([Al(H2O)6]3+).
  • Stability of lower oxidation states increases down the group due to the inert pair effect: Tl(I) > Tl(III), Pb(II) > Pb(IV), Bi(III) > Bi(V).
  • Fluorine is the strongest oxidizing agent in halogen group because of its low enthalpy of dissociation and high hydration enthalpy of F-.
  • Red phosphorus is polymeric and less reactive than white phosphorus, which consists of discrete tetrahedral P4 molecules.
  • The reducing character of group 15 hydrides increases from NH3 to BiH3 due to a decrease in E-H bond dissociation enthalpy.
  • Sulfuric acid acts as a strong dehydrating agent and non-volatile acid, displacing more volatile acids from their salts.
🚫 Common Errors & Confusions
  • Assuming electron gain enthalpy becomes uniformly less negative down every group without checking Group 17 (F vs Cl).
  • Forgetting that carbon forms stable multiple bonds (pπ-pπ) while silicon forms single bonds due to poor diffuse orbital overlap.
  • Confusing the inert pair effect with shielding; the inert pair effect specifically denotes the non-participation of ns2 valence electrons in heavier p-block elements.
  • Neglecting the impact of lanthanide contraction when comparing atomic radii of 5th and 6th period post-transition elements.

⚡ Quick Revision: Variation in Groups

Core formulas, rapid recall points, and distinguishing criteria at a glance.

Periodic Group Gradients

Vertical trends in groups are governed by the balance between increasing nuclear charge and increasing principal shell distance. This causes metallic character to rise down the group.

Oxidation State Shifts

The stability of higher oxidation states decreases down groups 13-17, whereas lower oxidation states stabilized by the inert pair effect become predominant.

Anomalous Behavior of Period 2

First-row elements display unique properties like small size, high electronegativity, and absence of d-orbitals, leading to strong pπ-pπ bonding and a maximum coordination number of 4.

Hydride and Halide Trends

Thermal stability of group hydrides decreases downward, while their reducing character and basicity show systematic variations driven by bond dissociation energies.

📚 Terms & Units Glossary

Inert Pair Effect (ns² non-participation)
Reluctance of ns² electrons to participate in bond formation in heavier p-block elements.
Lanthanide Contraction (Δr across 4f)
Gradual decrease in atomic/ionic radii of post-lanthanide elements due to poor shielding by 4f electrons.
Cathenation (Self-linking property)
Ability of an element to form bonds with atoms of the same element to create long chains or rings.
Polarizing Power (φ = q/r)
Ability of a cation to distort the electron cloud of an anion, quantified by charge-to-radius ratio.
Electron Gain Enthalpy (ΔegH)
Enthalpy change when an electron is added to a gaseous neutral atom to form a negative ion.
Effective Nuclear Charge (Zeff = Z - S)
Net positive charge experienced by valence electrons in a multi-electron atom.

🎯 Key Takeaways

  • Group oxidation state = Total valence electrons (for top elements); Group oxidation state - 2 (for bottom elements due to inert pair effect).
  • Period 2 elements strictly obey the octet rule and maximum coordination number 4.
  • Electron affinity anomaly: Cl (-349 kJ/mol) is more negative than F (-333 kJ/mol).
  • Hydride reducing character increases down the group: NH3 < PH3 < AsH3 < SbH3 < BiH3.
  • Thermal stability of group 16 hydrides: H2O > H2S > H2Se > H2Te.

📌 Quick Recall

  • Atomic and ionic radii increase down every p-block group.
  • Electronegativity and ionization enthalpy decrease descending a group.
  • Metallic character intensifies toward the bottom of groups.
  • Inert pair effect governs the stable oxidation states of heavier post-transition p-block elements.

⚖️ Key Differences

Electron Gain Enthalpy VS Electronegativity

Electron gain enthalpy is an energetic quantity (energy released adding an electron), whereas electronegativity is a dimensionless tendency of a bonded atom to attract shared electrons.

Question 1 of 19

Which element in Group 13 exhibits the maximum tendency to form stable Tl(I) compounds?

Explanation: Thallium experiences a pronounced inert pair effect, making Tl(I) thermodynamically much more stable than Tl(III).

Question 2 of 19

Why is the electron gain enthalpy of fluorine less negative than that of chlorine?

Explanation: The extremely compact 2p subshell of fluorine causes high electron-electron repulsion, dampening the energy release upon electron addition.

Question 3 of 19

The maximum coordination number of boron is:

Explanation: Boron lacks vacant d-orbitals in its valence shell, restricting its maximum coordination number to 4.

Question 4 of 19

Which hydride of Group 15 is the strongest reducing agent?

Explanation: BiH3 has the weakest E-H bond due to poor orbital overlap, making it release hydrogen most readily and act as the strongest reducing agent.

Question 5 of 19

The lanthanide contraction is responsible for which of the following pairs having nearly identical radii?

Explanation: Zirconium (5th period) and Hafnium (6th period) have nearly identical radii because the filling of 4f orbitals prior to Hf offsets the normal periodic increase.

Question 6 of 19

Which element shows the highest catenation property in Group 14?

Explanation: Carbon has exceptionally high C-C bond energy and small atomic size, allowing extensive catenation.

Question 7 of 19

Which of the following oxides is amphoteric?

Explanation: Tin dioxide (SnO2) reacts with both acids and bases, rendering it amphoteric, unlike acidic CO2, SO3, and weakly acidic SiO2.

Question 8 of 19

The tendency of halogens to act as oxidizing agents decreases in the order:

Explanation: Standard reduction potentials dictate that oxidizing power decreases down the group from fluorine to iodine.

Question 9 of 19

Which of the following is the most acidic hydride of Group 16?

Explanation: H2Te has the weakest H-E bond, releasing protons most readily and thus possessing the highest acidity among Group 16 hydrides.

Question 10 of 19

The stability of +4 oxidation state in Group 14 decreases in the order:

Explanation: Heavier elements increasingly favor the +2 oxidation state due to the inert pair effect, making +4 stability decrease from carbon to lead.

Question 11 of 19

Which halogen exhibits positive oxidation states (+1, +3, +5, +7) in its compounds?

Explanation: Chlorine possesses vacant d-orbitals enabling expanded octets and positive oxidation states, whereas fluorine is strictly -1 due to its high electronegativity.

Question 12 of 19

The correct order of first ionization enthalpy for Group 13 elements is:

Explanation: Due to d-block and f-block contractions, Ga has a higher ionization enthalpy than Al, giving the sequence B > Ga > Al > In > Tl.

Question 13 of 19

Which group 15 element does not form pentahalides?

Explanation: Nitrogen lacks vacant d-orbitals in its valence shell and cannot expand its octet to form pentahalides like NX5.

Question 14 of 19

The physical state of fluorine and chlorine at room temperature is:

Explanation: Both fluorine and chlorine exist as diatomic gases at standard temperature and pressure.

Question 15 of 19

Which of the following carbon allotropes is thermodynamically the most stable at room temperature?

Explanation: Graphite has a standard enthalpy of formation defined as zero and is the most stable allotrope of carbon under standard conditions.

Question 16 of 19

The basic strength of group 15 trihydrides follows which trend?

Explanation: Lewis base strength depends on electron pair availability; smaller nitrogen size concentrates electron density, making NH3 the strongest base.

Question 17 of 19

Which group 16 element shows pronounced metallic luster and semiconductor properties?

Explanation: Tellurium and polonium at the bottom of Group 16 display distinct metallic characteristics and semiconductor behavior.

Question 18 of 19

The property that decreases uniformly down Group 17 is:

Explanation: Electron gain enthalpy becomes less negative down the halogen group (with fluorine as an exception compared to chlorine).

Question 19 of 19

Which metal in group 13 has the lowest melting point and melts in hand?

Explanation: Gallium has an exceptionally low melting point of 303 K (30 °C), allowing it to melt near human body temperature.

Which Group 14 element forms an amphoteric monoxide?

Solution: Lead monoxide (PbO) is amphoteric, dissolving in both acids and bases.

Question 2 of 10
The correct order of boiling points for Group 15 hydrides is:

Solution: NH3 has an anomalously high boiling point due to intermolecular hydrogen bonding, followed by an increase from PH3 to SbH3 due to molecular mass.

Question 3 of 10
Which of the following is the strongest oxidizing agent among halogens?

Solution: Fluorine has the highest standard reduction potential and lowest bond dissociation enthalpy combined with high hydration energy, making F2 the strongest oxidant.

Question 4 of 10
The stable oxidation state of bismuth is +3 rather than +5 because of:

Solution: The inert pair effect stabilizes the +3 oxidation state by preventing the 6s2 electron pair from participating in bonding.

Question 5 of 10
Which of the following elements has the highest electronegativity?

Solution: Fluorine is the most electronegative element in the periodic table (Pauling scale value ~4.0).

Question 6 of 10
The formula of borazole (inorganic benzene) is:

Solution: Borazole (B3N3H6) is isoelectronic and structurally analogous to benzene.

Question 7 of 10
Which Group 16 element exhibits allotropy in both rhombic and monoclinic crystal forms?

Solution: Sulfur famously exhibits extensive allotropy, with rhombic and monoclinic sulfur being the primary crystalline modifications.

Question 8 of 10
The shape of [SiF6]2- ion is:

Solution: Silicon uses sp3d2 hybridization to form an octahedral [SiF6]2- complex ion.

Question 9 of 10
Which halogen does not form an oxoacid containing multiple bonds with oxygen?

Solution: Fluorine is too electronegative and lacks d-orbitals, forming only hypo-fluorous acid (HFO) without higher oxoacids.

Question 10 of 10
The trend in thermal stability of Group 17 hydrogen halides is:

Solution: Thermal stability decreases down the group as H-X bond length increases and bond dissociation energy decreases.
