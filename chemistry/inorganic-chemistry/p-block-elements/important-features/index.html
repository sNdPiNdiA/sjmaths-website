Inorganic Chemistry

Important Features of P-Block Elements

The p-block elements occupy groups 13 to 18 of the periodic table, where the differentiating electron enters the outermost p-orbital. Their chemical properties exhibit wide variation, transitioning from metallic to non-metallic behavior down the groups and across the periods.

UP PGT Chemistry Subject Code 02 B.Sc. / M.Sc. Level

Electronic Configuration and Periodicity

  • The valence shell configuration ranges from ns2np1 in Group 13 to ns2np6 in Group 18.
  • Atomic and ionic radii increase down the group due to the addition of principal quantum shells.
  • Ionization enthalpy generally decreases down the group, accompanied by irregular trends due to d- and f-block contraction.
  • Electron gain enthalpy becomes less negative down the group, though the first member of each group often shows anomalous behavior.

Oxidation States and Chemical Bonding

  • Maximum oxidation state shown by a p-block element equals the total number of valence electrons (group number minus 10).
  • Heavier elements exhibit the inert pair effect, favoring oxidation states two units lower than the group oxidation state.
  • Due to the absence of d-orbitals, period 2 elements exhibit a maximum covalency of 4, whereas period 3 and lower elements can expand their octet using vacant d-orbitals.
  • pπ–pπ multiple bonding is prominent in period 2 elements (C, N, O), while heavier elements form weaker multiple bonds or prefer sigma bonding.

Acid-Base Character and Hydride Trends

  • Oxides transition from strongly basic to amphoteric and finally to acidic as electronegativity increases across a period.
  • Hydrides of p-block elements show decreasing thermal stability and increasing reducing character down the group due to weakening E–H bond dissociation enthalpy.
  • Lewis acid character of trihalides of group 13 elements follows the order: BI3 > BBr3 > BCl3 > BF3 due to back-bonding.
  • The acidic strength of hydrogen halides increases in the order: HF < HCl < HBr < HI.

⚠️ Important Exceptions & Anomalies
Exception Electron gain enthalpy becomes less negative down the group

Observation: Fluorine has a less negative electron gain enthalpy than chlorine — Small atomic size of fluorine results in high interelectronic repulsion within the compact 2p orbitals.

Exception Covalency of group elements is limited by the number of valence electrons

Observation: Boron and carbon cannot expand their octet beyond 4 — Complete absence of low-lying vacant d-orbitals in the second shell.

Exception Boiling points of hydrides increase with molecular mass

Observation: NH3, H2O, and HF possess unusually high boiling points — Presence of strong intermolecular hydrogen bonding in these hydrides.

🎯 Key Exam Points
  • The maximum oxidation state of sulfur in SF6 is +6.
  • Boric acid (H3BO3) acts as a weak Lewis acid by accepting a hydroxyl ion, not by donating a proton.
  • Carbon monoxide (CO) is a neutral oxide, whereas CO2 is acidic.
  • White phosphorus is extremely reactive due to high angular strain in the P4 tetrahedron (bond angle 60°).
  • Silicon forms stable complexes like [SiF6]2–, while carbon cannot form [CF6]2– due to steric hindrance and lack of d-orbitals.
  • Ozone is a powerful oxidizing agent used in water purification and bleaching.
🚫 Common Errors & Confusions
  • Assuming boron trihalides are strong Lewis acids because fluorine is highly electronegative; remember that F→B back-bonding reduces Lewis acidity in BF3.
  • Confusing the melting point trend of group 14 hydrides or group 15 hydrides with anomalous boiling points caused by hydrogen bonding.
  • Believing that all group 15 elements form stable pentahalides; nitrogen does not form NX5 because it lacks d-orbitals.
  • Treating H3BO3 as a Brønsted-Lowry monoprotic acid instead of a Lewis acid.

⚡ Quick Revision: Important Features of P-Block Elements

Core formulas, rapid recall points, and distinguishing criteria at a glance.

Periodic Variations in p-Block

Properties depend on effective nuclear charge and shielding, leading to distinct metallic, metalloid, and non-metallic divisions.

The Inert Pair Effect

Reluctance of s-electrons to participate in bonding down the heavy p-block groups, stabilizing lower oxidation states.

Anomalous Properties of Period 2

Small size, high electronegativity, high ionization enthalpy, and absence of d-orbitals differentiate period 2 elements from heavier congeners.

Bonding and Structure

Ability to form multiple bonds via pπ–pπ overlap distinguishes lighter elements, while heavier elements favor single sigma networks.

📚 Terms & Units Glossary

Inert Pair Effect (ns2 stability)
Reluctance of valence s-electrons to engage in chemical bonding in heavier post-transition elements.
Back Bonding (pπ–pπ donation)
Overlap between filled p-orbital of a substituent and vacant p- or d-orbital of the central atom.
Catenation (Self-linking)
Ability of atoms of the same element to form long chains or rings via covalent bonds.
Allotropy (Structural forms)
Existence of an element in two or more different physical forms in the same physical state.
Disproportionation (Self-redox)
A chemical reaction where a single substance is simultaneously oxidized and reduced.
Amphoteric Oxide (Dual reactivity)
An oxide that reacts with both acids and bases to form salts and water.

🎯 Key Takeaways

  • General valence shell configuration is ns2np1–6.
  • Lewis acidity order of boron halides: BI3 > BBr3 > BCl3 > BF3.
  • Thermal stability of group 16 hydrides decreases from H2O to H2Te.
  • Nitrogen exists as a diatomic gas with a strong triple bond (945 kJ/mol).
  • Fluorine shows only –1 oxidation state due to high electronegativity and absence of d-orbitals.

📌 Quick Recall

  • p-Block elements display broad chemical diversity across groups 13 to 18.
  • Period 2 elements show marked differences due to small size and lack of vacant d-orbitals.
  • Inert pair effect governs the stability of lower oxidation states in heavy p-block elements.
  • Acid-base and redox characteristics exhibit systematic periodic trends down groups and across periods.

⚖️ Key Differences

BF3 Lewis Acidity VS BCl3 Lewis Acidity

BF3 has the strongest back-bonding, making it the weakest Lewis acid among boron trihalides.

Question 1 of 19

Which of the following elements does not exhibit allotropy?

Explanation: Nitrogen does not exhibit allotropy under ordinary conditions due to the high dissociation energy of its N≡N triple bond.

Question 2 of 19

What is the correct order of Lewis acid strength for boron halides?

Explanation: Due to pπ–pπ back bonding which is strongest in BF3 and weakest in BI3, the Lewis acid strength order is BI3 > BBr3 > BCl3 > BF3.

Question 3 of 19

Which of the following hydrides has the highest boiling point?

Explanation: H2O has the highest boiling point due to strong intermolecular hydrogen bonding.

Question 4 of 19

The inert pair effect is most pronounced in which group 13 element?

Explanation: Thallium is the heaviest group 13 element and exhibits the strongest inert pair effect, making the +1 oxidation state exceptionally stable.

Question 5 of 19

Which halogen has the most negative electron gain enthalpy?

Explanation: Chlorine has the most negative electron gain enthalpy because fluorine's small 2p orbital creates high electron-electron repulsion.

Question 6 of 19

The maximum covalency of carbon is:

Explanation: Carbon has no vacant d-orbitals in its valence shell, limiting its maximum covalency to 4.

Question 7 of 19

Which of the following is a neutral oxide?

Explanation: Carbon monoxide (CO) is a neutral oxide, whereas SO2, CO2, and NO2 are acidic oxides.

Question 8 of 19

White phosphorus consists of which molecular unit?

Explanation: White phosphorus exists as discrete tetrahedral P4 molecules with 60° bond angles.

Question 9 of 19

The strongest reducing agent among group 16 hydrides is:

Explanation: H2Te has the weakest E–H bond dissociation enthalpy, making it the strongest reducing agent in the group.

Question 10 of 19

Which boron compound is used as a primary reference acid in volumetric analysis?

Explanation: Orthoboric acid (H3BO3) is a weak monobasic Lewis acid used in volumetric estimations.

Question 11 of 19

The shape of the chlorine trifluoride (ClF3) molecule according to VSEPR theory is:

Explanation: ClF3 has 3 bond pairs and 2 lone pairs, resulting in a T-shaped geometry.

Question 12 of 19

Which species does not exist?

Explanation: [CF6]2– does not exist because carbon lacks d-orbitals to expand its octet and fluorine atoms cause severe steric crowding.

Question 13 of 19

The oxidation state of sulfur in H2SO5 (Caro's acid) is:

Explanation: H2SO5 contains a peroxy linkage (–O–O–); calculating oxidation numbers yields +6 for sulfur.

Question 14 of 19

Which element forms chains (catenation) to the maximum extent in group 14?

Explanation: Carbon has the highest catenation tendency due to its small size and high C–C bond enthalpy.

Question 15 of 19

The gas liberated when concentrated nitric acid reacts with copper is:

Explanation: Reaction of copper with concentrated HNO3 yields nitrogen dioxide (NO2).

Question 16 of 19

Which interhalogen compound has a square pyramidal geometry?

Explanation: IF5 has 5 bond pairs and 1 lone pair, giving it a square pyramidal molecular geometry.

Question 17 of 19

The basicity of orthophosphoric acid (H3PO4) is:

Explanation: H3PO4 contains three ionizable P–OH protons, making it tribasic.

Question 18 of 19

Which of the following nitrogen oxides is paramagnetic?

Explanation: Nitric oxide (NO) contains an odd number of electrons (11 valence electrons), making it paramagnetic.

Question 19 of 19

The compound responsible for the ozone layer depletion is primarily:

Explanation: Chlorofluorocarbons (CFCs) release chlorine radicals in the stratosphere, catalytically destroying ozone.
