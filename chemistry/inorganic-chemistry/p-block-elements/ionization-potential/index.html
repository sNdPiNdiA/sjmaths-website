Inorganic Chemistry

Ionization Potential

Ionization potential, or ionization energy, is the minimum energy required to remove an electron from an isolated gaseous atom in its ground state. It is a quantitative measure of the tendency of an element to form positive ions.

UP PGT Chemistry Subject Code 02 B.Sc. / M.Sc. Level

Periodic Trends Across Periods and Groups

  • Ionization potential increases across a period from left to right due to a steady increase in effective nuclear charge and a decrease in atomic radius.
  • Ionization potential decreases down a group as the principal quantum number increases, leading to greater distance of valence electrons from the nucleus and increased inner-shell shielding.
  • The atomic radius is inversely proportional to ionization potential; smaller atoms hold their valence electrons more tightly.

Electronic Configuration and Stability Effects

  • Half-filled (p3, d5, f7) and completely filled (s2, p6, d10, f14) subshells exhibit extraordinary stability, resulting in anomalously high ionization potentials.
  • The removal of an electron from a stable configuration requires significantly more energy than predicted by general periodic trends.
  • Elements with a single electron outside a stable core (e.g., alkali metals) exhibit very low ionization potentials.

Factors Governing Ionization Potential

  • Nuclear charge (Z) is directly proportional to ionization potential; higher nuclear pull demands more energy for electron removal.
  • Shielding or screening effect by inner-shell electrons reduces the effective nuclear charge experienced by valence electrons, lowering ionization potential.
  • Penetration effect of orbitals follows the order s > p > d > f; s-electrons are held closer to the nucleus and possess higher ionization energies than p-electrons in the same shell.

⚠️ Important Exceptions & Anomalies
Exception IP should continuously increase from B to C to N

Observation: IP of B (801 kJ/mol) > IP of Be (899 kJ/mol) is false; actually IP of Be > B — Be has a fully filled 2s2 configuration, whereas B has a single 2p1 electron which is easier to remove due to lesser penetration and poorer shielding.

Exception IP should continuously increase from N to O to F

Observation: IP of N (1402 kJ/mol) > IP of O (1314 kJ/mol) — Nitrogen possesses a stable, half-filled 2p3 configuration, whereas oxygen has a 2p4 configuration where electron-electron repulsion in the paired p-orbital makes removal easier.

Exception IP should strictly decrease down Group 13

Observation: IP of Tl (589 kJ/mol) > IP of In (558 kJ/mol) — Poor shielding of the nuclear charge by intervening 4f and 5d electrons in thallium (lanthanide contraction) increases the effective nuclear charge on the valence 6p electron.

💡 Shortcuts & Memory Aids
Half-Filled & Full-Filled Stability Rule
s2 > s1 and p3 > p4

Successive IP Jump Analysis
Large ΔIP = Breaking of Inert Gas Core

Application: Count valence electrons by identifying where the ionization energy jumps massively (e.g., Na has 1 valence electron, so IP2 >> IP1).

s > p > d > f

Application: Remember that within the same shell, s-electrons are hardest to remove because they spend more time near the nucleus.

🎯 Key Exam Points
  • Successive ionization potentials are always endothermic, hence IP1 < IP2 < IP3 is universally true.
  • The ionization potential of noble gases is the highest in their respective periods due to stable octet (ns2np6) configurations.
  • Alkali metals possess the lowest ionization potentials in their respective periods.
  • The unit of ionization potential is typically expressed in electron-volts per atom (eV/atom) or kilojoules per mole (kJ/mol).
  • Boron has a lower ionization energy than beryllium because of the easier removal of a 2p electron compared to a 2s electron.
  • Oxygen has a lower ionization energy than nitrogen because removing an electron from paired 2p4 orbitals relieves inter-electronic repulsion.
🚫 Common Errors & Confusions
  • Assuming ionization potential is an exothermic process; it always requires the input of energy and has a positive sign.
  • Confusing the ionization energy trend down Group 13 where thallium has a higher IP than indium due to poor d and f shielding.
  • Forgetting that successive ionization energies apply to cations, meaning each subsequent electron is pulled from an increasingly positive ion.
  • Treating electron affinity and ionization potential as identical magnitudes for neutral atoms and their corresponding ions.

⚡ Quick Revision: Ionization Potential

Core formulas, rapid recall points, and distinguishing criteria at a glance.

Definition and Thermodynamics

Ionization potential measures the energy barrier to strip an electron from an isolated gaseous atom. The process is strictly endothermic with positive enthalpy changes.

Periodic Variations

IP increases across periods due to rising effective nuclear charge and decreases down groups due to increased atomic radius and principal shell number.

Electronic Configuration Anomalies

Symmetric electronic configurations (fully filled and half-filled subshells) exhibit exceptional stability, causing localized reversals in periodic IP trends.

Successive Ionizations

Successive removal of electrons requires progressively higher energy because the remaining electrons experience a higher net positive charge from the nucleus.

📚 Terms & Units Glossary

Ionization Potential (IP / IE)
Energy required to remove an electron from a gaseous atom in ground state; kJ/mol.
Effective Nuclear Charge (Zeff)
Net positive charge experienced by valence electrons accounting for screening.
Penetration Effect (Orbital proximity)
Closeness of an orbital's electron density to the atomic nucleus (s > p > d > f).
Shielding Effect (Inner-shell screening)
Reduction of attractive force between nucleus and outer electrons by core electrons.
Lanthanide Contraction (Poor f-shielding)
Gradual decrease in atomic/ionic radii across lanthanides leading to anomalous post-lanthanide properties.
Mulliken Scale (χM)
Arithmetic mean of ionization potential and electron affinity.

🎯 Key Takeaways

  • IP increases across a period and decreases down a group.
  • Be and Mg have higher IP than B and Al due to full s-orbitals.
  • N and P have higher IP than O and S due to half-filled p-orbitals.
  • Successive IP values always increase: IP1 < IP2 < IP3.
  • Noble gases possess the absolute maximum IP in any given period.

📌 Quick Recall

  • IP is a fundamental periodic property reflecting electron-holding capacity.
  • Exceptions to general trends are rationalized by half-filled and fully filled subshell stabilities.
  • Atomic size and effective nuclear charge are the two primary deterministic factors.
  • Successive IP analysis allows definitive determination of valence electron counts.

⚖️ Key Differences

Ionization Potential VS Electron Affinity

IP is energy required to remove an electron (endothermic), whereas EA is energy released or absorbed when adding an electron.

Why does Beryllium have a higher ionization potential than Boron?

Explanation: Beryllium (1s2 2s2) has a stable fully filled 2s orbital, whereas Boron has a single 2p electron which is easily removed.

Question 4 of 19

Successive ionization energies (IP1, IP2, IP3) for any atom are related as:

Explanation: Removing successive electrons from an increasingly positive ion requires more energy due to higher electrostatic attraction.

Question 5 of 19

Which group of elements generally exhibits the lowest ionization potential in the periodic table?

Explanation: Alkali metals have a single valence electron outside a stable noble gas core, leading to very low ionization potentials.

Question 6 of 19

The penetration power of orbitals follows the order:

Explanation: s-orbitals have the highest electron density near the nucleus, giving them maximum penetration, followed by p, d, and f.

Question 7 of 19

Which element in Group 13 has an unexpectedly higher ionization potential than the preceding element in the group?

Explanation: Thallium has a higher IP than indium due to the poor shielding of intervening 4f and 5d electrons (lanthanide contraction).

Question 8 of 19

Ionization potential is measured in which of the following units?

Explanation: Ionization potential is expressed as energy per mole, typically in kJ/mol or eV/atom.

Question 9 of 19

What happens to the ionization potential down a group?

Explanation: Down a group, atomic size increases and valence electrons are further from the nucleus, lowering the ionization potential.

Question 10 of 19

Which species will have the highest second ionization potential (IP2)?

Explanation: Sodium has only 1 valence electron; removing the second electron means breaking into its stable noble gas core (Ne configuration), requiring massive energy.

Question 11 of 19

The process of ionization of an atom is:

Explanation: Removing an electron requires overcoming attractive forces, meaning energy must be supplied (endothermic process).

Question 12 of 19

Which of the following pairs has elements with nearly identical ionization potentials due to diagonal relationship?

Explanation: Lithium and magnesium exhibit diagonal relationships and share similar charge-to-radius ratios and ionization characteristics.

Question 13 of 19

In a multi-electron system, the effective nuclear charge (Zeff) experienced by an electron is given by:

Explanation: Effective nuclear charge is the actual nuclear charge (Z) minus the shielding constant (σ).

Question 14 of 19

Which noble gas has the highest ionization potential?

Explanation: Helium is the smallest noble gas with electrons closest to the nucleus, giving it the highest ionization potential of all elements.

Question 15 of 19

The ionization energy of an element X is 500 kJ/mol. The process representing this is:

Explanation: Ionization energy is defined for gaseous atoms losing an electron to form gaseous cations.

Question 16 of 19

Which transition metal series generally shows a relatively constant ionization potential across the period?

Explanation: In the 3d transition series, added electrons enter inner d-orbitals which effectively shield the outer 4s electrons, keeping IP relatively constant.

Question 17 of 19

What is the primary factor responsible for the exceptionally high IP of nitrogen compared to oxygen?

Explanation: Nitrogen's 2p3 configuration is half-filled, imparting extra exchange energy stability.

Question 18 of 19

An element with electronic configuration 1s2 2s2 2p6 3s1 will show a sharp jump in ionization energy at:

Explanation: After removing the single 3s electron (IP1), the second electron must be removed from the stable 2p6 core, causing a massive jump at IP2.

Question 19 of 19

Electronegativity on Mulliken's scale is calculated as the average of:

Explanation: Mulliken defined electronegativity as the arithmetic mean of ionization potential and electron affinity.

Question 1 of 10
Which element has an ionization energy higher than expected from general periodic trends due to relativistic effects on its 6s orbital?

Solution: Gold experiences strong relativistic contraction of its 6s orbital, leading to an unusually high ionization energy and electron affinity.

Question 2 of 10
For which of the following pairs is the ionization energy of the second element higher than the first?

Solution: Both Na < Mg (due to fully filled 3s2) and P > S (due to half-filled 3p3) invert the normal trend.

Question 3 of 10
What is the relationship between atomic radius (r) and ionization potential (IP)?

Solution: Ionization potential is inversely proportional to atomic radius because electrons farther from the nucleus require less energy to remove.

Question 4 of 10
Which of the following has the highest IP3 value?

Solution: Magnesium has 2 valence electrons; its third ionization energy (IP3) involves removing an electron from the inner 2p6 noble gas core.

Question 5 of 10
The penetration order of orbitals of the same principal quantum number 'n' is:

Solution: s-orbitals have the highest probability density near the nucleus, followed by p, d, and f.

Question 6 of 10
Which of the following electronic configurations represents an atom with the lowest first ionization energy?

Solution: Elements with ns1 configuration (alkali metals) require the least energy to lose their single valence electron.

Question 7 of 10
The decrease in ionization energy down a group is primarily due to:

Solution: As principal shell number increases down a group, valence electrons are further away and inner shells provide greater shielding.

Question 8 of 10
Which factor does NOT affect the ionization energy of an atom?

Solution: Neutrons reside in the nucleus and do not directly interact with valence electrons or affect shielding.

Question 9 of 10
The ionization energy of hydrogen atom in ground state is 13.6 eV. What is the ionization energy of He+ in ground state?

Solution: For hydrogen-like species, E = 13.6 × Z2 / n2 eV. For He+, Z = 2, so E = 13.6 × 22 = 54.4 eV.

Question 10 of 10
Which of the following species is isoelectronic with Neon and has the highest ionization energy?

Solution: Among isoelectronic species, the one with the highest nuclear charge (Mg2+, Z = 12) holds its electrons most tightly and has the highest ionization energy.
