Anomalous Behavior and Limitations of Group 13 to 18 Elements

The periodic anomalies and structural limitations observed in p-block elements are primarily attributed to small atomic radius, high electronegativity, and the absence of d-orbitals in period 2 elements. Subsequent group members exhibit variable oxidation states, inert pair effect, and maximum covalency expansion utilizing vacant d-orbitals.

UP PGT Chemistry Subject Code 02 B.Sc. / M.Sc. Level

First Member Anomalies in p-Block

  • Period 2 elements (B, C, N, O, F) exhibit anomalous behavior due to extremely small atomic size, high ionization energy, and high electronegativity.
  • The absence of low-lying vacant d-orbitals strictly limits the maximum covalency of period 2 elements to four (sp3 hybridization).
  • Carbon, nitrogen, and oxygen form strong multiple bonds (pπ-pπ) with themselves and other second-period elements due to effective sideways overlap.
  • Heavier congeners prefer single bonds and fail to form stable pπ-pπ bonds efficiently due to diffuse valence orbitals.

Inert Pair Effect and Oxidation State Variation

  • The reluctance of ns2 electrons to participate in chemical bonding is termed the inert pair effect.
  • It becomes progressively more pronounced down the group in p-block elements, stabilizing lower oxidation states (e.g., Tl+, Pb2+, Bi3+).
  • The energy required to unpair ns2 electrons is not compensated by the lattice or hydration energy released during higher oxidation state formation.
  • Consequently, higher oxidation states become strong oxidizing agents down the group (e.g., PbO2, Bi(V)).

Lewis Acid Character and Covalency Limitations

  • Boron trihalides (BX3) act as strong Lewis acids due to an incomplete octet (6 valence electrons around boron).
  • The Lewis acid strength sequence for boron halides is BF3 < BCl3 < BBr3, governed by concurrent pπ-pπ back-bonding.
  • Elements of period 3 and below (like Al, Si, P) expand their octets beyond an octet by utilizing vacant nd-orbitals.
  • Silicon forms [SiF6]2- easily, whereas carbon cannot form [CF6]2- due to steric crowding and lack of d-orbitals.

⚠️ Important Exceptions & Anomalies
Exception Lewis acid strength increases with decreasing electronegativity of substituents (F < Cl < Br < I).

Observation: BF3 is a weaker Lewis acid than BCl3. — Intramolecular pπ-pπ back-bonding from filled 2p orbital of fluorine to vacant 2p orbital of boron decreases electron deficiency.

Exception Group 14 elements form stable dioxides with network structures.

Observation: CO2 exists as discrete, volatile linear molecules. — Carbon forms strong carbon-oxygen double bonds (pπ-pπ overlap), unlike silicon which forms single bonds in an extended SiO2 network.

Exception Hydrides of group 15 elements show regular boiling point increase with molecular mass.

Observation: Ammonia (NH3) has an abnormally high boiling point compared to PH3. — Strong intermolecular hydrogen bonding is present in liquid ammonia due to high nitrogen electronegativity.

🎯 Key Exam Points
  • Boron shows diagonal relationship with silicon, exhibiting similar chemistry in hydrides and oxides.
  • Boric acid (H3BO3) acts as a Lewis acid by accepting OH- ions, not as a Brønsted proton donor.
  • Thallium primarily exhibits +1 oxidation state due to pronounced inert pair effect.
  • Silicon tetrachloride undergoes rapid hydrolysis to form silicic acid, unlike carbon tetrachloride which is inert to water.
  • Graphite is a good conductor of electricity parallel to the carbon layers due to delocalized free π-electrons.
  • Phosphorus exists in multiple allotropic forms (white, red, black), with white phosphorus being highly reactive and toxic.
🚫 Common Errors & Confusions
  • Assuming H3BO3 is a triprotic Brønsted acid; it is actually a monoprotic Lewis acid.
  • Confusing carbon's inability to expand its octet with the behavior of silicon, which readily forms 6-coordinated species.
  • Neglecting the stabilizing effect of d-orbital participation in hypervalent molecules like PCl5 and SF6.
  • Applying hybridization rules blindly to hydrides like PH3 where Drago's rule applies.

⚡ Quick Revision: Anomalous Behavior and Limitations of Group 13 to 18 Elements

Core formulas, rapid recall points, and distinguishing criteria at a glance.

Anomalous Properties of First-Row Elements

Period 2 elements exhibit unique chemical behaviors due to small atomic radii, high electronegativity, and absence of vacant d-orbitals.

Inert Pair Effect Mechanics

Heavier p-block post-transition elements retain ns2 electrons in bonding, making lower oxidation states thermodynamically favored.

Lewis Acidity and Back-Bonding in Boron Halides

BX3 Lewis acid strength is inversely related to the extent of intramolecular pπ-pπ halogen-to-boron donation.

Octet Expansion Limits

Presence of low-lying vacant nd orbitals allows elements of period 3 and below to exceed octet rule limitations.

📚 Terms & Units Glossary

Inert Pair Effect (ns2 retention)
Reluctance of ns2 valence electrons to participate in bond formation.
Back Bonding (pπ-pπ overlap)
Intramolecular donation from filled p-orbital to vacant p-orbital.
Hypervalent (Expanded Octet)
Molecules containing more than eight electrons around the central atom.
Catensation (Self-linking)
Ability of atoms of the same element to form long chains or rings.
Drago's Rule (Pure p-bonding)
Absence of hybridization in central atom hydrides when electronegativity is low.
Allotropy (Structural forms)
Existence of an element in two or more distinct physical forms.

🎯 Key Takeaways

  • Lewis acid order: BF3 < BCl3 < BBr3
  • Maximum covalency of boron is 4
  • PbO2 is a powerful oxidizing agent due to inert pair effect
  • H3BO3 is a Lewis acid
  • Silicon does not form stable multiple bonds easily

📌 Quick Recall

  • Period 2 elements show marked differences from heavier group congeners.
  • Inert pair effect governs the stability of lower oxidation states in heavier p-block elements.
  • Octet expansion requires vacant d-orbitals in period 3 and beyond.
  • Lewis acid character of boron halides is governed by back-bonding.

⚖️ Key Differences

CCl4 hydrolysis VS SiCl4 hydrolysis

CCl4 does not hydrolyze due to lack of d-orbitals for water coordination, whereas SiCl4 readily hydrolyzes.

Which of the following boron halides is the strongest Lewis acid?

Explanation: BI3 has the weakest pπ-pπ back-bonding, making the boron atom most electron-deficient and thus the strongest Lewis acid.

Question 2 of 19

The inert pair effect is most prominent in which of the following elements?

Explanation: Thallium is the heaviest group 13 element, where the inert pair effect is maximum, making Tl(I) more stable than Tl(III).

Question 3 of 19

Why does carbon not form [CF6]2- unlike silicon which forms [SiF6]2-?

Explanation: Carbon belongs to period 2 and lacks vacant d-orbitals, restricting its maximum coordination number to 4.

Question 4 of 19

Boric acid behaves as:

Explanation: H3BO3 accepts a hydroxyl ion from water to act as a weak monoprotic Lewis acid.

Question 5 of 19

Which molecule has a bond angle closest to 90 degrees according to Drago's rule?

Explanation: PH3 has a central atom from period 3 with electronegativity ≤ 2.5, resulting in unhybridized bonding orbitals with ~90° angles.

Question 6 of 19

What is the maximum covalency of aluminum in [AlF6]3-?

Explanation: Aluminum utilizes vacant 3d orbitals to expand its coordination number to 6.

Question 7 of 19

Which oxide is amphoteric in nature among group 13 elements?

Explanation: Al2O3 dissolves in both acids and bases, displaying amphoteric behavior.

Question 8 of 19

The tendency for catenation is highest in:

Explanation: Carbon has high C-C bond enthalpy, giving it the maximum catenation ability in the periodic table.

Question 9 of 19

Which species does not exist due to steric or electronic reasons?

Explanation: [CCl6]2- does not exist because carbon lacks d-orbitals and suffers from extreme steric crowding around a small carbon atom.

Question 10 of 19

The stability of +1 oxidation state in group 13 follows the order:

Explanation: Due to the progressive operation of the inert pair effect down group 13, the stability of the +1 oxidation state increases downwards.

Question 11 of 19

Which of the following elements does not show allotropy?

Explanation: Gallium does not exhibit common allotropy compared to carbon, silicon, and phosphorus.

Question 12 of 19

Why is CO a neutral oxide while other group 14 oxides are acidic?

Explanation: Carbon monoxide has a triple bond with matching polarity and formal charges that prevent it from reacting significantly as an acid.

Question 13 of 19

Which halide of group 14 is most resistant to hydrolysis?

Explanation: Carbon tetrachloride lacks d-orbitals for water molecule attack, rendering it completely resistant to hydrolysis.

Question 14 of 19

The hybridization of boron in BF4- is:

Explanation: Boron in BF4- has four bond pairs and zero lone pairs, corresponding to sp3 hybridization.

Question 15 of 19

Which element forms strong pπ-pπ multiple bonds easily?

Explanation: Carbon has a small atomic radius allowing effective lateral p-orbital overlap for pπ-pπ bonding.

Question 16 of 19

In graphite, carbon atoms are hybridized as:

Explanation: Each carbon atom in graphite is bonded to three other carbons in a trigonal planar geometry, requiring sp2 hybridization.

Question 17 of 19

Which hydrides of group 15 are strong reducing agents?

Explanation: BiH3 has the lowest thermal stability and longest M-H bond length, making it the strongest reducing agent among group 15 hydrides.

Question 18 of 19

The correct order of boiling points for group 15 hydrides is:

Explanation: Ammonia has an abnormally high boiling point due to hydrogen bonding, followed by the increasing molecular mass trend from PH3 to BiH3.

Question 19 of 19

What causes the anomalous behavior of fluorine compared to other halogens?

Explanation: Fluorine has an extremely small atomic radius leading to lone-pair repulsion and low F-F bond dissociation enthalpy.

Question 1 of 10
Why is CO2 a gas while SiO2 is a high-melting solid at room temperature?

Solution: Carbon forms stable double bonds with oxygen giving discrete CO2 molecules, whereas silicon forms single Si-O bonds extending into a 3D network.

Question 2 of 10
The reason for the stability of Tl(I) over Tl(III) is:

Solution: The inert pair effect causes the 6s2 electrons of thallium to remain unionized, stabilizing the +1 state.

Question 3 of 10
Which boron hydride represents a nido-borane structure?

Solution: B4H10 follows the BnHn+6 or general open cage nido stoichiometry framework depending on structural classification rules.

Question 4 of 10
In aqueous solution, boric acid acts as:

Solution: H3BO3 accepts a hydroxide ion from water molecules to generate hydronium ions, functioning as a Lewis acid.

Question 5 of 10
What is the coordination number of silicon in silica (SiO2)?

Solution: In silica, each silicon atom is tetrahedrally surrounded by four oxygen atoms, giving a coordination number of 4.

Question 6 of 10
Which of the following statements is incorrect regarding group 14 elements?

Solution: Not all group 14 dioxides are exclusively acidic; CO and GeO are neutral or amphoteric.

Question 7 of 10
The reluctance of s-electrons to participate in chemical bonding is due to:

Solution: Poor shielding by d and f electrons increases effective nuclear charge on ns2 electrons, holding them tightly.

Question 8 of 10
Which halide does not exist due to thermodynamic instability caused by size mismatch?

Solution: TlI3 actually exists as Tl+[I3]- because Tl(III) is oxidising and iodide is reducing, but pure covalent TlI3 is unstable due to size and oxidation state mismatch.

Question 9 of 10
The type of bonding present in diborane (B2H6) involves:

Solution: Diborane features banana bonds which are three-center two-electron (3c-2e) B-H-B bonds.

Question 10 of 10
Which element in group 15 shows the strongest tendency for catenation?

Solution: Phosphorus exhibits catenation to form various polyphosphorus chains and rings, more than nitrogen due to weaker N-N single bond repulsion.
