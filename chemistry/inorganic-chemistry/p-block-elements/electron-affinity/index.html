Inorganic Chemistry

Electron Affinity

Electron affinity is defined as the enthalpy change accompanying the addition of an electron to a gaseous neutral atom to form a gaseous anion. It serves as a quantitative measure of the tendency of an atom to accept an electron.

UP PGT Chemistry Subject Code 02 B.Sc. / M.Sc. Level

Periodic Trends Across Periods and Groups

  • Electron affinity generally increases across a period from left to right as atomic radius decreases and effective nuclear charge increases.
  • Electron affinity generally decreases down a group as atomic size increases, reducing the attractive force exerted by the nucleus on incoming electrons.
  • Halogens possess the highest exothermic electron gain enthalpies in their respective periods due to their one electron short noble gas configuration.
  • Noble gases exhibit positive electron gain enthalpies because incoming electrons must enter a higher principal energy level due to completely filled valence shells.

Anomalies in Periodic Variation

  • Chlorine has a higher electron affinity than fluorine because the extremely compact 2p subshell of fluorine causes severe inter-electronic repulsion.
  • Oxygen and sulfur exhibit unusually low electron affinities compared to adjacent groups due to their electronic configuration and small atomic volume.
  • Elements with stable configuration like nitrogen (half-filled p-subshell) have near-zero or positive electron gain enthalpies.
  • The second electron gain enthalpy for all elements is strictly endothermic due to the work required to overcome electrostatic repulsion between the negative ion and the incoming electron.

Factors Governing Electron Affinity

  • Atomic size is inversely proportional to electron affinity; smaller atoms place the incoming electron closer to the attractive nuclear charge.
  • Nuclear charge is directly proportional to electron affinity; higher positive charge increases the electrostatic pull on added electrons.
  • Electronic configuration dictates that stable half-filled or completely filled subshells resist electron addition, lowering electron affinity values.
  • Screening effect of inner core electrons shields the valence shell, decreasing the effective nuclear charge and thereby lowering electron affinity.

⚠️ Important Exceptions & Anomalies
Exception Electron affinity increases down Group 17 (Halogens).

Observation: Fluorine has a lower electron gain enthalpy (-328 kJ mol-1) than Chlorine (-349 kJ mol-1). — The extremely small size of the F atom creates high electron-electron repulsion in the compact 2p orbital.

Exception Electron affinity increases across Period 2.

Observation: Oxygen has a lower electron affinity than Nitrogen or Boron anomalies relative to strict periodic progression due to pairing energy. — Stable symmetrical configurations or specific electron repulsion structures alter the expected smooth monotonic rise.

Exception Electron addition is always an exothermic process releasing energy.

Observation: Addition of an electron to O- to form O2- or addition to noble gases is endothermic. — Electrostatic repulsion between the negatively charged anion and the incoming electron requires external energy input.

🎯 Key Exam Points
  • Chlorine possesses the maximum electron affinity among all known chemical elements (-349 kJ mol-1).
  • Noble gases have positive electron gain enthalpy values due to stable ns2np6 configurations.
  • The second electron gain enthalpy is always endothermic due to coulombic repulsion.
  • Fluorine's electron affinity is lower than chlorine's due to high electron-electron repulsion in the small 2p orbital.
  • Nitrogen has a near-zero electron affinity because adding an electron disrupts its stable half-filled 2p3 configuration.
  • Electron affinity values are experimentally determined using the Born-Haber cycle for ionic compounds.
🚫 Common Errors & Confusions
  • Assuming fluorine has the highest electron affinity in the periodic table just because it is the most electronegative element.
  • Confusing electron gain enthalpy (ΔHeg) sign with electron affinity magnitude; an exothermic process has a negative ΔH.
  • Treating the second electron addition as an energy-releasing process when it requires energy to overcome ionic repulsion.
  • Neglecting the impact of inner-shell shielding when predicting periodic trends for heavier p-block elements.

⚡ Quick Revision: Electron Affinity

Core formulas, rapid recall points, and distinguishing criteria at a glance.

Definition and Thermodynamics

Electron affinity measures the energy released when a gaseous atom gains an electron. It is directly related to electron gain enthalpy with reversed sign conventions.

Periodic Variations

Electron affinity increases across a period from left to right and decreases down a group, driven by variations in effective nuclear charge and atomic radii.

Anomalous Behavior of Period 2

Compact atomic volumes in second-period elements cause intense inter-electronic repulsion, resulting in lower electron affinities than their third-period homologs.

Multi-Electron Addition

Subsequent electron additions (such as O- to O2-) are endothermic because the incoming electron must overcome the negative electrostatic field of the existing anion.

📚 Terms & Units Glossary

Electron Affinity (EA)
Energy released when an isolated gaseous atom accepts an electron, measured in kJ mol-1.
Electron Gain Enthalpy (ΔHeg)
Enthalpy change when an electron is added to a gaseous atom at standard conditions.
Effective Nuclear Charge (Zeff)
Net positive charge experienced by valence electrons in a multi-electron atom.
Screening Effect (S)
Reduction in attractive force between nucleus and valence electrons due to inner shell electrons.
Born-Haber Cycle (Thermochemical cycle)
Cyclic thermodynamic approach to calculate lattice energies and electron affinities.
Endothermic (+ΔH)
Process that absorbs thermal energy from the surroundings.

🎯 Key Takeaways

  • Cl has the highest electron affinity value in the periodic table.
  • Noble gases have positive electron gain enthalpies.
  • Second electron gain enthalpies are always endothermic.
  • Fluorine electron affinity < Chlorine electron affinity.
  • Nitrogen has near zero electron affinity due to half-filled stability.

📌 Quick Recall

  • Electron affinity depends on atomic size, nuclear charge, and electronic configuration.
  • Periodic trends mirror ionization energy with specific localized anomalies.
  • Compactness of second-period p-block elements leads to inter-electronic repulsion anomalies.
  • Thermodynamic sign conventions reverse electron affinity values versus electron gain enthalpies.

⚖️ Key Differences

Electronegativity VS Electron Affinity

Electronegativity is a bond property (tendency to attract shared pairs), whereas electron affinity is an energetic property of an isolated gaseous atom.
