Inorganic Chemistry

Interhalogens

Interhalogen compounds are binary chemical species formed by the combination of two different halogen atoms. Their general formula is represented as AXn, where A is the larger, less electronegative halogen and X is the smaller, more electronegative halogen with n taking values of 1, 3, 5, or 7.

UP PGT Chemistry Subject Code 02 B.Sc. / M.Sc. Level

Types and Stoichiometry of Interhalogen Compounds

  • Interhalogens are classified into four distinct types based on stoichiometry: AX, AX3, AX5, and AX7.
  • The central atom A is always the larger halogen with lower electronegativity, occupying a central position bonded to n peripheral halogen atoms.
  • The value of n is restricted to odd numbers (1, 3, 5, 7) due to the constraints of valence shell electron pair distribution and pairing of unpaired electrons.
  • As the radius ratio between the central halogen and the surrounding halogen increases, the maximum coordination number n also increases.

Molecular Geometries and VSEPR Theory

  • Molecules of type AX adopt a linear geometry with a diatomic framework.
  • Molecules of type AX3 exhibit a bent T-shape geometry due to two lone pairs on the central atom, corresponding to sp3d hybridization.
  • Molecules of type AX5 possess a square pyramidal geometry with one lone pair, corresponding to sp3d2 hybridization.
  • The highest fluoride IF7 adopts a pentagonal bipyramidal geometry with sp3d3 hybridization and zero lone pairs on iodine.

Chemical Reactivity and Physical Properties

  • Interhalogens are more reactive than constituent halogens because the A-X bond is weaker than the homonuclear X-X or A-A single bonds.
  • All interhalogen compounds act as strong halogenating agents and undergo complete hydrolysis to yield halide and hypohalous, halous, halic, or perhalic acids.
  • They are volatile covalent solids, liquids, or gases at room temperature; their melting and boiling points increase with an increase in molecular mass and polarity.
  • Liquid interhalogen compounds such as ICl and BrF3 undergo autoionization and function effectively as non-aqueous ionizing solvents.

⚠️ Important Exceptions & Anomalies
Exception Maximum coordination number rule based on halogen size

Observation: Fluorine cannot form AX7 or AX5 as a central atom; it only acts as the peripheral ligand X. — Fluorine is the smallest halogen, possesses the highest electronegativity, and lacks d-orbitals in its valence shell to expand its octet.

Exception Interhalogens are purely binary species of two different halogens

Observation: Polyhalogen ions like ICl2-, IBr2-, and IF6- exist and exhibit similar bonding characteristics. — Acceptance or loss of a halide ion generates stable ionic species that mirror neutral interhalogen structures.

🎯 Key Exam Points
  • Interhalogens are covalent and diamagnetic molecules.
  • The thermal stability of interhalogen compounds decreases as the electronegativity difference between the two constituent halogens decreases.
  • Iodine heptafluoride (IF7) has a pentagonal bipyramidal structure.
  • Chlorine trifluoride (ClF3) is a powerful fluorinating agent and liquidizes at 11.3 °C.
  • Bromine pentafluoride (BrF5) has a square pyramidal geometry with one lone pair.
  • Interhalogens hydrolyze in water to give a halide ion and an oxyhalide/oxoacid species.
🚫 Common Errors & Confusions
  • Assuming interhalogens are ionic compounds; they are strictly covalent due to small electronegativity differences.
  • Confusing the central atom A with the smaller peripheral halogen X in structural formulas.
  • Predicting even values of n (like AX2 or AX4) which do not exist in stable neutral interhalogens.
  • Neglecting lone pairs when determining the geometry of AX3 or AX5 molecules.

⚡ Quick Revision: Interhalogens

Core formulas, rapid recall points, and distinguishing criteria at a glance.

Classification and Naming

Interhalogens are named as polyhalides where the more electronegative halogen is written last and ends in '-ide' (e.g., chlorine trifluoride).

Bonding and Structure

VSEPR theory successfully predicts the shape of interhalogens by accounting for bond pairs and lone pairs around the central halogen atom.

Reactivity and Synthesis

Prepared by direct combination of halogen elements under controlled stoichiometric ratios and temperature conditions.

Solvent Properties

Liquid interhalogens participate in autoionization equilibria, serving as non-aqueous ionizing media for conducting chemical reactions.

🎯 Key Takeaways

  • All interhalogens are covalent and diamagnetic.
  • Value of n in AXn is always an odd integer (1, 3, 5, 7).
  • Central atom A is always the larger, less electronegative halogen.
  • IF7 is the only known AX7 interhalogen compound.
  • Interhalogens are stronger oxidizing and halogenating agents than parent halogens.

📌 Quick Recall

  • Interhalogens bridge the properties of constituent halogens.
  • Structural geometries are dictated by VSEPR theory including central lone pairs.
  • Hydrolysis yields corresponding halide and oxoacid.
  • Liquid interhalogens act as ionizing non-aqueous solvents.

⚖️ Key Differences

AX3 Structure VS Trigonal Planar

AX3 interhalogens are T-shaped due to 2 lone pairs, not trigonal planar.

What is the hybridization of the central iodine atom in IF7?

Explanation: IF7 has 7 bond pairs and 0 lone pairs, giving a steric number of 7 which corresponds to sp3d3 hybridization and pentagonal bipyramidal geometry.

Question 2 of 19

Which of the following interhalogen compounds does not exist?

Explanation: Chlorine cannot act as a central atom surrounded by 3 larger fluorine atoms in FCl3 because fluorine is more electronegative than chlorine and cannot be the central atom in a positive oxidation state with F as peripheral ligands.

Question 3 of 19

What is the molecular geometry of ClF3?

Explanation: ClF3 has 3 bond pairs and 2 lone pairs on the central chlorine atom, resulting in a T-shape geometry.

Question 4 of 19

Why are interhalogen compounds more reactive than parent halogens?

Explanation: The bond dissociation enthalpy of the A-X bond in interhalogens is generally lower than that of homonuclear X-X bonds, making them exceptionally reactive.

Question 5 of 19

What is the oxidation state of iodine in IF5?

Explanation: In IF5, each fluorine has an oxidation state of -1, so iodine must be in the +5 oxidation state to balance the molecule.

Question 6 of 19

Which interhalogen compound has a square pyramidal structure?

Explanation: IF5 has 5 bond pairs and 1 lone pair, which corresponds to square pyramidal geometry.

Question 7 of 19

Hydrolysis of chlorine monofluoride (ClF) yields which products?

Explanation: ClF undergoes hydrolysis with water to form hydrogen fluoride (HF) and hypochlorous acid (HOCl).

Question 8 of 19

The central atom in an interhalogen AXn is always:

Explanation: By definition, the central atom A is the larger halogen with lower electronegativity, while X is the smaller, more electronegative halogen.

Question 9 of 19

What is the shape of an AX5 type interhalogen?

Explanation: AX5 molecules have 5 bonding pairs and 1 lone pair, arranging themselves into a square pyramidal geometry.

Question 10 of 19

Which of the following represents a neutral AX7 type interhalogen?

Explanation: Iodine heptafluoride (IF7) is the only stable and well-characterized AX7 interhalogen compound known.

Question 12 of 19

Which type of interhalogen compound has three lone pairs on the central atom?

Explanation: Di-atomics of type AX have 7 valence electrons on A, sharing 1 with X, leaving 3 lone pairs on the central atom.

Question 13 of 19

In the self-ionization of liquid ICl, the cation formed is:

Explanation: Liquid ICl autoionizes as 2ICl ⇌ I+ + ICl2-, producing the iodonium cation.

Question 14 of 19

Which halogen combination yields the most thermally stable interhalogen?

Explanation: A larger difference in electronegativity enhances ionic character contribution, increasing the thermal stability of the interhalogen bond.

Question 15 of 19

What is the hybridization of bromine in BrF5?

Explanation: BrF5 has 5 bond pairs and 1 lone pair, totaling a steric number of 6, which corresponds to sp3d2 hybridization.

Question 16 of 19

Which statement is incorrect regarding interhalogen compounds?

Explanation: Interhalogens are significantly more reactive than parent halogens due to weaker A-X bond dissociation energies.

Question 17 of 19

The number of lone pairs on the central iodine atom in IF7 is:

Explanation: Iodine utilizes all 7 of its valence electrons to form 7 single bonds with fluorine atoms, leaving zero lone pairs.

Question 18 of 19

Which of the following is a pseudo-interhalogen ion?

Explanation: ICl2- is a well-known polyhalogen ion sharing structural and electronic analogies with interhalogens.

Question 19 of 19

What is the geometry of an AX molecule?

Explanation: Any diatomic molecule of type AX must be linear.

Question 4 of 10
Which halogen can never act as the central atom 'A' in an interhalogen compound?

Solution: Fluorine is the most electronegative halogen and cannot expand its octet; it is always the peripheral atom X in interhalogens.

Solution: Chlorine has 7 valence electrons, uses 3 for bonding with fluorine, leaving 4 electrons which form 2 lone pairs.

Solution: Chlorine monofluoride (ClF) is a gas at room temperature, while most other interhalogens are low-boiling liquids or solids.

Solution: Interhalogens are formed by sharing electrons between two halogens, resulting in polar covalent bonds.

Question 8 of 10

Solution: With three fluorine atoms each contributing -1, chlorine must exhibit a +3 oxidation state.

Question 9 of 10
