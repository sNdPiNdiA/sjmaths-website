Inorganic Chemistry

Halogen Compounds

Halogen compounds encompass binary halides, interhalogen species, and oxygen acids formed by group 17 elements. Their chemical reactivity is dictated by high electronegativity, variable oxidation states, and bond dissociation enthalpy variations.

UP PGT Chemistry Subject Code 02 B.Sc. / M.Sc. Level

Preparation and Properties of Hydrogen Halides

  • Hydrogen halides (HX) are synthesized via the direct combination of elements or the reaction of ionic halides with concentrated H2SO4.
  • HF is a liquid at room temperature due to extensive intermolecular hydrogen bonding, resulting in an anomalously high boiling point.
  • Reducing character of hydrogen halides increases in the order HF < HCl < HBr < HI owing to the progressive weakening of the H-X bond.
  • Aqueous solutions of all hydrogen halides act as strong acids except for HF, which is classified as a weak acid due to strong ion-pair formation.

Interhalogen Compounds and Structural Geometries

  • Interhalogens are classified into four distinct types: AX, AX3, AX5, and AX7 based on the stoichiometric ratio.
  • The central atom in interhalogen molecules always possesses the larger size and lower electronegativity, utilizing sp3d or sp3d3 hybridization.
  • IF7 adopts a pentagonal bipyramidal geometry, whereas ClF3 exhibits a T-shape due to two lone pairs occupying equatorial positions.
  • All interhalogen compounds are covalent and undergo hydrolysis to yield halide and halate ions.

Oxoacids of Halogens and Thermal Trends

  • Chlorine forms four major series of oxoacids: Hypochlorous (HOCl), Chlorous (HClO2), Chloric (HClO3), and Perchloric (HClO4).
  • Acid strength of oxoacids increases directly with the oxidation state of the central halogen atom and the number of terminal oxygen atoms.
  • Thermal stability of oxoacids and their corresponding salts increases as the oxidation state of the halogen increases.
  • Perchloric acid (HClO4) is one of the strongest mineral acids known, exhibiting high oxidizing power when anhydrous.

⚠️ Important Exceptions & Anomalies
Exception Fluorine exhibits the lowest electron gain enthalpy in Group 17.

Observation: Chlorine has a more negative electron gain enthalpy than fluorine. — The extremely small atomic volume of fluorine causes high interelectronic repulsion within its compact 2p orbitals.

Exception Oxidizing power increases down the group in halogen families.

Observation: Fluorine is the strongest oxidizing agent in aqueous solution despite having a less negative standard reduction potential than some expectations. — Its low enthalpy of dissociation and exceptionally high hydration enthalpy compensate for other energetic terms.

Exception Hydrogen halides are strong reducing agents.

Observation: HF does not act as a reducing agent. — The H-F bond enthalpy is exceptionally high (574 kJ mol-1), making hydrogen abstraction or oxidation exceedingly difficult.

Oxoacid Acid Strength Rule

Fluorine Oxidation State Trick
Fluorine = Always -1 (except in elemental F2)

Application: Immediately assign -1 in all compounds due to maximum electronegativity among all elements.

🎯 Key Exam Points
  • Fluorine never exhibits positive oxidation states due to the absence of vacant d-orbitals and highest electronegativity.
  • Bleaching action of chlorine is permanent and occurs via an oxidation mechanism in the presence of moisture.
  • Interhalogen compound ICl is more reactive than diatomic iodine due to fractional ionic character and bond polarization.
  • Hydriodic acid (HI) reduces sulfuric acid to sulfur dioxide or hydrogen sulfide because of its powerful reducing nature.
  • Bromine is a dark-red volatile liquid at room temperature with a standard reduction potential of +1.07 V.
  • Chlorine reacts with cold and dilute NaOH to form sodium chloride, sodium hypochlorite, and water.
🚫 Common Errors & Confusions
  • Confusing the order of electron gain enthalpy (F < Cl) with electronegativity (F > Cl).
  • Assuming HF is a strong acid because fluorine is the most electronegative element.
  • Writing incorrect formulas for interhalogens such as IF8, which violates steric and valence limitations.
  • Neglecting the role of hydration enthalpy when evaluating standard reduction potentials of halogens.

⚡ Quick Revision: Halogen Compounds

Core formulas, rapid recall points, and distinguishing criteria at a glance.

Halogen Hydrides

Binary compounds of hydrogen and halogens display a regular decrease in thermal stability and bond strength from HF to HI, accompanied by a sharp rise in reducing capacity.

Interhalogen Chemistry

Interhalogen compounds are covalent molecules formed by the combination of two different halogens, adopting geometries predicted by VSEPR theory involving lone pair-bond pair repulsions.

Oxoacid Series

Chlorine, bromine, and iodine form comprehensive series of oxoacids where acidity and thermal stability scale directly with the central halogen's oxidation state.

Anomalous Fluorine Behavior

Fluorine diverges significantly from heavier halogens due to its extremely small size, high electronegativity, low F-F bond dissociation energy, and lack of d-orbitals.

🎯 Key Takeaways

  • Electronegativity order: F > Cl > Br > I
  • Electron gain enthalpy magnitude order: Cl > F > Br > I
  • Acid strength of hydrohalic acids: HF < HCl < HBr < HI
  • Acid strength of hypohalous acids: HOCl > HOBr > HOI
  • Fluorine shows only -1 oxidation state in compounds.

📌 Quick Recall

  • Halogen compounds exhibit systematic variations in physical and chemical properties down Group 17.
  • Interhalogen structures are governed strictly by VSEPR theory rules.
  • Oxoacid strength and thermal stability correlate with central atom oxidation states.
  • Fluorine displays anomalous behaviors stemming from its compact atomic radius.

⚖️ Key Differences

Electron Gain Enthalpy VS Electronegativity

Electron gain enthalpy is the energy released when an isolated gaseous atom gains an electron, whereas electronegativity is the tendency of a bonded atom to attract shared electron pairs.

Question 3 of 19

Which oxoacid of chlorine is the strongest acid?

Explanation: HClO4 has chlorine in the +7 oxidation state with three terminal oxygen atoms, maximizing electron withdrawal and conjugate base stabilization.

Question 4 of 19

Why does fluorine have a less negative electron gain enthalpy than chlorine?

Explanation: The small 2p orbitals of fluorine concentrate electron density, generating high repulsion when an incoming electron is added.

Question 5 of 19

Which interhalogen molecule possesses a T-shaped geometry?

Explanation: ClF3 has 3 bond pairs and 2 lone pairs on chlorine, arranging into a T-shape according to VSEPR theory.

Question 6 of 19

Which halogen does not exhibit positive oxidation states in its compounds?

Explanation: Fluorine is the most electronegative element and lacks vacant d-orbitals, restricting it exclusively to the -1 oxidation state.

Question 7 of 19

What is the primary catalyst used in the commercial Deacon process for chlorine production?

Explanation: Deacon's process utilizes copper(II) chloride (CuCl2) at 723 K to oxidize hydrogen chloride with oxygen.

Question 8 of 19

Which of the following represents the correct decreasing order of reducing character for hydrogen halides?

Explanation: Reducing character increases as H-X bond strength decreases down the group, making HI the strongest and HF the weakest reducing agent.

Question 9 of 19

What are the products formed when chlorine reacts with hot and concentrated sodium hydroxide?

Explanation: Hot and concentrated NaOH reacts with chlorine in a disproportionation reaction to yield sodium chloride, sodium chlorate, and water.

Question 10 of 19

Which type of interhalogen compound is IF5?

Explanation: IF5 consists of one iodine atom bonded to five fluorine atoms, corresponding to the AX5 general classification.

Question 11 of 19

What is the oxidation state of chlorine in perchloric acid (HClO4)?

Explanation: In HClO4, assigning oxidation numbers (+1 for H, -2 for each of the four O atoms) yields +7 for chlorine.

Question 12 of 19

Which hydrogen halide is a weak acid in aqueous solution?

Explanation: HF has high hydrogen-bond energy and strong ion-pair association in water, limiting its dissociation compared to other strong hydrohalic acids.

Question 13 of 19

What is the geometry of an AX5 interhalogen molecule with one lone pair like BrF5?

Explanation: Six electron pairs with five bond pairs and one lone pair arrange into a square pyramidal geometry.

Question 14 of 19

Which reagent is used in the laboratory preparation of chlorine gas from sodium chloride?

Explanation: MnO2 mixed with NaCl and concentrated sulfuric acid generates chlorine gas via oxidation.

Question 15 of 19

Which halogen acts as the strongest oxidizing agent in aqueous solution?

Explanation: Fluorine possesses the highest standard reduction potential and overall oxidizing capability in aqueous solution.

Question 16 of 19

What is the hybridization and shape of ClF5?

Explanation: ClF5 utilizes sp3d2 hybridization with five bond pairs and one lone pair, yielding a square pyramidal structure.

Question 17 of 19

Which of the following oxoacids does not exist?

Explanation: Hypofluorous acid (HFO) is unstable and does not exist in significant stable quantities due to fluorine's extreme electronegativity.

Question 18 of 19

What property is observed when iodine reacts with concentrated nitric acid?

Explanation: Concentrated nitric acid oxidizes iodine directly to iodic acid (HIO3) along with nitrogen dioxide gas.

Question 19 of 19

Why are interhalogen compounds more reactive than diatomic halogens?

Explanation: The covalent bond in interhalogens (X-X') is generally weaker than the homonuclear bond (X-X) in pure halogens, enhancing chemical reactivity.
