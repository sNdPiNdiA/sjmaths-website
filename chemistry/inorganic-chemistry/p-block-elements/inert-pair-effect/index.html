Inorganic Chemistry

Inert-Pair Effect

The inert-pair effect describes the reluctance of the ns2 valence electrons to participate in chemical bonding as one moves down a group in the p-block. This phenomenon increases down the groups due to poor shielding of nuclear charge by intervening d- and f-orbitals.

UP PGT Chemistry Subject Code 02 B.Sc. / M.Sc. Level

Origin and Electronic Factors

  • The inert-pair effect is predominantly observed in heavier p-block elements of groups 13, 14, 15, and 16.
  • The penetration of ns orbitals towards the nucleus is high, holding the s-electrons tightly bound and energetically stable.
  • Poor shielding by intervening d- and f-electrons increases the effective nuclear charge experienced by the ns2 electrons.
  • The energy required to promote an ns electron outweighs the bond energy gained by forming additional covalent bonds.

Oxidation State Trends Down Groups

  • In Group 13, boron and aluminium show stable +3 oxidation states, whereas thallium (Tl) predominantly exhibits the +1 oxidation state.
  • In Group 14, carbon and silicon prefer +4, while lead (Pb) forms stable compounds in the +2 oxidation state and acts as an oxidizing agent in +4.
  • In Group 15, bismuth forms stable Bi(III) compounds, making Bi(V) species like NaBiO3 powerful oxidizing agents.
  • The lower oxidation state becomes thermodynamically more stable than the group oxidation state minus two as atomic number increases.

Chemical Consequences and Reactivity

  • Compounds in lower oxidation states down the group exhibit distinct ionic character compared to the covalent character of lighter homologs.
  • Species possessing an inert pair often exhibit distinct stereochemical activity of the lone pair, influencing molecular geometry such as in SnCl3-.
  • Higher oxidation states of heavy p-block elements act as strong oxidizing agents because they readily accept two electrons to drop to the stable inert-pair state.
  • Tl(OH)3 is a much stronger oxidizing agent and weaker base compared to B(OH)3 or Al(OH)3.

⚠️ Important Exceptions & Anomalies
Exception Group oxidation states are expected to be the most stable state for all elements in a group.

Observation: Thallium(I), Lead(II), and Bismuth(III) are more stable than Tl(III), Pb(IV), and Bi(V). — Relativistic contraction and poor shielding of nuclear charge by d- and f-electrons stabilize the ns2 pair.

Exception Higher oxidation states of group 14 elements (e.g., PbO2) should be stable reducing agents.

Observation: PbO2 is a powerful oxidizing agent. — Pb(IV) is reduced to the more stable Pb(II) state, driven by the inert-pair effect.

Exception All group 15 pentahalides should be thermally stable.

Observation: BiF5 exists, but BiCl5 and BiBr5 are unstable or non-existent. — The oxidizing power of Bi(V) combined with large size mismatch and inert-pair effect prevents higher halide formation.

Group 13: Tl+ > Tl3+ | Group 14: Pb2+ > Pb4+ | Group 15: Bi3+ > Bi5+

Application: Immediately select the lower oxidation state for heavy p-block elements (Tl, Pb, Bi, Po) when asked for the most stable species.

Oxidizing Power Trend
Higher OS of heavy p-block = Strong Oxidant

Application: Remember that PbO2, Tl3+ salts, and Bi(V) compounds are strong oxidizers because they want to gain 2 electrons to form the inert ns2 configuration.

Ionic vs Covalent Shift
Lower OS = Ionic, Higher OS = Covalent

🎯 Key Exam Points
  • Thallium(I) resembles group 1 alkali metal ions in forming soluble hydroxide TlOH and carbonate Tl2CO3.
  • Lead dioxide (PbO2) reacts with concentrated HCl to liberate chlorine gas, demonstrating its oxidizing nature.
  • Bismuth(V) in sodium bismuthate (NaBiO3) oxidizes manganese(II) to permanganate in acidic solution.
  • The inert-pair effect is particularly pronounced due to the cumulative lanthanide contraction preceding the 6p elements.
  • Tin(II) compounds function as reducing agents, converting Fe3+ to Fe2+.
  • Germanium exists predominantly in the +4 state, whereas tin shows both +2 and +4, and lead is predominantly +2.
🚫 Common Errors & Confusions
  • Assuming all elements in a group strictly follow the group valency oxidation state.
  • Confusing the stability of PbO (ionic, basic) with PbO2 (amphoteric, oxidizing).
  • Forgetting that relativistic effects play a major role in stabilizing the 6s2 pair in heavier elements like Tl, Pb, and Bi.
  • Mistakenly predicting that BiCl5 is a stable covalent compound.

⚡ Quick Revision: Inert-Pair Effect

Core formulas, rapid recall points, and distinguishing criteria at a glance.

Definition and Origin

The inert-pair effect is the non-participation of ns2 valence electrons in bonding due to high ionization energy and poor shielding by d/f electrons.

Group 13 Trends

Boron and aluminium exhibit +3 states, gallium and indium show both +1 and +3, while thallium is most stable in the +1 oxidation state.

Group 14 Trends

Carbon and silicon are strictly +4, germanium and tin show +2 and +4, with lead forming stable +2 species and powerful +4 oxidizers.

Group 15 Trends

Nitrogen, phosphorus, arsenic, and antimony form stable +5 and +3 states, whereas bismuth forms stable +3 and highly reactive +5 species.

📚 Terms & Units Glossary

Inert-Pair Effect (ns2 reluctance)
Reluctance of ns electrons to participate in bond formation.
Effective Nuclear Charge (Zeff)
Net positive charge experienced by valence electrons.
Lanthanide Contraction (Poor 4f shielding)
Gradual decrease in atomic radii across the lanthanide series.
Oxidizing Agent (Electron acceptor)
Species that undergoes reduction by accepting electrons.
Relativistic Effect (Mass-velocity contraction)
Relativistic contraction of s-orbitals in heavy atoms.
Amphoteric Oxide (Acid-base oxide)
Oxide that reacts with both acids and bases.

🎯 Key Takeaways

  • Tl+ is more stable than Tl3+.
  • Pb2+ is more stable than Pb4+.
  • Bi3+ is more stable than Bi5+.
  • PbO2 is a strong oxidizing agent.
  • Inert pair stability increases down the group.

📌 Quick Recall

  • ns2 electrons become increasingly inert down the p-block groups.
  • Caused by poor shielding from d and f orbitals and relativistic effects.
  • Leads to stability of oxidation states equal to (group number - 2).
  • Higher oxidation states of heavier elements act as strong oxidants.

⚖️ Key Differences

TlCl VS TlCl3

TlCl is stable and ionic due to inert-pair effect, whereas TlCl3 is unstable and easily decomposes to TlCl and Cl2.
