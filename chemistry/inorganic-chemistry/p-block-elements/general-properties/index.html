General Properties of p-Block Elements

The p-block elements encompass groups 13 to 18 of the periodic table, where the differentiating electron enters the outermost p-subshell. Their general properties exhibit distinct periodic trends down the groups and across the periods, dictated by electronic configurations ranging from ns2np1 to ns2np6.

UP PGT Chemistry Subject Code 02 B.Sc. / M.Sc. Level

Electronic Configuration and Periodic Trends

  • The p-block elements possess a valence shell configuration of ns2np1-6, with the exception of Helium which is 1s2.
  • Atomic and ionic radii increase down the group due to the addition of principal quantum shells, despite increased nuclear charge.
  • Across a period from left to right, atomic radii decrease due to a progressive increase in effective nuclear charge (Zeff).
  • Ionization energies generally increase across a period and decrease down a group, modulated by half-filled and fully-filled orbital stabilities.

Oxidation States and Inert Pair Effect

  • The maximum oxidation state shown by a p-block element equals the total number of valence electrons (group number minus 10).
  • Heavier elements in groups 13, 14, 15, and 16 exhibit lower oxidation states two units less than the group oxidation state due to the inert pair effect.
  • The inert pair effect arises from the poor shielding power of intervening d- and f-electrons, making ns2 electrons reluctant to participate in bonding.
  • Catelation properties decrease down a group; carbon shows maximum catenation in group 14 due to high C-C bond energy.

Metallic Character and Chemical Reactivity

  • Metallic character increases down the group as ionization energies decrease, transitioning the elements from non-metals to metalloids and finally to metals.
  • Electronegativity values are generally higher than those of s-block elements, leading to predominantly covalent bond formation in many binary compounds.
  • Oxides of p-block elements shift from strongly acidic on the right to amphoteric in the center and basic on the heavier lower-left boundary.
  • The maximum covalency of second-row elements is restricted to four due to the absence of vacant d-orbitals, whereas heavier elements can expand their octets.

⚠️ Important Exceptions & Anomalies
Exception Ionization energy should increase continuously across a period.

Observation: Group 13 (B < Al > Ga < In > Tl) and Group 15/16 (e.g., N > O) irregularities. — Half-filled (p3) and fully-filled (p6) configurations confer extra exchange energy stability, requiring higher energy to remove an electron.

Exception Electron gain enthalpy becomes progressively more negative down a group.

Observation: Fluorine (F) has a less negative electron gain enthalpy than Chlorine (Cl). — The extremely small 2p size of fluorine results in high interelectronic repulsion, hindering the incoming electron.

Exception Atomic radius increases strictly down every group.

Observation: Gallium (Ga) has a slightly smaller atomic radius than Aluminum (Al). — Poor shielding of the intervening 3d electrons in gallium leads to a sharp increase in Zeff, contracting the atomic cloud.

💡 Shortcuts & Memory Aids
Inert Pair Trend Mnemonic
Lower oxidation state stability: Tl+ > Tl3+, Pb2+ > Pb4+, Bi3+ > Bi5+

Application: Always select the lower oxidation state for heavy p-block elements (Tl, Pb, Bi) in redox stability questions.

Maximum Covalency Rule
Period 2 elements max covalency = 4 (sp3); Period 3+ elements max covalency > 4 (sp3d, sp3d2)

Application: Instantly eliminate species like [BF6]3- while accepting [SiF6]2-.

Acidic Strength of Oxides
Acidic strength ∝ Oxidation state ∝ Electronegativity

Application: Compare central atom oxidation states first; higher positive charge gives stronger acid.

🚫 Common Errors & Confusions
  • Assuming electron gain enthalpy values are always positive; they are typically exothermic (negative) except for noble gases and group 2/15 elements in specific states.
  • Confusing the inert pair effect with shielding effect; inert pair specifically denotes reluctance of s-electrons to ionize in heavy post-transition p-block elements.
  • Expecting boron to form [BF6]3-; boron lacks vacant d-orbitals in its valence shell and cannot expand its octet.
  • Neglecting d-orbital contraction (lanthanide contraction) when comparing atomic radii of period 5 and period 6 transition and post-transition elements.

⚡ Quick Revision: General Properties of p-Block Elements

Core formulas, rapid recall points, and distinguishing criteria at a glance.

Electronic Structure Foundation

The p-block elements span groups 13 to 18, characterized by filling of np orbitals. Their chemistry is governed by variable oxidation states and periodic contraction effects.

Periodic Properties and Trends

Atomic size, ionization energy, and electronegativity show consistent periodicity modulated by nuclear charge, shielding efficiency, and electronic subshell stabilities.

Inert Pair Effect Mechanism

Reluctance of ns2 valence electrons to participate in bond formation increases down heavy groups due to inefficient shielding by d- and f-orbitals.

Anomalous Behavior of Period 2

First-row elements display small atomic radii, high electronegativity, absence of vacant d-orbitals, and unique ability to form strong pπ-pπ multiple bonds.

📚 Terms & Units Glossary

Inert Pair Effect (Reluctance of ns2 electrons)
Persistence of lower oxidation states in heavier p-block elements.
Effective Nuclear Charge (Zeff = Z - σ)
Net positive charge experienced by valence electrons.
Catenation (Self-linking capacity)
Ability of atoms of the same element to form long chains or rings.
Electron Gain Enthalpy (ΔHeg)
Enthalpy change when an electron is added to a gaseous atom.
Allred-Rochow Scale (χ electronegativity)
Electronegativity based on electrostatic force of attraction at atomic surface.
Anomalous Behavior (First-element divergence)
Unique chemical properties of period 2 elements compared to group homologs.

🎯 Key Takeaways

  • Valence configuration: ns2np1-6
  • Period 2 elements strictly obey the octet rule
  • Inert pair effect stabilizes lower oxidation states down the group
  • Fluorine has less negative electron gain enthalpy than chlorine
  • Metallic character increases down any p-block group

📌 Quick Recall

  • p-Block chemistry transitions from non-metallic to metallic down groups.
  • Oxidizing power decreases down halogen group; reducing power increases.
  • Lewis acid character of group 13 halides decreases from BF3 to BI3 in terms of back-bonding, but increases in terms of acceptor strength.
  • Oxide character shifts from acidic to basic down the groups.

⚖️ Key Differences

Ionization Enthalpy VS Electron Gain Enthalpy

Ionization enthalpy is endothermic energy required to remove an electron; electron gain enthalpy is typically exothermic energy released upon electron addition.
