Mechanism of Dehydration of Alcohols

Dehydration of alcohols involves the elimination of a water molecule in the presence of an acid catalyst to yield alkenes via carbocation or concerted pathways. The reaction rate and pathway strongly depend on the degree of substitution of the alcohol carbon.

UP PGT Chemistry Subject Code 02 B.Sc. / M.Sc. Level

Protonation and Leaving Group Formation

  • The hydroxyl group (-OH) is a poor leaving group and must be converted into a good leaving group via rapid, reversible protonation by an acid catalyst such as H2SO4 or H3PO4.
  • The protonated alcohol species, an oxonium ion (R-OH2+), weakens the C-O bond and facilitates cleavage.
  • Equilibrium strongly favors the protonated alcohol at low pH, establishing the starting concentration for subsequent elimination steps.

Carbocation Formation and Rearrangement

  • In the E1 mechanism, rate-determining cleavage of the C-O bond of the oxonium ion yields a planar, sp2-hybridized carbocation intermediate.
  • Secondary and tertiary carbocations readily undergo 1,2-hydride or 1,2-methyl shifts to form more stable tertiary or resonance-stabilized carbocations.
  • The stability order of carbocation intermediates follows 3° > 2° > 1°, dictating the overall reactivity sequence of alcohols toward dehydration.

Deprotonation and Alkene Formation

  • A weak base (such as H2O or HSO4-) removes a β-hydrogen from the adjacent carbon to form the carbon-carbon double bond.
  • Elimination strictly follows Zaitsev's rule, yielding the most thermodynamically stable, highly substituted, and trans-configured alkene as the major product.
  • High temperatures (Δ) and concentrated strong acids shift the equilibrium toward the elimination product rather than competing ether formation.
📐 Key Formulas & Equations
E2 Elimination Rate Law
Rate = k[R-OH2+][Base]
Conditions: Second-order kinetics, applicable primarily to primary alcohols
Units: Rate in mol L-1 s-1, k in L mol-1 s-1
Zaitsev's Rule Product Ratio
Major Product = More Substituted Alkene
Conditions: Thermodynamic control under acidic dehydration conditions
Units: Percentage yield relative to minor Hofmann product
Arrhenius Temperature Dependence
k = A exp(-Ea / RT)
Conditions: High temperature favors elimination over substitution
Units: Ea in J mol-1, T in Kelvin
E1 Elimination Rate Law
Rate = k[R-OH2+]
Conditions: First-order kinetics, applicable to secondary and tertiary alcohols
Units: Rate in mol L-1 s-1, k in s-1
⚠️ Important Exceptions & Anomalies
Exception Primary alcohols dehydrate via E1 carbocation mechanisms

Observation: Primary alcohols dehydrate via concerted E2 pathways or require direct rearrangement — Primary carbocations are extremely high in energy and unstable, preventing direct formation via E1.

Exception Dehydration yields only Zaitsev alkenes

Observation: Hofmann product predominates when bulky acid catalysts or hindered bases are used — Steric hindrance prevents the base from abstracting the more hindered internal β-hydrogen.

Exception Alcohols undergo smooth dehydration without skeletal change

Observation: Pinacol rearrangement occurs during dehydration of 1,2-diols — Formation of an exceptionally stable oxocarbocation drives the migration of an alkyl group.

💡 Shortcuts & Memory Aids
Carbocation Stability First
3° > 2° > 1° (Reactivity order)

Application: Always draw the carbocation first, check for possible 1,2-shifts to a more substituted carbon, and then eliminate.

Zaitsev's Double Bond Count
More α-hydrogens = More stable alkene

Application: Count the hyperconjugative structures (sum of α-H on sp2 carbons) to identify the major alkene product instantly.

Temperature Control Rule
Low T = Ether, High T = Alkene

Application: Use 413 K with excess primary alcohol for ethers, and 443 K with concentrated H2SO4 for alkenes.

🎯 Key Exam Points
  • Reactivity order of alcohols toward acidic dehydration is 3° > 2° > 1°.
  • Concentrated H2SO4 or H3PO4 acts as both acid catalyst and dehydrating agent.
  • Primary alcohols dehydrate via an E2-like concerted pathway or undergo rearrangement before elimination.
  • Carbocation intermediates can undergo 1,2-hydride or 1,2-methyl shifts to achieve higher stability.
  • Dehydration of 2,2-dimethylpropan-1-ol yields 2-methylbut-2-ene due to skeletal rearrangement.
  • Alkenes formed follow Zaitsev's rule where the most substituted alkene predominates.
🚫 Common Errors & Confusions
  • Failing to check for carbocation rearrangements before writing the final alkene structure.
  • Assuming primary alcohols form primary carbocations during acid-catalyzed dehydration.
  • Confusing conditions for intermolecular ether formation (lower temp) with intramolecular alkene formation (higher temp).
  • Ignoring stereochemistry in elimination products where E/Z isomers are possible.

⚡ Quick Revision: Mechanism of Dehydration of Alcohols

Core formulas, rapid recall points, and distinguishing criteria at a glance.

Protonation Equilibrium

Alcohols are rapidly protonated by strong mineral acids to generate oxonium ions, converting a poor leaving group (-OH) into a good leaving group (-OH2+).

Carbocation Generation

Loss of water from the oxonium ion generates an sp2 hybridized carbocation, representing the rate-determining step for secondary and tertiary alcohols.

Rearrangement Pathways

Less stable carbocations undergo skeletal rearrangements via 1,2-shifts of hydrogen or alkyl groups to form more stable thermodynamic intermediates.

Elimination and Product Distribution

Loss of a proton from the β-carbon yields the thermodynamically most stable substituted alkene in accordance with Zaitsev's rule.

📚 Terms & Units Glossary

Oxonium Ion (R-OH2+)
Protonated alcohol species possessing a good leaving group.
Zaitsev's Rule (Regioselectivity rule)
Elimination yields the most substituted alkene as major product.
Carbocation (R3C+)
Electrophilic intermediate with sp2 planar geometry.
Hyperconjugation (σ-π conjugation)
Stabilization of alkenes through delocalization of σ electrons.
E1 Mechanism (Elimination Unimolecular)
Two-step elimination proceeding via a carbocation intermediate.
1,2-Shift (Rearrangement)
Migration of a group with its electron pair to an adjacent carbocation center.

🎯 Key Takeaways

  • Reactivity: Tertiary > Secondary > Primary alcohol
  • Catalysts: Conc. H2SO4, H3PO4, Al2O3
  • Key intermediate in E1: Carbocation (prone to rearrangement)
  • Major product: Most substituted alkene (Zaitsev rule)
  • Primary alcohol dehydration often requires high temperature and catalyst

📌 Quick Recall

  • Alcohol dehydration converts alcohols to alkenes via acid catalysis.
  • Mechanism shifts from E2 (primary) to E1 (secondary and tertiary).
  • Rearrangements are diagnostic signatures of carbocation intermediates.
  • Thermodynamic control favors Zaitsev elimination products.

⚖️ Key Differences

E1 Dehydration VS E2 Dehydration

E1 involves a discrete carbocation intermediate with rearrangements; E2 proceeds via a concerted transition state without rearrangement.

Question 1 of 19

What is the reactive intermediate formed during the acid-catalyzed dehydration of tert-butyl alcohol?

Explanation: Tert-butyl alcohol loses water after protonation to generate a stable tertiary carbocation intermediate.

Question 2 of 19

Which of the following alcohols dehydrates most rapidly with concentrated H2SO4?

Explanation: 2-Methylpropan-2-ol is a tertiary alcohol, forming the most stable tertiary carbocation and reacting the fastest.

Question 3 of 19

What is the primary role of concentrated H2SO4 in the dehydration of alcohols?

Explanation: H2SO4 protonates the hydroxyl oxygen, converting -OH (poor leaving group) into -OH2+ (good leaving group).

Question 4 of 19

Which rule governs the regiochemistry of alkene formation in alcohol dehydration?

Explanation: Zaitsev's rule dictates that elimination yields the more substituted and stable alkene as the major product.

Question 5 of 19

During the dehydration of 3,3-dimethylbutan-2-ol, the major product is formed via which process?

Explanation: A secondary carbocation is formed initially, which undergoes a 1,2-methyl shift to form a more stable tertiary carbocation.

Question 6 of 19

What is the geometry of the carbocation intermediate in E1 dehydration?

Explanation: Carbocation carbon atoms are sp2 hybridized with trigonal planar geometry, allowing attack from either face.

Question 7 of 19

Why do primary alcohols typically not undergo simple E1 dehydration?

Explanation: Primary carbocations possess high energy and are extremely unstable, making unimolecular E1 pathways unfavorable for primary substrates.

Question 8 of 19

Which reagent is commonly used for catalytic vapor-phase dehydration of ethanol to ethene?

Explanation: Passing ethanol vapor over heated alumina (Al2O3) at high temperatures produces ethene efficiently.

Question 9 of 19

Which of the following alcohols will yield 2-methylbut-2-ene as the major product upon acid dehydration?

Explanation: 2-Methylbutan-1-ol undergoes rearrangement upon dehydration to yield 2-methylbut-2-ene as the major Zaitsev product.

Question 10 of 19

What is the molecularity of the rate-determining step in the acid-catalyzed dehydration of a tertiary alcohol?

Explanation: The slow step in tertiary alcohol dehydration is the unimolecular departure of water to form a carbocation (E1).

Question 11 of 19

Which intermediate species is formed when ethanol is treated with concentrated sulfuric acid at 413 K?

Explanation: At 413 K with excess ethanol, nucleophilic attack of ethanol on the protonated alcohol yields diethyl ether.

Question 12 of 19

In the dehydration of cyclohexanol, what is the major organic product?

Explanation: Cyclohexanol undergoes dehydration to form cyclohexene via a cyclohexyl carbocation intermediate.

Question 13 of 19

Which factor increases the ease of dehydration of aliphatic alcohols?

Explanation: Increased alkyl branching stabilizes the resulting carbocation, increasing the rate and ease of dehydration.

Question 14 of 19

What type of rearrangement is observed during the dehydration of 2-methylpropan-1-ol?

Explanation: Protonation and loss of water from 2-methylpropan-1-ol generates a primary carbocation that undergoes a 1,2-hydride shift to form a tertiary butyl carbocation.

Question 15 of 19

Which acid is generally avoided for alcohol dehydration due to its strong oxidizing property?

Explanation: Concentrated nitric acid (HNO3) is a strong oxidizing agent and will oxidize alcohols rather than cleanly dehydrating them.

Question 16 of 19

What is the role of the base (such as H2O or HSO4-) in the final step of E1 dehydration?

Explanation: A weak base abstracts a beta-proton from the carbocation, forcing electrons inward to form the carbon-carbon double bond.

Question 17 of 19

Which of the following compounds does not undergo acid-catalyzed dehydration?

Explanation: Methanol cannot form a stable carbocation or an alkene upon dehydration as it lacks beta-hydrogens and yields formaldehyde or dimethyl ether under extreme conditions.

Question 18 of 19

What is the stereochemical outcome of an E1 dehydration reaction forming a chiral alkene?

Explanation: Because the carbocation intermediate is planar, attack from either side can yield mixtures of geometric (E/Z) isomers.

Question 19 of 19

In the dehydration of alcohols, what does the term 'catalytic' imply regarding the acid?

Explanation: The acid catalyst (e.g., H+) is consumed in the initial protonation step and regenerated in the final deprotonation step.

In the dehydration of alcohols, what is the effect of increasing the concentration of acid catalyst?
