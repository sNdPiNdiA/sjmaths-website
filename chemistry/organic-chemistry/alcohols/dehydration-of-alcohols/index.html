Dehydration of Alcohols

The dehydration of alcohols is an elimination reaction yielding alkenes through the loss of a water molecule in the presence of an acid catalyst. The reaction proceeds via unimolecular (E1) or bimolecular (E2) pathways depending on the class of the alcohol.

UP PGT Chemistry Subject Code 02 B.Sc. / M.Sc. Level

Reaction Mechanisms (E1 vs E2)

  • Primary alcohols undergo dehydration predominantly via the E2 mechanism requiring harsh conditions and strong acid catalysts due to unstable primary carbocation intermediates.
  • Secondary and tertiary alcohols follow the E1 mechanism involving rapid protonation of the hydroxyl group followed by rate-determining water departure to form a carbocation.
  • The relative reactivity order for dehydration is Tertiary > Secondary > Primary reflecting carbocation stability.

Regioselectivity and Rearrangements

  • Dehydration reactions strictly follow Zaitsev's rule, yielding the most substituted and thermodynamically stable alkene as the major product.
  • Carbocation intermediates formed during E1 dehydration undergo 1,2-hydride or 1,2-methyl shifts to generate more stable tertiary or resonance-stabilized carbocations.
  • Dehydration of 3,3-dimethylbutan-2-ol yields 2,3-dimethylbut-2-ene as the primary product due to skeletal rearrangement.

Catalysts and Reagents

  • Concentrated H2SO4 or H3PO4 serve as standard protonating agents at elevated temperatures (443 K for primary, 393 K for secondary, 358 K for tertiary).
  • Alternative mild reagents include anhydrous Al2O3 at 623 K and POCl3 in pyridine for base-promoted elimination pathways.
  • Removal of the volatile alkene product via fractional distillation shifts the equilibrium forward according to Le Chatelier's principle.

⚠️ Important Exceptions & Anomalies
Exception Primary alcohols always undergo E2 elimination without rearrangement.

Observation: Neopentyl alcohol (2,2-dimethylpropan-1-ol) undergoes E1 dehydration with rearrangement. — Formation of the primary carbocation is bypassed by simultaneous departure of water and a 1,2-methyl shift to yield a stable tertiary carbocation.

Exception Dehydration yields the most substituted alkene (Zaitsev product).

Observation: Bulky bases or specific steric constraints can yield the Hoffman (less substituted) alkene. — Steric hindrance prevents abstraction of internal protons, favoring the kinetically accessible terminal proton.

💡 Shortcuts & Memory Aids
Reactivity Sequence Mnemonic
3 > 2 > 1

Application: Remember that tertiary alcohols dehydrate easiest (lowest temperature, weakest acid) because they form stable 3° carbocations.

Carbocation Shift Check
Look left, look right, check adjacent carbon for H or CH3

Application: Always check if the adjacent carbon can provide a hydride or alkyl shift to generate a tertiary or resonance-stabilized carbocation before writing the final alkene.

Temperature-Catalyst Correlation
Mild acid + Low Temp = Ether; Strong acid + High Temp = Alkene

Application: Distinguish between intermolecular dehydration (ether formation at lower temperatures) and intramolecular dehydration (alkene formation at higher temperatures).

🎯 Key Exam Points
  • Tertiary alcohols require 85% H3PO4 at 358 K for dehydration.
  • Secondary alcohols require 60% H2SO4 at 373 K to 393 K.
  • Primary alcohols require concentrated H2SO4 at 443 K.
  • Pinacol rearrangement involves dehydration of 1,2-diols to yield ketones or aldehydes via a carbocation intermediate.
  • Cyclohexanol dehydrates to cyclohexene upon heating with concentrated H3PO4.
  • Dehydration of ethanol at 413 K yields diethyl ether, whereas at 443 K it yields ethene.
🚫 Common Errors & Confusions
  • Failing to account for carbocation rearrangements when predicting major alkene products.
  • Confusing the temperature conditions for primary versus tertiary alcohol dehydration.
  • Assuming primary alcohols proceed via stable primary carbocation intermediates.
  • Ignoring Le Chatelier's principle regarding the continuous removal of the gaseous alkene.

⚡ Quick Revision: Dehydration of Alcohols

Core formulas, rapid recall points, and distinguishing criteria at a glance.

Protonation of Hydroxyl Group

Alcohols react with acidic protons to convert the poor leaving group (-OH) into an excellent leaving group (-OH2+).

Carbocation Formation and Stability

Loss of water generates a carbocation intermediate whose stability dictates the ease of dehydration and potential for molecular rearrangement.

Elimination and Zaitsev Orientation

Deprotonation of the carbocation or concerted E2 elimination yields alkenes following Zaitsev's rule for thermodynamic stability.

Competing Pathways

Intermolecular dehydration yielding ethers competes with intramolecular dehydration yielding alkenes depending on temperature and alcohol concentration.

📚 Terms & Units Glossary

Dehydration (-H2O)
Elimination of a water molecule from an organic compound.
Zaitsev's Rule (Stability Rule)
Elimination yields the most substituted alkene as the major product.
Carbocation (R3C+)
Reaction intermediate possessing a trivalent positively charged carbon atom.
E1 Elimination (Unimolecular)
Two-step elimination proceeding via a carbocation intermediate.
E2 Elimination (Bimolecular)
Concerted one-step elimination occurring without an intermediate.
Pinacol (1,2-Diol)
Vicinal diol that undergoes acid-catalyzed dehydration and rearrangement.

🎯 Key Takeaways

  • Reactivity order: 3° > 2° > 1°
  • Catalysts: Concentrated H2SO4 or H3PO4
  • Primary product follows Zaitsev's rule
  • Rearrangements occur via 1,2-shifts to form more stable carbocations
  • Higher temperatures favor alkene formation over ether formation

📌 Quick Recall

  • Alcohols undergo acid-catalyzed elimination to form alkenes.
  • Tertiary alcohols react fastest via E1 mechanism.
  • Primary alcohols react via E2 mechanism requiring high temperatures.
  • Carbocation rearrangements are characteristic of E1 dehydration.

⚖️ Key Differences

E1 Dehydration VS E2 Dehydration

E1 involves a carbocation intermediate and potential rearrangements, whereas E2 is a concerted single-step process.

What intermediate is formed during the E1 dehydration of secondary and tertiary alcohols?

What type of rearrangement is commonly observed during the E1 dehydration of alcohols?

Explanation: Carbocations undergo 1,2-shifts to achieve higher stability.

Question 7 of 18

What is the primary product formed when ethanol is heated with excess concentrated H2SO4 at 443 K?

Explanation: Heating ethanol with concentrated H2SO4 at 443 K yields ethene via intramolecular dehydration.

Question 8 of 18

Which mechanism is followed predominantly during the dehydration of primary alcohols?

Explanation: Primary alcohols cannot form stable primary carbocations, so they react via the concerted E2 mechanism.

Question 9 of 18

What role does the acid catalyst play in the initial step of alcohol dehydration?

Explanation: Protonation converts -OH into -OH2+, turning it into a good leaving group (water).

Question 10 of 18

Which compound is formed when ethanol is heated with concentrated H2SO4 at 413 K?

Explanation: At a lower temperature of 413 K, intermolecular dehydration of ethanol yields diethyl ether.

Question 11 of 18

Why does neopentyl alcohol undergo rearrangement upon dehydration?

Explanation: A methyl shift occurs simultaneously with water departure to convert a less stable primary carbocation into a stable tertiary carbocation.

Question 12 of 18

Which of the following is a characteristic feature of E1 elimination?

Explanation: E1 is a two-step process featuring a carbocation intermediate.

Question 13 of 18

What is the hybridization change of the reacting carbon atoms during alcohol dehydration?

Explanation: The carbon atoms involved in forming the double bond change from sp3 hybridized in the alcohol to sp2 in the alkene.

Question 14 of 18

Which acid is preferred to minimize oxidation side reactions during alcohol dehydration?

Explanation: Phosphoric acid is non-oxidizing compared to sulfuric acid and prevents charring or oxidation side reactions.

Question 15 of 18

In the dehydration of cyclohexanol, what is the product?

Explanation: Cyclohexanol undergoes intramolecular dehydration to yield cyclohexene.

Question 16 of 18

What is the molecularity of the rate-determining step in E1 dehydration?

Explanation: The rate-determining step involves only the protonated alcohol leaving to form a carbocation, making it unimolecular.

Question 17 of 18

Which of the following does not undergo acid-catalyzed dehydration easily?

Explanation: Methanol cannot form a stable carbocation and lacks beta-hydrogens required for simple dehydration to an alkene.

Question 18 of 18

What drives the forward reaction in the dehydration of alcohols to alkenes?

Explanation: Distilling off the alkene product shifts the reversible equilibrium towards completion.
