Organic Chemistry

Proteins

Proteins are high-molecular-weight heteropolymers composed of α-amino acids linked together by peptide bonds. They serve structural, catalytic, regulatory, and transport functions in biological systems.

UP PGT Chemistry Subject Code 02 B.Sc. / M.Sc. Level

Amino Acid Building Blocks and Classification

  • All naturally occurring protein amino acids except glycine possess a chiral α-carbon and exist exclusively in the L-configuration.
  • Amino acids exist as zwitterions (dipolar ions) at their isoelectric point, possessing both positive (-NH3+) and negative (-COO-) charges.
  • Essential amino acids cannot be synthesized de novo by humans and must be obtained via diet (e.g., valine, leucine, lysine).
  • Classification is based on the nature of the side chain R group: non-polar aliphatic, aromatic, polar uncharged, positively charged, and negatively charged.

Structural Organization of Proteins

  • Primary structure refers to the linear sequence of amino acids held together by covalent peptide bonds.
  • Secondary structure includes α-helix and β-pleated sheets stabilized by interchain and intrachain hydrogen bonding between peptide C=O and N-H groups.
  • Tertiary structure describes the overall 3D folding driven by hydrophobic interactions, hydrogen bonds, ionic bonds, and disulfide linkages.
  • Quaternary structure involves the association of multiple polypeptide subunits via non-covalent interactions, as seen in hemoglobin.

Denaturation and Chemical Properties

  • Denaturation disrupts the secondary, tertiary, and quaternary structures of proteins via heat, pH extremes, or denaturing agents like urea without breaking peptide bonds.
  • The biuret test yields a characteristic purple/violet complex when Cu2+ coordinates with peptide nitrogen atoms in alkaline medium.
  • Xanthoproteic test gives a yellow color with proteins containing nitratable aromatic rings (tyrosine and tryptophan) upon heating with concentrated HNO3.
  • Sanger's reagent (1-fluoro-2,4-dinitrobenzene) and Edman's reagent (phenyl isothiocyanate) are utilized for N-terminal amino acid sequencing.

⚠️ Important Exceptions & Anomalies
Exception All proteinogenic amino acids are optically active and chiral.

Observation: Glycine is optically inactive. — Glycine contains two hydrogen atoms bonded to the α-carbon, making it achiral (lacks an asymmetric carbon center).

Exception Peptide bonds adopt a trans conformation exclusively.

Observation: Xanthine peptide bonds preceding proline can adopt a cis conformation. — The cyclic side chain of proline creates similar energy states for both cis and trans peptide isomers.

Exception Proteins are completely insoluble in non-polar organic solvents.

Observation: Certain membrane proteins are soluble in organic solvent mixtures. — Hydrophobic exterior residues allow stable dissolution within lipid bilayers and organic solvents.

🎯 Key Exam Points
  • The peptide bond (-CO-NH-) has partial double-bond character due to resonance, restricting rotation around the C-N bond.
  • Collagen contains an unusual high proportion of hydroxyproline and hydroxylysine residues stabilized by a triple helix.
  • Sanger reagent is 1-fluoro-2,4-dinitrobenzene (FDNB); Edman reagent is phenyl isothiocyanate (PITC).
  • Denaturation destroys biological activity while preserving the primary structure and sequence of amino acids.
  • Hemoglobin exhibits cooperative binding of oxygen, described by a sigmoidal binding curve.
  • Cysteine residues form covalent disulfide bridges (-S-S-) which provide tertiary structure stability.
🚫 Common Errors & Confusions
  • Confusing primary structure cleavage (hydrolysis of peptide bonds) with protein denaturation (disruption of 3D folding).
  • Assuming glycine is optically active because it is an amino acid.
  • Forgetting that proline is technically an imino acid due to its secondary amine ring structure.
  • Confusing the isoelectric point with neutral pH 7.0 for all amino acids.

⚡ Quick Revision: Proteins

Core formulas, rapid recall points, and distinguishing criteria at a glance.

Amino Acid Stereochemistry

Amino acids are bifunctional organic compounds containing a carboxylic acid group and a primary or secondary amine group attached to the α-carbon.

Peptide Bond Mechanics

The peptide bond is planar, rigid, and exists in a trans conformation with restricted torsional rotation.

Protein Folding Hierarchy

Secondary, tertiary, and quaternary protein structures are stabilized predominantly by non-covalent interactions and disulfide cross-links.

Analytical Identification

Colorimetric assays including Biuret, Ninhydrin, and Xanthoproteic tests verify the presence of peptide bonds, free amines, and aromatic rings.

📚 Terms & Units Glossary

Zwitterion (Dipolar ion)
Internal salt form with both positive and negative charges.
Isoelectric Point (pI)
pH at which net electrical charge of an amino acid or protein is zero.
Denaturation (Loss of 3D structure)
Unfolding of proteins without breaking peptide bonds.
Peptide Bond (-CO-NH-)
Amide linkage connecting successive amino acid residues.
Chirality (Asymmetric center)
Property of non-superimposable mirror images.
Ninhydrin Test (Ruhemann's purple)
Colorimetric test yielding purple dye upon reaction with α-amino acids.

🎯 Key Takeaways

  • All proteinogenic amino acids except glycine are optically active.
  • All natural amino acids belong to the L-series.
  • Proline contains a secondary amine group forming a five-membered ring with the α-carbon.
  • The biuret test is positive for all compounds containing two or more peptide bonds.
  • Edman degradation removes N-terminal residues sequentially without destroying the rest of the chain.

📌 Quick Recall

  • Proteins are polyamide polymers of L-α-amino acids.
  • Structural stability depends on hydrogen bonding, hydrophobic interactions, and disulfide bridges.
  • Enzymatic catalysis and structural integrity rely on proper conformational folding.
  • Chemical tests distinguish amino acids and proteins based on functional group reactivity.

⚖️ Key Differences

Peptide bond VS Glycosidic linkage

Peptide bonds link amino acids in proteins, whereas glycosidic linkages connect monosaccharides in carbohydrates.

Question 1 of 10
What is the net charge of a neutral amino acid at a pH significantly below its isoelectric point (pI)?

Solution: At a pH below pI, the carboxyl group is protonated (-COOH) and the amino group remains protonated (-NH3+), conferring a net positive charge.

Question 2 of 10
Which amino acid contains a phenolic hydroxyl group?

Solution: Tyrosine features a phenol group attached to its aromatic benzene ring.

Question 3 of 10
Secondary structure of proteins includes which of the following conformations?

Solution: α-helix and β-pleated sheets are standard secondary structural motifs stabilized by hydrogen bonding.

Question 4 of 10
Which chemical linkage joins amino acids in a polypeptide chain?

Solution: Peptide bonds are amide linkages formed between the carboxyl group of one amino acid and the amino group of another.

Question 5 of 10
The sulfur-containing amino acid among the following is:

Solution: Cysteine contains a thiol group (-SH) containing sulfur.

Question 6 of 10
Which reagent is employed for identifying the N-terminal residue via phenylthiohydantoin derivatives?

Solution: Edman's reagent (phenyl isothiocyanate) cleaves and identifies the N-terminal residue as a PTH-amino acid.

Question 7 of 10
What causes sickle-cell anemia at the molecular level?

Solution: Sickle-cell hemoglobin results from the substitution of hydrophilic glutamic acid by hydrophobic valine at position 6 of the β-chain.

Question 8 of 10
Which color is observed in a positive Xanthoproteic test?

Solution: Nitration of aromatic amino acid rings by concentrated nitric acid yields a yellow color.

Question 9 of 10
At pH values above the isoelectric point, an amino acid migrates toward which electrode in electrophoresis?

Solution: At pH > pI, amino acids possess a net negative charge and migrate toward the positive anode.

Question 10 of 10
Which protein structural feature is responsible for enzyme specificity and active site formation?

Solution: Tertiary structure forms the specific 3D spatial conformation required for enzyme active sites and substrate binding.
