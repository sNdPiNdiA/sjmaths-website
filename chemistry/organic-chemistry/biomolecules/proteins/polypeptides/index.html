Organic Chemistry

Polypeptides

Polypeptides are linear polymers formed by condensation reactions between the amino group of one α-amino acid and the carboxyl group of another. They are linked together by characteristic peptide bonds with partial double-bond character.

UP PGT Chemistry Subject Code 02 B.Sc. / M.Sc. Level

Peptide Bond Structure and Stereochemistry

  • The peptide bond (-CO-NH-) possesses approximately 40% double-bond character due to resonance stabilization involving the carbonyl oxygen and the nitrogen lone pair.
  • Rotation around the C-N peptide bond is restricted, locking the six atoms of the peptide group into a rigid planar configuration.
  • Peptide bonds predominantly adopt the trans configuration to minimize steric hindrance between adjacent side chains (R groups).

Primary Structure and N/C Terminus Convention

  • The primary structure of a polypeptide refers to the exact linear sequence of amino acid residues connected by covalent peptide bonds.
  • By universal convention, polypeptide sequences are written starting from the N-terminus (free amino group) on the left to the C-terminus (free carboxyl group) on the right.
  • Terminal functional groups determine the overall charge and buffering capacity of the polypeptide chain at varying physiological pH values.

Acid-Base Properties and Zwitterionic Form

  • In aqueous solution, amino acids and polypeptides exist predominantly as zwitterions containing both a positive and negative charge.
  • The isoelectric point (pI) is the specific pH at which the net electrical charge of the polypeptide is zero, resulting in minimal electrophoretic migration.
  • At a pH well above the pI, polypeptides carry a net negative charge, whereas at a pH below the pI, they carry a net positive charge.

⚠️ Important Exceptions & Anomalies
Exception Peptide bonds are generally found in the trans conformation.

Observation: Proline peptide bonds — Proline is an imino acid whose cyclic structure gives comparable energy stability to both cis and trans conformations, allowing a significant fraction of X-Pro peptide bonds to exist in the cis form.

Exception Primary structure alone dictates the final three-dimensional conformation.

Observation: Prion proteins and denatured structural variants — Certain protein chains can adopt stable alternative structural isoforms (prions) or require specific chaperones to overcome kinetic activation barriers during folding.

💡 Shortcuts & Memory Aids
Peptide Bond Count Mnemonic
Bonds = Residues - 1

Application: Quickly determine water molecules released or peptide bonds formed in an n-mer polypeptide chain.

N to C Direction Rule
Read Left to Right: N to C

Application: Always identify the terminal nitrogen with the free amino group on the far left when sequencing or naming.

pI Estimation Shortcut
pI = (pKa,basic group + pKa,acidic group) / 2

Application: Rapidly calculate the isoelectric point for simple neutral peptides by averaging the two relevant pKa values flanking the neutral species.

🎯 Key Exam Points
  • The peptide bond length is approximately 1.32 Å, which is shorter than a standard single C-N bond (1.49 Å) due to resonance.
  • Sanger's reagent (1-fluoro-2,4-dinitrobenzene, FDNB) is classically used to identify the N-terminal amino acid in a polypeptide.
  • Edman degradation uses phenyl isothiocyanate (PITC) for sequential stepwise degradation to determine amino acid sequences from the N-terminus.
  • Hydrolases such as trypsin and chymotrypsin catalyze the specific cleavage of peptide bonds adjacent to specific amino acid residues.
  • A tripeptide contains three amino acid residues linked by exactly two peptide bonds and releases two water molecules upon formation.
  • At the isoelectric point (pI), the solubility of a polypeptide in water is typically at its minimum.
🚫 Common Errors & Confusions
  • Confusing the number of amino acids with the number of peptide bonds in a linear chain (N vs N-1).
  • Assuming rotation is completely free around the C-N peptide bond, ignoring its partial double-bond character.
  • Forgetting that proline lacks a primary amino group, forming a tertiary amide bond instead.
  • Incorrectly identifying the C-terminus as the end containing the free amino group.

⚡ Quick Revision: Polypeptides

Core formulas, rapid recall points, and distinguishing criteria at a glance.

Peptide Linkage

Formed via elimination of water between a carboxyl and an amino group, creating a planar amide linkage with restricted rotation.

Sequence Determination

Determined chemically via Edman degradation or enzymatically via selective endopeptidases and exopeptidases.

Acid-Base Behavior

Exhibits amphoteric properties with distinct titration curves and an isoelectric point dictated by constituent side chains.

Conformational Stability

Stabilized by backbone hydrogen bonding, hydrophobic interactions, and disulfide bridges formed between cysteine residues.

📚 Terms & Units Glossary

Peptide Bond (-CO-NH-)
Covalent amide bond linking successive amino acids in a polypeptide chain.
N-Terminus (Amino end)
The end of a polypeptide possessing a free α-amino group.
C-Terminus (Carboxyl end)
The end of a polypeptide possessing a free α-carboxyl group.
Isoelectric Point (pI)
pH at which net electrical charge of the polypeptide is zero.
Zwitterion (Dipolar ion)
Molecular species carrying both positive and negative charges simultaneously.
Edman Degradation (PITC method)
Sequential procedure to identify N-terminal amino acids without destroying the rest of the chain.

🎯 Key Takeaways

  • Number of peptide bonds = n - 1 for a linear chain of n residues.
  • Peptide bond has 40% double bond character and is planar.
  • Primary sequence is always written from N-terminus to C-terminus.
  • Edman reagent uses phenyl isothiocyanate for N-terminal sequencing.
  • Isoelectric point (pI) is the pH of zero net migration in an electric field.

📌 Quick Recall

  • Polypeptides are condensation polymers of amino acids linked by amide bonds.
  • Resonance gives peptide bonds restricted rotation and trans-preference.
  • Acid-base properties depend on terminal groups and ionizable side chains.
  • Chemical degradation techniques allow precise primary sequence determination.

⚖️ Key Differences

Peptide bond VS Glycosidic bond

Peptide bond links amino acids via amide linkage, whereas glycosidic bond links monosaccharides via ether linkage.

Question 1 of 19

How many peptide bonds are present in a hexapeptide?

Explanation: A hexapeptide contains 6 amino acid residues. The number of peptide bonds is n - 1 = 6 - 1 = 5.

Question 2 of 19

What is the structural characteristic of a peptide bond?

Explanation: Resonance stabilization gives the peptide bond a partial double bond character (~40%), restricting rotation and enforcing a planar geometry.

Question 3 of 19

By convention, how is the sequence of a polypeptide written?

Explanation: Universal biochemical convention dictates that polypeptide sequences are written with the N-terminus on the left and the C-terminus on the right.

Question 4 of 19

Which reagent is classically used for Edman degradation of polypeptides?

Explanation: Phenyl isothiocyanate (PITC) reacts with the N-terminal amino group in Edman degradation to release an anilinothiazolinone derivative.

Question 5 of 19

What is a zwitterion form of an amino acid or peptide?

Explanation: A zwitterion possesses both a positive charge (protonated amino group) and a negative charge (deprotonated carboxyl group), resulting in a net neutral charge.

Question 6 of 19

What is the isoelectric point (pI) of a polypeptide?

Explanation: The isoelectric point is defined as the specific pH at which the net electrical charge of the peptide or protein is zero.

Question 7 of 19

Which configuration is predominantly adopted by peptide bonds to minimize steric hindrance?

Explanation: Peptide bonds predominantly adopt the trans configuration where adjacent Cα atoms are on opposite sides of the peptide bond to minimize steric clash.

Question 8 of 19

How many water molecules are released when forming a pentapeptide from individual amino acids?

Explanation: Formation of an n-residue peptide requires the creation of n - 1 peptide bonds, releasing an equal number of water molecules (5 - 1 = 4).

Question 9 of 19

Which amino acid frequently violates the trans-peptide bond preference due to its cyclic side chain?

Explanation: Proline is an imino acid whose rigid cyclic ring gives comparable stability to both cis and trans peptide bonds.

Question 10 of 19

What type of chemical reaction forms a peptide bond?

Explanation: A peptide bond is formed via a condensation reaction involving the elimination of a water molecule between a carboxyl group and an amino group.

Question 11 of 19

Sanger's reagent is chemically known as:

Explanation: Sanger's reagent is 1-fluoro-2,4-dinitrobenzene (FDNB), used to tag N-terminal residues.

Question 12 of 19

What is the approximate length of a peptide bond compared to a standard single C-N bond?

Explanation: Due to partial double-bond character, the peptide C-N bond length is ~1.32 Å, shorter than a typical single C-N bond (~1.49 Å).

Question 13 of 19

Which level of protein structure is directly defined by the polypeptide's amino acid sequence?

Explanation: The primary structure is precisely the covalent linear sequence of amino acid residues in the polypeptide chain.

Question 14 of 19

At a pH significantly higher than its isoelectric point, a polypeptide will:

Explanation: At a pH above the pI, protons are stripped from acidic and weakly basic groups, imparting a net negative charge to the polypeptide.

Question 15 of 19

Which functional groups comprise the repeating backbone of a polypeptide chain?

Explanation: The repeating backbone framework of a polypeptide consists of the sequence -N-Cα-C(=O)-.

Question 16 of 19

What is produced when a polypeptide is completely hydrolyzed in aqueous acid?

Explanation: Complete acid hydrolysis (e.g., 6M HCl at 110°C) cleaves all peptide bonds, yielding free amino acids.

Question 17 of 19

Which bond is NOT typically involved in maintaining higher-level polypeptide folding?

Explanation: Glycosidic bonds link carbohydrates, not amino acids within a polypeptide chain.

Question 18 of 19

What term describes proteins or polypeptides composed of a single continuous chain of amino acids without branching?

Explanation: Standard ribosomal polypeptides are unbranched, linear polymers of amino acid residues.

Question 19 of 19

In a peptide chain, rotation is permitted around which of the backbone single bonds connected to the α-carbon?

Explanation: Rotation is restricted around the peptide C-N bond, but free rotation is permitted around the N-Cα (φ dihedral angle) and Cα-C (ψ dihedral angle) single bonds.

Question 1 of 10
How many water molecules are liberated in the synthesis of a decapeptide?

Solution: A decapeptide has 10 residues, requiring n - 1 = 9 peptide bond formations, releasing 9 water molecules.

Question 2 of 10
Which statement regarding the peptide bond is incorrect?

Solution: The peptide bond has partial double-bond character, which prevents free rotation around the C-N axis.

Question 3 of 10
The C-terminus of a peptide chain terminates with which functional group?

Solution: The C-terminus always features a free, unreacted α-carboxyl group (-COOH).

Question 4 of 10
What is the net charge of a neutral amino acid or simple peptide at a pH equal to its pI?

Solution: At the isoelectric point (pI), positive and negative charges balance out, resulting in a net charge of zero.

Question 5 of 10
Which chemical is employed in Edman degradation?

Solution: Edman degradation specifically employs phenyl isothiocyanate (PITC) to cleave and identify N-terminal amino acids.

Question 6 of 10
In a polypeptide chain, the repeating backbone atoms are represented by:

Solution: The backbone consists of the repeating sequence of atoms: nitrogen, alpha-carbon, and carbonyl carbon (-N-C-C-).

Question 7 of 10
Which amino acid residue disrupts α-helices due to its inability to form backbone hydrogen bonds and its rigid ring structure?

Solution: Proline lacks an amide hydrogen for hydrogen bonding and introduces structural rigidity, acting as a helix breaker.

Question 8 of 10
What type of linkage connects amino acids in a polypeptide?

Solution: The peptide bond is chemically an amide linkage formed between a carboxyl group and an amino group.

Question 9 of 10
If a polypeptide has a pI of 6.0, what charge does it carry at pH 4.0?

Solution: At a pH below the pI (4.0 < 6.0), the polypeptide picks up protons, acquiring a net positive charge.

Question 10 of 10
Which of the following describes the primary structure of a polypeptide?

Solution: Primary structure refers exclusively to the covalent linear sequence of amino acids linked by peptide bonds.
