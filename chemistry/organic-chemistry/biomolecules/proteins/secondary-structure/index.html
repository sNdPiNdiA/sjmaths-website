Secondary Structure of Proteins

The secondary structure of proteins refers to the local folded conformations assumed by the polypeptide backbone. It is stabilized predominantly by hydrogen bonding between the carbonyl oxygen and amide hydrogen of the peptide bonds.

UP PGT Chemistry Subject Code 02 B.Sc. / M.Sc. Level

Alpha-Helix Conformation and Geometry

  • The α-helix is a tightly coiled conformation stabilized by intrachain hydrogen bonds between the C=O of residue i and the N-H of residue i + 4.
  • Each turn of the helix contains 3.6 amino acid residues with a translational rise of 1.5 Å per residue, giving a pitch of 5.4 Å.
  • Side chains (R groups) point outward from the helical axis to minimize steric hindrance and electrostatic repulsion.
  • Proline acts as an helix breaker because its rigid cyclic structure prevents free rotation around the N-Cα bond and lacks an amide hydrogen for H-bonding.

Beta-Pleated Sheet Architectures

  • β-sheets consist of fully extended polypeptide chains called β-strands aligned adjacent to each other, stabilized by interchain hydrogen bonds.
  • Antiparallel β-sheets feature alternating N-to-C directions with linear, highly stable perpendicular hydrogen bonds.
  • Parallel β-sheets run in the same N-to-C direction, resulting in distorted, less stable angled hydrogen bonds.
  • Side chains project alternately above and below the plane of the sheet.

Turns, Loops, and Ramachandran Plots

  • β-turns (reverse turns) allow the polypeptide chain to fold back on itself, typically involving 4 successive residues stabilized by a hydrogen bond between residue i and i + 3.
  • Glycine and Proline are frequently found in β-turns; glycine provides high conformational flexibility, while proline forces a cis-peptide bond configuration favorable for tight turns.
  • The Ramachandran plot maps the empirically allowed regions of (φ, ψ) torsional angles, showing dense clustering corresponding to α-helices and β-sheets.
  • Steric clashes between the backbone atoms and side chains restrict large portions of the (φ, ψ) conformational space.

⚠️ Important Exceptions & Anomalies
Exception Polypeptide bonds generally adopt the trans conformation due to lower steric hindrance.

Observation: X-Pro peptide bonds frequently adopt the cis conformation. — The energy difference between cis and trans isomers for X-Pro peptide bonds is relatively small compared to other peptide bonds.

Exception Amino acids with chiral Cα carbons form stable right-handed α-helices.

Observation: Poly-L-alanine forms a right-handed helix, while glycine can form left-handed arrangements. — Glycine lacks a side-chain methyl group, removing chiral steric constraints and allowing access to left-handed helical regions.

💡 Shortcuts & Memory Aids
Hydrogen Bond Rule in Alpha-Helix
n = i + 4

Application: Remember that the carbonyl oxygen of residue i always hydrogen bonds with the amide nitrogen of residue i + 4.

Helix Geometry Calculation
Pitch = 3.6 × 1.5 Å = 5.4 Å

Application: Quickly compute the total length of an α-helix segment by multiplying the number of turns by 5.4 Å or residues by 1.5 Å.

Sheet Stability Mnemonic
Anti = Aligned (Straight H-Bonds)

Application: Antiparallel sheets have straight, stronger hydrogen bonds making them thermodynamically more stable than parallel sheets.

🎯 Key Exam Points
  • The standard α-helix has 3.6 residues per turn and a pitch of 5.4 Å.
  • Proline and hydroxyproline act as strong α-helix breakers.
  • Glycine provides exceptional conformational flexibility and is common in β-turns.
  • Antiparallel β-sheets possess linear, energetically favored hydrogen bonds.
  • Ramachandran plots depict allowed torsional angles φ and ψ for peptide backbones.
  • The peptide bond possesses partial double bond character due to resonance, restricting rotation around the C-N bond.
🚫 Common Errors & Confusions
  • Confusing intrachain hydrogen bonds (α-helix) with interchain hydrogen bonds (β-sheet).
  • Assuming parallel β-sheets are more stable than antiparallel ones; antiparallel is more stable due to linear H-bonds.
  • Forgetting that side chains point outward in α-helices and alternate perpendicular to the strand in β-sheets.
  • Believing that proline can easily accommodate an internal position within an α-helix.

⚡ Quick Revision: Secondary Structure of Proteins

Core formulas, rapid recall points, and distinguishing criteria at a glance.

Backbone Conformation

Secondary structures are localized folded patterns dictated by backbone dihedral angles φ and ψ constrained by peptide bond planarity.

Hydrogen Bonding Network

Stabilization relies entirely on non-covalent hydrogen bonding between backbone amide protons and carbonyl oxygens.

Helix Breaking Residues

Proline and glycine disrupt regular secondary structures due to conformational rigidity and extreme flexibility respectively.

Sheet Topology

β-sheets organize as pleated sheets in parallel or antiparallel orientations providing structural rigidity to fibrous proteins.

📚 Terms & Units Glossary

Alpha-Helix (α-helix)
A right-handed coiled secondary structure stabilized by intrachain H-bonds.
Beta-Sheet (β-sheet)
Extended sheet-like structure formed by hydrogen-bonded adjacent strands.
Ramachandran Plot ((φ, ψ) map)
A scatter plot of torsional angles illustrating sterically allowed protein conformations.
Pitch (P)
The linear distance covered by one complete turn of an α-helix (5.4 Å).
Beta-Turn (Reverse turn)
A short structural motif that reverses the direction of a polypeptide chain.
Helix Breaker (Proline)
An amino acid residue that destabilizes or terminates α-helical regions.

🎯 Key Takeaways

  • Residues per turn in α-helix = 3.6
  • Rise per residue in α-helix = 1.5 Å
  • Pitch of α-helix = 5.4 Å
  • Antiparallel β-sheets have linear, stronger hydrogen bonds
  • Proline and glycine are helix breakers and turn promoters

📌 Quick Recall

  • Secondary structures are local conformations maintained by backbone hydrogen bonds.
  • Alpha-helices are stabilized by intrachain bonds between residue i and i+4.
  • Beta-sheets feature extended strands linked via interchain hydrogen bonds.
  • Steric hindrance limits backbone angles mapped on Ramachandran plots.

⚖️ Key Differences

Parallel Beta-Sheet VS Antiparallel Beta-Sheet

Parallel strands run in the same N-to-C direction with angled H-bonds; antiparallel strands run in opposite directions with linear H-bonds.

In an alpha-helix, a hydrogen bond is formed between the C=O of residue i and the N-H of which residue?

Question 5 of 19

Which type of beta-sheet structure possesses linear, more stable hydrogen bonds?

Explanation: Antiparallel beta-sheets have straight, perpendicular hydrogen bonds that make them thermodynamically more stable than parallel sheets.

Question 6 of 19

What do the axes of a Ramachandran plot represent?

Explanation: A Ramachandran plot maps the backbone dihedral angles φ (phi) and ψ (psi).

Question 7 of 19

Why is glycine often found in beta-turns?

Explanation: Glycine has only a hydrogen atom as its side chain, granting it unique conformational flexibility required for tight turns.

Question 8 of 19

What is the axial rise per residue in a standard alpha-helix?

Explanation: The translation along the helical axis per amino acid residue is 1.5 Å.

Question 9 of 19

Which structural element reverses the direction of a polypeptide chain?

Explanation: Beta-turns (or reverse turns) allow the polypeptide chain to fold back on itself over four residues.

Question 10 of 19

The partial double bond character of the peptide bond is due to:

Explanation: Resonance delocalization of nitrogen lone pair electrons into the carbonyl group gives the peptide C-N bond partial double bond character.

Question 11 of 19

In a parallel beta-sheet, adjacent strands run in:

Explanation: Parallel beta-sheet strands run in the same amino-to-carboxyl direction.

Question 12 of 19

Which amino acid residue is least likely to be found in the interior of an alpha-helix?

Explanation: Proline disrupts alpha-helices and is typically found in turns or at the ends of helices.

Question 13 of 19

What stabilizes the secondary structure of proteins?

Explanation: Secondary structure is primarily stabilized by hydrogen bonds between the carbonyl oxygens and amide hydrogens of the peptide backbone.

Question 14 of 19

Which of the following describes the orientation of R-groups in an alpha-helix?

Explanation: Side chains in an alpha-helix project outward from the central axis to minimize steric hindrance.

Question 15 of 19

What is the approximate distance between adjacent residues in an antiparallel beta-strand?

Explanation: The axial distance per residue in an extended antiparallel beta-strand is approximately 3.5 Å.

Question 16 of 19

Which region on a Ramachandran plot corresponds to right-handed alpha-helices?

Explanation: Right-handed alpha-helices cluster in the negative quadrant of the Ramachandran plot, specifically the top-left region with negative φ and ψ values.

Question 17 of 19

Explanation: Silk fibroin is rich in stacked antiparallel beta-pleated sheets providing high tensile strength.

Question 18 of 19

Explanation: The C-N peptide bond has restricted rotation due to resonance.

Question 19 of 19

Which structural motif features three intertwined polypeptide chains as seen in collagen?

Explanation: Collagen features a unique triple-helical conformation rich in glycine, proline, and hydroxyproline.
