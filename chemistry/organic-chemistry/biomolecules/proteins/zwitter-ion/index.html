Zwitter Ion

A zwitter ion is a neutral molecule containing both positive and negative electrical charges residing on different atoms within the same structure. This dipolar species arises from intramolecular proton transfer between acidic and basic functional groups.

UP PGT Chemistry Subject Code 02 B.Sc. / M.Sc. Level

Acid-Base Equilibrium and Structure

  • Amino acids exist predominantly as dipolar ions or zwitter ions (+H3N-R-COO-) in aqueous solution and solid state.
  • The carboxyl group acts as an internal acid (-COOH $\rightarrow$ -COO- + H+) donating a proton to the amino group acting as an internal base (-NH2 $\rightarrow$ -NH3+).
  • Infrared spectra of zwitter ions display characteristic carboxylate stretching frequencies (1550-1610 cm-1 and 1400-1425 cm-1) rather than free carboxylic acid peaks.
  • Zwitter ionic forms exhibit high melting points and large dipole moments due to strong intermolecular electrostatic crystal lattice forces.

Isoelectric Point and Electrophoretic Behavior

  • The isoelectric point (pI) is the specific pH at which the concentration of the zwitter ion is maximal and the net electrical charge is precisely zero.
  • At a pH below pI, the zwitter ion accepts a proton, converting into a positively charged cation (migrates toward the cathode in an electric field).
  • At a pH above pI, the zwitter ion loses a proton, converting into a negatively charged anion (migrates toward the anode in an electric field).
  • Zwitter ions have minimum solubility and zero electrophoretic mobility at their respective isoelectric points.

Solubility and Physical Properties

  • Because of their ionic character, amino acids and related zwitter ions are highly soluble in water but practically insoluble in non-polar organic solvents like ether or benzene.
  • Zwitter ionic structures impart high polarity to molecules, resulting in elevated boiling and melting points compared to corresponding structural isomers with covalent union.
  • Formaldehyde titration converts amino acid zwitter ions into methylol derivatives, releasing free protons to allow volumetric acid-base quantification.

⚠️ Important Exceptions & Anomalies
Exception Amino acids exist purely as neutral unionized molecules (R-CH(NH2)-COOH) in solution

Observation: They exist predominantly as zwitter ions (R-CH(NH3+)-COO-) — Intramolecular proton transfer is thermodynamically favored due to the high basicity of the amino group and acidity of the carboxyl group in water.

Exception Compounds with low molecular weight and simple functional groups are volatile liquids or low-melting solids

Observation: Amino acids are high-melting crystalline solids decomposing before melting — Strong electrostatic crystal lattice forces exist between the positive ammonium and negative carboxylate centers of adjacent zwitter ions.

Exception Aqueous solutions of neutral amino acids should exhibit a neutral pH of 7.0

Observation: Aqueous solutions of simple amino acids show a slight acidic pH (typically 5.5 to 6.5) — The acid dissociation constant (Ka1) of the protonated amino group is slightly greater than the base dissociation constant (Kb) of the carboxylate group.

💡 Shortcuts & Memory Aids
pI Calculation Rule
pI = (pKa1 + pKa2) / 2

Application: For neutral amino acids, simply average the two pKa values flanking the zwitterionic form to find the isoelectric point.

Migration Direction Mnemonic
Acidic: Cat (+) to Cathode | Basic: An (-) to Anode

Application: Remember that at pH < pI the species is a cation moving to cathode, and at pH > pI it is an anion moving to anode.

Solubility Rule of Thumb
Zwitter = Water soluble, Ether insoluble

Application: Use dipole-dipole/ionic hydration interactions to instantly predict insolubility in non-polar organic solvents.

🎯 Key Exam Points
  • Zwitter ions possess both cationic and anionic centers within the same neutral molecule.
  • The isoelectric point (pI) is defined as the pH where net electrical charge is zero.
  • Amino acids exhibit amphoteric behavior, reacting with both strong acids and strong bases.
  • In an electric field at pH = pI, amino acids show zero electrophoretic migration.
  • Infrared spectra confirm zwitter ionic structures through carboxylate asymmetric stretching near 1600 cm-1.
  • Solid-state structure determinations using X-ray crystallography confirm the zwitter ionic lattice.
🚫 Common Errors & Confusions
  • Assuming amino acids exist as uncharged molecules with free -NH2 and -COOH groups in aqueous solution.
  • Forgetting that at the isoelectric point, the molecule is electrically neutral but exists as a polar zwitterion, not an uncharged covalent isomer.
  • Confusing the pI calculation formula for acidic or basic amino acids with that of simple neutral monoamino monocarboxylic acids.
  • Assuming high lipid solubility for zwitter ionic biomolecules.

⚡ Quick Revision: Zwitter Ion

Core formulas, rapid recall points, and distinguishing criteria at a glance.

Dipolar Nature

Zwitter ions are internal salts formed via proton transfer from the carboxyl group to the amino group, generating a stable dipolar hybrid.

Amphoteric Behavior

The zwitter ionic species acts as an acid in the presence of base (accepting OH-) and as a base in the presence of acid (accepting H+).

Isoelectric Point

The isoelectric point is the characteristic pH where zwitter ion concentration peaks and net migration in an electric field ceases entirely.

Physical Manifestations

High melting points, crystal lattice stability, and aqueous solubility stem directly from internal electrostatic attractions.

Ammonium (R-NH3+)

🎯 Key Takeaways

  • pI = (pKa1 + pKa2) / 2 for simple amino acids
  • Zwitter ions dominate in water and solid phase
  • Net charge is zero at the isoelectric point
  • Electrophoretic mobility is zero at pI
  • High melting points due to ionic crystal lattice forces

📌 Quick Recall

  • Internal proton transfer yields zwitter ionic structures in amino acids.
  • Zwitter ions display amphoteric properties in acid-base reactions.
  • Solubility in water is high, while solubility in non-polar organic solvents is negligible.
  • Isoelectric point represents the state of minimum solubility and zero net charge.

⚖️ Key Differences

Uncharged Covalent Form VS Zwitter Ionic Form

The uncharged form lacks internal proton transfer (R-CH(NH2)COOH), whereas the zwitter ionic form has transferred a proton (R-CH(NH3+)COO-).

Question 1 of 18

Which of the following best defines a zwitter ion?

Explanation: A zwitter ion is a neutral compound carrying both positive and localized negative charges on different atoms.

Question 2 of 18

What is the net electrical charge of an amino acid at its isoelectric point (pI)?

Explanation: At the isoelectric point, the positive and negative charges balance out completely, resulting in a net charge of zero.

Question 3 of 18

How is the isoelectric point (pI) calculated for a simple monoamino monocarboxylic acid?

Explanation: The pI of a simple neutral amino acid is the arithmetic mean of the two pKa values associated with the zwitterionic equilibrium.

Question 4 of 18

In an electric field, what happens to an amino acid at a pH above its isoelectric point?

Explanation: At a pH above pI, the amino acid exists predominantly as an anion and migrates toward the positive electrode (anode).

Question 5 of 18

Why do amino acids have unusually high melting points compared to typical organic compounds of similar molecular weight?

Explanation: The ionic attractions between positive ammonium and negative carboxylate groups form a rigid crystal lattice requiring high thermal energy to disrupt.

Question 6 of 18

Which functional groups participate in internal proton transfer to form a zwitter ion in amino acids?

Explanation: The acidic carboxyl group (-COOH) donates a proton to the basic amino group (-NH2).

Question 7 of 18

What is the solubility behavior of amino acids in non-polar organic solvents like ether?

Explanation: Because of their internal ionic/zwitter ionic nature, amino acids have very low solubility in non-polar solvents.

Question 8 of 18

What type of behavior do zwitter ions exhibit when treated with acids or bases?

Explanation: Zwitter ions are amphoteric because they can react with acids (via the carboxylate group) and with bases (via the ammonium group).

Question 9 of 18

Which analytical technique is directly used to determine the absence of free -COOH groups in amino acids?

Explanation: Infrared spectroscopy reveals carboxylate (-COO-) stretching frequencies instead of free carboxylic acid carbonyl stretches.

Question 10 of 18

At a pH below the isoelectric point, an amino acid exists primarily as a:

Explanation: In acidic media (pH < pI), the carboxylate group is protonated, leaving a net positive charge on the ammonium group (cation).

Question 11 of 18

Which property is at its minimum value at the isoelectric point of an amino acid?

Explanation: At pI, precipitation is maximum (minimum solubility) and net migration under an electric field is zero.

Question 12 of 18

The formation of a zwitter ion involves which type of intramolecular process?

Explanation: It is an internal acid-base reaction involving the transfer of a proton from -COOH to -NH2.

Question 13 of 18

What is the predominant form of glycine in neutral water (pH 7)?

Explanation: At neutral pH, glycine exists almost entirely as the zwitter ion (+H3N-CH2-COO-).

Question 14 of 18

Which of the following pairs represents conjugate acid-base species in zwitter ion chemistry?

Explanation: The zwitter ion can accept a proton to form the cation, making them a conjugate acid-base pair.

Question 15 of 18

Glycine has pKa1 = 2.34 and pKa2 = 9.60. What is its approximate isoelectric point?

Explanation: pI = (2.34 + 9.60) / 2 = 11.94 / 2 = 5.97.

Question 16 of 18

Why is the pH of a pure aqueous solution of glycine slightly less than 7?

Explanation: The acidic dissociation constant of the ammonium group is slightly greater than the basic dissociation constant of the carboxylate group.

Question 17 of 18

What is the charge on a zwitter ion?

Explanation: A zwitter ion possesses equal and opposite charges, resulting in a net neutral (zero) charge.

Question 18 of 18

Which of the following compounds cannot form a stable zwitter ion easily?

Explanation: Ethanol lacks both a strongly acidic group and a strongly basic group to undergo internal proton transfer.

Which of the following is true regarding the dipole moment of zwitter ions?

Solution: Separation of full unit positive and negative charges across short bond distances produces very high dipole moments.
