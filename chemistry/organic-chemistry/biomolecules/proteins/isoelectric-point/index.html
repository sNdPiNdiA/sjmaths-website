Organic Chemistry

Isoelectric Point

The isoelectric point (pI) is the specific pH value at which a protein or amino acid carries zero net electrical charge in solution. At this pH, the molecule exhibits minimum solubility and fails to migrate under the influence of an electric field.

UP PGT Chemistry Subject Code 02 B.Sc. / M.Sc. Level

Acid-Base Behavior and Zwitterionic Form

  • Amino acids exist predominantly as zwitterions (dipolar ions) containing both positive (-NH3+) and negative (-COO-) centers.
  • In highly acidic solutions (low pH), amino acids exist as cations and migrate toward the cathode during electrophoresis.
  • In highly alkaline solutions (high pH), amino acids exist as anions and migrate toward the anode.
  • The isoelectric point (pI) represents the intermediate pH where the concentration of the zwitterion is maximal.

Mathematical Derivation for Amino Acids

  • For simple neutral amino acids like alanine, pI is the arithmetic mean of the α-carboxyl pKa1 and α-amino pKa2.
  • For acidic amino acids containing an extra carboxyl group, pI is calculated using the pKa of the carboxyl side chain and the α-carboxyl pKa1.
  • For basic amino acids containing an extra amino or guanidino group, pI is averaged using the pKa of the basic side chain and the α-amino pKa2.
  • Proteins compute their pI by averaging the pKa values of the two ionizable groups flanking the zwitterionic species state.

Physicochemical Properties at pI

  • Proteins exhibit minimum solubility at their isoelectric point due to the lack of net electrostatic repulsion between molecules.
  • Mobility in an electric field drops to zero at the pI, forming the foundational principle of isoelectric focusing (IEF).
  • Buffering capacity is typically minimal at the exact isoelectric point because the zwitterionic species predominates.
  • Precipitation or isoelectric precipitation is frequently utilized industrially to isolate specific proteins from complex mixtures.

⚠️ Important Exceptions & Anomalies
Exception Neutral amino acids have pI values around 6.0

Observation: Glycine and alanine have pI values near 6.0, but cysteine and tyrosine possess slightly different ranges due to thiol and phenolic side chain pKa contributions. — Ionizable side chains alter the macro-pKa equilibrium constants despite neutral classification.

Exception All amino acids migrate in an electric field

Observation: Amino acids remain stationary exclusively at pH = pI. — Net charge becomes identically zero, nullifying electrostatic force vectors.

Side-Chain Selection Rule for pI
Acidic: Mean of two lowest pKa; Basic: Mean of two highest pKa

Application: Immediately identify which two pKa values to average without drawing the titration curve.

Charge State Estimation

Neutral Amino Acid pI Estimate
pI ≈ (2.3 + 9.7) / 2 = 6.0

🎯 Key Exam Points
  • The isoelectric point is the pH at which net charge is zero.
  • Glycine has a pKa1 of 2.34 and pKa2 of 9.60, yielding a pI of 5.97.
  • At pH > pI, proteins carry a net negative charge and migrate to the anode.
  • At pH < pI, amino acids act as bases and accept protons.
  • Acidic amino acids (Aspartate, Glutamate) have pI values significantly below 7.
  • Basic amino acids (Lysine, Arginine, Histidine) have pI values significantly above 7.
🚫 Common Errors & Confusions
  • Confusing pKa of a specific group with the isoelectric point pI of the entire molecule.
  • Assuming all neutral amino acids have an exact pI of 7.0.
  • Forgetting to use the side-chain pKa instead of α-amino pKa for basic amino acids.
  • Assuming proteins are most soluble at their isoelectric point.

⚡ Quick Revision: Isoelectric Point

Core formulas, rapid recall points, and distinguishing criteria at a glance.

Zwitterion Equilibrium

Amino acids exist as dipolar ions where proton transfer from the carboxyl group to the amino group creates internal charge balance.

Mathematical Calculation

The pI is determined by averaging the two pKa values that bracket the electrically neutral zwitterionic form.

Solubility Minimum

Electrostatic repulsion is minimized at the pI, causing protein molecules to aggregate and precipitate out of solution.

Electrophoretic Behavior

Zero net charge at the isoelectric point halts migration in an applied electric field, forming the basis of analytical separation methods.

📚 Terms & Units Glossary

Isoelectric Point (pI)
pH at which net electrical charge is zero.
Zwitterion (Dipolar Ion)
Molecule with localized positive and negative charges summing to zero.
Electrophoresis (Migration Velocity)
Movement of charged particles in an electric field.
Acid Dissociation Constant (pKa)
Negative logarithm of the acid dissociation constant.
Isoelectric Precipitation (Protein Insolubility)
Method of separating proteins at their minimum solubility pH.
Cation (Positive Ion)
Positively charged ionic species migrating to the cathode.

🎯 Key Takeaways

  • pI for neutral amino acid = (pKa1 + pKa2) / 2
  • Net charge at pI = 0
  • pH < pI means positive charge
  • pH > pI means negative charge
  • Proteins exhibit minimum solubility at pI

📌 Quick Recall

  • pI is a characteristic constant for every amino acid and protein.
  • Determined entirely by flanking ionizable group pKa values.
  • Governs solubility and electrophoretic mobility.
  • Crucial parameter in biochemical separation techniques.

⚖️ Key Differences

Neutral pH (7.0) VS Isoelectric Point (pI)

Neutral pH is strictly 7.0 at 25°C, whereas the pI is a unique specific pH value unique to each amphoteric molecule.

Question 1 of 19

What is the net electrical charge of an amino acid at its isoelectric point?

Explanation: At the isoelectric point (pI), the concentration of the zwitterion is maximal, resulting in a net electrical charge of zero.

Question 2 of 19

How is the isoelectric point of a neutral amino acid like alanine calculated?

Explanation: For neutral amino acids, pI is the arithmetic mean of the carboxyl pKa1 and amino pKa2.

Question 3 of 19

In an electric field at a pH below its pI, a monoamino monocarboxylic amino acid will migrate toward which electrode?

Explanation: At a pH below its pI, the amino acid gains a net positive charge (cation) and migrates toward the negatively charged cathode.

Question 4 of 19

Which of the following describes the solubility of a protein at its isoelectric point?

Explanation: At the pI, electrostatic repulsion between protein molecules is at a minimum, leading to minimum solubility and potential precipitation.

Question 5 of 19

Which pKa values are averaged to find the pI of an acidic amino acid such as aspartic acid?

Explanation: For acidic amino acids, the zwitterion is formed between the two carboxyl groups and the amino group; thus, the two lowest pKa values (α-carboxyl and side-chain carboxyl) are averaged.

Question 6 of 19

Which of the following amino acids has a basic side chain and a high pI?

Explanation: Lysine possesses an extra amino group in its side chain, making it a basic amino acid with a high isoelectric point.

Question 7 of 19

What happens to the electrophoretic mobility of a protein when the buffer pH equals its pI?

Explanation: When pH = pI, net charge is zero, so the electrical force acting on the molecule is zero, halting electrophoretic migration.

Question 8 of 19

If an amino acid has a pI of 5.8, what is its net charge at pH 8.0?

Explanation: When the solution pH is higher than the pI (pH > pI), the amino acid loses protons and carries a net negative charge.

Question 9 of 19

Which term describes the dipolar ion form of an amino acid?

Explanation: A zwitterion is a neutral molecule possessing both positive and negative ionic functional groups.

Question 10 of 19

For a basic amino acid like lysine, which two pKa values are averaged to calculate its pI?

Explanation: The pI of basic amino acids is calculated by averaging the pKa of the α-amino group and the basic side chain (pKa(R)).

Question 11 of 19

At a pH well above the isoelectric point, an amino acid exists primarily as a:

Explanation: At high pH, protons are stripped from the amino acid, leaving it with a net negative charge (anion).

Question 12 of 19

Why is the buffering capacity of an amino acid lowest at its isoelectric point?

Explanation: Buffering requires conjugate acid-base pairs; at the exact pI, the conversion to the zwitterion minimizes the concentration of intermediate ions available to neutralize added strong acid or base.

Question 13 of 19

Which of the following amino acids has the lowest molecular weight and a pI of approximately 5.97?

Explanation: Glycine is the simplest and smallest amino acid, with pKa1 = 2.34 and pKa2 = 9.60, giving a pI of 5.97.

Question 14 of 19

In isoelectric focusing (IEF), proteins are separated based on differences in their:

Explanation: Isoelectric focusing utilizes a pH gradient to separate proteins based on their unique isoelectric point values.

Question 15 of 19

What is the primary factor determining the pI of a polypeptide chain compared to free amino acids?

Explanation: In polypeptides, terminal α-amino and α-carboxyl groups plus ionizable R-groups dictate the overall pI.

Question 16 of 19

If a protein has a pI of 4.5, what will happen if placed in a buffer of pH 2.0?

Explanation: At pH 2.0 (which is less than the pI of 4.5), the protein picks up protons, acquiring a net positive charge.

Question 17 of 19

Which functional group is primarily responsible for the positive charge of an amino acid at low pH?

Explanation: Protonation of the amino group to form -NH3+ imparts a positive charge to the molecule.

Question 18 of 19

Which functional group is primarily responsible for the negative charge of an amino acid at high pH?

Explanation: Deprotonation of the carboxyl group to form -COO- imparts a negative charge to the molecule.

Question 19 of 19

What is the primary reason acidic amino acids have lower pI values than basic amino acids?

Explanation: Extra carboxyl groups lower the pH required to neutralize the molecule, shifting the pI toward more acidic pH values.

Question 1 of 10
Calculate the approximate isoelectric point of alanine given pKa1 = 2.35 and pKa2 = 9.87.

Solution: pI = (2.35 + 9.87) / 2 = 12.22 / 2 = 6.11.

Question 2 of 10
Which of the following statements about the zwitterionic form of an amino acid is correct?

Solution: The zwitterion contains both -COO- and -NH3+ groups, resulting in internal charge balance and zero net charge.

Question 3 of 10
What is the net charge of glutamic acid (an acidic amino acid) at a pH significantly higher than its pI?

Solution: At a pH greater than its pI, all acidic and amino groups are deprotonated, imparting a net negative charge.

Question 4 of 10
Why do proteins exhibit minimum solubility at their isoelectric point?

Solution: Zero net charge removes repulsive forces between protein molecules, facilitating hydrophobic interactions and precipitation.

Question 5 of 10
Which ionizable groups are used to compute the pI of histidine?

Solution: Histidine is a basic amino acid; its neutral zwitterionic form lies between the protonated imidazole ring and the uncharged amino/carboxyl state, requiring the average of pKa2 and pKa(R).

Question 6 of 10
When an amino acid is titrated with a strong base starting from its fully protonated cationic form, the zwitterion forms at:

Solution: The zwitterion is the principal species present at the isoelectric point pH.

Question 7 of 10
Which amino acid has a side chain with a pKa(R) value that contributes to a pI below 3.0?

Solution: Aspartic acid has an acidic side-chain carboxyl group with a low pKa, resulting in a low pI (~2.77).

Question 8 of 10
In an experiment, two proteins with pI values of 6.0 and 8.5 are placed in a buffer at pH 6.0. What will be their behaviors?

Solution: At pH 6.0, the protein with pI 6.0 is at its isoelectric point (no migration), whereas the protein with pI 8.5 has a net positive charge (pH < pI) and migrates to the cathode.

Question 9 of 10
What is the primary experimental technique used to separate proteins based on their isoelectric points?

Solution: Isoelectric focusing (IEF) uses an immobilized pH gradient to separate proteins precisely at their respective pI values.

Question 10 of 10
Which factor causes the pI of amino acids to differ from one another?

Solution: The unique side chain (R-group) of each amino acid dictates its distinct set of pKa values and therefore its specific pI.
