Organic Chemistry

Glucose

Glucose is an aldohexose with the molecular formula C6H12O6, serving as the primary monomer for many complex carbohydrates. It exhibits optical isomerism and exists primarily in cyclic hemiacetal forms in equilibrium with its open-chain structure.

UP PGT Chemistry Subject Code 02 B.Sc. / M.Sc. Level

Structural Elucidation and Chemical Reactions

  • Reaction of glucose with hydroxylamine (NH2OH) yields an oxime, proving the presence of a carbonyl group (>C=O).
  • Oxidation of glucose with bromine water (mild oxidizing agent) produces gluconic acid, confirming the carbonyl is an aldehyde.
  • Treatment of glucose with nitric acid (HNO3) oxidizes both C1 and C6 to carboxylic acids, yielding saccharic acid (glucaric acid).
  • Reduction of glucose with sodium amalgam or catalytic hydrogenation yields sorbitol (glucitol), a hexahydric alcohol.

Stereochemistry and Cyclic Structures

  • Glucose contains four chiral centers (C2, C3, C4, C5), giving a total of 24 = 16 stereoisomers.
  • Natural glucose belongs to the D-series because the OH group on C5 is on the right in Fischer projection.
  • Crystallization of glucose yields two anomers: α-D-glucopyranose ([α]D = +112°") and β-D-glucopyranose ([α]D = +19°").
  • In aqueous solution, both anomers interconvert via the open-chain form to reach an equilibrium specific rotation of +52.5°, a process known as mutarotation.

Haworth Projections and Conformational Analysis

  • Cyclic hemiacetal formation involves intramolecular nucleophilic attack of the C5-OH oxygen on the C1 carbonyl carbon, forming a six-membered pyranose ring.
  • In the Haworth projection, substituents pointing right in the Fischer projection are directed downward.
  • The most stable conformation of β-D-glucopyranose is the ${}^4C_1$ chair conformation where all bulky substituents (OH and CH2OH groups) occupy equatorial positions.
  • Fructose and glucose form the exact same osazone derivative because carbons 3, 4, 5, and 6 have identical configurations and C1-C2 are converted to the phenylosazone moiety.

⚠️ Important Exceptions & Anomalies
Exception Aldehydes typically react with Schiff's reagent and form bisulfite addition compounds.

Observation: Glucose does not give Schiff's test and fails to form a crystalline bisulfite addition compound. — Glucose exists predominantly in stable cyclic hemiacetal ring structures where the free aldehyde concentration in solution is extremely low.

Exception Mutarotation is characteristic of all open-chain sugars with free carbonyl groups.

Observation: Glycosides (methyl glucosides) do not exhibit mutarotation in neutral or basic aqueous solutions. — The hemiacetal OH group is converted to an acetal (ether linkage), locking the ring in a non-reducing, fixed configuration.

💡 Shortcuts & Memory Aids
D- vs L- Configuration Rule
Fischer projection: OH on C5 RIGHT = D, LEFT = L

Application: Directly assign the D or L stereochemical descriptor without calculating absolute configurations (R/S).

α- vs β- Anomer Assignment in Haworth Projections
C1-OH DOWN = α, C1-OH UP = β (for D-aldohexoses)

Application: Instantly identify anomers from cyclic structures by observing the relative orientation of the anomeric hydroxyl group.

Mutarotation Equilibrium Calculation
[α]eq = (fraction α × [α]α) + (fraction β × [α]β)

Application: Predict the final specific rotation at equilibrium knowing the percentages of anomers (~36% α, ~64% β).

🎯 Key Exam Points
  • Glucose contains five hydroxyl groups (-OH) and one aldehyde group (-CHO).
  • Reaction with phenylhydrazine yields glucosazone in a 1:3 molar ratio.
  • Bromine water oxidizes glucose to gluconic acid without attacking the secondary alcohol groups.
  • Nitric acid oxidation of glucose yields dicarboxylic saccharic acid.
  • Mutarotation of glucose involves a change in specific rotation over time until equilibrium is attained.
  • Reduction with HI and red phosphorus produces n-hexane, confirming a continuous chain of six carbon atoms.
🚫 Common Errors & Confusions
  • Confusing anomers (α and β) with epimers (such as glucose and mannose).
  • Assuming glucose has a free open-chain aldehyde group in all chemical environments.
  • Forgetting that osazone formation involves carbons 1 and 2, rendering C3, C4, and C5 configuration identical.
  • Confusing glycosidic linkage formation (acetal) with hemiacetal ring closure.

⚡ Quick Revision: Glucose

Core formulas, rapid recall points, and distinguishing criteria at a glance.

Constitution and Carbon Backbone

Elemental analysis and molecular weight determinations establish the formula C6H12O6. Reduction with HI yields n-hexane, confirming a straight chain of six carbon atoms.

Functional Group Verification

Acetylation with acetic anhydride proves the presence of five hydroxyl groups. Oxidation reactions with bromine water and nitric acid verify the presence of one primary alcoholic group and one aldehyde group.

Stereoisomerism and Cyclic Hemiacetal

Intramolecular nucleophilic addition between C5-OH and C1=O creates a chiral center at C1, generating α and β anomers which undergo mutarotation in solution.

Conformational Preferences

Pyranose rings adopt chair conformations where β-D-glucopyranose is the most stable conformer due to minimal steric hindrance and all substituents in equatorial positions.

📚 Terms & Units Glossary

Anomers (C1 Epimers)
Diastereomers differing in configuration only at the hemiacetal carbon (C1).
Mutarotation (Spontaneous rotation change)
The change in specific rotation of an optically active solution over time.
Osazone (Bis-phenylhydrazone derivative)
Yellow crystalline derivative formed by reducing sugars with phenylhydrazine.
Glycoside (Acetal derivative)
Compound formed when the hemiacetal OH of a sugar reacts with an alcohol.
Epimers (C2/C3/C4 Diastereomers)
Stereoisomers that differ in configuration at only one specific chiral carbon.
Pyranose (6-membered cyclic form)
Cyclic monosaccharide containing a six-membered ring with five carbons and one oxygen.

🎯 Key Takeaways

  • Molecular formula: C6H12O6
  • Specific rotation of equilibrium glucose: +52.5°
  • Number of stereoisomers: 24 = 16
  • Oxidation product with Br2 water: Gluconic acid
  • Oxidation product with conc. HNO3: Saccharic (glucaric) acid

📌 Quick Recall

  • Glucose is an aldohexose with five -OH groups and one aldehyde group.
  • It exists predominantly in cyclic pyranose hemiacetal forms (α and β anomers).
  • Mutarotation is the interconversion of α and β anomers through the open-chain form.
  • β-D-glucopyranose is the most stable aldohexose conformation due to equatorial substituents.

⚖️ Key Differences

Anomers VS Epimers

Anomers specifically differ at the anomeric carbon (C1), whereas epimers can differ at any chiral center (e.g., C2 in mannose vs glucose).

Question 1 of 18

What is the product formed when glucose is oxidized with bromine water?

Explanation: Bromine water is a mild oxidizing agent that selectively oxidizes the aldehyde group of glucose to a carboxylic acid, yielding gluconic acid.

Question 2 of 18

How many chiral carbon atoms are present in an open-chain D-glucose molecule?

Explanation: Open-chain glucose (CH2OH-(CHOH)4-CHO) contains four chiral carbon atoms (C2, C3, C4, and C5).

Question 3 of 18

Which reagent converts glucose into n-hexane upon prolonged heating?

Explanation: Heating glucose with concentrated hydriodic acid (HI) and red phosphorus reduces all hydroxyl and carbonyl groups to methylene groups, yielding n-hexane.

Question 4 of 18

What is the phenomenon called when the specific rotation of an aqueous solution of glucose changes over time to reach a constant value?

Explanation: Mutarotation is the spontaneous change in specific rotation observed when an anomer of a sugar is dissolved in water until equilibrium with the other anomer is attained.

Question 5 of 18

Which of the following derivatives is formed when glucose reacts with an excess of phenylhydrazine?

Explanation: Glucose reacts with three molecules of phenylhydrazine to form a yellow crystalline derivative called glucosazone.

Question 6 of 18

Which carbon atom becomes the anomeric carbon when open-chain glucose cyclizes to form a pyranose ring?

Explanation: The carbonyl carbon (C1) of the aldehyde group reacts with the hydroxyl group at C5 to form a hemiacetal, becoming the chiral anomeric carbon (C1).

Question 7 of 18

What is the stable conformation of β-D-glucopyranose?

Explanation: The chair conformation with all bulky groups in equatorial positions (${}^4C_1$ conformation for β-D-glucose) represents the energy minimum.

Question 8 of 18

Glucose and fructose form the same osazone because their configurations differ only at which carbon atoms?

Explanation: Osazone formation involves carbons C1 and C2. Since glucose and fructose differ only at these two positions while C3-C6 are identical, they yield the identical osazone.

Question 9 of 18

Which oxidizing agent converts glucose into a dicarboxylic acid (saccharic acid)?

Explanation: Concentrated nitric acid is a strong oxidizing agent that oxidizes both the terminal aldehyde group (C1) and the primary alcohol group (C6) of glucose to carboxylic acid groups.

Question 10 of 18

What is the role of acetic anhydride in the reaction with glucose?

Explanation: Reaction of glucose with acetic anhydride forms glucose pentaacetate, establishing the presence of five hydroxyl groups in the molecule.

Question 11 of 18

What is the specific rotation of pure α-D-glucopyranose?

Explanation: Pure α-D-glucopyranose exhibits a specific optical rotation of +112° in aqueous solution before mutarotation.

Question 12 of 18

Which of the following is true regarding D-glucose and L-glucose?

Explanation: D-glucose and L-glucose are non-superimposable mirror images of each other, making them enantiomers.

Question 13 of 18

Why does glucose fail to give Schiff's test for aldehydes?

Explanation: The concentration of the free open-chain aldehyde form in aqueous equilibrium is too low to react with Schiff's reagent.

Question 14 of 18

Reduction of glucose with sodium amalgam (Na/Hg) and water yields:

Explanation: Sodium amalgam reduces the aldehyde carbonyl group of glucose to a primary alcohol, yielding sorbitol (glucitol).

Question 15 of 18

Epimers of glucose differ in configuration at:

Explanation: Epimers are diastereomers that differ in configuration at only one chiral carbon atom. (For example, mannose is a C2 epimer and galactose is a C4 epimer of glucose).

Question 16 of 18

What type of linkage joins monosaccharide units in complex carbohydrates?

Explanation: Monosaccharides are linked together through ether bonds known as glycosidic linkages.

Question 17 of 18

Which of the following sugars is a non-reducing sugar?

Explanation: Sucrose is a non-reducing disaccharide because the reducing carbonyl carbons of both glucose and fructose are involved in the glycosidic bond.

Question 18 of 18

In the Haworth projection of α-D-glucopyranose, the hydroxyl group at C1 is directed:

Explanation: For D-aldohexoses, the α-anomer has the anomeric hydroxyl group directed downward in the Haworth projection.

Question 1 of 10
What is the final equilibrium specific rotation of an aqueous solution of glucose?

Solution: At equilibrium, glucose solution contains ~36% α-anomer (+112°) and ~64% β-anomer (+19°), resulting in an equilibrium specific rotation of +52.5°.

Question 2 of 10
Which of the following compounds has the same osazone derivative as D-glucose?

Solution: D-Fructose has identical stereochemistry at C3, C4, and C5 as D-glucose, yielding the exact same osazone.

Question 3 of 10
The conversion of α-D-glucose to β-D-glucose in solution is known as:

Solution: The interconversion of anomers via the open-chain form resulting in a change of optical rotation is called mutarotation.

Question 4 of 10
Heating glucose with HI and red P gives:

Solution: Reduction with HI and red P reduces all oxygen-containing functional groups to yield n-hexane.

Question 5 of 10
How many stereoisomers are theoretically possible for an aldohexose such as glucose?

Solution: With 4 chiral centers, the number of stereoisomers is 24 = 16.

Question 6 of 10
Which functional group is NOT present in the open-chain structure of glucose?

Solution: Glucose is an aldohexose containing an aldehyde group, one primary alcohol, and four secondary alcohols, but no ketone group.

Question 7 of 10
What is formed when glucose is reduced with sodium borohydride (NaBH4)?

Solution: Reduction of the carbonyl group in glucose with NaBH4 yields sorbitol (glucitol).

Question 8 of 10
In the Haworth projection of β-D-glucopyranose, the -CH2OH group at C5 is oriented:

Solution: In standard D-aldohexopyranose Haworth projections, the C6 -CH2OH group is projected upward.

Question 9 of 10
Which of the following test reagents is reduced by glucose?

Solution: Glucose is a reducing sugar and reduces Tollens', Fehling's, and Benedict's reagents.

Question 10 of 10
What term describes stereoisomers that differ in configuration at C2?

Solution: Epimers are diastereomers differing at a single carbon center. Specifically, isomers differing at C2 are C2-epimers (e.g., D-mannose).
