Preparation of Glucose

Glucose is an aldohexose industrially prepared via the acid-catalyzed hydrolysis of sucrose or starch. It serves as the primary monomeric unit of major structural and storage polysaccharides.

UP PGT Chemistry Subject Code 02 B.Sc. / M.Sc. Level

Laboratory and Industrial Preparation from Sucrose

  • Sucrose is dissolved in ethanol and hydrolyzed by boiling with dilute hydrochloric acid or sulfuric acid.
  • During hydrolysis, the glycosidic linkage between C1 of glucose and C2 of fructose is cleaved.
  • Upon cooling, glucose crystallizes out first due to its lower solubility in ethanol-water mixtures compared to fructose.
  • The reaction changes the optical rotation from dextrorotatory (+66.5°) to laevorotatory (-39.9°), a process known as the inversion of cane sugar.

Commercial Manufacture from Starch

  • Starch is obtained from corn, potatoes, or rice and serves as the cheap, primary raw material for commercial glucose production.
  • Starch slurry is heated with dilute H2SO4 at 393 K under 2 to 3 atmospheres pressure in an autoclave.
  • The polysaccharide chain is progressively cleaved into smaller dextrins and ultimately into D-glucose.
  • Excess sulfuric acid is neutralized by adding calcium carbonate (chalk), precipitating calcium sulfate as an insoluble solid filtered off.

Purification and Isolation of Glucose

  • The filtrate containing glucose is treated with animal charcoal to decolorize the brown syrup by adsorption of colored impurities.
  • The decolorized solution is concentrated under reduced pressure to prevent thermal decomposition or caramelization.
  • Concentrated syrup is seeded with pure glucose crystals to induce rapid crystallization yielding white crystalline D(+)-glucose.
  • Pure glucose melts at 146°C and exhibits the property of mutarotation in aqueous solution.

⚠️ Important Exceptions & Anomalies
Exception Aldehydes typically form bisulfite addition compounds and give positive Schiff's test.

Observation: Glucose does not form a sodium bisulfite addition compound and gives a negative Schiff's test. — Glucose predominantly exists in a stable cyclic hemiacetal pyranose structure where the free aldehyde group is masked.

Exception Pentasubstituted derivatives of monosaccharides typically yield open-chain configurations upon acetylation.

Observation: Glucose pentaacetate does not react with hydroxylamine (NH2OH) to form an oxime. — The cyclic hemiacetal oxygen lacks a free carbonyl group in the pentaacetate derivative, confirming the cyclic pyranose ring closure.

Exception Monosaccharides with primary and secondary alcohol groups undergo selective oxidation.

Observation: Nitric acid oxidation of glucose yields dicarboxylic acid (saccharic acid) without cleaving the carbon skeleton. — HNO3 simultaneously oxidizes the terminal primary alcohol (C6) and the aldehydic/hemiacetal carbon (C1) to carboxylic groups.

🎯 Key Exam Points
  • Industrial preparation of glucose uses cheap starch as the starting material.
  • Starch hydrolysis is conducted with dilute H2SO4 at 393 K under 2-3 atm pressure.
  • Sucrose hydrolysis yields an equimolar mixture of D-glucose and D-fructose in alcoholic solution.
  • Calcium carbonate is added to neutralize excess sulfuric acid, precipitating calcium sulfate.
  • Animal charcoal is used for decolorizing the glucose solution during purification.
  • Glucose exhibits mutarotation with an equilibrium specific rotation of +52.5°.
🚫 Common Errors & Confusions
  • Assuming starch requires no pressure for hydrolysis; high pressure (2-3 atm) and 393 K are mandatory.
  • Forgetting that sucrose hydrolysis produces fructose alongside glucose.
  • Confusing the role of animal charcoal (adsorption of colored impurities) with chemical reaction.
  • Assuming glucose gives a positive Schiff's test despite possessing an aldehyde function in its open-chain form.

⚡ Quick Revision: Preparation of Glucose

Core formulas, rapid recall points, and distinguishing criteria at a glance.

Sucrose Hydrolysis

Acid-catalyzed cleavage of the glycosidic linkage in sucrose yields equimolar D-glucose and D-fructose.

Starch Depolymerization

Polysaccharide starch breaks down under hydrothermal acid catalysis to yield high purity D-glucose.

Purification Steps

Neutralization with CaCO3 followed by charcoal decolorization isolates pure crystalline glucose.

Cyclic Equilibrium

Glucose exists primarily in cyclic pyranose forms exhibiting mutarotation in aqueous solution.

📚 Terms & Units Glossary

Mutarotation ([α])
Spontaneous change in specific rotation of an optically active solution.
Inversion of Sugar (Sucrose → G + F)
Change of optical rotation sign from dextro to laevo during hydrolysis.
Glycosidic Linkage (C-O-C ether bond)
Covalent bond joining carbohydrate monosaccharide units.
Pyranose (6-membered ring)
Cyclic hemiacetal structure containing 5 carbon atoms and 1 oxygen atom.
Glucosazone (Osazone derivative)
Yellow crystalline derivative formed with excess phenylhydrazine.
Saccharic Acid (HOOC-(CHOH)4-COOH)
Dicarboxylic acid formed by HNO3 oxidation of glucose.

🎯 Key Takeaways

  • Starch formula: (C6H10O5)n
  • Sucrose formula: C12H22O11
  • Glucose formula: C6H12O6
  • Starch hydrolysis conditions: 393 K, 2-3 atm, dilute H2SO4
  • Equilibrium specific rotation of glucose: +52.5°

📌 Quick Recall

  • Glucose is manufactured from starch via acid hydrolysis.
  • Sucrose gives an equimolar mixture of glucose and fructose.
  • Purification involves neutralization with CaCO3 and charcoal treatment.
  • Glucose exhibits mutarotation between alpha and beta anomers.

⚖️ Key Differences

Alpha-D-Glucose VS Beta-D-Glucose

Differ in configuration only at the C1 asymmetric carbon (anomeric carbon).

Which reagent is used for the commercial preparation of glucose from starch?

Question 2 of 19

Hydrolysis of sucrose yields which of the following?

Explanation: Sucrose on hydrolysis yields one molecule of D-glucose and one molecule of D-fructose.

Question 3 of 19

Why is calcium carbonate added during the preparation of glucose from starch?

Explanation: Calcium carbonate neutralizes the remaining sulfuric acid, precipitating calcium sulfate as an insoluble salt.

Question 4 of 19

What is the optical rotation change observed during the inversion of cane sugar?

Explanation: Sucrose is dextrorotatory (+66.5°), but the mixture becomes laevorotatory (-39.9°) because fructose has a stronger laevo rotation than glucose's dextro rotation.

Question 5 of 19

Which chemical is used to decolorize the glucose solution during purification?

Explanation: Animal charcoal is employed to adsorb colored impurities from the glucose syrup.

Question 6 of 19

What is the molecular formula of glucose?

Explanation: Glucose is a monosaccharide with the molecular formula C6H12O6.

Question 7 of 19

Which of the following describes the structure of glucose in its crystalline state?

Explanation: In the solid crystalline state, D-glucose exists as a stable six-membered cyclic hemiacetal known as pyranose.

Question 8 of 19

Mutarotation of glucose involves:

Explanation: Mutarotation is the spontaneous change in the specific rotation of an aqueous solution of a chiral compound over time until a constant equilibrium value is reached.

Question 9 of 19

What happens when glucose is heated with excess phenylhydrazine?

Explanation: Reaction with excess phenylhydrazine at high temperature converts glucose into glucosazone crystals.

Question 10 of 19

Which carbon atom is the anomeric carbon in D-glucopyranose?

Explanation: C1 is the hemiacetal carbon (anomeric carbon) formed by ring closure in aldoses.

Question 11 of 19

Starch belongs to which class of carbohydrates?

Explanation: Starch is a high molecular weight polysaccharide composed of numerous glucose units.

Question 12 of 19

The specific rotation of pure α-D-glucose is:

Explanation: Pure α-D-glucose exhibits a specific rotation of +112° which changes to +52.5° at equilibrium.

Question 13 of 19

Which solvent is typically used to dissolve sucrose before laboratory hydrolysis?

Explanation: Sucrose is dissolved in alcoholic solution (aqueous ethanol) during laboratory preparation of glucose so that glucose crystallizes out preferentially.

Question 14 of 19

The conversion of starch into glucose is an example of:

Explanation: Adding water across glycosidic bonds with acid catalysis is a hydrolysis reaction.

Question 15 of 19

Which of the following does not reduce Fehling's solution?

Explanation: Sucrose is a non-reducing sugar because its glycosidic bond involves both anomeric carbons (C1 of glucose and C2 of fructose).

Question 16 of 19

Glucose gives a positive test with which of the following reagents?

Explanation: Glucose reduces Tollens' reagent (silver mirror test) due to the presence of free or easily accessible carbonyl function in solution.

Question 17 of 19

What is the product of bromine water oxidation of glucose?

Explanation: Mild oxidizing agent bromine water oxidizes the aldehydic group (C1) of glucose to carboxylic acid, forming gluconic acid.

Question 18 of 19

Concentrated nitric acid oxidation of glucose yields:

Explanation: Strong oxidizing agent HNO3 oxidizes both C1 and C6 carbons of glucose to form saccharic acid.

Question 19 of 19

Reduction of glucose with sodium amalgam (Na/Hg) yields:

Explanation: Reduction of the carbonyl group of glucose yields the hexahydric alcohol sorbitol.
