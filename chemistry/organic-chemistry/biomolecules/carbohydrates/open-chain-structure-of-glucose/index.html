Open-Chain Structure of Glucose

Glucose is an aldohexose possessing a straight-chain carbon skeleton with one terminal aldehyde group and five secondary hydroxyl groups. Its open-chain constitution is established through reduction, oxidation, and cyanohydrin formation reactions.

UP PGT Chemistry Subject Code 02 B.Sc. / M.Sc. Level

Carbon Skeleton and Functional Group Verification

  • Reduction of glucose with concentrated HI and red phosphorus yields n-hexane, confirming a continuous chain of six carbon atoms.
  • Reaction of glucose with hydroxylamine (NH2OH) produces an oxime, proving the presence of a carbonyl group.
  • Reaction with hydrogen cyanide (HCN) yields a cyanohydrin, confirming the presence of a C=O double bond in the open-chain form.

Hydroxyl Group Enumeration and Oxidation Behavior

  • Acetylation of glucose with acetic anhydride yields a pentaacetate derivative, proving the exact presence of five hydroxyl (-OH) groups.
  • Mild oxidation of glucose using bromine water (Br2/H2O) converts the aldehyde function into a gluconic acid monocarboxylic acid without cleaving the carbon chain.
  • Strong oxidation with nitric acid (HNO3) oxidizes both the terminal aldehyde and primary alcohol groups to yield a dicarboxylic acid known as saccharic acid (glucaric acid).

Stereochemical Configuration and D/L Designation

  • Glucose contains four chiral carbon atoms (C-2, C-3, C-4, and C-5), yielding a maximum of 24 = 16 stereoisomers.
  • The D-configuration of glucose is assigned by correlating the lowest chiral center (C-5) with the configuration of (+)-glyceraldehyde.
  • The open-chain Fischer projection places the aldehyde group at C-1 (top) and the primary alcohol at C-6 (bottom).

⚠️ Important Exceptions & Anomalies
Exception Aldehydes normally undergo rapid Schiff's test and form sodium bisulfite addition compounds.

Observation: Glucose fails to give Schiff's test and does not form a crystalline bisulfite addition compound. — Glucose predominantly exists in cyclic hemiacetal forms (pyranose ring) in equilibrium, keeping the free open-chain aldehyde concentration extremely low.

Exception Carbonyl compounds typically show a strong >C=O stretching infrared absorption band near 1725 cm-1.

Observation: Solid-state IR spectra of glucose show negligible free carbonyl absorption. — Intramolecular cyclization between C-1 aldehyde and C-5 hydroxyl groups forms a stable six-membered cyclic hemiacetal.

🎯 Key Exam Points
  • Glucose is a naturally occurring aldohexose also known as dextrose or grape sugar.
  • Reaction with hydroxylamine confirms the presence of a carbonyl group via oxime formation.
  • Acetylation with acetic anhydride gives glucose pentaacetate, confirming five -OH groups.
  • Oxidation with Br2 water yields gluconic acid, confirming the aldehyde is terminal.
  • Reduction with HI and red phosphorus yields n-hexane, confirming a straight-chain carbon skeleton.
  • Nitric acid oxidation converts glucose into saccharic acid (glucaric acid).
🚫 Common Errors & Confusions
  • Assuming glucose gives a positive Schiff test just because it contains an aldehyde group.
  • Confusing gluconic acid (monocarboxylic) with saccharic acid (dicarboxylic).
  • Assuming the open-chain form is the dominant species in aqueous solution.
  • Incorrectly counting the number of chiral centers as five instead of four.

⚡ Quick Revision: Open-Chain Structure of Glucose

Core formulas, rapid recall points, and distinguishing criteria at a glance.

Straight-Chain Proof

Heating glucose with concentrated HI reduces all hydroxyl groups to hydrogens, yielding n-hexane and proving a continuous six-carbon chain.

Carbonyl Identification

Formation of an oxime with hydroxylamine and a cyanohydrin with HCN confirms the presence of a carbonyl group within the open-chain structure.

Hydroxyl Group Count

Reaction with acetic anhydride yields a pentaacetate derivative, demonstrating the presence of five distinct hydroxyl groups in the molecule.

Oxidation Characteristics

Bromine water selectively oxidizes the aldehyde group to a carboxylic acid, whereas nitric acid oxidizes both terminal ends to form saccharic acid.

📚 Terms & Units Glossary

Aldohexose (C6H12O6)
Monosaccharide containing six carbon atoms and a terminal aldehyde group.
Gluconic Acid (HOCH2(CHOH)4COOH)
Monocarboxylic acid obtained by mild oxidation of glucose.
Saccharic Acid (COOH(CHOH)4COOH)
Dicarboxylic acid obtained by strong oxidation of glucose with nitric acid.
Cyanohydrin (R-CH(OH)-CN)
Addition product formed by reaction of glucose with hydrogen cyanide.
Oxime (R-CH=N-OH)
Derivative formed by reaction of glucose with hydroxylamine.
Pentaacetate (CH3COOCH2(CHOH)4CHO)
Ester derivative confirming five hydroxyl groups in glucose.

🎯 Key Takeaways

  • Molecular formula: C6H12O6
  • HI reduction yields n-hexane
  • Acetic anhydride gives pentaacetate
  • Bromine water gives gluconic acid
  • Nitric acid gives saccharic acid

📌 Quick Recall

  • Open-chain structure features one aldehyde and five hydroxyl groups.
  • Four chiral centers generate 16 stereoisomers.
  • Cyclic hemiacetal formation explains the absence of standard free aldehyde reactions.
  • D-configuration is assigned based on C-5 correlation with D-glyceraldehyde.

⚖️ Key Differences

Gluconic Acid VS Saccharic Acid

Gluconic acid is a monocarboxylic acid formed by Br2 oxidation; saccharic acid is a dicarboxylic acid formed by HNO3 oxidation.
