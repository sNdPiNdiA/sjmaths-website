Electrophilic Aromatic Substitution

Electrophilic aromatic substitution involves the replacement of a hydrogen atom on an aromatic ring by an electrophile via a two-step mechanism. The process proceeds through the formation of a resonance-stabilized carbocation intermediate known as the sigma complex or Wheland intermediate.

UP PGT Chemistry Subject Code 02 B.Sc. / M.Sc. Level

Reaction Mechanism and Wheland Intermediate

  • The reaction mechanism proceeds via two distinct steps: electrophilic attack forming the sigma complex, followed by proton loss restoring aromaticity.
  • The Wheland intermediate (sigma complex) loses aromaticity temporarily, which accounts for the high activation energy of the first step.
  • Energy profile diagrams show two transition states separated by a high-energy intermediate, with the first transition state being rate-determining.
  • Kinetic isotope effects are typically negligible (KIE ≈ 1) because C-H bond cleavage occurs in the fast, second step of the sequence.

Directing Effects and Substituent Activation

  • Activating groups such as -OH, -NH2, and alkyl groups increase electron density via resonance or hyperconjugation, directing incoming electrophiles to ortho/para positions.
  • Deactivating groups such as -NO2, -CN, and - carbonyls withdraw electron density inductively and by resonance, directing incoming electrophiles to meta positions.
  • Halogens are deactivating yet ortho/para-directing due to the dominant inductive electron withdrawal counterbalanced by lone-pair resonance donation.
  • Steric hindrance in ortho-substitution can limit yields when bulky alkyl groups or large electrophiles are employed.

Named Electrophilic Aromatic Substitutions

  • Nitration uses concentrated nitric and sulfuric acids to generate the nitronium ion (NO2+) electrophile.
  • Sulfonation uses fuming sulfuric acid (H2SO4/SO3) and is a reversible reaction useful for temporary blocking groups.
  • Friedel-Crafts alkylation attaches alkyl chains using alkyl halides and Lewis acids, but suffers from carbocation rearrangements and polyalkylation.
  • Friedel-Crafts acylation introduces acyl groups via acylium ions stabilized by resonance, preventing carbocation rearrangements and preventing over-acylation.

⚠️ Important Exceptions & Anomalies
Exception Friedel-Crafts alkylation works well with primary alkyl halides.

Observation: Primary alkyl halides undergo rearrangement via hydride or alkyl shifts. — Carbocation intermediates rearrange to form more stable secondary or tertiary carbocations before attacking the aromatic ring.

Exception Friedel-Crafts reactions succeed on all substituted benzenes.

Observation: Strongly deactivated rings (e.g., nitrobenzene) do not undergo Friedel-Crafts reactions. — The ring electron density is too low to coordinate with the Lewis acid-alkyl halide complex.

Exception Aniline undergoes smooth nitration and halogenation under standard conditions.

Observation: Direct nitration yields extensive oxidation and tarry products, while halogenation yields 2,4,6-tribromoaniline instantaneously. — The -NH2 group is extremely strongly activating; amino group protection via acetylation is required for mono-substitution.

💡 Shortcuts & Memory Aids
DIRECT Rule for Orientation
All single-bonded heteroatoms with lone pairs = ortho/para directors

Application: Quickly identify directing groups by checking if the atom directly attached to the ring has lone pairs (activators) or multiple bonds to electronegative atoms (deactivators).

Friedel-Crafts Acylation over Alkylation
Acylation + Clemmensen/Wolff-Kishner Reduction = Straight-chain Alkylbenzene

Application: Avoid unwanted carbocation rearrangements by using acyl substitution followed by carbonyl reduction to synthesize pure primary alkylbenzenes.

Blocking Group Strategy
Sulfonation is reversible (ArSO3H + H2O + H+ → ArH + H2SO4)

Application: Use a sulfonic acid group to block the para position temporarily, direct substitution to the ortho position, and then remove the sulfonic group thermally.

🎯 Key Exam Points
  • The electrophile in nitration is the nitronium ion (NO2+).
  • Friedel-Crafts alkylation of benzene with 1-chloropropane yields isopropylbenzene as the major product due to carbocation rearrangement.
  • Halogens are deactivating because of electron withdrawal via induction, yet direct ortho/para due to pi-donation.
  • Sulfonation of naphthalene yields the 1-sulfonic acid at low temperature (kinetic product) and 2-sulfonic acid at high temperature (thermodynamic product).
  • Aniline must be protected via acetylation (acetanilide formation) before monobromination or mononitration.
  • Gattermann-Koch reaction uses CO and HCl with AlCl3/CuCl to form benzaldehyde from benzene.
🚫 Common Errors & Confusions
  • Assuming Friedel-Crafts alkylation gives unbranched chains when using primary alkyl halides.
  • Forgetting that aniline undergoes oxidation and tar formation during direct nitration.
  • Treating sulfonation as irreversible under all temperature conditions.
  • Classifying halogens as activating groups just because they direct ortho/para.

⚡ Quick Revision: Electrophilic Aromatic Substitution

Core formulas, rapid recall points, and distinguishing criteria at a glance.

Mechanism of Electrophilic Substitution

Electrophilic aromatic substitution proceeds via attack of an electrophile on the pi electron cloud, generating a resonance-stabilized carbocation intermediate (Wheland intermediate). Deprotonation restores aromaticity.

Substituent Effects on Reactivity

Activating substituents increase ring electron density, lowering activation energy and accelerating reaction rates. Deactivating substituents withdraw electron density, raising activation energy and retarding substitution.

Directing Effects

Electron-donating groups and halogens direct incoming electrophiles to ortho and para positions. Electron-withdrawing groups without lone pairs direct substitution exclusively to the meta position.

Friedel-Crafts Limitations

Friedel-Crafts alkylations are limited by carbocation rearrangements and polyalkylation. Acylation avoids these issues through resonance stabilization of the acylium ion intermediate.

📚 Terms & Units Glossary

Wheland Intermediate (Sigma Complex)
A resonance-stabilized non-aromatic carbocation intermediate formed during electrophilic aromatic substitution.
Activating Group (Electron-Donating Group)
Substituent that increases the rate of electrophilic aromatic substitution compared to benzene.
Deactivating Group (Electron-Withdrawing Group)
Substituent that decreases the rate of electrophilic aromatic substitution compared to benzene.
Nitration (Ar-NO2 Formation)
Introduction of a nitro group using nitric and sulfuric acids.
Sulfonation (Ar-SO3H Formation)
Introduction of a sulfonic acid group using fuming sulfuric acid.
Acylation (Ar-CO-R Formation)
Introduction of an acyl group using an acyl chloride and a Lewis acid catalyst.

🎯 Key Takeaways

  • Nitronium ion (NO2+) is the active electrophile in nitration.
  • Acylium ion (R-C+=O) does not rearrange during Friedel-Crafts acylation.
  • Halogens deactivate the ring but direct ortho/para.
  • Primary alkyl halides undergo rearrangement in Friedel-Crafts alkylation.
  • Sulfonation is a reversible reaction.

📌 Quick Recall

  • Electrophilic aromatic substitution occurs in two steps: addition and elimination.
  • Substituents control both the reaction rate and the regioselectivity.
  • Friedel-Crafts reactions require anhydrous Lewis acid catalysts like AlCl3.
  • Resonance structures of the sigma complex explain ortho/para versus meta directing effects.

⚖️ Key Differences

Inductive Effect of Halogens VS Resonance Effect of Halogens

Inductive effect withdraws electron density (deactivating), while resonance donation directs incoming electrophiles to ortho/para positions.

Question 1 of 19

What is the active electrophile in aromatic nitration?

Explanation: Nitric acid is protonated by sulfuric acid and loses water to generate the nitronium ion (NO2+).

Question 2 of 19

Which of the following groups is an activator and ortho/para-director?

Explanation: The methoxy group (-OCH3) donates electrons through resonance via its oxygen lone pairs, activating the ring for ortho/para substitution.

Question 3 of 19

What is the major product when benzene reacts with 1-chloropropane in the presence of AlCl3?

Explanation: The primary propyl carbocation rearranges via a 1,2-hydride shift to form the more stable secondary isopropyl carbocation before attacking benzene.

Question 4 of 19

Which reaction avoids carbocation rearrangements during ring alkylation?

Explanation: Friedel-Crafts acylation forms a resonance-stabilized acylium ion that does not rearrange; subsequent reduction yields the unbranched alkyl chain.

Question 5 of 19

Why are halogens ortho/para-directing despite being deactivating?

Explanation: Halogens withdraw electron density inductively across the sigma bond, but donate electron density via pi resonance, which stabilizes the positive charge at ortho and para sigma-complex intermediates.

Question 6 of 19

Which of the following is a meta-directing group?

Explanation: The acetyl group (-COCH3) contains a carbonyl pi bond conjugated with the ring, withdrawing electron density inductively and by resonance, directing meta.

Question 7 of 19

In the sulfonation of benzene, what is the actual sulfonating agent when using fuming sulfuric acid?

Explanation: Sulfur trioxide (SO3) is a powerful electrophile present in fuming sulfuric acid that attacks the aromatic ring.

Question 8 of 19

What is the role of AlCl3 in Friedel-Crafts reactions?

Explanation: AlCl3 acts as a Lewis acid, accepting a chloride ion from alkyl or acyl halides to generate the active carbocation or acylium ion electrophile.

Question 9 of 19

Which of the following rings is most reactive toward electrophilic aromatic substitution?

Explanation: Toluene possesses a methyl group which is electron-donating via hyperconjugation and inductive effect, activating the ring compared to benzene.

Question 10 of 19

Why does direct nitration of aniline fail to give a good yield of p-nitroaniline?

Explanation: Strong acidic nitration conditions protonate aniline to form the -NH3+ group, which is strongly deactivating and meta-directing, while also causing oxidation.

Question 11 of 19

Which intermediate is common to all electrophilic aromatic substitution reactions?

Explanation: All electrophilic aromatic substitution pathways proceed through the formation of a resonance-stabilized sigma complex (Wheland intermediate).

Question 12 of 19

What happens to the rate of electrophilic substitution when a strong deactivating group is present on the ring?

Explanation: Strong deactivating groups deplete ring electron density, increasing the activation energy and slowing down electrophilic attack.

Question 13 of 19

Which isomer is usually the major product in electrophilic substitution of monosubstituted benzenes due to steric factors?

Explanation: Para isomers are typically formed in higher yields than ortho isomers due to steric hindrance at the ortho positions.

Question 14 of 19

What is the product of the reaction between benzene and acetyl chloride in the presence of anhydrous AlCl3?

Explanation: Friedel-Crafts acylation of benzene with acetyl chloride yields acetophenone (phenyl methyl ketone).

Question 15 of 19

Which of the following compounds cannot undergo Friedel-Crafts alkylation?

Explanation: Nitrobenzene is strongly deactivated by the nitro group and does not react with alkyl halides and AlCl3.

Question 16 of 19

What is the kinetic isotope effect (kH/kD) for typical electrophilic aromatic substitutions?

Explanation: Since C-H bond cleavage occurs after the rate-determining step, isotopic substitution does not affect the overall reaction rate.

Question 17 of 19

Which electrophile is generated in the Gattermann-Koch formylation reaction?

Explanation: Carbon monoxide and HCl react in the presence of AlCl3/CuCl to form the formyl cation (HCO+).

Question 18 of 19

Which directing group category includes the -COR, -CHO, and -COOH groups?

Explanation: Carbonyl-containing substituents are electron-withdrawing via resonance and induction, making them deactivating and meta-directing.

Question 19 of 19

How can aniline be selectively monobrominated without forming 2,4,6-tribromoaniline?

Explanation: Acetylation of aniline to acetanilide reduces the activating power of the nitrogen atom, allowing controlled monobromination.

Which intermediate species is responsible for the temporary loss of aromaticity in electrophilic aromatic substitution?

Solution: The Wheland intermediate is a non-aromatic carbocation where aromaticity is temporarily disrupted.

What is the primary limitation of Friedel-Crafts alkylation?

Solution: Carbocation intermediates undergo rearrangements, and the alkylated product is more reactive than the starting material, leading to polyalkylation.

Question 3 of 10
Which compound yields meta-nitrobenzoic acid upon nitration?

Solution: Benzoic acid contains a carbonyl-containing substituent which is electron-withdrawing and meta-directing.

Question 4 of 10
What is the role of sulfuric acid in the nitrating mixture of HNO3 and H2SO4?

Solution: Sulfuric acid protonates nitric acid, facilitating the loss of water and generating the nitronium electrophile.

Question 5 of 10
In electrophilic substitution of toluene, substitution occurs predominantly at which positions?

Solution: The methyl group is activating and directs incoming electrophiles to the ortho and para positions.

Question 6 of 10
Which reagent is used for Friedel-Crafts acylation?

Solution: Friedel-Crafts acylation employs an acyl chloride (R-COCl) in the presence of a Lewis acid like AlCl3.

Question 7 of 10
Why does chlorobenzene undergo electrophilic substitution slower than benzene?

Solution: The electronegative chlorine atom withdraws electron density inductively, deactivating the ring despite its resonance donation.

Question 8 of 10
What is formed when benzene is treated with excess chlorine in the presence of sunlight (UV light)?

Solution: In the presence of UV light, chlorine adds across the aromatic double bonds via a free-radical addition mechanism to form benzene hexachloride, not substitution.

Question 9 of 10
Which directing group activates the benzene ring the most?

Solution: The amino group (-NH2) is a strongly activating group due to lone pair resonance donation into the ring.

Question 10 of 10
What is the purpose of converting aniline to acetanilide prior to bromination?

Solution: Acetylation decreases the electron-donating ability of the nitrogen atom by delocalizing its lone pair into the acetyl carbonyl group, preventing tribromination.
