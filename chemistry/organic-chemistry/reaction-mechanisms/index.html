Organic Chemistry

Reaction Mechanisms

Reaction mechanisms describe the step-by-step sequence of elementary reactions by which overall chemical changes occur. They involve the breaking and forming of covalent bonds via intermediates, transition states, and reactive species.

UP PGT Chemistry Subject Code 02 B.Sc. / M.Sc. Level

Bond Cleavage and Reactive Intermediates

  • Homolytic cleavage yields free radicals, whereas heterolytic cleavage yields carbocations and carbanions.
  • Carbocations are sp2 hybridized with a vacant p-orbital and exhibit trigonal planar geometry.
  • Free radical stability follows the order: 3° > 2° > 1° > methyl, driven by hyperconjugation and inductive effects.
  • Carbanions are sp3 hybridized with a pyramidal geometry and a lone pair of electrons.

Substitution and Elimination Pathways

  • SN1 reactions proceed via a two-step mechanism through a carbocation intermediate and exhibit first-order kinetics.
  • SN2 reactions proceed via a concerted single-step mechanism with Walden inversion and second-order kinetics.
  • E1 eliminations proceed via carbocation intermediates, whereas E2 eliminations are concerted, requiring anti-coplanar geometry.
  • Zaitsev's rule predicts that the more substituted and stable alkene is the major product in E1 and E2 eliminations.

Electronic Effects and Reaction Kinetics

  • Inductive effect (-I or +I) operates through sigma bonds and decreases rapidly with distance.
  • Resonance (mesomeric) effect operates through pi systems and can delocalize charge effectively across conjugated networks.
  • Hyperconjugation involves the delocalizing of sigma electrons of a C-H bond into an adjacent empty or partially filled p-orbital.
  • Rate-determining step (RDS) is the slowest elementary step that dictates the overall rate law of the reaction.

Conditions: Linear free energy relationship for aromatic side-chain reactions
Units: ρ (reaction constant), σ (substituent constant) are dimensionless

Conditions: Elementary or overall rate determination
Units: Rate in mol L-1 s-1

Conditions: Primary isotope effect involving C-H versus C-D bond cleavage
Units: Dimensionless ratio
Arrhenius Equation
k = A e-Ea / RT
Conditions: Temperature dependence of reaction rate constants
Units: k in s-1 or M-1s-1, Ea in J mol-1
⚠️ Important Exceptions & Anomalies
Exception Tertiary alkyl halides undergo SN1 substitution

Observation: Bridgehead tertiary halides (e.g., 1-bromobicyclo[2.2.1]heptane) are completely inert to SN1 and SN2 reactions. — Carbocations cannot achieve planar sp2 geometry at bridgehead positions due to ring strain (Bredt's rule).

Exception E2 eliminations follow Zaitsev's rule yielding the most substituted alkene

Observation: Bulky bases (e.g., potassium tert-butoxide) yield the Hofmann (less substituted) alkene as the major product. — Steric hindrance prevents the base from abstracting the more hindered internal proton, favoring the accessible terminal proton.

Exception SN2 reactions proceed with complete inversion of configuration

Observation: Ambident nucleophiles can attack from multiple centers, and neighboring group participation (NGP) can result in retention of configuration. — Intramolecular double inversion (anchimeric assistance) preserves the initial stereochemical configuration.

🎯 Key Exam Points
  • SN1 reactions feature carbocation intermediates which are prone to rearrangements via hydride or alkyl shifts.
  • The Hammett reaction constant (ρ) measures susceptibility to electronic effects; positive ρ indicates negative charge buildup in the transition state.
  • Primary alkyl halides almost exclusively undergo SN2 reactions due to severe steric hindrance preventing carbocation formation.
  • E2 mechanism is stereospecific and requires anti-periplanar geometry of the leaving group and the beta-hydrogen.
  • Friedel-Crafts alkylation often suffers from carbocation rearrangements and polyalkylation side reactions.
  • Isotope labeling studies utilizing deuterium (D) demonstrate primary kinetic isotope effects when C-H cleavage occurs in the rate-determining step.
🚫 Common Errors & Confusions
  • Assuming all tertiary substrates undergo SN1 without considering strong bases that enforce E2 elimination.
  • Forgetting that carbocation rearrangements can occur in SN1, E1, and electrophilic addition reactions to yield more stable isomers.
  • Confusing polar protic solvents (which stabilize carbocations via hydrogen bonding) with polar aprotic solvents (which enhance nucleophile reactivity).
  • Neglecting steric hindrance in SN2 transition states when evaluating branched primary substrates.

⚡ Quick Revision: Reaction Mechanisms

Core formulas, rapid recall points, and distinguishing criteria at a glance.

Reactive Intermediates

Carbocations, carbanions, free radicals, and carbenes represent transient high-energy species dictating organic reaction pathways.

Substitution Kinetics

SN1 is first-order involving planar carbocations, whereas SN2 is second-order proceeding via backside attack with inversion.

Elimination Regioselectivity

Zaitsev's rule governs standard eliminations to yield thermodynamically stable alkenes, while steric factors dictate Hofmann products.

Electronic Effects

Inductive, resonance, and hyperconjugative effects redistribute electron density, directly dictating stability and reactivity.

📚 Terms & Units Glossary

Carbocation (R3C+)
A positively charged carbon species with six electrons in its valence shell, sp2 hybridized.
Carbanion (R3C-)
A negatively charged carbon species possessing a lone pair, sp3 hybridized.
Free Radical (R3C•)
A neutral reactive intermediate containing an unpaired electron.
Nucleophile (Nu-)
An electron-rich species that donates an electron pair to form a covalent bond.
Electrophile (E+)
An electron-deficient species that accepts an electron pair.
Transition State (TS)
An energy maximum along the reaction coordinate representing a state of bonds breaking and forming.

🎯 Key Takeaways

  • SN2 involves inversion of configuration and second-order kinetics.
  • SN1 involves racemization and first-order kinetics via carbocation.
  • E2 requires anti-coplanar alignment of H and leaving group.
  • Tertiary carbocations rearrange only if a more stable tertiary or resonance-stabilized system can be formed.
  • Polar aprotic solvents accelerate SN2 reactions by leaving anions un-solvated and active.

📌 Quick Recall

  • Reaction mechanisms track bond reorganization from reactants through intermediates to products.
  • Kinetics and stereochemistry provide definitive experimental proof for mechanistic pathways.
  • Substituent electronic effects systematically alter reaction rates and intermediate stabilities.
  • Solvent polarity and nucleophile/base strength dictate the competition between substitution and elimination.

⚖️ Key Differences

Inversion of Configuration VS Racemization

Inversion (SN2) flips stereochemistry 100%, whereas racemization (SN1) yields a 50:50 mixture of enantiomers.

Which solvent favors SN1 reactions?

Explanation: Polar protic solvents like water or ethanol stabilize both carbocations and leaving group anions through hydrogen bonding.

Question 5 of 18

What is the stereochemical outcome of an SN2 reaction at a chiral center?

Explanation: Nucleophilic backside attack leads to inversion of configuration at the stereogenic carbon.

Question 6 of 18

Which of the following represents the correct stability order of free radicals?

Explanation: Free radical stability parallels carbocations (3° > 2° > 1°) due to hyperconjugation and inductive donation.

Question 7 of 18

Which effect explains the stability of propene radical over ethyl radical?

Explanation: Hyperconjugation involving adjacent C-H sigma bonds stabilizes the radical center.

Question 8 of 18

In elimination reactions, Zaitsev's rule predicts formation of:

Explanation: Zaitsev's rule states that the major elimination product is the more substituted, thermodynamically more stable alkene.

Question 9 of 18

Which species acts as an electrophile?

Explanation: BF3 has an incomplete octet on boron, making it a Lewis acid and a strong electrophile.

Question 10 of 18

What order of reaction is an SN1 mechanism overall?

Explanation: The rate-determining step involves only the substrate, making it unimolecular and first-order overall.

Question 11 of 18

Which of the following is a polar aprotic solvent?

Explanation: Acetone lacks acidic hydrogen atoms capable of hydrogen bonding, classifying it as a polar aprotic solvent.

Question 12 of 18

Which compound is most reactive toward electrophilic aromatic substitution?

Question 14 of 18

What is the geometry of a carbanion?

Explanation: Carbanions possess three bond pairs and one lone pair, adopting a pyramidal geometry analogous to amines.

Question 15 of 18

Which of the following reagents promotes E2 elimination exclusively?

Explanation: Strong, bulky bases favor bimolecular elimination (E2) over substitution.

Question 16 of 18

What is the primary factor determining the rate of an SN2 reaction?

Explanation: SN2 kinetics depend on both reactant concentrations (second order).

Question 17 of 18

Which intermediate is formed in the Reimer-Tiemann reaction?

Explanation: Reaction of chloroform with base generates dichlorocarbene (:CCl2) as the reactive intermediate.

Question 18 of 18

Which of the following groups is meta-directing in electrophilic aromatic substitution?

Explanation: The nitro group (-NO2) is electron-withdrawing via -I and -R effects, directing incoming electrophiles to the meta position.

Question 1 of 10
Which mechanism type is favored by high temperature in alkyl halide reactions?

Solution: Elimination reactions involve an increase in moles of gaseous or molecular species, yielding positive entropy change (ΔS), thus favored at high temperatures.

Question 2 of 10
Which of the following carbocations is most stable?

Solution: Benzyl carbocation (C6H5CH2+) is exceptionally stable due to extensive resonance delocalization into the aromatic ring.

Question 3 of 10
What is the role of AlCl3 in Friedel-Crafts alkylation?

Solution: AlCl3 acts as a Lewis acid, coordinating with alkyl halides to generate carbocation electrophiles.

Question 4 of 10
Which of the following compounds exhibits optical activity upon SN1 solvolysis?

Solution: SN1 solvolysis of an optically active chiral halide produces a racemic mixture with net loss of optical activity.

Question 5 of 10
What type of cleavage results in the formation of free radicals?

Solution: Homolytic bond cleavage involves symmetrical distribution of bonding electrons, forming free radicals.

Question 6 of 10
Which substrate is completely unreactive toward SN2 displacement?

Solution: Tertiary alkyl halides are sterically hindered, preventing backside nucleophilic attack required for SN2.

Question 7 of 10
In E1 elimination, the base:

Solution: E1 is unimolecular; the base removes the proton in the rapid second step after slow carbocation formation.

Question 8 of 10
Which intermediate is characteristic of addition of HX to alkenes with peroxides (anti-Markovnikov)?

Solution: Peroxide-mediated addition of HBr proceeds via a free-radical chain mechanism.

Question 9 of 10
What is the principal reason for the stability of tertiary carbanions being lowest?

Solution: +I effect of multiple alkyl groups increases electron density on an already negatively charged carbon, destabilizing it.

Question 10 of 10
Which substituent directs an incoming electrophile to the ortho and para positions?

Solution: The hydroxyl group (-OH) is activating and ortho/para-directing due to lone pair resonance donation.
