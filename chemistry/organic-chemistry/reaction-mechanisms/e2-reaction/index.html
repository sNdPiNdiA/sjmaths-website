E2 Reaction

The E2 reaction is a concerted, bimolecular elimination process proceeding via a single transition state with simultaneous proton abstraction and leaving group departure. It requires a strict anti-coplanar geometry for optimal orbital overlap between the C-H sigma bond and the C-X sigma star orbital.

UP PGT Chemistry Subject Code 02 B.Sc. / M.Sc. Level

Stereoelectronic Requirements and Transition State Geometry

  • The E2 mechanism requires an anti-periplanar (180° dihedral angle) conformation to allow maximum overlap of the breaking C-H σ bond electrons with the developing π system and the departing C-X σ* orbital.
  • Syn-periplanar elimination occurs only in rigid molecular frameworks where anti-conformation is geometrically inaccessible, proceeding with higher activation energy.
  • The transition state possesses significant alkene character, where bond breaking of C-H and C-X is more advanced than the formation of the C=C π bond.
  • Because no carbocation intermediate is formed, skeletal rearrangements are strictly absent in standard E2 pathways.

Regiochemistry: Zaitsev vs. Hofmann Orientation

  • Zaitsev orientation yields the more substituted, thermodynamically more stable thermodynamic alkene as the major product.
  • Hofmann orientation yields the less substituted, kinetically controlled terminal alkene, favored when sterically hindered bases like potassium tert-butoxide are employed.
  • Poor leaving groups such as ammonium ions (-NR3+) or fluoride (-F) lead to carbanion-like transition states, favoring the Hofmann product via an E1cB-like spectrum of E2 mechanisms.
  • The acidity of the β-proton and steric bulk of the base dictate the exact kinetic vs thermodynamic product ratio.

Kinetic Evidence and Isotope Effects

  • The reaction exhibits second-order kinetics, depending linearly on both substrate and base concentrations: Rate = k[R-X][Base].
  • A substantial primary kinetic isotope effect (kH/kD > 1) confirms that C-H bond cleavage is involved in the rate-determining step.
  • Substituent effects on aryl halides or alkyl tosylates show a positive Hammett reaction constant (ρ > 0), indicating a build-up of negative charge on the carbon framework in the transition state.
  • Strong, negatively charged bases such as alkoxides (RO-) and hydroxide (OH-) promote the bimolecular pathway over unimolecular routes.

⚠️ Important Exceptions & Anomalies
Exception Small bases give Zaitsev (more substituted) alkenes as major products.

Observation: Cyclohexyl systems with fixed conformation (e.g., trans-1-bromo-4-tert-butylcyclohexane) — Axial proton availability dictates elimination; equatorial leaving groups cannot achieve the mandatory anti-periplanar geometry.

Exception Good leaving groups (I-, Br-, TsO-) give Zaitsev orientation with hindered bases.

Observation: Quaternary ammonium salts (-NR3+) and sulfonium salts (-SR2+) — Strongly electron-withdrawing cationic leaving groups impart high acidity to β-hydrogens, causing E1cB-like carbanionic transition states that favor Hofmann products.

Exception E2 reactions do not undergo skeletal rearrangements.

Observation: Rearrangements can occur only if the E2 process operates via an irreversible reversible E1cB mechanism with a stable carbanion intermediate. — Concerted E2 transition states do not provide sufficient lifetime for carbocation or carbbanion migration unless pre-existing carbanionic stability exists.

💡 Shortcuts & Memory Aids
Base Bulkiness Mnemonic
Bulky Base = Hofmann, Small Base = Zaitsev

Application: When potassium tert-butoxide (t-BuOK) is used, automatically draw the less substituted alkene as major product.

Leaving Group Ability vs Hofmann Rule
Bad Leaving Group (-F, -NR3+) = Hofmann Product

Application: Identify the leaving group first; if it is a poor leaving group, favor the terminal alkene regardless of base size.

Anti-Periplanar Check in Rings
Both groups must be Trans-Diaxial

Application: In cyclohexane chairs, ensure both the β-hydrogen and the leaving group are in axial positions before performing elimination.

🎯 Key Exam Points
  • E2 is a concerted, single-step reaction with zero intermediates.
  • The reaction rate is second order overall: first order in substrate and first order in base.
  • Anti-periplanar geometry (180° dihedral angle) is strictly required for optimal orbital overlap.
  • Zaitsev's rule predicts the more substituted alkene when using unhindered bases.
  • Hofmann's rule predicts the less substituted terminal alkene when using bulky bases like t-BuOK.
  • A primary kinetic isotope effect (kH/kD > 1) is observed because C-H bond breaking occurs in the transition state.
🚫 Common Errors & Confusions
  • Assuming E2 reactions can occur with syn-coplanar geometry without energy penalties.
  • Confusing Zaitsev elimination with Hofmann elimination when bulky bases are employed.
  • Forgetting that poor leaving groups (-F, -NR3+) override base size to give Hofmann products.
  • Expecting carbocation rearrangements in a concerted E2 mechanism.

⚡ Quick Revision: E2 Reaction

Core formulas, rapid recall points, and distinguishing criteria at a glance.

Concerted Bimolecular Kinetics

E2 kinetics follow Rate = k[Substrate][Base], confirming that both species are involved in the transition state without discrete intermediates.

Stereoelectronic Control

Strict anti-periplanar alignment between the leaving group and β-hydrogen is mandatory for optimal p-orbital overlap during bond cleavage and formation.

Regioselectivity Factors

Base steric bulk and leaving group electronic character govern whether Zaitsev (thermodynamic) or Hofmann (kinetic) alkenes predominate.

Absence of Rearrangement

Unlike E1 reactions, concerted E2 mechanisms prevent carbocation formation, thereby eliminating skeletal rearrangements.

📚 Terms & Units Glossary

Anti-periplanar (180° Dihedral Conformation)
Spatial arrangement where leaving group and β-H are on opposite sides in the same plane.
Zaitsev Rule (Thermodynamic Control)
Empirical rule stating more substituted alkene is the major product.
Hofmann Rule (Kinetic / Steric Control)
Empirical rule stating less substituted alkene is the major product.
Kinetic Isotope Effect (kH / kD)
Ratio of reaction rates for compounds differing only in isotopic substitution at the bond breaking site.
Bimolecular (Second Order)
Reaction whose rate determining step involves two chemical entities.
Concerted (Single Transition State)
Reaction occurring in a single step with simultaneous bond breaking and making.

🎯 Key Takeaways

  • Rate = k[R-X][Base]
  • Anti-periplanar geometry required (180°)
  • Bulky base (t-BuOK) gives Hofmann alkene
  • Small base (EtO-) gives Zaitsev alkene
  • Primary KIE (kH/kD > 1) confirms concerted proton abstraction

📌 Quick Recall

  • E2 proceeds via a single concerted transition state.
  • Requires anti-periplanar geometry for maximum orbital overlap.
  • Steric hindrance of the base shifts regioselectivity from Zaitsev to Hofmann.
  • No carbocation intermediates means no skeletal rearrangements.

⚖️ Key Differences

E2 Mechanism VS E1 Mechanism

E2 is concerted and bimolecular with second-order kinetics; E1 is stepwise via a carbocation with first-order kinetics.

Which of the following conditions favors the E2 mechanism over E1?

Explanation: Strong bases and polar aprotic solvents increase the rate of bimolecular elimination (E2) while suppressing substitution and ionization (E1).

Question 2 of 18

What is the required dihedral angle between the β-hydrogen and the leaving group in an E2 reaction for optimal geometry?

Explanation: An anti-periplanar conformation with a 180° dihedral angle allows maximum overlap between the C-H σ bond and the C-X σ* orbital.

Question 3 of 18

Which base promotes the formation of the Hofmann product (less substituted alkene) as the major product from 2-bromobutane?

Explanation: Potassium tert-butoxide is sterically hindered, abstracting the more accessible terminal proton to yield the Hofmann product.

Question 4 of 18

What is the overall order of an E2 reaction rate law?

Explanation: The rate law is Rate = k[Substrate][Base], making it second order overall.

Question 5 of 18

Which phenomenon confirms that C-H bond cleavage occurs in the rate-determining step of an E2 reaction?

Explanation: A significant primary kinetic isotope effect (kH/kD > 1) proves that the C-H bond is actively cleaved in the rate-limiting transition state.

Question 6 of 18

Which leaving group typically leads to predominant formation of the Hofmann product even with small bases?

Explanation: Quaternary ammonium ions (-NR3+) impart carbanionic character to the transition state due to strong electron withdrawal, favoring the Hofmann alkene.

Question 7 of 18

Do E2 reactions typically undergo skeletal carbocation rearrangements?

Explanation: Because E2 reactions are concerted processes with no carbocation intermediate, skeletal rearrangements do not occur.

Question 8 of 18

In cyclohexane systems, elimination via E2 requires the leaving group and β-hydrogen to be in which orientation?

Explanation: Trans-diaxial geometry in cyclohexane chairs fulfills the mandatory 180° anti-periplanar requirement.

Question 9 of 18

What type of transition state is generally proposed for an ideal E2 reaction?

Explanation: An ideal E2 transition state is synchronous with simultaneous partial bond breaking and making, though varying degrees of carbanion or carbocation character can exist.

Question 10 of 18

Which of the following solvents is best suited to promote an E2 reaction?

Explanation: Polar aprotic solvents like DMSO solvate cations effectively while leaving anions (bases) highly reactive and un-solvated, favoring E2.

Question 11 of 18

What is the major product of dehydrohalogenation of 2-bromo-2-methylbutane with sodium ethoxide?

Explanation: With a small base (ethoxide), Zaitsev's rule dominates, yielding the more substituted tetrasubstituted/trisubstituted alkene, 2-methylbut-2-ene.

Question 12 of 18

How does an increase in base strength affect the rate of an E2 reaction?

Explanation: Since the base concentration is part of the rate law (Rate = k[Substrate][Base]), increasing base concentration increases the reaction rate linearly.

Question 13 of 18

Which halogen leaving group provides the fastest E2 elimination rate under identical conditions?

Explanation: Iodide is the best leaving group among halides due to the weak C-I bond strength and excellent polarizability.

Question 14 of 18

In a Hammett plot for substituted phenylethyl halides undergoing E2 reaction, what sign of ρ (rho) is observed?

Explanation: A positive ρ value indicates that negative charge develops in the transition state on the carbon framework during proton abstraction.

Question 15 of 18

Why is syn-elimination less favorable than anti-elimination in open-chain systems?

Explanation: The syn conformation forces eclipsing interactions of groups, raising the energy barrier compared to the staggered anti-periplanar conformation.

Question 16 of 18

Which compound will not undergo E2 elimination readily because it lacks β-hydrogens?

Explanation: Methyl chloride (CH3Cl) possesses no β-hydrogens and cannot undergo elimination.

Question 17 of 18

What is the stereochemical outcome at the carbon bearing the leaving group in an E2 elimination?

Explanation: The carbon atom transitions from sp3 to sp2 hybridization as the double bond forms.

Question 18 of 18

Which reagent combination promotes E2 elimination of alkyl halides with high yield?

Explanation: Sodium hydroxide in ethanol provides a strong alkoxide/hydroxide base necessary to drive bimolecular elimination.

Question 2 of 10
What is the effect of switching from a small base (CH3CH2O-) to a bulky base ((CH3)3CO-) on regioselectivity?

Solution: Bulky bases favor the less sterically hindered proton, shifting the major product from the Zaitsev alkene to the Hofmann alkene.

Question 3 of 10
Which kinetic order is observed for standard E2 elimination reactions?

Solution: E2 is bimolecular, exhibiting second-order kinetics: Rate = k[Substrate][Base].

Question 4 of 10
Why do quaternary ammonium hydroxides undergo Hofmann elimination predominantly?

Solution: The strong electron-withdrawing cationic leaving group (-NR3+) imparts high acidity to β-hydrogens, leading to an E1cB-like transition state favoring the Hofmann product.

Question 5 of 10
What is the primary reason skeletal rearrangements are absent in E2 reactions?

Solution: Because E2 is a concerted process without a carbocation intermediate, migrating groups have no carbocation center to shift toward.

Question 6 of 10
In cyclohexane ring eliminations, why is trans-1-bromo-4-tert-butylcyclohexane much more reactive than cis-1-bromo-4-tert-butylcyclohexane?

Solution: The trans isomer locks the bulky tert-butyl group equatorial, forcing the bromine to be axial, which readily achieves trans-diaxial anti-periplanar alignment with axial β-hydrogens.

Question 7 of 10
What role does the solvent play in bimolecular E2 eliminations?

Solution: Polar aprotic solvents solvate cations well while leaving attacking anions (bases) naked and highly reactive, favoring E2.

Question 8 of 10

Solution: 3-Bromopentane is symmetrical, yielding pent-2-ene regardless of regioselectivity.

Question 9 of 10
What does a Hammett reaction constant ρ > 0 indicate for an elimination reaction?

Solution: A positive ρ value indicates that electron density increases (negative charge develops) on the reaction center during the rate-determining step.

Question 10 of 10
Which bond cleavage is responsible for the kinetic isotope effect observed in E2 reactions?

Solution: The C-H (or C-D) bond cleavage during proton abstraction by the base causes the primary kinetic isotope effect.
