Organic Chemistry

Amides

Amides are derivatives of carboxylic acids where the hydroxyl group is replaced by an amino group of the general formula R-CONH2. They exhibit significant resonance stabilization, imparting partial double-bond character to the C-N bond.

UP PGT Chemistry Subject Code 02 B.Sc. / M.Sc. Level

Structure and Bonding in Amides

  • The carbonyl carbon is sp2 hybridized, resulting in a planar geometry around the amide group.
  • Significant resonance between the lone pair on nitrogen and the carbonyl oxygen creates a partial double bond between carbon and nitrogen (C=N bond order ~1.28).
  • Due to restricted rotation around the C-N bond, cis-trans isomerism is observed in monosubstituted amides.
  • Amides possess unusually high boiling points and melting points compared to corresponding esters due to extensive intermolecular hydrogen bonding.

Synthesis of Amides

  • Amides are prepared by the reaction of acyl chlorides, acid anhydrides, or esters with ammonia or primary/secondary amines.
  • Thermal dehydration of ammonium salts of carboxylic acids yields primary amides (e.g., R-COONH4 → R-CONH2 + H2O).
  • Partial hydrolysis of alkyl cyanides (nitriles) using alkaline hydrogen peroxide or acid yields primary amides.
  • The Ritter reaction converts nitriles and alkenes in the presence of strong acid into N-substituted amides.

Chemical Reactions of Amides

  • Amides act as very weak bases due to resonance delocalization of the nitrogen lone pair into the carbonyl group, forming N-protonated species only under strongly acidic conditions.
  • Dehydration of primary amides with phosphorus pentoxide (P2O5) or thionyl chloride yields nitriles.
  • Reduction with lithium aluminum hydride (LiAlH4) yields primary, secondary, or tertiary amines depending on the substitution of the starting amide.
  • The Hofmann bromamide degradation converts primary amides into primary amines with loss of one carbon atom via a nitrene intermediate.
📐 Key Formulas & Equations
Hofmann Rearrangement Reaction
R-CONH2 + Br2 + 4NaOH → R-NH2 + Na2CO3 + 2NaBr + 2H2O
Conditions: Primary amide treated with aqueous or ethanolic halogen and base.
Units: Stoichiometric reaction yielding primary amine with one less carbon.
Acidic Hydrolysis of Amides
R-CONH2 + H2O + HCl → R-COOH + NH4Cl
Conditions: Reflux with mineral acid catalyst.
Units: Stoichiometric conversion to carboxylic acid.
Basic Hydrolysis of Amides
R-CONH2 + NaOH → R-COONa + NH3
Conditions: Reflux with aqueous base.
Units: Stoichiometric conversion to carboxylate salt.
Reduction of Amides
R-CONH2 + 4[H] → (LiAlH4) → R-CH2-NH2 + H2O
Conditions: Dry ether solvent, strong reducing agent.
Units: Conversion to primary amine with same number of carbons.

⚠️ Important Exceptions & Anomalies
Exception Amines are typically strong bases due to the lone pair on nitrogen.

Observation: Amides are extremely weak bases (effectively neutral). — The lone pair on the nitrogen atom is delocalized into the carbonyl π-system via resonance, making it unavailable for protonation.

Exception Acid derivatives like acyl chlorides and anhydrides react violently with water to form acids.

Observation: Formamide and simple aliphatic amides dissolve in water without rapid violent hydrolysis. — High resonance stabilization energy and strong intermolecular hydrogen bonding increase kinetic stability toward nucleophilic attack by water.

Exception Reduction of carbonyl compounds with NaBH4 generally reduces aldehydes and ketones.

Observation: Standard NaBH4 does not normally reduce primary amides to amines under ambient conditions. — Amide carbonyl carbon is significantly less electrophilic due to nitrogen lone pair electron donation.

💡 Shortcuts & Memory Aids
Hofmann Degradation Carbon Loss Shortcut
R-CONH2 → R-NH2 (Carbon count: n → n-1)

Application: Whenever a primary amide reacts with Br2/KOH, simply erase the carbonyl carbon (CO) to get the resulting amine product.

Amide vs Amine Basicity Mnemonic
Amide = Neutral (Resonance tied up), Amine = Basic (Lone pair free)

Application: Use this comparison instantly when ranking nitrogenous compounds by pKa or pKb values.

Reduction Product Carbon Count Rule
LiAlH4 reduction keeps carbons: R-CONH2 → R-CH2-NH2 (n → n)

Application: Distinguish between Hofmann degradation (loses 1 carbon) and LiAlH4 reduction (retains all carbons).

🎯 Key Exam Points
  • Amides exhibit restricted rotation around the C-N bond due to partial double-bond character.
  • Formamide is the only amide that is liquid at room temperature; all other simple aliphatic amides are solids.
  • Hofmann bromamide degradation proceeds via an acyl nitrene intermediate with rearrangement to an isocyanate.
  • Reduction of N,N-disubstituted amides with LiAlH4 yields tertiary amines without loss of carbon atoms.
  • Amides undergo acid or base catalyzed hydrolysis to yield carboxylic acids or carboxylate salts respectively.
  • Dehydration of primary amides with P2O5 produces alkyl cyanides (nitriles).
🚫 Common Errors & Confusions
  • Confusing Hofmann bromamide reaction (which removes a carbon) with standard amine formation via reduction (which keeps all carbons).
  • Assuming amides are basic like aliphatic amines; failing to account for resonance delocalization of the nitrogen lone pair.
  • Forgetting that secondary and tertiary amides cannot undergo Hofmann bromamide degradation because they lack the necessary N-H protons.
  • Incorrectly assigning the C-N bond order as single; it has significant double bond character (~1.28).

⚡ Quick Revision: Amides

Core formulas, rapid recall points, and distinguishing criteria at a glance.

Amide Bonding and Structure

Amides feature an sp2 hybridized carbonyl carbon bonded to a nitrogen atom. Resonance delocalization creates a partial C-N double bond, restricting rotation and enforcing planar geometry.

Methods of Preparation

Amides are synthesized via ammonolysis of acyl chlorides, anhydrides, and esters, or through thermal dehydration of ammonium carboxylate salts and partial hydration of nitriles.

Reactivity and Hydrolysis

Amides are the least reactive carboxylic acid derivatives toward nucleophilic acyl substitution. They undergo both acidic and basic hydrolysis to reform carboxylic acid derivatives.

Key Name Reactions

The Hofmann bromamide degradation converts primary amides to primary amines with one less carbon atom using Br2 and caustic alkali, proceeding through an isocyanate intermediate.

📚 Terms & Units Glossary

Amide (R-CONH2)

Hofmann Degradation (Br2 / KOH)
Conversion of primary amide to primary amine with loss of one carbon.
Peptide Bond (-CO-NH-)
Amide linkage joining amino acids in proteins and peptides.
Imidogen (=NH group)
Divalent functional group encountered in substituted amides or imides.
Nitrile (R-C≡N)
Product obtained on dehydration of primary amides using P2O5.
Isocyanate (R-N=C=O)
Reactive intermediate formed during Hofmann bromamide rearrangement.

🎯 Key Takeaways

  • Amides are neutral to very weakly basic due to nitrogen lone pair resonance delocalization.
  • Hofmann bromamide degradation results in the loss of the carbonyl carbon atom.
  • Reduction of amides with LiAlH4 yields amines with the exact same carbon skeleton.
  • Primary amides have unusually high boiling points due to intermolecular hydrogen bonding.
  • Dehydration of primary amides yields nitriles.

📌 Quick Recall

  • Amide bond has high double bond character restricting free rotation.
  • Synthesized from acyl derivatives and ammonia or amines.
  • Least reactive among carboxylic acid derivatives toward nucleophiles.
  • Undergoes unique degradation to amines via Hofmann reaction.

⚖️ Key Differences

Hofmann Bromamide Reaction VS Schmidt Reaction

Hofmann uses Br2/OH- on primary amide to lose 1 carbon; Schmidt uses hydrazoic acid (HN3) on carboxylic acid.

Question 1 of 18

What is the hybridization of the nitrogen atom in a simple primary amide like acetamide?

Explanation: Due to resonance delocalization of the lone pair into the carbonyl group, the nitrogen atom adopts planar sp2 hybridization.

Question 2 of 18

Which of the following reagents converts acetamide into methylamine?

Explanation: Hofmann bromamide degradation (Br2 + NaOH) converts primary amides into primary amines with the loss of one carbon atom.

Question 3 of 18

Why are amides much weaker bases than amines?

Explanation: Resonance delocalization of the nitrogen lone pair into the adjacent carbonyl oxygen makes the lone pair unavailable for protonation.

Question 4 of 18

What is the product when propanamide is treated with LiAlH4?

Explanation: Reduction of primary amides with lithium aluminum hydride yields primary amines containing the same number of carbon atoms (propanamide → propylamine).

Question 5 of 18

Which intermediate is formed during the Hofmann bromamide reaction?

Explanation: The Hofmann rearrangement proceeds via an N-bromoamide intermediate that generates an acyl nitrene rearranging to an alkyl isocyanate.

Question 6 of 18

What is the IUPAC name of dimethylformamide (DMF)?

Explanation: DMF is derived from formic acid (methanoic acid) with two methyl groups on nitrogen, giving N,N-Dimethylmethanamide.

Question 7 of 18

Which carboxylic acid derivative is the most reactive toward nucleophilic acyl substitution?

Explanation: Acyl chlorides have the best leaving group (Cl-) and lowest resonance stabilization, making them the most reactive.

Question 8 of 18

Dehydration of primary amides using phosphorus pentoxide (P2O5) yields:

Explanation: Dehydration removes the elements of water from the primary amide (-CONH2) to form a cyanide/nitrile (-C≡N).

Question 9 of 18

Which of the following amides will NOT undergo the Hofmann bromamide reaction?

Explanation: N,N-Dimethylethanamide is a tertiary amide lacking N-H hydrogen atoms, which are strictly required for salt formation and bromination in Hofmann degradation.

Question 10 of 18

The boiling point of amides is abnormally high compared to corresponding esters because of:

Explanation: Amides possess both N-H donor sites and C=O acceptor sites, allowing extensive intermolecular hydrogen bonding networks.

Question 11 of 18

What is the product of acidic hydrolysis of benzamide?

Explanation: Acidic hydrolysis of benzamide yields benzoic acid and an ammonium salt.

Question 12 of 18

Rotation around the C-N bond in amides is restricted due to:

Explanation: Resonance places partial π-bond character between the carbon and nitrogen, raising the rotational barrier.

Question 13 of 18

Which reagent converts an amide into a primary amine with the SAME number of carbon atoms?

Explanation: LiAlH4 reduces the carbonyl group of the amide to a methylene group without altering the carbon chain length.

Question 14 of 18

What is formed when acetamide reacts with P4O10 under heat?

Explanation: Heating primary amides with a dehydrating agent like P4O10 (or P2O5) yields acetonitrile.

Question 15 of 18

Amides exhibit which type of structural isomerism due to restricted rotation?

Explanation: Monosubstituted amides display cis-trans isomerism across the C-N partial double bond.

Question 16 of 18

Which of the following compounds is a solid at room temperature except formamide?

Explanation: Due to strong intermolecular hydrogen bonding, all lower aliphatic primary amides except formamide are crystalline solids.

Question 17 of 18

When urea (a diamide) is heated, it decomposes to form:

Explanation: Heating urea leads to condensation forming biuret, which gives a positive biuret test for peptide bonds.

Question 18 of 18

The functional group isomer of a primary amide is:

Explanation: Amides are isomeric with oximes, nitroalkanes, and other nitrogen-oxygen-carbon multifunctional compounds sharing the same empirical formula.

Question 1 of 10
What is the principal organic product obtained when benzamide is treated with bromine and aqueous sodium hydroxide?

Solution: Benzamide undergoes Hofmann degradation with Br2/NaOH to yield aniline with loss of the carbonyl carbon.

Question 2 of 10
Which of the following reagents will selectively reduce the amide group in a molecule containing both an ester and an amide?

Solution: Lithium aluminum hydride reduces amides to amines, whereas esters are reduced to alcohols; LiAlH4 is strong enough to reduce both, but selective reducing agents or controlled conditions target specific groups.

Question 3 of 10
What is formed when acetamide reacts with nitrous acid (HNO2)?

Solution: Primary amides react with nitrous acid to form carboxylic acids along with evolution of nitrogen gas.

Question 4 of 10
Which spectroscopic technique clearly distinguishes primary, secondary, and tertiary amides based on N-H stretching bands?

Solution: IR spectroscopy shows two N-H stretching bands for primary amides, one band for secondary amides, and none for tertiary amides.

Question 5 of 10
In the resonance structures of amides, the major charge separation places a negative charge on:

Solution: Oxygen is more electronegative than nitrogen and carbon, so the major contributing resonance structure places negative charge on the carbonyl oxygen.

Question 6 of 10
Which of the following compounds gives methylamine upon treatment with alkaline bromine solution?

Solution: Acetamide (ethanamide) is a primary amide that yields methylamine via Hofmann degradation.

Question 7 of 10
The basic character of amides is attributed to:

Solution: When amides are protonated under strongly acidic conditions, protonation occurs predominantly at the carbonyl oxygen due to greater resonance stabilization of the resulting cation.

Question 8 of 10
What is the product when an N-substituted amide is reduced with LiAlH4?

Solution: Reduction of an N-substituted (secondary) amide yields a secondary amine.

Question 9 of 10
Which derivative of carboxylic acid is most resistant to hydrolysis?

Solution: Amides are the most stable and least reactive toward nucleophilic acyl substitution and hydrolysis due to strong resonance stabilization.

Question 10 of 10
What is the state of matter of formamide at standard room temperature and pressure?

Solution: Formamide is the sole primary aliphatic amide that exists as a clear liquid at room temperature.
