Substituent Effect on Acidity

The acidity of carboxylic acids and substituted phenols is governed by the electronic and steric effects of substituents on the conjugate base stabilization. Electron-withdrawing groups stabilize the carboxylate anion via inductive and resonance effects, increasing acidity.

UP PGT Chemistry Subject Code 02 B.Sc. / M.Sc. Level

Inductive and Resonance Effects

  • Inductive effect (-I) of electron-withdrawing groups decreases electron density at the reaction center, stabilizing the negative charge on the conjugate base.
  • Resonance effect (-M) operates effectively at ortho and para positions, providing additional delocalization of the negative charge in aromatic systems.
  • Alkyl groups (+I) destabilize the carboxylate anion by intensifying the negative charge, thereby decreasing the acidity of the parent acid.

Distance and Positional Dependence

  • The magnitude of the inductive effect falls off rapidly with increasing distance between the substituent and the carboxyl carbon.
  • Ortho effect in benzoic acid derivatives generally increases acidity regardless of whether the substituent is electron-donating or electron-withdrawing due to steric inhibition of resonance.
  • Para-substituted benzoic acids show strong resonance interactions, whereas meta-substituted derivatives rely primarily on the inductive effect.

Quantitative Correlations

  • Hammett substituent constant (σ) measures the electronic effect of substituents relative to hydrogen in benzoic acid dissociation.
  • A positive σ value indicates an electron-withdrawing group that enhances acidity.
  • A negative ρ value for a given reaction indicates that the reaction is facilitated by electron-donating groups that build up positive charge in the transition state.

⚠️ Important Exceptions & Anomalies
Exception Alkyl groups typically decrease acidity via +I effect.

Observation: Formic acid (HCOOH) is stronger than acetic acid (CH3COOH). — The methyl group in acetic acid exerts a +I effect which destabilizes the acetate anion compared to the formate anion.

Exception Electron-withdrawing groups uniformly enhance acidity at all positions.

Observation: p-Methoxybenzoic acid is a weaker acid than benzoic acid despite the -I effect of the methoxy oxygen. — The dominant +R (resonance electron donation) effect of the -OCH3 group destabilizes the conjugate base by pushing electron density into the ring.

Exception Halogens at the alpha position increase acidity based on electronegativity.

Observation: Iodoacetic acid is stronger than chloroacetic acid in certain extended solvent models, but standard aqueous pKa follows F > Cl > Br > I based on -I. — Polarizability and subtle solvation shell dynamics can occasionally invert standard halogen inductive trends in non-standard media.

🎯 Key Exam Points
  • Trifluoroacetic acid (CF3COOH) is one of the strongest aliphatic carboxylic acids due to the intense -I effect of three fluorine atoms.
  • The pKa of acetic acid is approximately 4.76, whereas chloroacetic acid has a pKa of 2.86.
  • Electron-withdrawing substituents on phenols lower the pKa, making them more acidic than unsubstituted phenol.
  • Picric acid (2,4,6-trinitrophenol) is strongly acidic due to the powerful -I and -R effects of three nitro groups.
  • Benzoic acid is a stronger acid than acetic acid because the sp2 hybridized carbon of the benzene ring exerts a stronger electron-withdrawing inductive effect than an sp3 methyl carbon.
  • Hammett reaction constant (ρ) for the ionization of benzoic acid in water at 25°C is defined as exactly 1.00.
🚫 Common Errors & Confusions
  • Confusing +I effect of alkyl groups with acid strengthening; alkyl groups decrease acidity.
  • Assuming meta-substituted groups exert significant resonance effects; meta positions only experience inductive effects.
  • Forgetting that lower pKa value corresponds to a stronger acid.
  • Ignoring the ortho effect when comparing substituted benzoic acid isomers.

⚡ Quick Revision: Substituent Effect on Acidity

Core formulas, rapid recall points, and distinguishing criteria at a glance.

Conjugate Base Stability

Acidity is directly proportional to the thermodynamic stability of the conjugate base anion formed after proton loss.

Electronic Substituent Effects

Electron-withdrawing groups (-I, -M) stabilize the carboxylate anion, while electron-donating groups (+I, +R) destabilize it.

Steric and Ortho Effects

Substituents at the ortho position force the carboxyl group out of coplanarity, relieving resonance stabilization of the acid and enhancing acidity.

Linear Free Energy Relationships

Hammett plots quantitatively correlate substituent constants (σ) with reaction constants (ρ) to predict acid strengths.

📚 Terms & Units Glossary

Inductive Effect (-I / +I)
Permanent polarization of σ-bonds caused by electronegativity differences.
Resonance Effect (-M / +M)
Electron delocalization through overlapping p-orbitals in a conjugated system.
Hammett Constant (σ)
Quantitative measure of polar substituent effects in aromatic systems.
Reaction Constant (ρ)
Susceptibility of a reaction series to substituent electronic effects.
Ortho Effect (Steric Inhibition)
Anomalous acidity increase of ortho-substituted benzoic acids.
Conjugate Base (RCOO-)
Anion remaining after the donation of a proton from an acid.

🎯 Key Takeaways

  • pKa = -log10(Ka)
  • EWG increases acidity (-ΔG°)
  • EDG decreases acidity (+ΔG°)
  • Hammett equation: log(K/K0) = ρσ
  • Ortho effect enhances benzoic acid acidity regardless of electronic nature

📌 Quick Recall

  • Substituents alter acidity by modifying charge distribution in the conjugate base.
  • Inductive effects decrease exponentially with distance along carbon chains.
  • Resonance effects apply specifically to ortho and para positions in benzene derivatives.
  • Quantitative prediction of acid strength utilizes Hammett substituent parameters.

⚖️ Key Differences

Inductive Effect VS Resonance Effect

Inductive operates via σ-bonds over short distances; resonance operates via π-systems over conjugated paths.
