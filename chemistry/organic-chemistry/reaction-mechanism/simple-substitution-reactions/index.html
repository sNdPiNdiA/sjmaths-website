Organic Chemistry

Simple Substitution Reactions

Simple substitution reactions involve the replacement of an atom or a group in an organic molecule by another incoming reagent. These processes proceed primarily via aliphatic nucleophilic substitution (SN1 and SN2) or electrophilic aromatic substitution pathways.

UP PGT Chemistry Subject Code 02 B.Sc. / M.Sc. Level

Aliphatic Nucleophilic Substitution Mechanisms

  • The SN2 mechanism proceeds via a concerted synchronous pathway with complete inversion of configuration (Walden inversion).
  • The SN1 mechanism proceeds in two steps via a planar carbocation intermediate, yielding a racemic mixture upon attack from either face.
  • Steric hindrance strongly retards SN2 reactions, establishing the reactivity order: methyl > 1° > 2° >> 3°.
  • Stability of the intermediate carbocation dictates SN1 reactivity, establishing the reverse order: 3° > 2° >> 1° > methyl.

Effects of Reagents and Solvents

  • Polar protic solvents (e.g., H2O, alcohols) solvate both cations and anions effectively, thereby favoring SN1 mechanisms through stabilization of the carbocation.
  • Polar aprotic solvents (e.g., DMSO, acetone, DMF) do not solvate anions strongly, leaving bare nucleophiles that accelerate SN2 mechanisms.
  • Strong, negatively charged nucleophiles (e.g., CN-, RS-, I-) strongly favor the SN2 pathway.
  • Weak nucleophiles (e.g., H2O, ROH) act as solvolysis reagents favoring the SN1 pathway.

Leaving Group Ability and Substrate Structure

  • Good leaving groups are the conjugate bases of strong acids, following the nucleofugality sequence: I- > Br- > Cl- >> F-.
  • Sulfonate esters like tosylates (OTs) and triflates (OTf) are exceptional leaving groups due to resonance stabilization of the departed anion.
  • Vinylic and aryl halides are generally unreactive toward simple aliphatic substitution due to partial double-bond character and sp2 hybridization.
📐 Key Formulas & Equations
SN1 Rate Law
Rate = k[Substrate]
Conditions: Unimolecular first-order kinetics involving a carbocation intermediate
Units: Rate in mol L-1 s-1, k in s-1
Arrhenius Equation for Substitution
k = A e-Ea / RT
Conditions: Temperature dependence of substitution rate constant
Units: Ea in J mol-1, R in J K-1 mol-1, T in Kelvin
Hammett Equation
log(k / k0) = ρσ
Conditions: Linear free-energy relationship for aromatic substitutions
Units: ρ is reaction constant, σ is substituent constant
SN2 Rate Law
Rate = k[Substrate][Nucleophile]
Conditions: Bimolecular second-order kinetics
Units: Rate in mol L-1 s-1, k in L mol-1 s-1
⚠️ Important Exceptions & Anomalies
Exception Tertiary alkyl halides undergo SN1 substitution exclusively.

Observation: Tertiary alkyl halides with strong base/nucleophile at high temperature undergo elimination instead. — Steric hindrance prevents nucleophilic attack while promoting elimination (E2).

Exception Bridgehead halides are inert to SN1 and SN2 reactions.

Observation: Small-ring bridgehead systems like 1-bromobicyclo[2.2.1]heptane fail completely in SN1. — Bridgehead carbocations cannot adopt the required sp2 planar geometry due to rigid framework strain (Bredt's rule).

Exception Allylic and benzylic halides react rapidly via SN2.

Observation: They also exhibit extremely rapid SN1 rates. — Resonance stabilization of the resulting allylic or benzylic carbocation drastically lowers activation energy.

💡 Shortcuts & Memory Aids
CARB Mnemonic for SN1 Reactivity
C - A - R - B (Cation Stability)

Application: Remember SN1 depends entirely on carbocation stability: 3° > 2° > 1°.

STERIC Mnemonic for SN2 Kinetics
Backside attack needs open space

Application: If the carbon is crowded (bulky groups), SN2 is blocked; look for methyl or primary carbon.

SOLVENT Rule
Protic = SN1, Aprotic = SN2

Application: Check the solvent: water/alcohol means SN1/E1; acetone/DMSO/DMF means SN2/E2.

🎯 Key Exam Points
  • SN2 reactions proceed with 100% inversion of stereochemical configuration at the reaction center.
  • SN1 reactions give racemic mixtures, though slight excess of inversion is often observed due to ion-pair shielding.
  • Rate of SN2 is independent of nucleophile concentration only in appearance if the nucleophile is the solvent (solvolysis).
  • Fluoride ion is a poor leaving group because of strong carbon-fluorine bond energy and high solvation energy in protic media.
  • Aryl halides undergo nucleophilic aromatic substitution (SNAr) only when strongly activated by electron-withdrawing groups at ortho/para positions.
  • Primary neopentyl halides resist SN2 substitution entirely due to extreme steric hindrance by adjacent methyl groups.
🚫 Common Errors & Confusions
  • Assuming SN1 always gives a completely racemic product without considering ion-pair shielding effects.
  • Confusing polar protic and polar aprotic solvents when determining reaction mechanisms.
  • Forgetting that strong nucleophiles can also act as strong bases, leading to elimination side products.
  • Neglecting carbocation rearrangements (hydride or methyl shifts) in SN1 reactions.

⚡ Quick Revision: Simple Substitution Reactions

Core formulas, rapid recall points, and distinguishing criteria at a glance.

Substitution Kinetics

Aliphatic substitution proceeds via bimolecular (SN2) or unimolecular (SN1) pathways depending on substrate structure and reaction conditions.

Stereochemical Outcomes

SN2 results in inversion of configuration via backside attack, whereas SN1 proceeds through a planar carbocation yielding a racemic mixture.

Substrate Effects

Steric hindrance retards SN2 reactions (methyl > 1° > 2°), whereas carbocation stability accelerates SN1 reactions (3° > 2° > 1°).

Reagents and Environment

Polar aprotic solvents and strong nucleophiles favor SN2, whereas polar protic solvents and weak nucleophiles favor SN1 pathways.

📚 Terms & Units Glossary

Nucleophile (Nu-)
An electron-rich species that donates an electron pair to form a chemical bond.
Leaving Group (X-)
An atom or group that departs with an electron pair during heterolytic bond cleavage.
Walden Inversion (Stereochemical reversal)
The inversion of a chiral center during a concerted SN2 displacement.
Solvolysis (Reaction with solvent)
Substitution reaction where the solvent acts as the nucleophile.
Carbocation (R3C+)
A reactive intermediate featuring a trivalent, sp2-hybridized carbon with a positive charge.
Ion Pair (Solvent-separated pair)
Associated carbocation and leaving group held together by electrostatic forces in solution.

🎯 Key Takeaways

  • SN2 rate depends on both substrate and nucleophile concentrations.
  • SN1 rate depends solely on the substrate concentration.
  • Leaving group ability follows: I- > Br- > Cl- >> F-.
  • Polar protic solvents favor SN1; polar aprotic solvents favor SN2.
  • Carbocation rearrangements occur in SN1 via 1,2-hydride or 1,2-alkyl shifts.

📌 Quick Recall

  • Simple substitution reactions are classified into aliphatic nucleophilic and aromatic electrophilic/nucleophilic types.
  • Steric hindrance controls SN2 while electronic stabilization controls SN1.
  • Stereochemistry is a definitive diagnostic tool to distinguish SN1 and SN2 pathways.
  • Solvent polarity and nucleophile strength dictate the operative mechanism.

⚖️ Key Differences

SN1 Mechanism VS SN2 Mechanism

SN1 is unimolecular with a carbocation intermediate and racemization; SN2 is bimolecular, concerted, and gives inversion.

Question 1 of 18

Which alkyl halide undergoes the fastest SN2 reaction with sodium iodide in acetone?

Explanation: Bromide is a better leaving group than chloride, and primary substrates react much faster via SN2 than secondary ones. 1-Bromobutane reacts faster than 1-chlorobutane or 2-bromobutane.

Question 2 of 18

What is the stereochemical outcome of an SN2 reaction at a chiral carbon center?

Explanation: The SN2 mechanism proceeds via backside attack by the nucleophile, resulting in complete inversion of configuration (Walden inversion).

Question 3 of 18

Which solvent best promotes an SN2 reaction?

Explanation: Acetone is a polar aprotic solvent. Polar aprotic solvents enhance nucleophile reactivity by not strongly solvating anions, favoring SN2 reactions.

Question 4 of 18

Which of the following carbocations is the most stable intermediate in an SN1 reaction?

Explanation: Tertiary carbocations are stabilized by hyperconjugation and inductive effects from three alkyl groups, making (CH3)3C+ the most stable.

Question 5 of 18

What is the rate law for an SN1 reaction?

Explanation: SN1 is unimolecular in the rate-determining step, so the rate depends only on the concentration of the substrate: Rate = k[Substrate].

Question 6 of 18

Why does neopentyl chloride react extremely slowly in SN2 reactions?

Explanation: Neopentyl chloride has three methyl groups on the beta-carbon, creating severe steric crowding that blocks the backside attack required for SN2.

Question 7 of 18

Which of the following is the best leaving group in nucleophilic substitution?

Explanation: Iodide is the weakest base among the halide ions and the conjugate acid of a strong acid (HI), making it the best leaving group.

Question 8 of 18

Which reaction intermediate is characteristic of SN1 reactions?

Explanation: SN1 reactions proceed in two steps via a carbocation intermediate formed in the rate-determining ionization step.

Question 9 of 18

Which condition favors elimination (E2) over substitution (SN2) with a primary alkyl halide?

Explanation: Bulky strong bases hinder nucleophilic substitution and promote bimolecular elimination (E2), especially at higher temperatures.

Question 10 of 18

What is the molecularity of an SN2 reaction?

Explanation: SN2 stands for Substitution Nucleophilic Bimolecular, meaning two species are involved in the transition state of the rate-determining step.

Question 11 of 18

Which of the following compounds will solvolyze most rapidly in aqueous ethanol via SN1?

Explanation: 2-Chloro-2-methylpropane is a tertiary alkyl halide and forms a stable tertiary carbocation, reacting fastest via SN1.

Question 12 of 18

What effect does increasing solvent polarity (from ethanol to water) have on the rate of SN1 solvolysis?

Explanation: More polar protic solvents better stabilize the polar transition state and the ionic intermediate formed in SN1, increasing the rate.

Question 13 of 18

Which of the following nucleophiles is ambidentate?

Explanation: The cyanide ion (CN-) can attack through carbon (forming nitriles) or nitrogen (forming isocyanides), making it an ambident nucleophile.

Question 14 of 18

In the reaction of (R)-2-bromobutane with sodium hydroxide to give 2-butanol via SN2, what is the stereochemical result?

Explanation: SN2 gives complete inversion of configuration. Starting with (R)-2-bromobutane yields (S)-2-butanol with 100% inversion.

Question 15 of 18

Which functional group cannot act as a leaving group without prior protonation or conversion?

Explanation: Hydroxide ion (OH-) is a strong base and a poor leaving group. It must be protonated to water (-OH2+) to leave.

Question 16 of 18

Which substrate undergoes SN1 substitution with rearrangement?

Explanation: Secondary substrates prone to carbocation formation often undergo hydride or alkyl shifts to form more stable tertiary carbocations.

Question 17 of 18

What is the role of polar aprotic solvents in SN2 reactions?

Explanation: Polar aprotic solvents solvate cations well but leave anions (nucleophiles) bare and active, accelerating SN2 rates.

Question 18 of 18

Which alkyl halide is completely unreactive toward SN1 and SN2 mechanisms under normal conditions?

Explanation: Vinyl chloride has sp2 hybridization and partial double-bond character, making the C-Cl bond strong and resistant to substitution.

Question 5 of 10
In an SN2 reaction, if the configuration of the reactant is (S), what is the stereochemistry of the inverted product?

Solution: SN2 proceeds with complete inversion of configuration, converting an (S) configuration to an (R) configuration.

Which halide is least reactive in both SN1 and SN2 reactions?

Solution: Vinyl chloride has sp2 carbon-halogen bonding and partial double-bond character, rendering it inert to simple substitution.

Question 7 of 10

Solution: Water is a neutral, weak nucleophile that participates in solvolysis reactions following the SN1 mechanism.

Question 8 of 10

Solution: Tosylates and iodides are exceptional leaving groups due to resonance stabilization and weak basicity.

Solution: Protic solvents like water hydrogen-bond to nucleophiles, lowering their ground-state energy and decreasing SN2 reactivity.
