Organic Chemistry

Simple Neutralization Reactions

Simple neutralization reactions involve the stoichiometric combination of an acid and a base to yield a salt and water. The fundamental chemical driving force is the combination of hydronium ions with hydroxide ions to form undissociated water.

UP PGT Chemistry Subject Code 02 B.Sc. / M.Sc. Level

Stoichiometry and Ionic Mechanisms

  • A simple neutralization reaction proceeds via the net ionic equation H+(aq) + OH-(aq) → H2O(l).
  • The reaction of a strong acid and a strong base produces a neutral aqueous solution with a pH of 7.0 at 298 K.
  • Equivalence point determination relies on the stoichiometric mole ratio defined by the basicity of the acid and acidity of the base.

Thermodynamics of Neutralization

  • The standard enthalpy of neutralization for strong acids and strong bases is constant at approximately -57.1 kJ/mol due to the identical net ionic process.
  • Weak acids or weak bases exhibit lower absolute neutralization enthalpies because a fraction of the reaction energy is consumed in their dissociation.
  • Neutralization is an exothermic process characterized by a negative Gibbs free energy change (ΔG < 0).

Salt Hydrolysis and Solution pH

  • Salts derived from strong acids and weak bases undergo cationic hydrolysis, yielding acidic solutions with pH < 7.
  • Salts derived from weak acids and strong bases undergo anionic hydrolysis, yielding basic solutions with pH > 7.
  • Salts of strong acids and strong bases do not undergo hydrolysis, maintaining neutral pH equivalence.
📐 Key Formulas & Equations
Enthalpy of Neutralization
ΔHneut = -57.1 kJ/mol
Conditions: Standard neutralization of a strong acid by a strong base in dilute aqueous solution.
Units: ΔH in kJ per mole of water formed.
Degree of Hydrolysis for Salt of Weak Acid and Strong Base
h = √(Kw / (Ka × C))
Conditions: Dilute aqueous solutions at 298 K.
Units: Kw, Ka are dissociation constants; C is molar concentration.
pH of Salt Solution (Weak Acid and Strong Base)
pH = 7 + 12pKa + 12log C
Conditions: Hydrolysis of anion derived from weak acid.
Units: Concentration C in mol/L.
Titration Stoichiometry Equation
N1V1 = N2V2
Conditions: Applicable at the equivalence point for complete neutralization.
Units: N1, N2 in normality (eq/L); V1, V2 in volume units (mL or L).
⚠️ Important Exceptions & Anomalies
Exception Enthalpy of neutralization for strong acid and strong base is universally -57.1 kJ/mol.

Observation: Fluorhydric acid (HF) or cyanic acid (HCN) neutralization yields different enthalpy values. — HF is a weak acid and forms strong hydrogen bonds; additional energy is required to break H-F bonds during dissociation.

Exception Neutralization of polyprotic acids occurs in a single step.

Observation: Polyprotic acids like H3PO4 or H2SO4 neutralize in stepwise dissociation equilibria. — Successive dissociation constants (Ka1, Ka2, Ka3) differ significantly, causing distinct inflection points on titration curves.

💡 Shortcuts & Memory Aids
Normality Factor Shortcut
Milliequivalents = N × V(mL) = (Mass / Equivalent Weight) × 1000

Application: Use milliequivalents directly in titration problems to avoid tedious conversion of volumes to liters and moles.

Nature of Salt Mnemonic
SA + SB = Neutral; WA + SB = Basic; SA + WB = Acidic

Application: Instantly predict the pH nature of salt solutions by identifying the parent acid and base strengths.

Enthalpy Deficit Calculation
ΔHobserved = -57.1 + Enthalpy of Ionization

Application: Quickly compute weak acid neutralization thermochemistry by accounting for ionization endothermicity.

🎯 Key Exam Points
  • The net ionic equation for the neutralization of any strong acid by a strong base is H+ + OH- → H2O.
  • Enthalpy of neutralization of strong acid and strong base is independent of the nature of the acid and base.
  • The pH at the equivalence point of a weak acid-strong base titration is always greater than 7.
  • Phenolphthalein is the suitable indicator for titrating a weak acid against a strong base due to its pH transition range of 8.2 to 10.0.
  • Methyl orange is suitable for titrating a strong acid against a weak base with a pH transition range of 3.1 to 4.4.
  • Normality equation N1V1 = N2V2 is valid only at the exact equivalence point of neutralization.
🚫 Common Errors & Confusions
  • Confusing molarity and normality for polyprotic acids like H2SO4 during stoichiometric calculations.
  • Assuming the enthalpy of neutralization for weak acids is identical to that of strong acids.
  • Selecting methyl orange as an indicator for titrating acetic acid with sodium hydroxide.
  • Neglecting the temperature dependence of water autoionization constant (Kw) during pH calculations at non-standard temperatures.

⚡ Quick Revision: Simple Neutralization Reactions

Core formulas, rapid recall points, and distinguishing criteria at a glance.

Stoichiometric Neutralization

Neutralization relies on the exact stoichiometric equivalence of hydronium and hydroxide ions. Calculations utilize normality equations to determine unknown concentrations.

Thermochemical Consistency

Strong acid-base reactions exhibit a constant enthalpy change of -57.1 kJ/mol. Deviations in weak systems correspond to enthalpies of ionization.

Salt Hydrolysis Effects

Neutralization products undergo hydrolysis depending on the parent electrolyte strengths, determining the resultant equivalence pH.

Indicator Selection Principles

Indicators must exhibit a pKin close to the equivalence point pH to ensure sharp color transitions during titrimetric analysis.

📚 Terms & Units Glossary

Neutralization (Acid + Base → Salt + Water)
Chemical reaction between an acid and a base.
Equivalence Point (Stoichiometric point)
Point where moles of added titrant equal moles of analyte.
Enthalpy of Neutralization (ΔHneut)
Heat released when one mole of water is formed by neutralization in kJ/mol.
Salt Hydrolysis (Anion/Cation reaction with H2O)
Reaction of salt ions with water altering pH.
Indicator (pH-sensitive dye)
Organic compound changing color at specific pH ranges.
Normality (N = eq/L)
Gram equivalent weight of solute per liter of solution.

🎯 Key Takeaways

  • Strong acid + Strong base → Neutral salt + H2O (ΔH = -57.1 kJ/mol)
  • Weak acid + Strong base → Basic salt + H2O (pH > 7)
  • Strong acid + Weak base → Acidic salt + H2O (pH < 7)
  • Phenolphthalein range: 8.2 - 10.0 (Colorless to Pink)
  • Methyl orange range: 3.1 - 4.4 (Red to Yellow)

📌 Quick Recall

  • Neutralization is fundamentally the combination of H+ and OH- ions.
  • Thermochemistry confirms constant heat release for strong electrolytes.
  • Hydrolysis of conjugate species determines salt solution pH.
  • Titration curves reflect the buffering regions and equivalence inflections.

⚖️ Key Differences

End Point VS Equivalence Point

End point is observed color change of indicator; equivalence point is exact stoichiometric neutralization.

Question 1 of 20

What is the standard enthalpy of neutralization of a strong acid by a strong base in dilute aqueous solution?

Explanation: The standard enthalpy of neutralization for strong acids and bases is constant at -57.1 kJ/mol because it corresponds to the formation of one mole of liquid water from H+ and OH-.

Question 2 of 20

Which of the following salts produces an alkaline solution upon dissolution in water?

Explanation: CH3COONa is a salt of a weak acid (CH3COOH) and a strong base (NaOH). The acetate ion undergoes anionic hydrolysis, producing OH- ions and rendering the solution alkaline.

Question 3 of 20

What is the pH at the equivalence point when a strong acid is titrated against a strong base at 298 K?

Explanation: The titration of a strong acid with a strong base yields a neutral salt and water, resulting in a neutral pH of 7 at 298 K.

Question 4 of 20

Which indicator is most suitable for titrating acetic acid with sodium hydroxide?

Explanation: Acetic acid is a weak acid and sodium hydroxide is a strong base; the equivalence point lies in the basic pH range (8-10), making phenolphthalein ideal.

Question 5 of 20

What is the net ionic equation for the neutralization of HCN by KOH?

Explanation: Since HCN is a weak acid, it remains largely undissociated in solution; hence, the net ionic equation must include the intact weak acid molecule reacting with hydroxide.

Question 6 of 20

Calculate the volume of 0.1 M NaOH required to completely neutralize 50 mL of 0.2 M HCl.

Explanation: Using N1V1 = N2V2: (0.2 M)(50 mL) = (0.1 M)(V2), yielding V2 = 100 mL.

Question 7 of 20

Why is the enthalpy of neutralization of HF less than -57.1 kJ/mol?

Explanation: HF is a weak acid with high bond dissociation energy; part of the heat released during H+ + OH- combination is absorbed in dissociating undissociated HF molecules.

Question 8 of 20

Which salt solution will have a pH less than 7?

Explanation: NH4Cl is derived from a strong acid (HCl) and a weak base (NH4OH). Cationic hydrolysis of NH4+ produces hydronium ions, resulting in an acidic pH.

Question 9 of 20

What term describes the point in a titration where the indicator changes color?

Explanation: The experimental point where the indicator changes color is defined as the end point, which approximates the theoretical equivalence point.

Question 10 of 20

What is the normality of a solution containing 4.9 g of H2SO4 in 500 mL of solution?

Explanation: Equivalent weight of H2SO4 = 98/2 = 49 g/eq. Gram equivalents = 4.9/49 = 0.1 eq. Normality = 0.1 eq / 0.5 L = 0.2 N. Wait, 0.1 eq in 0.5 L is 0.2 N. Let's verify: (4.9/49)/(500/1000) = 0.1/0.5 = 0.2 N.

Question 11 of 20

Which of the following acid-base pairs yields a neutral solution upon complete reaction?

Explanation: HNO3 is a strong acid and KOH is a strong base; their reaction produces KNO3, which does not hydrolyze, yielding a neutral solution.

Question 12 of 20

During the titration of a weak acid with a strong base, the buffer region occurs:

Explanation: Before the equivalence point, unreacted weak acid and its conjugate base salt form a buffer solution.

Question 13 of 20

What is the basicity of phosphoric acid (H3PO4)?

Explanation: Phosphoric acid contains three replaceable hydrogen atoms bonded to oxygen, giving it a basicity of 3.

Question 14 of 20

Which ion undergoes hydrolysis in an aqueous solution of sodium cyanide (NaCN)?

Explanation: Na+ is the spectator ion of a strong base (NaOH), while CN- is the conjugate base of a weak acid (HCN) and undergoes hydrolysis.

Question 15 of 20

What is the equivalent weight of H3PO4 when it reacts with excess NaOH to form Na3PO4?

Explanation: Equivalent weight = Molecular weight / n-factor. Here n-factor = 3. Eq. Wt = 98 / 3 = 32.67 g/eq.

Question 16 of 20

Which of the following statements is true regarding neutralization reactions?

Explanation: Neutralization reactions release heat and are invariably exothermic (ΔH < 0).

Question 17 of 20

What happens to the pH when a strong acid is added dropwise to a strong base solution?

Explanation: Adding a strong acid consumes hydroxide ions and increases hydronium ion concentration, causing the pH to decrease.

Question 18 of 20

The degree of hydrolysis (h) of a salt of a weak acid and weak base is:

Explanation: For salts of weak acids and weak bases (e.g., CH3COONH4), the hydrolysis formula h = √(Kw / (KaKb)) shows it is independent of concentration C.

Question 19 of 20

Which apparatus is standard for delivering precise volumes of titrant in neutralization titrations?

Explanation: A burette is calibrated to deliver variable, highly accurate volumes of titrant during titration.

Question 20 of 20

What is the product formed when sulfur dioxide (an acidic oxide) reacts with sodium hydroxide?

Explanation: Acidic oxides like SO2 react with bases to form corresponding salts and water: SO2 + 2NaOH → Na2SO3 + H2O.

Question 1 of 10
What volume of 0.2 M H2SO4 is required to neutralize 40 mL of 0.1 M NaOH?

Solution: Using N1V1 = N2V2 with normality of H2SO4 = 0.4 N and NaOH = 0.1 N: (0.4)(V1) = (0.1)(40), so V1 = 10 mL.

Question 2 of 10
Which of the following pairs represents a buffer system when mixed in equivalent proportions?

Solution: A weak acid (CH3COOH) and its salt with a strong base (CH3COONa) constitute an acidic buffer solution.

Question 3 of 10
What is the equivalent weight of oxalic acid dihydrate (H2C2O4·2H2O, Mol. Wt. = 126) in an acid-base neutralization reaction?

Solution: Oxalic acid is a dibasic acid (n-factor = 2). Equivalent weight = 126 / 2 = 63 g/eq.

Question 4 of 10
The pH of a solution formed by mixing 50 mL of 0.1 M HCl and 50 mL of 0.1 M NaOH is:

Solution: Equimolar quantities of strong acid and strong base react completely to form a neutral salt solution with pH 7.

Question 5 of 10
Which oxide reacts with water to form an acid and with alkali to form a salt?

Solution: SO2 is an acidic oxide; it reacts with water to form H2SO3 and with alkali to form sulfite salts.

Question 6 of 10
In the titration of a weak base against a strong acid, the pH at the equivalence point is:

Solution: Titration of a weak base with a strong acid produces a salt that undergoes cationic hydrolysis, resulting in an acidic equivalence pH (< 7).

Question 7 of 10
What is the degree of dissociation of water at 298 K given Kw = 10-14?

Solution: α = [H+] / [H2O] = (10-7) / 55.5 = 1.8 × 10-9.

Question 8 of 10
Which indicator color change marks the end point in titration of HCl with NaOH using methyl orange?

Solution: Methyl orange is red in acidic medium and turns yellow in basic medium; during titration of acid with base, it transitions from red to yellow.

Question 9 of 10
Calculate the mass of NaOH required to neutralize 100 mL of 0.5 M H2SO4.

Solution: Milliequivalents of H2SO4 = 0.5 N × 2 × 100 = 100 meq. Mass of NaOH = (100 × 40) / 1000 = 4.0 g.

Question 10 of 10
Why does acetic acid have a lower heat of neutralization than hydrochloric acid when reacted with sodium hydroxide?

Solution: Acetic acid is a weak electrolyte and requires endothermic bond dissociation of undissociated molecules, lowering the net exothermic heat output.
