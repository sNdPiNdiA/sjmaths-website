Organic Chemistry

Nitration

Nitration involves the electrophilic aromatic substitution of a hydrogen atom on an aromatic ring by a nitro group using a nitrating mixture. The active electrophile generated in standard mixed-acid nitration is the nitronium ion (NO2+).

UP PGT Chemistry Subject Code 02 B.Sc. / M.Sc. Level

Electrophile Generation and Acid-Base Equilibria

  • Nitric acid acts as a base in the presence of concentrated sulfuric acid due to the stronger protonating capability of H2SO4.
  • Protonated nitric acid loses a water molecule to generate the active electrophile, the nitronium ion (NO2+).
  • The concentration of NO2+ is directly proportional to the acidity function (H0) of the medium.

Reaction Mechanism and Kinetics

  • Nitration proceeds via an Electrophilic Aromatic Substitution (SEAr) mechanism comprising two primary steps: electrophile addition and proton loss.
  • The formation of the non-aromatic carbocation intermediate (Wheland intermediate or sigma complex) is the rate-determining step.
  • Primary kinetic isotope effect (KIE) is typically unity (≈ 1) because C-H bond cleavage occurs rapidly after the rate-determining step.

Substrate Directing and Activating Effects

  • Activating groups (-OH, -NH2, -R) increase electron density on the ring and direct incoming nitro groups to ortho and para positions.
  • Deactivating groups (-NO2, -CN, -COOH) withdraw electron density and direct substitution exclusively to the meta position.
  • Steric hindrance in ortho-para directing groups often dictates the thermodynamic versus kinetic ratio of ortho to para isomers.

⚠️ Important Exceptions & Anomalies
Exception Anilinium ion undergoes meta-nitration due to strong electron-withdrawing ammonium (-NH3+) group.

Observation: Direct nitration of aniline yields significant amounts of tar and para-nitroaniline along with meta isomer. — Acid-base equilibrium forms free aniline in trace amounts, which is extremely reactive toward electrophilic attack, alongside oxidation side-reactions.

Exception Electrophilic aromatic substitution exhibits zero primary kinetic isotope effect when C-H bond breaking is not rate-determining.

Observation: Certain highly hindered or sterically congested substrates show a substantial secondary or primary KIE. — Reversibility of the sigma complex formation step makes proton abstraction partially rate-determining.

🎯 Key Exam Points
  • The active nitrating species in concentrated nitric-sulfuric acid mixture is the nitronium ion (NO2+).
  • Sulfuric acid acts as a Brønsted-Lowry acid, whereas nitric acid acts as a Brønsted-Lowry base in the electrophile generation step.
  • The rate law for standard aromatic nitration is first order in aromatic substrate and first order in nitronium ion: Rate = k[ArH][NO2+].
  • Halogens (-Cl, -Br, -I) are deactivating groups but direct incoming electrophiles to the ortho and para positions due to lone pair resonance donation.
  • Toluene undergoes nitration approximately 25 times faster than benzene due to the +I and hyperconjugative electron-releasing effects of the methyl group.
  • Nitrobenzene requires harsh reaction conditions (fuming HNO3 and concentrated H2SO4 at elevated temperatures) to achieve meta-dinitration.
🚫 Common Errors & Confusions
  • Assuming nitric acid acts as the acid in the electrophile generation step; it acts as a base when mixed with sulfuric acid.
  • Confusing halogen substitution behavior: forgetting that halogens deactivate the ring yet remain ortho/para directors.
  • Forgetting that aniline cannot be directly nitrated with mixed acid without protection (acetylation) due to oxidation and salt formation.
  • Writing incorrect formulas for the Wheland intermediate by placing the positive charge on the wrong carbon atom.

⚡ Quick Revision: Nitration

Core formulas, rapid recall points, and distinguishing criteria at a glance.

Electrophile Generation

Concentrated sulfuric acid protonates nitric acid, causing the elimination of water and forming the reactive nitronium ion (NO2+).

Wheland Intermediate

Attack of the nitronium ion on the aromatic ring yields a resonance-stabilized, non-aromatic sigma complex where sp3 and sp2 carbons coexist.

Substituent Effects

Electron-donating groups stabilize the Wheland intermediate and accelerate ortho/para nitration, whereas electron-withdrawing groups deactivate the ring toward meta attack.

Synthetic Applications

Aromatic nitro compounds serve as critical intermediates for subsequent reduction to primary aromatic amines, feeding into diazonium salt chemistry.

📚 Terms & Units Glossary

Nitronium Ion (NO2+)
The active linear electrophile species in aromatic nitration.
Wheland Intermediate (Sigma Complex)
Non-aromatic charged carbocation intermediate formed during SEAr.
Activating Group (+I / +R Substituent)
Substituent that increases electron density and accelerates ring substitution.
Deactivating Group (-I / -R Substituent)
Substituent that withdraws electron density and deactivates the aromatic ring.
Mixed Acid (HNO3 + H2SO4)
Standard reagent mixture utilized for generating nitronium ions.
Kinetic Isotope Effect (KIE)
Ratio of reaction rates using isotopically labeled reactants.

🎯 Key Takeaways

  • HNO3 + 2H2SO4 → NO2+ + H3O+ + 2HSO4-
  • Rate = k[ArH][NO2+]
  • Halogens deactivate the ring but are ortho/para directors.
  • Aniline requires acetylation prior to nitration to prevent oxidation.
  • Nitro group is a strongly deactivating meta-director.

📌 Quick Recall

  • Nitration proceeds via electrophilic aromatic substitution involving NO2+.
  • H2SO4 protonates HNO3 to generate the nitronium electrophile.
  • Ring substituents dictate regioselectivity (ortho/para vs meta) and relative reaction rates.
  • The rate-determining step is the formation of the non-aromatic sigma complex.

⚖️ Key Differences

Activating Ortho/Para Director VS Deactivating Ortho/Para Director

Activating groups (e.g., -OH) increase ring electron density; halogens are deactivating due to electronegativity but direct ortho/para via lone-pair resonance.

Which of the following groups is meta-directing during electrophilic aromatic substitution?

Question 4 of 19

Why does direct nitration of aniline with mixed acid yield tar and side products?

Explanation: Strong oxidizing nitric acid oxidizes aniline into dark tarry masses, and salt formation deactivates the ring unpredictably.

Question 5 of 19

What is the rate-determining step in standard aromatic nitration?

Explanation: The addition of the electrophile to form the non-aromatic Wheland intermediate breaks aromaticity and is the slow, rate-determining step.

Question 6 of 19

Halogens are unusual in electrophilic substitution because they are:

Explanation: Halogens possess -I effect (deactivating) but +R effect through lone pairs, directing substitution to ortho/para positions.

Question 7 of 19

Which molecule undergoes nitration at the fastest rate under identical conditions?

Explanation: Toluene possesses an activating methyl group (+I and hyperconjugation) that increases electron density on the ring, speeding up nitration.

Question 8 of 19

What is the stoichiometric ratio of concentrated H2SO4 required to generate one mole of NO2+ from HNO3?

Explanation: Equation: HNO3 + 2H2SO4 → NO2+ + H3O+ + 2HSO4-. Two moles of sulfuric acid are consumed.

Question 9 of 19

The intermediate formed during electrophilic aromatic nitration is known as:

Explanation: The resonance-stabilized carbocation intermediate formed by electrophilic addition is called the Wheland intermediate or sigma complex.

Question 10 of 19

Which reagent is typically used to protect aniline before nitration?

Explanation: Aniline is reacted with acetic anhydride to form acetanilide, moderating the reactivity of the amino group.

Question 11 of 19

What is the primary kinetic isotope effect (kH/kD) observed in most standard aromatic nitrations?

Explanation: Since proton abstraction occurs rapidly after the rate-determining electrophilic addition, primary KIE is typically near unity.

Question 12 of 19

Which substituent exhibits both -I and +R effects?

Explanation: The methoxy group (-OCH3) withdraws electron density via induction (-I) due to oxygen electronegativity, but donates electron density via resonance (+R) through lone pairs.

Question 13 of 19

What type of reaction is aromatic nitration?

Explanation: Nitration is a classic example of Electrophilic Aromatic Substitution (SEAr).

Question 14 of 19

Why is nitrobenzene resistant to further nitration?

Explanation: The nitro group withdraws electron density strongly, deactivating the ring and requiring harsh conditions for dinitration.

Question 15 of 19

Which of the following is an activating group for electrophilic substitution?

Explanation: The amino group (-NH2) strongly activates the aromatic ring through lone-pair resonance donation.

Question 16 of 19

What is the role of sulfuric acid in the nitration of benzene?

Explanation: Sulfuric acid serves as a catalyst that protonates nitric acid to produce the active nitronium electrophile.

Question 17 of 19

In the nitration of chlorobenzene, the major product is:

Explanation: Halogens are ortho/para-directing groups despite being ring deactivators.

Question 18 of 19

What is the geometry of the nitronium ion (NO2+)?

Explanation: The nitronium ion (O=N=O)+ is a linear species with sp hybridization at the central nitrogen atom.

Question 19 of 19

Which acid combination is termed 'mixed acid'?

Explanation: The classic nitrating mixture consists of concentrated nitric acid and concentrated sulfuric acid.

Question 1 of 10
Which species is responsible for protonating nitric acid in the nitrating mixture?

Solution: Sulfuric acid has a higher acid dissociation constant and acts as the proton donor toward nitric acid.

Question 2 of 10
What is the hybridization state of the nitrogen atom in the nitronium ion?

Solution: The linear O=N=O structure of NO2+ corresponds to sp hybridization at the central nitrogen atom.

Question 3 of 10
Why is acetanilide used instead of aniline for mononitration?

Solution: Acetylation masks the strongly activating -NH2 group, preventing oxidation and extensive side reactions.

Question 4 of 10
Which of the following possesses the highest nitration rate?

Solution: Phenol contains a strongly activating -OH group, making its aromatic ring extremely reactive toward electrophilic attack.

Question 5 of 10
In the mechanism of nitration, loss of a proton from the sigma complex restores:

Solution: Proton abstraction by bisulfate ion restores the stable aromatic sextet of electrons.

Question 6 of 10
Which directing group category does the -SO3H group belong to?

Solution: The sulfonic acid group (-SO3H) is electron-withdrawing and acts as a meta-director.

Question 7 of 10
What happens to the rate of nitration when deuterium replaces hydrogen in benzene (C6D6 vs C6H6)?

Solution: Because C-H bond breaking occurs after the rate-determining step, there is no primary isotope effect, and rates are similar.

Question 8 of 10
Which reagent combination is known to achieve mild nitration of highly reactive substrates?

Solution: Acetyl nitrate provides milder nitrating conditions suitable for reactive aromatic rings like phenols and alkoxybenzenes.

Question 9 of 10
How many resonance structures can be drawn for the Wheland intermediate formed during benzene nitration?

Solution: The sigma complex has 3 major contributing resonance structures that delocalize the positive charge across the ortho and para positions.

Question 10 of 10
Which of the following nitro derivatives is formed as the major product during the nitration of toluene at room temperature?

Solution: The methyl group in toluene is an ortho/para-director, yielding a mixture of ortho- and para-nitrotoluene as major products.
