Preparation of Alkenes

Alkenes are unsaturated hydrocarbons containing a carbon-carbon double bond, synthesized via elimination or partial reduction methods. These pathways generally follow concerted or stepwise mechanisms yielding acyclic or cyclic monoenes and polyenes.

UP PGT Chemistry Subject Code 02 B.Sc. / M.Sc. Level

Dehydrohalogenation and Zaitsev's Rule

  • Alkyl halides undergo E2 elimination in the presence of strong alcoholic bases like KOH or NaOEt.
  • According to Zaitsev's rule, the major product is the most substituted and thermodynamically stable alkene.
  • Bulky bases (e.g., potassium tert-butoxide) direct elimination toward the less substituted hydrogen, yielding the Hoffmann product as the major isomer due to steric hindrance.

Dehydration of Alcohols and Carbocation Rearrangements

  • Acid-catalyzed dehydration of alcohols proceeds via an E1 mechanism involving protonation, water departure, and carbocation formation.
  • Secondary and tertiary carbocations frequently undergo hydride or alkyl shifts to form more stable carbocation intermediates prior to deprotonation.
  • Primary alcohols often react via concerted E2 pathways under harsh acidic conditions without forming free primary carbocations.

Reduction of Alkynes and Wittig Olefination

  • Catalytic hydrogenation of alkynes over Lindlar catalyst (palladium deposited on calcium carbonate or barium sulfate, poisoned with quinoline) yields cis-alkenes.
  • Dissolving metal reduction using sodium in liquid ammonia selectively produces trans-alkenes via radical-anion intermediates.
  • The Wittig reaction converts aldehydes or ketones into alkenes using phosphonium ylides, forming an oxaphosphetane intermediate that collapses to an alkene and phosphine oxide.
📐 Key Formulas & Equations
Dehydration of Alcohols
R-CH(OH)-CH3 + H2SO4 (conc.) → R-CH=CH2 + H2O
Conditions: Concentrated acid catalyst (H2SO4 or H3PO4), heat (Δ)
Units: Elimination reaction producing alkene and water
Partial Hydrogenation of Alkynes (Lindlar Catalyst)
R-C≡C-R + H2 + Pd/BaSO4 → cis-R-CH=CH-R
Conditions: Poisoned palladium catalyst, quinoline
Units: Stereospecific syn-addition producing cis-alkene
Birch Reduction of Alkynes
R-C≡C-R + Na + liquid NH3 → trans-R-CH=CH-R
Conditions: Dissolving metal reduction in liquid ammonia at low temperature
Units: Stereoselective anti-addition producing trans-alkene
Vicinal Dihalide Dehalogenation
R-CH(X)-CH(X)-R + Zn → R-CH=CH-R + ZnX2
Conditions: Zinc dust in polar protic or aprotic solvent (e.g., ethanol/acetone)
Units: Anti-elimination producing alkene and zinc halide
Dehydrohalogenation of Alkyl Halides
R-CH2-CH2-X + KOH (alc) → R-CH=CH2 + KX + H2O
Conditions: Alkaline alcoholic medium, heat
Units: Stoichiometric reaction yielding alkenes, potassium halide, and water
⚠️ Important Exceptions & Anomalies
Exception Zaitsev's rule predicts the most substituted alkene as the major product in elimination reactions.

Observation: Hoffmann (least substituted) alkene predominates. — Steric hindrance from bulky bases (like potassium tert-butoxide) or poor leaving groups (such as ammonium salts in Hofmann elimination) favor abstraction of the more accessible terminal proton.

Exception Dehydration of alcohols proceeds via carbocation intermediates with standard rearrangement.

Observation: Rearrangement does not occur in primary linear alcohols under milder catalytic conditions or concerted E2 routes. — Absence of a discrete primary carbocation intermediate prevents skeletal isomerization.

🎯 Key Exam Points
  • Dehydration of alcohols follows the reactivity order: 3° > 2° > 1° due to carbocation stability.
  • Lindlar catalyst is poisoned to prevent complete hydrogenation of alkynes to alkanes.
  • Dehydrohalogenation of alkyl halides follows E2 kinetics: Rate = k[Alkyl Halide][Base].
  • Geminal dihalides yield alkynes, whereas vicinal dihalides yield alkenes upon treatment with zinc dust.
  • Primary alcohols require concentrated H2SO4 at 443 K for dehydration, whereas tertiary alcohols require 20% H2SO4 at 358 K.
  • Kolbe electrolytic decarboxylation of dicarboxylate salts yields alkenes at the anode.
🚫 Common Errors & Confusions
  • Assuming all alcohol dehydrations yield the Zaitsev product without considering carbocation rearrangements.
  • Confusing the stereochemical outcome of Lindlar reduction (cis) with Birch reduction (trans).
  • Forgetting that bulky bases override Zaitsev's rule to yield Hofmann products.
  • Treating geminal halides with zinc instead of vicinal halides when attempting alkene preparation.

⚡ Quick Revision: Preparation of Alkenes

Core formulas, rapid recall points, and distinguishing criteria at a glance.

Elimination Reactions

Dehydrohalogenation of alkyl halides and dehydration of alcohols proceed through elimination pathways (E1 or E2), generating carbon-carbon double bonds under the influence of heat and reagents.

Reduction Methods

Alkynes are partially hydrogenated to alkenes using stereoselective methods, yielding cis-alkenes with poisoned palladium catalysts and trans-alkenes with alkali metals in liquid ammonia.

Vicinal Halide Cleavage

Treatment of vicinal dibromides with active metals like zinc removes halogen atoms via stereospecific anti-elimination to construct pure alkenes.

Carbonyl Olefination

Wittig reactions couple aldehydes or ketones with phosphorus ylides to form alkenes with precise positioning of the double bond.

📚 Terms & Units Glossary

Zaitsev's Rule (Saytzeff Rule)
Empirical rule stating that elimination reactions yield the most substituted alkene as the major product.
Lindlar Catalyst (Pd/CaCO3 / BaSO4)
Poisoned heterogeneous palladium catalyst used for stereospecific syn-hydrogenation of alkynes to cis-alkenes.
Birch Reduction (Na/NH3(l))
Dissolving metal reduction converting internal alkynes into trans-alkenes via radical intermediates.
E2 Mechanism (Bimolecular Elimination)
Concerted elimination mechanism where bond-breaking and bond-forming occur simultaneously in a single transition state.
E1 Mechanism (Unimolecular Elimination)
Two-step elimination mechanism proceeding through a carbocation intermediate.
Wittig Reagent (Phosphorus Ylide)
Organophosphorus compound used to convert carbonyl oxygen atoms into carbon-carbon double bonds.

🎯 Key Takeaways

  • Alcohol dehydration reactivity: 3° > 2° > 1°
  • Lindlar catalyst gives cis-alkenes
  • Birch reduction gives trans-alkenes
  • Bulky bases give Hofmann elimination products
  • Zinc dust dehalogenation requires vicinal dihalides

📌 Quick Recall

  • Alkenes are prepared by removing atoms or groups from adjacent carbon atoms.
  • Acid-catalyzed dehydration involves carbocation rearrangements.
  • Base-induced dehydrohalogenation follows E2 kinetics for secondary and primary halides.
  • Stereoselective catalytic reductions allow precise control over geometric isomerism.

⚖️ Key Differences

Lindlar Catalyst VS Birch Reduction

Lindlar yields cis-alkenes via syn-addition; Birch yields trans-alkenes via anti-addition.

Explanation: The Wittig reaction couples aldehydes or ketones with phosphorus ylides to generate alkenes.

Question 13 of 19

Question 14 of 19

What happens to the rate of E2 elimination when the leaving group changes from chloride to iodide?

Question 15 of 19

Which of the following functional groups is absent in an alkene?

Explanation: Alkenes contain carbon-carbon double bonds (one sigma and one pi bond) between sp2 hybridized carbons, but no triple bonds.

Question 16 of 19

Explanation: Heating ethanol with concentrated sulfuric acid at 443 K causes intramolecular dehydration to form ethene.

Question 17 of 19

Which reduction method yields a trans alkene from an alkyne?

Explanation: Sodium in liquid ammonia performs dissolving metal reduction yielding trans alkenes.

Question 18 of 19

During E2 elimination, the orientation of the hydrogen and the leaving group must be:

Explanation: E2 elimination exhibits strict stereoelectronic requirements, demanding an anti-periplanar geometry for optimal orbital overlap.

Question 19 of 19

Which of the following is a non-concerted elimination pathway?

Explanation: E1 is a two-step, non-concerted pathway involving a discrete carbocation intermediate.
