Physical Properties of Haloalkanes

Haloalkanes exhibit distinct physical properties driven by the presence of polar carbon-halogen bonds and varying molecular masses. These properties govern their boiling points, melting points, density, and solubility behaviors in polar and non-polar media.

UP PGT Chemistry Subject Code 02 B.Sc. / M.Sc. Level

Melting and Boiling Points

  • The boiling points of haloalkanes follow the order of halogen size: R–I > R–Br > R–Cl > R–F due to increasing surface area and magnitude of van der Waals forces.
  • For isomeric haloalkanes, boiling point decreases with branching: straight-chain > branched-chain due to a decrease in surface area and weaker intermolecular contacts.
  • The melting points of p-isomers are consistently higher than those of their o- and m-isomers due to symmetrical crystal lattice packing.

Density and Specific Gravity

  • Haloalkanes containing chlorine, bromine, and iodine are generally heavier than water.
  • Density increases sequentially with the atomic number of the halogen: fluoroalkanes < chloroalkanes < bromoalkanes < iodoalkanes.
  • Polyhalogen derivatives such as polychlorides sink in water as their density exceeds 1.0 g/cm3.

Solubility and Polarity

  • Haloalkanes are only slightly soluble in water because energy required to break existing hydrogen bonds between water molecules is greater than the energy released.
  • They are highly soluble in organic solvents (benzene, ether, chloroform) because solute-solvent interactions closely match solute-solute and solvent-solvent interactions.
  • The C–X bond is polar, yet the net dipole moment depends on molecular geometry and symmetry (e.g., CCl4 is non-polar due to tetrahedral cancellation).

⚠️ Important Exceptions & Anomalies
Exception Alkyl fluorides are heavier than alkanes and have higher boiling points due to high polarity.

Observation: CH3F has a boiling point lower than expected relative to size. — Extremely small size of fluorine atom reduces the magnitude of instantaneous dipole-induced dipole (van der Waals) forces.

Exception Boiling points consistently increase with molecular weight in all haloalkane series.

Observation: Chloromethane (CH3Cl) and bromomethane (CH3Br) are gases at room temperature, whereas lower alkanes of comparable mass are also gases. — Substantial dipole-dipole attractions in low molecular weight halocarbons elevate their condensation temperatures compared to non-polar hydrocarbons.

Exception All haloalkanes are denser than water.

Observation: Monofluoroalkanes and monochloroalkanes (with low carbon chain length like CH3Cl) are lighter than water. — The low atomic mass of fluorine and single chlorine atom relative to the alkyl chain mass results in an overall density less than 1.0 g/cm3.

🎯 Key Exam Points
  • Haloalkanes possess polar C–X bonds, yet are immiscible with water due to failure to form hydrogen bonds.
  • Boiling point order for alkyl groups with same halogen: Primary > Secondary > Tertiary.
  • p-Dichlorobenzene has a significantly higher melting point than o- and m-isomers due to crystal lattice packing symmetry.
  • Iodoalkanes develop a violet-brown color on standing due to photochemical decomposition liberating free iodine.
  • Density of haloalkanes increases down the halogen group: F < Cl < Br < I.
  • Carbon-halogen bond length increases progressively from fluorine to iodine.
🚫 Common Errors & Confusions
  • Assuming all haloalkanes are denser than water; monohalogenated lower alkanes float on water.
  • Confusing the melting point trend (symmetry-based) with the boiling point trend (surface area-based) for isomeric dihalobenzenes.
  • Expecting haloalkanes to form hydrogen bonds with water molecules due to electronegative halogen atoms.
  • Confusing dipole moment magnitude with bond strength or bond length.

⚡ Quick Revision: Physical Properties of Haloalkanes

Core formulas, rapid recall points, and distinguishing criteria at a glance.

Nature of C–X Bond

The carbon-halogen bond is polar because halogens are significantly more electronegative than carbon, inducing a partial positive charge on carbon and a partial negative charge on the halogen.

Intermolecular Forces

Molecules of haloalkanes are held together by dipole-dipole interactions and London dispersion forces, which are substantially stronger than the weak van der Waals forces in parent alkanes.

Solubility Characteristics

Haloalkanes are insoluble in water because they cannot break the extensive network of hydrogen bonds present in water molecules, despite their polar nature.

Melting Point Symmetry Factor

Para-isomers pack more closely and efficiently into crystal lattices than ortho- or meta-isomers, requiring more thermal energy to break the lattice structure.

📚 Terms & Units Glossary

Dipole Moment (μ)
Product of electric charge and distance between charges, measured in Debye.
Van der Waals Forces (Fvdw)
Weak intermolecular forces increasing with molecular mass and surface area.
Polarity (Bond Polarity)
Unequal sharing of electrons resulting in fractional charges across C–X.
Specific Gravity (Density Ratio)
Ratio of substance density to water density at a specified temperature.
Isomerism (Structural Isomers)
Compounds with identical molecular formulas but different atomic arrangements.
Crystal Lattice (Solid State Packing)
Ordered repeating three-dimensional array of particles in a crystalline solid.

🎯 Key Takeaways

  • Boiling point order: R–I > R–Br > R–Cl > R–F
  • Density order relative to water: Polyhalogen > Monohalogen (mostly)
  • Branching decreases boiling point due to reduced surface area contact
  • p-Isomer has higher melting point than o- and m-isomers
  • Haloalkanes dissolve in organic solvents via dipole-induced dipole and dispersion forces

📌 Quick Recall

  • Physical properties of haloalkanes depend directly on polarity and molecular mass.
  • Boiling points scale with molecular weight and surface area.
  • Water insolubility arises from inability to break water H-bonds.
  • Density increases down the halogen group from fluorine to iodine.

⚖️ Key Differences

Boiling Point Trend VS Melting Point Trend

Boiling point depends strictly on molecular mass and surface area, whereas melting point depends heavily on crystal lattice symmetry.

Question 2 of 10
Why are alkyl fluorides exceptions to the general density trend of haloalkanes relative to water?

Solution: The light mass of fluorine combined with the alkyl group yields an overall density lower than water.

Question 3 of 10
Which of the following dihalobenzenes has a dipole moment equal to zero?

Solution: Para-dihalobenzene is a symmetrical molecule where opposing carbon-halogen dipoles cancel out completely, resulting in μ = 0.

Question 4 of 10
Identify the compound with the lowest boiling point among C5H11Cl isomers.

Solution: 1-chloro-2,2-dimethylpropane has maximum branching, spherical symmetry, and minimum surface area, leading to the lowest boiling point.

Question 5 of 10
Which physical property is primarily responsible for the separation of haloalkanes from aqueous layers in extraction?

Solution: Immiscibility with water and a density different from 1.0 g/cm3 allows layer separation in a separating funnel.

Question 6 of 10
What happens to the carbon-halogen bond enthalpy as we move from CH3F to CH3I?

Solution: Bond enthalpy decreases as overlap worsens and bond length increases down the halogen group.

Question 7 of 10
Which of the following is a gas at room temperature?

Solution: Chloromethane, bromomethane, and chloroethane (ethyl chloride, bp 12.3 °C) exist as gases or low boiling liquids depending on exact ambient temperature, but chloroethane is borderline gas/liquid. Here CH3Cl and CH3Br are gases.

Question 8 of 10
Which statement correctly describes the solubility of haloalkanes in polar solvents like alcohol?

Solution: Haloalkanes dissolve well in alcohol and other organic solvents because solute-solvent interactions are comparable to solvent-solvent interactions.

Question 9 of 10
The variation of melting point among isomeric diiodobenzenes follows which order?

Solution: Para-isomers have the highest melting point due to crystal symmetry, followed usually by ortho- or meta-isomers depending on crystal packing specifics.

Question 10 of 10
Why do primary haloalkanes have higher boiling points than tertiary isomers of the same molecular formula?

Solution: Primary haloalkanes are elongated, providing greater surface area for intermolecular contact and stronger London dispersion forces.
