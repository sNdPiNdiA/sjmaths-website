Heterolytic Fission

Heterolytic fission is the unsymmetrical cleavage of a covalent bond where both shared electrons remain with one of the two fragments. This process generates charged species known as a carbocation and an anion.

UP PGT Chemistry Subject Code 02 B.Sc. / M.Sc. Level

Mechanism and Electron Distribution

  • During heterolytic cleavage, the covalent bond breaks unequally with the more electronegative atom retaining the electron pair.
  • The resulting species are an electrophilic cation (such as a carbocation) and a nucleophilic anion.
  • Polar solvents facilitate heterolytic fission by stabilizing the generated ions through solvation.

Factors Governing Heterolytic Fission

  • Greater difference in electronegativity between bonded atoms increases the propensity for heterolytic rather than homolytic fission.
  • Polar aprotic or protic solvents lower the activation energy of heterolytic cleavage by interacting with developing charges.
  • Steric crowding around the reaction center can destabilize the starting material and assist in leaving group departure.

Reactive Intermediates Formed

  • Carbocations possess an electron-deficient carbon center with an empty unhybridized p-orbital and a trigonal planar geometry.
  • Carbanions possess a negatively charged carbon center with an octet of electrons and typically adopt a pyramidal geometry.
  • The stability of carbocations is primarily dictated by hyperconjugation and inductive electron-donating effects of alkyl groups.

⚠️ Important Exceptions & Anomalies
Exception Tertiary carbocations are always the most stable alkyl carbocations.

Observation: Non-classical carbocations or bridged ions can exhibit anomalous thermodynamic stabilities exceeding simple tertiary acyclic ions. — Anchimeric assistance and extensive sigma-delocalization provide lower energy states than expected from simple hyperconjugation.

Exception Primary carbanions are more stable than tertiary carbanions due to inductive electron-release.

Observation: In systems where extensive resonance or polarizability operates, standard inductive rules can be overridden. — Delocalization of negative charge over conjugated pi systems or highly polarizable heteroatoms supersedes local inductive effects.

🎯 Key Exam Points
  • Heterolytic fission results in the formation of charged intermediates.
  • Carbocations are sp2 hybridized with a vacant p-orbital.
  • Carbanions are sp3 hybridized with a lone pair of electrons.
  • Polar solvents promote heterolytic cleavage by ion-dipole interactions.
  • Tertiary carbocation stability is explained by hyperconjugation and inductive effect.
  • Leaving groups depart with the bonding electron pair during nucleophilic substitution.
🚫 Common Errors & Confusions
  • Confusing heterolytic fission products (ions) with homolytic products (free radicals).
  • Assuming carbanions have planar geometries similar to carbocations instead of pyramidal.
  • Applying the carbocation stability order directly to carbanions without reversing the trend.
  • Neglecting solvent polarity effects when determining cleavage pathways.

⚡ Quick Revision: Heterolytic Fission

Core formulas, rapid recall points, and distinguishing criteria at a glance.

Bond Cleavage Dynamics

Covalent bonds break via heterolytic pathways when electronegativity differences are large, yielding discrete ionic species.

Carbocation Structure and Stability

Carbocations are electron-deficient electrophiles whose stability increases with alkyl substitution via hyperconjugation.

Carbanion Characteristics

Carbanions act as nucleophiles or bases, with stability decreasing as alkyl substitution increases due to electron-releasing inductive effects.

Role of Solvation

Polar solvents supply solvation energy that compensates for the high energetic cost of heterolytic bond dissociation.

📚 Terms & Units Glossary

Heterolytic Fission (Bond Cleavage)
Unsymmetrical cleavage producing ions with SI unit of energy change.
Carbocation (R3C+)
Positively charged carbon species with sp2 hybridization.
Carbanion (R3C−)
Negatively charged carbon species with sp3 hybridization.
Electrophile (E+)
Electron-deficient species seeking electron density.
Nucleophile (:Nu−)
Electron-rich species donating an electron pair.
Hyperconjugation (No-bond resonance)
Stabilization via overlap of σ and p orbitals.

🎯 Key Takeaways

  • Heterolytic cleavage yields ions.
  • Carbocation stability: 3° > 2° > 1° > CH3+.
  • Carbanion stability: CH3− > 1° > 2° > 3°.
  • Polar solvents lower heterolytic activation energy.
  • Carbocation geometry is trigonal planar.

📌 Quick Recall

  • Heterolytic fission creates charged ions.
  • Polarity and solvation drive the process.
  • Carbocations and carbanions are key intermediates.
  • Inductive and hyperconjugative effects dictate stability.

⚖️ Key Differences

Carbocation Stability VS Carbanion Stability

Carbocations are stabilized by electron-donating groups (+I), whereas carbanions are stabilized by electron-withdrawing groups (−I).

Question 16 of 18

Explanation: Chlorine is more electronegative than carbon, so it retains the bonding pair, yielding CH3+ and Cl−.

Question 17 of 18

Explanation: Hyperconjugation is the interaction of σ electrons (typically C—H) with an adjacent empty or partially filled p-orbital.

Explanation: Electron-withdrawing groups like −NO2 disperse negative charge, stabilizing carbanions.
