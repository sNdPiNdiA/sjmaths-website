Organic Chemistry

Reaction Mechanism

A reaction mechanism is the step-by-step sequence of elementary reactions by which overall chemical change occurs. It describes the bond-cleavage, bond-formation, and spatial reorganization of reacting species.

UP PGT Chemistry Subject Code 02 B.Sc. / M.Sc. Level

Fission of Covalent Bonds and Reactive Intermediates

  • Homolytic fission involves symmetrical cleavage of a covalent bond, generating neutral free radicals with unpaired electrons.
  • Heterolytic fission involves unsymmetrical cleavage, yielding ionic species such as carbocations (sp2 hybridized, planar) and carbanions (sp3 hybridized, pyramidal).
  • Free radicals and carbenes are neutral reactive intermediates possessing incomplete octets, whereas carbonium ions act as strong electrophiles.
  • Stability of carbocations follows the order: 3° > 2° > 1° > methyl, driven by hyperconjugation and inductive effects.

Electronic Displacements in Covalent Bonds

  • The inductive effect (-I and +I) is a permanent polarization of a σ-bond transmitted along the carbon chain, attenuating rapidly beyond three carbons.
  • The resonance effect (R or M effect) involves the delocalization of π-electrons or lone pairs through conjugation, influencing molecular stability significantly.
  • Hyperconjugation (no-bond resonance) stabilizes alkenes and carbocations through overlap of σ (C-H) orbitals with adjacent empty or partially filled p-orbitals.
  • Electromeric effect (+E and -E) is a temporary polarization induced entirely in the presence of an attacking reagent at a multiple bond.

Types of Reagents and Organic Reactions

  • Nucleophiles are electron-rich species donating an electron pair (e.g., OH-, CN-, NH3), acting as Lewis bases.
  • Electrophiles are electron-deficient species accepting an electron pair (e.g., NO2+, BF3, AlCl3), acting as Lewis acids.
  • Substitution, addition, elimination, and rearrangement represent the four primary classifications of organic chemical reactions.
  • Pericyclic reactions proceed via concerted cyclic transition states without the formation of discrete ionic or radical intermediates.
📐 Key Formulas & Equations
Rate Law Expression
Rate = k[A]x[B]y
Conditions: Determined experimentally for elementary or overall steps
Units: Rate (mol L-1 s-1), k (rate constant units vary), [A], [B] (mol L-1)
Hammett Equation
log(k / k0) = ρσ
Conditions: Linear free-energy relationship for meta- and para-substituted benzene derivatives
Units: k, k0 (rate constants), ρ (reaction constant), σ (substituent constant)
Isotope Effect Equation
kH / kD = exp[(ΔEzpe,H - ΔEzpe,D) / RT]
Conditions: Primary kinetic isotope effect where X-H bond cleavage is rate-determining
Units: kH, kD (rate constants for protium and deuterium isotopes)
Arrhenius Equation
k = A e-Ea / RT
Conditions: Temperature dependence of reaction rate constants
Units: k (s-1 or M-1s-1), Ea (J mol-1), R (J mol-1K-1), T (K)
⚠️ Important Exceptions & Anomalies
Exception 3° Carbocations are always more stable than 2° carbocations.

Observation: Bridgehead carbocations (e.g., 1-admantyl cation derivatives) resist planarity and are exceptionally unstable despite being tertiary. — Bredt's rule prohibits sp2 hybridization at bridgehead carbons in small and medium bicyclic systems due to extreme angle strain.

Exception Electrophilic aromatic substitution proceeds via a stable arenium ion intermediate.

Observation: In strongly basic conditions or with heavily deactivated substrates, reactions may proceed via elimination-addition (benzyne mechanism). — Formation of a highly strained transient triple bond (benzyne intermediate) bypasses standard direct electrophilic substitution.

Exception Tertiary alkyl halides undergo SN1 substitution exclusively.

Observation: In the presence of strong nucleophiles in polar aprotic solvents or under specific constrained geometries, steric crowding can alter pathways. — Solvent polarity and steric hindrance modulate the activation energy barrier between concerted and stepwise ionization pathways.

💡 Shortcuts & Memory Aids
Carbocation Stability Mnemonic
3 Hyper-Ind-Res → 3° > 2° > 1°

Application: Count the number of α-hydrogens for hyperconjugation; more α-H means greater stability for alkenes and carbocations.

Nucleophilicity vs Basicity Trend
Left-to-Right periodic: Basicity tracks Electronegativity; Down-the-group: Nucleophilicity tracks Polarizability.

Application: In protic solvents, larger halides (I-) are better nucleophiles despite being weaker bases due to reduced hydration shells.

Orientation in Elimination Reactions
Zaitsev for stable alkenes; Hofmann for bulky bases or poor leaving groups.

Application: Predict major alkene products by checking base bulkiness (e.g., tert-butoxide gives Hofmann alkene).

🎯 Key Exam Points
  • Homolytic cleavage requires UV light, peroxides, or high thermal energy to generate free radicals.
  • Heterolytic cleavage is favored by polar solvents which solvate and stabilize the resulting ions.
  • The stability of carbanions follows the reverse order of carbocations: 1° > 2° > 3° due to +I electron-donating alkyl groups intensifying negative charge.
  • Inductive effect operates exclusively through σ-bonds and decreases rapidly with distance.
  • Hyperconjugation involves the delocalization of σ-electrons of C-H bond into an adjacent empty p-orbital.
  • Electrophilic addition to unsymmetrical alkenes follows Markovnikov's rule, proceeding via the more stable carbocation intermediate.
🚫 Common Errors & Confusions
  • Confusing the temporary electromeric effect with the permanent inductive or resonance effects.
  • Assuming SN2 reactions occur with retention of configuration instead of complete inversion.
  • Forgetting that carbocation rearrangements (1,2-hydride or 1,2-methyl shifts) can occur to yield a more stable carbocation.
  • Treating radical substitution and electrophilic substitution as mechanistically identical processes.

⚡ Quick Revision: Reaction Mechanism

Core formulas, rapid recall points, and distinguishing criteria at a glance.

Bond Cleavage and Intermediates

Covalent bonds break homolytically to form free radicals or heterolytically to form carbocations and carbanions. The stability of these intermediates dictates reaction pathways.

Electronic Effects

Inductive, resonance, and hyperconjugative effects polarize bonds and stabilize reactive intermediates, directly dictating acidity, basicity, and reactivity.

Nucleophiles and Electrophiles

Organic reactions fundamentally involve electron-rich nucleophiles attacking electron-deficient electrophilic centers across various mechanistic paths.

Kinetic and Thermodynamic Control

Reaction outcomes can be governed by activation energy barriers (kinetic product) or thermodynamic stability of products under equilibrium conditions.

📚 Terms & Units Glossary

Carbocation (R3C+)
A carbon species with a positive charge and six electrons in its valence shell, sp2 hybridized.
Carbanion (R3C-)
A carbon species carrying a negative charge and an unshared electron pair, sp3 hybridized.
Free Radical (R3C•)
A neutral reactive species possessing an unpaired electron.
Electromeric Effect (+E / -E)
Complete transfer of a π-electron pair to an atom within a multiple bond under the influence of a reagent.
Hyperconjugation (No-bond resonance)
Interaction of σ-bonds with a π-system or vacant p-orbital.
Ambident Nucleophile (Dual-site nucleophile)
A nucleophile with two reactive nucleophilic sites (e.g., CN-, NO2-).

🎯 Key Takeaways

  • Carbocation stability order: 3° > 2° > 1° > CH3+
  • Carbanion stability order: CH3- > 1° > 2° > 3°
  • SN1 rate depends only on substrate concentration (First order)
  • SN2 rate depends on both substrate and nucleophile concentration (Second order)
  • Markonikov addition yields more substituted halogen alkane via stable intermediate

📌 Quick Recall

  • Reaction mechanisms track bond-breaking and bond-forming steps.
  • Intermediates like carbocations determine reaction speeds and selectivity.
  • Polarity of solvents shifts mechanisms between SN1 and SN2.
  • Electronic effects (Inductive, Resonance) explain structural stability.

⚖️ Key Differences

Inductive Effect VS Resonance Effect

Inductive effect operates through σ-bonds and attenuates with distance, whereas resonance involves delocalization of π-electrons or lone pairs across conjugated systems.

Question 1 of 18

Which of the following intermediates has an incomplete octet and acts as an electrophile?

Explanation: Carbocations possess six valence electrons around the central carbon, leaving an empty p-orbital and making them electron-deficient electrophiles.

Question 2 of 18

The stability order of free radicals is:

Explanation: Free radicals, like carbocations, are stabilized by hyperconjugation and inductive effects from surrounding alkyl groups, following the 3° > 2° > 1° trend.

Question 3 of 18

Which electronic effect is permanent and transmitted through sigma bonds?

Explanation: The inductive effect is a permanent polarization of sigma bonds due to electronegativity differences between atoms.

Question 4 of 18

In an SN2 reaction involving a chiral substrate, the stereochemical outcome is:

Explanation: SN2 attacks from the backside relative to the leaving group, causing a complete Walden inversion of configuration.

Question 5 of 18

Which solvent favors SN1 reaction mechanisms?

Explanation: Polar protic solvents effectively solvate and stabilize the ionic carbocation intermediate and leaving group.

Question 6 of 18

Which of the following molecules exhibits the highest -I effect?

Explanation: The nitro group (-NO2) possesses a strong electron-withdrawing inductive (-I) and resonance (-M) effect.

Question 7 of 18

Hyperconjugation involves the overlap of which orbitals?

Explanation: Hyperconjugation is the delocalization of electrons from a σ-bond (usually C-H) into an adjacent empty or partially filled p-orbital or π-system.

Question 8 of 18

Which carbocation is most stable?

Explanation: Tertiary butyl cation ((CH3)3C+) has nine alpha-hydrogens, maximizing hyperconjugative stabilization.

Question 9 of 18

The addition of HBr to propene in the presence of organic peroxides yields 1-bromopropane due to:

Explanation: Peroxides induce homolytic cleavage, leading to a free-radical chain addition that results in anti-Markovnikov orientation (Kharasch effect).

Question 10 of 18

Which species acts as an ambident nucleophile?

Explanation: Cyanide ion (CN-) can bond through either carbon or nitrogen, making it an ambident nucleophile.

Question 11 of 18

Homolytic bond fission of a hydrocarbon yields:

Explanation: Homolytic cleavage splits the bond equally, giving each bonded atom one of the shared electrons, producing free radicals.

Question 12 of 18

Which of the following is a Lewis acid and acts as an electrophile?

Explanation: Boron trifluoride (BF3) has an incomplete octet on boron, functioning as a strong Lewis acid and electrophile.

Question 13 of 18

The rate law for an SN1 reaction is:

Explanation: SN1 is unimolecular; the rate-determining step involves only the dissociation of the substrate.

Question 14 of 18

Which compound shows optical activity due to a chiral center formed in a mechanism?

Explanation: SN1 substitution of an optically active achiral or chiral center through a planar carbocation yields a racemic mixture.

Question 15 of 18

Which functional group exerts a +R (positive resonance) effect?

Explanation: Groups with lone pairs on the atom directly attached to the conjugated system, such as -OH, donate electrons via resonance (+R effect).

Question 16 of 18

Which reagent is classified as an electrophile?

Explanation: Aluminum chloride (AlCl3) is electron-deficient and acts as an electrophilic catalyst in Friedel-Crafts reactions.

Question 17 of 18

What is the hybridization of the central carbon atom in a carbocation?

Explanation: Carbocations have three bond pairs and an empty p-orbital, adopting a planar trigonal geometry with sp2 hybridization.

Question 18 of 18

Which type of reaction mechanism features a cyclic transition state without isolating intermediates?

Explanation: Pericyclic reactions occur in a single concerted step via a cyclic transition state.

Question 1 of 10
What is the primary factor stabilizing tertiary carbanions over primary carbanions?

Solution: Alkyl groups donate electron density via +I effect, intensifying negative charge on carbanions; thus primary carbanions are more stable than tertiary ones.

Question 2 of 10
Which of the following reaction types does not involve formation of a carbocation intermediate?

Solution: SN2 is a concerted, one-step bimolecular reaction proceeding through a transition state without a carbocation intermediate.

Question 3 of 10
Which of the following species is a strong nucleophile and weak base?

Solution: Large polarizable anions like iodide (I-) are excellent nucleophiles due to high polarizability but weak bases due to stable conjugate acids.

Question 4 of 10
What is the hybridization change of carbon when an alkene undergoes electrophilic addition?

Solution: During electrophilic addition, trigonal planar sp2 carbons of the double bond become tetrahedral sp3 hybridized carbons in the saturated product.

Question 5 of 10
Which organic reaction mechanism is favored by high temperature and strong bulky bases?

Solution: Strong bulky bases favor bimolecular elimination (E2) over substitution due to steric hindrance inhibiting nucleophilic attack.

Question 6 of 10
Which substituent directs an incoming electrophile to the ortho/para position in benzene rings?

Solution: Methyl (-CH3) is an activating group releasing electron density via hyperconjugation and +I effect, directing electrophiles to ortho and para positions.

Question 7 of 10
The rate-determining step in an E1 elimination mechanism is:

Solution: E1 is a two-step process where the slow, rate-determining step is the unimolecular ionization to form a carbocation intermediate.

Question 8 of 10
Which molecule has zero dipole moment due to its structural symmetry in reaction intermediates?

Solution: p-Dichlorobenzene has vectors of polar C-Cl bonds pointing in opposite directions, canceling each other out to yield a zero dipole moment.

Question 9 of 10
In the reaction of alkyl halides with aqueous KOH, tertiary halides react via:

Solution: Tertiary alkyl halides undergo rapid ionization to form stable tertiary carbocations, proceeding predominantly via SN1 mechanism.

Question 10 of 10
Which of the following species contains a carbon with a sextet of electrons?

Solution: A carbocation possesses three bonds containing six valence electrons around the carbon center.
