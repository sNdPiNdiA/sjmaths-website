Homolytic Fission

Homolytic fission is the symmetrical cleavage of a covalent bond where each bonded atom retains one of the shared electrons. This process generates electrically neutral reactive intermediates known as free radicals.

UP PGT Chemistry Subject Code 02 B.Sc. / M.Sc. Level

Mechanism and Electron Movement

  • Homolytic cleavage involves the symmetrical distribution of the bonding electron pair between two separating atoms.
  • Reaction arrows use the fishhook arrow (⇀) to represent the movement of a single electron.
  • Favored in the presence of high temperature (Δ), ultraviolet light (hν), peroxides, or in non-polar solvents.
  • Yields free radicals possessing an odd number of electrons and an incomplete octet.

Factors Favoring Homolysis

  • Low bond dissociation enthalpy (BDE) promotes easy homolytic cleavage, such as in I2 or organic peroxides.
  • Gas-phase reactions or non-polar media (like CCl4 or hexane) suppress ionic pathways and promote homolysis.
  • Presence of radical initiators like benzoyl peroxide or azobisisobutyronitrile (AIBN) drastically lowers activation energy.
  • Homolytic cleavage of symmetrical bonds like Cl−Cl or Br−Br requires lower energy inputs than highly polar bonds.

Stability and Reactivity of Radicals

  • Alkyl radical stability follows the order: 3° > 2° > 1° > methyl radical due to hyperconjugation and inductive effect.
  • Allylic and benzylic radicals exhibit enhanced stability as a result of resonance delocalization of the unpaired electron.
  • Radicals are electron-deficient species acting as electrophiles in radical addition and substitution reactions.
  • Propagation steps in radical chain mechanisms proceed via abstracting atoms to form stronger bonds while generating new radicals.

⚠️ Important Exceptions & Anomalies
Exception Tertiary radicals are generally the most stable alkyl radicals.

Observation: Bridgehead tertiary radicals (e.g., 1-adamantyl radical) resist formation. — Bridgehead carbons cannot achieve planar sp2 hybridization due to rigid cage geometry.

Exception Homolysis requires high thermal energy or UV irradiation.

Observation: Certain weak bonds like peroxides (R−O−O−R) cleave homolytically at moderate temperatures. — Extremely low bond dissociation energy (approx. 140-150 kJ mol−1) of the O−O single bond.

Exception Halogenation of alkanes proceeds through free radical chain mechanisms.

Observation: Fluorination is explosive and uncontrolled, whereas iodination is thermodynamically unfavorable. — Fluorination is excessively exothermic (ΔH is highly negative), while iodination is endothermic.

🎯 Key Exam Points
  • Homolytic fission produces neutral species with an odd electron known as free radicals.
  • The bond dissociation energy (BDE) represents the enthalpy change for homolytic cleavage in the gas phase.
  • Allylic and benzylic free radicals are stabilized by resonance structures.
  • Free radical halogenation of alkanes involves initiation, propagation, and termination steps.
  • Anti-Markovnikov addition of HBr to alkenes (peroxide effect) proceeds via homolytic cleavage of peroxides.
  • The geometry of a simple alkyl radical is planar or shallow pyramidal with sp2 hybridization.
🚫 Common Errors & Confusions
  • Using double-barbed arrows for homolytic bond cleavage instead of single-barbed fishhook arrows.
  • Assuming homolytic fission occurs readily in highly polar aqueous solvents without UV or radical initiators.
  • Confusing the stability order of carbocations with free radicals during hyperconjugation analysis.
  • Forgetting that termination steps in radical chain reactions always decrease the total number of free radicals.

⚡ Quick Revision: Homolytic Fission

Core formulas, rapid recall points, and distinguishing criteria at a glance.

Bond Cleavage Fundamentals

Covalent bonds cleave either symmetrically (homolytic) to form free radicals or unsymmetrically (heterolytic) to form ions depending on electronegativity and reaction environment.

Initiation and Propagation

Homolytic chain reactions begin with radical generation (initiation) followed by self-sustaining cycles where a radical reacts to generate another radical (propagation).

Stability Factors

Alkyl radical stability increases with substitution due to hyperconjugation, while resonance stabilization significantly lowers the activation energy for radical formation.

Solvent and Reagent Effects

Non-polar media, high temperatures, peroxides, and light suppress ionic pathways and exclusively promote homolytic pathways.

📚 Terms & Units Glossary

Homolytic Fission (A−B → A• + B•)
Symmetrical cleavage of a covalent bond where each atom takes one bonding electron.
Free Radical (R•)
Neutral chemical species containing an unpaired electron.
Bond Dissociation Energy (BDE)
Enthalpy change required for homolytic cleavage of a bond in the gas phase (kJ mol−1).
Initiator (ROOR / AIBN)
Substance that readily undergoes homolysis to generate initiating radicals.
Hyperconjugation (σ−p conjugation)
Delocalization of σ-electrons of C−H bond into the empty or singly occupied p-orbital.
Fishhook Arrow (⇀)
Curved arrow with a half arrowhead indicating the movement of a single electron.

🎯 Key Takeaways

  • Homolytic fission generates free radicals with odd electron counts.
  • Stability order of alkyl radicals: 3° > 2° > 1° > Methyl.
  • Peroxide effect operates via homolytic cleavage of the peroxide bond.
  • Fishhook arrows denote single electron transfer in homolytic mechanisms.
  • Non-polar solvents and light favor homolytic reaction pathways.

📌 Quick Recall

  • Symmetrical bond cleavage produces neutral radicals.
  • Hyperconjugation and resonance dictate radical stability.
  • Chain reactions involve initiation, propagation, and termination.
  • Peroxides serve as common thermal radical initiators.

⚖️ Key Differences

Homolytic Fission VS Heterolytic Fission

Homolytic results in neutral free radicals with symmetrical electron sharing, whereas heterolytic results in ions (cations and anions) with unsymmetrical electron transfer.

Which bond has the highest homolytic bond dissociation energy among halogens?

Solution: Fluorine has an anomalously low BDE due to lone pair repulsions; chlorine has the highest BDE among the common reactive halogens.

Question 4 of 10
In radical chlorination of alkanes, which step consumes radicals?

Solution: Termination steps combine radicals to form non-radical species, decreasing the total radical concentration.

Question 5 of 10
Which reagent undergoes homolytic cleavage most readily at room temperature under light?

Solution: Chlorine gas (Cl2) readily undergoes homolysis upon absorbing visible or UV light.

Question 6 of 10
Benzoyl peroxide acts as a radical initiator because it easily undergoes:

Solution: The weak peroxide oxygen-oxygen single bond undergoes homolytic fission upon gentle heating.

Question 7 of 10
What is the primary reason for the greater stability of tertiary alkyl radicals over primary alkyl radicals?

Solution: Hyperconjugation from more adjacent C−H sigma bonds stabilizes the singly occupied p-orbital in tertiary radicals.

Question 8 of 10
Which of the following reactions does NOT proceed via a free radical mechanism?

Solution: Hydrolysis of tert-butyl chloride with aqueous NaOH proceeds via heterolytic cleavage to form a carbocation (SN1 mechanism).

Question 9 of 10
Which radical is stabilized by both resonance and hyperconjugation?

Solution: The benzyl radical benefits from resonance delocalization into the benzene ring as well as hyperconjugation from the methylene hydrogens.

Solution: Breaking chemical bonds requires an input of energy, making bond dissociation enthalpy strictly endothermic (ΔH > 0).
