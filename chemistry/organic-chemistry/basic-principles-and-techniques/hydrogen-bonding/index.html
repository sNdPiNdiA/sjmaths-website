Organic Chemistry

Hydrogen Bonding

Hydrogen bonding is an electrostatic dipole-dipole attraction occurring when a hydrogen atom covalently bound to a highly electronegative atom (N, O, F) experiences electrostatic force from another electronegative atom. It significantly alters physical properties such as boiling point, viscosity, and solubility of organic compounds.

UP PGT Chemistry Subject Code 02 B.Sc. / M.Sc. Level

Fundamental Nature and Structural Requirements

  • Hydrogen bonding requires a hydrogen atom attached to a small, highly electronegative atom possessing lone pairs: Fluorine, Oxygen, or Nitrogen.
  • The interaction is primarily electrostatic with a significant contribution from orbital overlap and charge transfer.
  • The strength of the hydrogen bond increases with the electronegativity and decreases with the atomic size of the donor and acceptor atoms.

Intermolecular versus Intramolecular Hydrogen Bonding

  • Intermolecular hydrogen bonding occurs between separate molecules, leading to association, increased molecular weight, and higher boiling points and viscosities.
  • Intramolecular hydrogen bonding occurs within the same molecule, typically forming stable 5- or 6-membered ring structures called chelation rings.
  • Intramolecular hydrogen bonding decreases boiling points and water solubility due to the masking of polar functional groups from external solvent molecules.

Physicochemical Consequences in Organic Compounds

  • Presence of hydrogen bonding causes anomalous high boiling points in polar hydrides like H2O, HF, and NH3 compared to heavier congeners.
  • o-Nitrophenol exhibits intramolecular hydrogen bonding and is steam volatile, whereas p-nitrophenol forms intermolecular hydrogen networks and has a much higher boiling point.
  • Hydrogen bonding dictates the secondary and tertiary structures of biological macromolecules like proteins and nucleic acids (DNA double helix).

⚠️ Important Exceptions & Anomalies
Exception Boiling points of group 15, 16, and 17 hydrides increase down the group with increasing molecular weight.

Observation: NH3, H2O, and HF have anomalously high boiling points compared to PH3, H2S, and HCl. — Strong intermolecular hydrogen bonding is present only in hydrides of N, O, and F due to their high electronegativity and small size.

Exception o-Nitrophenol and p-nitrophenol have similar physical properties due to identical molecular formulas.

Observation: o-Nitrophenol is steam volatile and less soluble in water, while p-nitrophenol is non-volatile and more soluble. — o-Nitrophenol forms stable intramolecular hydrogen bonds, reducing intermolecular association, whereas p-nitrophenol forms extensive intermolecular hydrogen bonds.

Exception Carboxylic acids exist as monomeric units in the gas phase.

Observation: Carboxylic acids exist as cyclic dimers even in the vapor phase or non-polar solvents. — Exceptionally strong cooperative intermolecular hydrogen bonding holds two molecules together in a cyclic dimer structure.

💡 Shortcuts & Memory Aids
FON Rule for H-Bonding
H must be bonded to F, O, or N

Application: Quickly identify whether a molecule can form hydrogen bonds by checking if H is directly attached to Fluorine, Oxygen, or Nitrogen.

Ring Size Rule for Intramolecular H-Bonding
5 or 6-membered rings

Application: Intramolecular hydrogen bonding is most stable when it forms a 5- or 6-membered pseudo-aromatic ring (e.g., in salicylic acid or o-nitrophenol).

Boiling Point vs Solubility Trick
Inter = High BP; Intra = Low BP & High Volatility

Application: Use intermolecular bonding to justify high boiling points/viscosity and intramolecular bonding for steam volatility and lower melting points.

🎯 Key Exam Points
  • Fluorine forms the strongest hydrogen bond due to its maximum electronegativity.
  • Hydrogen fluoride (HF) forms a zigzag polymeric chain structure in the liquid and solid states.
  • Ice has a cage-like open crystal structure due to tetrahedral hydrogen bonding, making its density lower than liquid water at 4°C.
  • The boiling point order of hydrogen halides is HF > HI > HBr > HCl.
  • Salicylaldehyde exhibits intramolecular hydrogen bonding, resulting in a lower boiling point than p-hydroxybenzaldehyde.
  • The DNA double helix is stabilized primarily by specific complementary hydrogen bonding between base pairs (A=T and G≡C).
🚫 Common Errors & Confusions
  • Assuming C-H bonds can participate in hydrogen bonding; only highly polarized X-H bonds (X = F, O, N) form classic hydrogen bonds.
  • Confusing intermolecular hydrogen bonds with covalent bonds; hydrogen bonds are significantly longer and weaker.
  • Believing intramolecular hydrogen bonds increase boiling points; they actually decrease intermolecular association and lower boiling points.
  • Forgetting that hydrogen bonding can occur between different chemical species (e.g., water and acetone).

⚡ Quick Revision: Hydrogen Bonding

Core formulas, rapid recall points, and distinguishing criteria at a glance.

Nature of Hydrogen Bond

A dipole-dipole attraction where hydrogen acts as a bridge between two electronegative atoms, possessing electrostatic and partial covalent character.

Intermolecular Associations

Association of multiple molecules leads to abnormal physical properties, including elevated boiling points, melting points, and viscosities.

Intramolecular Chelation

Formation of stable cyclic structures within a single molecule prevents intermolecular association, enhancing volatility and reducing polarity effects.

Biological Significance

Hydrogen bonding provides conformational stability to proteins, nucleic acids, and cellulose, governing biological specificity and structure.

📚 Terms & Units Glossary

Hydrogen Bond (X-H···Y)
Electrostatic attraction between a proton-donor group and a proton-acceptor.
Donor (X-H)
Electronegative atom covalently bonded to hydrogen.
Acceptor (Y)
Electronegative atom with a lone pair providing electrostatic attraction.
Chelation (Intramolecular H-bond ring)
Ring formation within a molecule via internal hydrogen bonding.
Dimerization ((RCOOH)2)
Pairing of two molecules via mutual intermolecular hydrogen bonding.
Cooperative Effect (Chain polarization)
Enhancement of subsequent hydrogen bonds in a polymeric network.

🎯 Key Takeaways

  • H-bond enthalpy ranges from 10 to 40 kJ mol-1.
  • Only F, O, and N participate in strong hydrogen bonding.
  • Intermolecular H-bonding increases boiling point; intramolecular decreases it.
  • Ice has lower density than liquid water due to open cage structure.
  • HF forms linear or zigzag polymeric chains in liquid state.

📌 Quick Recall

  • Hydrogen bonding is an electrostatic dipole-dipole interaction involving F, O, and N.
  • It explains anomalies in physical properties of water, ammonia, and hydrogen fluoride.
  • Intermolecular hydrogen bonding causes molecular association and high boiling points.
  • Intramolecular hydrogen bonding causes chelation and increased steam volatility.

⚖️ Key Differences

Intermolecular H-bonding VS Intramolecular H-bonding

Intermolecular links different molecules (raising BP); intramolecular occurs within the same molecule (lowering BP).

Question 14 of 19

Explanation: Hydrogen bonds are moderate secondary interactions with typical bond enthalpies between 10 and 40 kJ mol-1.

Question 15 of 19

Explanation: Glycerol contains three hydroxyl groups per molecule, leading to an extensive 3D intermolecular hydrogen-bonded network and high viscosity.

Explanation: Nuclear mass is an intrinsic nuclear property, whereas boiling point, melting point, and viscosity depend on intermolecular forces.

Question 17 of 19

Explanation: HF has the highest boiling point due to strong hydrogen bonding, while HI, HBr, and HCl follow an increasing order based on van der Waals forces.
