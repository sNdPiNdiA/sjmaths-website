Periodic Classification and Periodicity

Periodic Classification and Periodicity

Elements are arranged in the modern periodic table in order of increasing atomic number, reflecting periodic recurrence of physical and chemical properties. Periodicity arises from the repetition of similar valence shell electronic configurations at regular intervals.

UP PGT Chemistry Subject Code 02 B.Sc. / M.Sc. Level

Electronic Configuration and Periodic Law

  • The Modern Periodic Law states that physical and chemical properties of elements are periodic functions of their atomic numbers.
  • Elements are classified into four blocks (s, p, d, f) based on the subshell receiving the differentiating electron.
  • The valence shell electronic configuration dictates the chemical reactivity and group number of the respective element.
  • Noble gases possess stable closed-shell configurations (ns2np6, except He as 1s2).

Periodic Trends in Atomic and Ionic Properties

  • Atomic radii generally decrease across a period due to increasing Zeff and increase down a group due to the addition of principal quantum shells (n).
  • Ionization enthalpy (IE) increases across a period and decreases down a group, subject to irregularities from half-filled and fully-filled subshell stabilities.
  • Electron gain enthalpy (ΔegH) becomes more negative across a period, except for elements with stable configurations.
  • Electronegativity quantifies the tendency of an atom to attract shared electron pairs, increasing from left to right across periods.

Chemical Reactivity and Diagonal Relationships

  • Diagonal relationships occur between certain adjacent elements in neighboring groups (e.g., Li and Mg, Be and Al) due to comparable charge-to-radius ratios (ionic potential).
  • Metallic character decreases across a period and increases down a group, while non-metallic character shows the reverse trend.
  • Acid-base character of oxides shifts from strongly basic (left) to amphoteric (center) to strongly acidic (right) across a period.
  • The inert pair effect causes heavier p-block elements in groups 13 to 15 to exhibit stable oxidation states two units lower than their group oxidation state.
📐 Key Formulas & Equations
Slater's Rule for Shielding Constant
S = ∑(ni × 0.35) + ∑(nj × 0.85) + ∑(nk × 1.0)
Conditions: Applied to electrons in ns/np, (n-1) shell, and (n-2) or lower shells respectively
Units: Dimensionless
Allred-Rochow Electronegativity
χ = 0.359 × (Zeff / r2) + 0.744
Conditions: r expressed in Angstroms (Å)
Units: Dimensionless on Pauling/Allred-Rochow scale
Mulliken Electronegativity
χM = (IE + EA) / 2
Conditions: IE = Ionization Energy, EA = Electron Affinity, both on same energy scale
Units: eV or kJ mol-1
Radius Ratio Rule
r+ / r-
Conditions: Used for predicting coordination number and ionic crystal geometry
Units: Dimensionless ratio
Effective Nuclear Charge
Zeff = Z - S
Conditions: Z = atomic number, S = screening constant (Slater's constant)
Units: Dimensionless (units of positive elementary charge)
⚠️ Important Exceptions & Anomalies
Exception Ionization Energy increases continuously across Period 2.

Observation: IE1 of Boron (B) < Beryllium (Be), and Oxygen (O) < Nitrogen (N). — Be has a fully filled stable 2s2 configuration, and N has a half-filled stable 2p3 configuration, requiring extra energy to remove an electron.

Exception Electron Gain Enthalpy becomes more negative across a Period.

Observation: Fluorine (F) has a less negative electron gain enthalpy than Chlorine (Cl). — Small atomic size of F leads to high interelectronic repulsion within its compact 2p orbitals, hindering incoming electron addition.

Exception Atomic size increases down Group 13.

Observation: Atomic radius of Gallium (Ga) is slightly smaller than that of Aluminum (Al). — Incomplete shielding of nuclear charge by intervening 3d electrons in Ga leads to higher effective nuclear charge.

Exception Ionization energy decreases down Group 13.

Observation: IE of Thallium (Tl) > Indium (In). — Poor shielding by 4f and 5d electrons (lanthanide contraction) increases Zeff for Tl.

💡 Shortcuts & Memory Aids
Slater's Rules Screening Shortcut
Outer electrons contribute 0.35; (n-1) shell contributes 0.85; (n-2) or lower shells contribute 1.0.

Application: Quickly compute S for s and p valence electrons to estimate Zeff without lengthy manual expansions.

Diagonal Relationship Mnemonic
Li → Mg, Be → Al, B → Si

Application: Instantly recall diagonally linked elements that share similar polarizabilities, hydrolysis products, and covalent bond characters.

Oxide Acid-Base Gradient Check
Left = Basic, Center = Amphoteric, Right = Acidic

Application: Rapidly categorize unknown period oxides by their position in the periodic table (e.g., Na2O basic, Al2O3 amphoteric, SO3 acidic).

🎯 Key Exam Points
  • The lanthanide contraction is responsible for the nearly identical radii of Zirconium (Zr) and Hafnium (Hf).
  • Fluorine possesses the highest electronegativity (4.0 on Pauling scale) in the periodic table.
  • Chlorine possesses the most negative electron gain enthalpy among all elements.
  • Elements with atomic numbers greater than 100 are known as transuranic or transactinide elements.
  • The maximum coordination number of boron in coordination complexes is four due to the absence of vacant d-orbitals.
  • Radon is the heaviest naturally occurring radioactive noble gas.
🚫 Common Errors & Confusions
  • Confusing electron gain enthalpy (energy released/absorbed) with electronegativity (tendency to attract shared pairs).
  • Assuming noble gases have zero electron affinity; some have negative (endothermic) values.
  • Forgetting that cationic radii are always smaller and anionic radii are always larger than their parent neutral atoms.
  • Neglecting the effect of d- and f-block contractions (transition and lanthanide contraction) when comparing atomic sizes of period 5 and 6 elements.

⚡ Quick Revision: Periodic Classification and Periodicity

Core formulas, rapid recall points, and distinguishing criteria at a glance.

Periodic Law and Atomic Number

Properties of elements are periodic functions of atomic number Z, governed by electronic configurations in subshells.

Effective Nuclear Charge

Zeff = Z - S accounts for core electron shielding, dictating size and ionization energy trends across periods.

Periodicity of Energetics

Ionization energy and electron affinity exhibit predictable periodic trends modulated by exchange energy and orbital symmetry stabilities.

Anomalous Behavior and Diagonal Relationships

First-row elements of s- and p-blocks show anomalous small sizes and high charge densities, resembling diagonal neighbors.

📚 Terms & Units Glossary

Effective Nuclear Charge (Zeff)
Net positive charge experienced by valence electrons after shielding correction.
Ionization Enthalpy (IE)
Energy required to remove an electron from an isolated gaseous atom in ground state.
Electron Gain Enthalpy (ΔegH)
Enthalpy change when an electron is added to an isolated gaseous atom.
Electronegativity (χ)
Measure of the power of an atom to attract shared electrons in a covalent bond.
Lanthanide Contraction (LC)
Progressive decrease in atomic and ionic radii across the lanthanide series.
Inert Pair Effect (IPE)
Reluctance of s-electrons to participate in bonding due to poor penetration and relativistic stabilization.

🎯 Key Takeaways

  • F has highest electronegativity; Cl has most negative electron gain enthalpy.
  • Cs has lowest ionization energy and largest stable alkali atomic radius.
  • Be and Al, Li and Mg exhibit diagonal relationships.
  • Zeff increases across a period.
  • Anions are larger than neutral atoms; cations are smaller.

📌 Quick Recall

  • Modern periodic table classifies 118 elements into groups and periods based on atomic number.
  • Periodic trends are governed by Zeff, principal quantum number, and interelectronic repulsions.
  • Irregularities in trends are explained by half-filled and fully-filled orbital stabilities.
  • Chemical properties like acidic/basic nature of oxides and metallic character follow consistent periodic paths.

⚖️ Key Differences

Electron Gain Enthalpy VS Electronegativity

Electron gain enthalpy is an energy term for isolated gaseous atoms; electronegativity is a bond property indicating shared electron attraction.

Question 1 of 18

Which of the following elements has the highest electronegativity?

Explanation: Fluorine is the most electronegative element with a value of 4.0 on the Pauling scale.

Question 2 of 18

The correct order of ionic radii for isoelectronic species O^2-, F^-, Na^+, and Mg^2+ is:

Explanation: For isoelectronic species, radius decreases as nuclear charge (Z) increases. O^2- has lowest Z (8) hence largest radius, while Mg^2+ has highest Z (12) hence smallest radius.

Question 3 of 18

Why is the first ionization energy of Be greater than that of B?

Explanation: Beryllium has a fully filled 2s^2 configuration which requires more energy to disrupt compared to removing the single 2p electron of Boron.

Question 4 of 18

Which element has the most negative electron gain enthalpy?

Explanation: Chlorine has the most negative electron gain enthalpy (-349 kJ/mol) due to interelectronic repulsions in the smaller 2p orbital of Fluorine.

Question 5 of 18

Diagonal relationship is shown by which of the following pairs?

Explanation: Lithium and Magnesium exhibit a diagonal relationship due to comparable ionic potentials (charge/radius ratio).

Question 6 of 18

The general electronic configuration of d-block elements is:

Explanation: d-Block elements involve filling of the penultimate (n-1) d-orbitals, giving the general configuration (n-1)d^1-10 ns^1-2.

Question 7 of 18

Which oxide is amphoteric in nature?

Explanation: Aluminium oxide (Al_2O_3) reacts with both acids and bases, rendering it amphoteric.

Question 8 of 18

The lanthanide contraction is primarily due to:

Explanation: Diffuse 4f orbitals provide poor shielding, causing Z_eff to increase and contractual shrinkage of subsequent elements.

Question 9 of 18

Which of the following species has the largest radius?

Explanation: Anions are larger than their parent neutral atoms due to increased electron-electron repulsion, and iodide is larger than chloride.

Question 10 of 18

The IUPAC name for element with atomic number 104 is:

Explanation: According to IUPAC nomenclature, 1 = un, 0 = nil, 4 = quad, giving Unnilquadium (Rf).

Question 11 of 18

First ionization energy is lowest for:

Explanation: Cesium is an alkali metal located at the bottom-left of the representative elements, giving it the lowest ionization energy.

Question 12 of 18

The inert pair effect is most pronounced in:

Explanation: Heavier post-transition p-block elements (like Tl, Pb, Bi) exhibit reluctance of ns electrons to bond due to relativistic and shielding effects.

Question 13 of 18

Which property generally decreases down a group?

Explanation: Ionization enthalpy decreases down a group as the valence electrons get further from the nucleus and experience more shielding.

Question 14 of 18

The second ionization energy is always:

Explanation: Removing an electron from a positively charged ion requires more energy than from a neutral atom due to higher electrostatic attraction.

Question 15 of 18

Which pair of elements has nearly identical atomic radii?

Explanation: Due to the lanthanide contraction, Zr and Hf have almost identical atomic and ionic radii.

Question 16 of 18

Allred-Rochow electronegativity scale is based on:

Explanation: Allred-Rochow electronegativity is calculated using electrostatic force (Z_eff / r^2) exerted by the nucleus on valence electrons.

Question 17 of 18

Which group contains the coinage metals?

Explanation: Group 11 comprises Copper, Silver, and Gold, traditionally known as coinage metals.

Question 18 of 18

The maximum number of elements in the 6th period of the periodic table is:

Explanation: The 6th period fills 6s, 4f, 5d, and 6p orbitals, accommodating 2 + 14 + 10 + 6 = 32 elements.

Question 1 of 10
What is the general valence shell electronic configuration of alkali metals?

Solution: Alkali metals (Group 1) have a single valence electron in their outermost s-orbital, giving ns^1.

Question 2 of 10
Which of the following species has the smallest radius?

Solution: Among isoelectronic species, Mg^2+ has the highest nuclear charge (Z = 12), pulling the electron cloud inward most strongly.

Question 3 of 10
The correct decreasing order of metallic character for Na, Si, Mg, Al is:

Solution: Metallic character decreases across a period from left to right: Na (Group 1) > Mg (Group 2) > Al (Group 13) > Si (Group 14).

Question 4 of 10
In Mulliken's scale, electronegativity is taken as the average of:

Solution: Mulliken electronegativity is defined as the arithmetic mean of ionization energy and electron affinity.

Question 5 of 10
Which element exhibits only a +3 oxidation state in its compounds?

Solution: Scandium (Sc) forms [Ar] 3d^1 4s^2; upon losing 3 electrons it attains stable noble gas configuration and shows exclusively +3 oxidation state.

Question 6 of 10
The elements with atomic numbers 90 to 103 are called:

Solution: Elements 90 (Th) through 103 (Lr) comprise the actinide series, characterized by filling of the 5f subshell.

Question 7 of 10
Which of the following oxides is purely basic?

Solution: Sodium oxide (Na_2O) is a metal oxide of an alkali metal, making it strongly basic.

Question 8 of 10
The shielding power of d-electrons follows the order:

Solution: Penetration effect and shielding power of orbitals follow the order s > p > d > f.

Question 9 of 10
Which group element has maximum tendency for catenation?

Solution: Carbon in Group 14 exhibits maximum catenation due to strong carbon-carbon covalent bonds.

Question 10 of 10
The second period contains how many elements?

Solution: The second period fills 2s and 2p orbitals, accommodating 8 elements (Li to Ne).
