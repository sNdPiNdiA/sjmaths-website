Freundlich Adsorption Isotherm

The Freundlich adsorption isotherm is an empirical relationship describing the variation of adsorption of a gas on a solid adsorbent with pressure at a constant temperature. It mathematically links the mass of gas adsorbed per unit mass of adsorbent to the equilibrium pressure.

UP PGT Chemistry Subject Code 02 B.Sc. / M.Sc. Level

Mathematical Formulation and Pressure Dependence

  • The term x/m represents the mass of adsorbate (x) adsorbed per unit mass of adsorbent (m).
  • At low pressure, the plot of x/m versus P is nearly linear, making 1/n approximately equal to 1.
  • At high pressure, x/m becomes independent of pressure, rendering 1/n equal to 0 so that x/m = k.
  • The constant n is always greater than 1, meaning the value of 1/n typically lies between 0 and 1.

Graphical Interpretation and Constant Evaluation

  • A plot of log(x/m) versus log P yields a straight line verifying the validity of the empirical isotherm.
  • The intercept of the linear plot on the y-axis gives the value of log k.
  • The slope of the straight line corresponds directly to the exponent 1/n.
  • Deviations from linearity are observed at very high pressures, where the empirical model fails.

Limitations and Scope of Application

  • The Freundlich isotherm is strictly empirical and lacks a rigorous theoretical thermodynamic foundation.
  • It fails at high pressures because the experimental adsorption reaches a saturation limit not accounted for in the power law.
  • The constants k and n are temperature-dependent and vary with the specific gas-solid system.
  • It provides a good approximation primarily for multilayer physical adsorption over limited pressure intervals.
📐 Key Formulas & Equations
Logarithmic Form of Freundlich Isotherm
log(x / m) = log k + (1 / n) log P
Conditions: Used for graphical determination of constants k and n
Units: log(x/m) dimensionless, log P dimensionless
Solution Phase Adsorption Isotherm
x / m = k · C1/n
Conditions: Adsorption from solution where C is equilibrium concentration
Units: C in mol L-1
Logarithmic Solution Phase Isotherm
log(x / m) = log k + (1 / n) log C
Conditions: Linear form for adsorption from liquid solutions
Units: log C dimensionless
Freundlich Adsorption Isotherm (General Form)
x / m = k · P1/n
Conditions: Intermediate pressure range and constant temperature
Units: x in g, m in g, P in atm or kPa, k and n are constants
⚠️ Important Exceptions & Anomalies
Exception Adsorption increases continuously with rising pressure according to x/m = k · P1/n.

Observation: At very high pressures, adsorption becomes independent of pressure. — Surface sites become completely saturated, forming a full monolayer or multilayer where additional gas molecules find no vacant sites.

Exception The exponent 1/n generally ranges between 0 and 1.

Observation: At extremely low temperatures or specific microporous structures, 1/n can approach unity across wider pressure spans. — Enhanced capillary condensation and energetic heterogeneity alter surface coverage dynamics.

Exception Freundlich isotherm holds true over all pressure ranges.

Observation: It completely fails at very high pressures. — The mathematical form predicts infinite adsorption at infinite pressure, which contradicts physical saturation.

🎯 Key Exam Points
  • The Freundlich adsorption isotherm equation is expressed as x/m = k · P1/n.
  • In the logarithmic form log(x/m) = log k + (1/n) log P, the slope of the line equals 1/n.
  • The value of 1/n in the Freundlich isotherm generally lies between 0 and 1.
  • When 1/n = 0, adsorption is independent of pressure.
  • When 1/n = 1, adsorption is directly proportional to pressure (x/m = k · P).
  • The Freundlich isotherm fails at high pressures where adsorption reaches saturation.
🚫 Common Errors & Confusions
  • Assuming the Freundlich isotherm is derived from fundamental kinetic principles; it is strictly empirical.
  • Forgetting that the y-intercept of the log(x/m) vs log P plot gives log k, not k directly.
  • Confusing the exponent 1/n with the value of n itself when solving numerical slope problems.
  • Applying the Freundlich equation at very high pressures where saturation invalidates the power law.

⚡ Quick Revision: Freundlich Adsorption Isotherm

Core formulas, rapid recall points, and distinguishing criteria at a glance.

Empirical Power Law

The Freundlich isotherm relates adsorption capacity x/m to pressure P through the equation x/m = k · P1/n.

Logarithmic Linearization

Taking the logarithm transforms the empirical curve into a straight-line equation: log(x/m) = log k + (1/n) log P.

Pressure Regimes

At low pressure 1/n approaches 1 (linear); at high pressure 1/n approaches 0 (saturation independent of pressure).

Scope and Limitations

The model is empirical and restricted to intermediate pressure ranges, failing at extreme high pressures.

📚 Terms & Units Glossary

Adsorbate (Substance adsorbed)
The gas or liquid substance that accumulates on the surface. Dimensionless quantity.
Adsorbent (Surface solid)
The solid or liquid material on whose surface adsorption occurs.
x/m (Extent of adsorption)
Mass of adsorbate per unit mass of adsorbent. Unit: g g-1.
k (Freundlich constant)
Constant related to adsorption capacity depending on system and temperature.
n (Freundlich constant)
Constant related to adsorption intensity, where 1/n is the slope.
Equilibrium Pressure (P)
Pressure of the gas when adsorption equilibrium is attained. Unit: atm.

🎯 Key Takeaways

  • Freundlich equation: x / m = k · P1/n
  • Linear form: log(x / m) = log k + (1 / n) log P
  • Slope of log plot = 1 / n
  • Intercept of log plot = log k
  • Valid only at intermediate pressures

📌 Quick Recall

  • Freundlich isotherm is purely empirical.
  • It correlates extent of adsorption with pressure.
  • It fails at high pressures due to surface saturation.
  • Constants k and n depend on temperature and nature of gas and solid.

⚖️ Key Differences

Constant k VS Constant 1/n

k determines the overall capacity of adsorption, whereas 1/n determines the intensity and dependence on pressure.
