Stability of Colloids

Colloidal stability is governed by the delicate balance of attractive van der Waals forces and repulsive electrostatic forces described by DLVO theory. The presence of electrical double layers and solvation shells prevents particle aggregation and precipitation.

UP PGT Chemistry Subject Code 02 B.Sc. / M.Sc. Level

Origin of Charge and Electrical Double Layer

  • Colloidal particles acquire net charge via preferential adsorption of common ions from solution or dissociation of surface groups.
  • The Stern Model divides the electrical double layer into a fixed inner layer (Stern layer) and a diffuse mobile outer layer (Gouy-Chapman layer).
  • The potential drop across the diffuse mobile layer is defined as the zeta potential (ζ), which dictates colloidal stability.
  • A high magnitude of zeta potential (> +30 mV or < -30 mV) ensures strong interparticle electrostatic repulsion preventing aggregation.

DLVO Theory of Colloidal Stability

  • DLVO Theory (Derjaguin, Landau, Verwey, Overbeek) quantitatively correlates stability with the sum of attractive and repulsive potential energy curves.
  • Attractive potential (VA) arises from dispersion forces operating universally between all atoms and molecules.
  • Repulsive potential (VR) arises from the overlap of electrical double layers surrounding adjacent approaching particles.
  • The presence of a primary energy barrier prevents particles from falling into the deep primary minimum where irreversible coagulation occurs.

Coagulation and Flocculation Mechanisms

  • Coagulation involves neutralizing surface charge by adding electrolytes, thereby collapsing the electrical double layer.
  • The Hardy-Schulze Rule states that the flocculating power of an active ion is directly proportional to its valence raised to the sixth power.
  • Protective colloids (lyophilic sols) form a physical solvation shell around lyophobic colloids, quantified by their gold number.
  • Sensitization occurs when insufficient protective colloid is added, lowering stability rather than enhancing it.
📐 Key Formulas & Equations
Electrokinetic Potential (Zeta Potential)
ζ = 4 π η vE ε
Conditions: Helmholtz-Smoluchowski relation for electrophoresis
Units: ζ in volts (V) or millivolts (mV), η in poise, v in cm s-1
Total Potential Energy in DLVO Theory
VT = VA + VR
Conditions: Combination of attractive van der Waals potential (VA) and repulsive electrostatic potential (VR)
Units: Energy in Joules (J) or kBT
London-van der Waals Attractive Potential
VA = - A H12 π D2
Conditions: For two parallel flat plates separated by distance D
Units: A = Hamaker constant (J), H = area, D = distance (m)
Hardy-Schulze Rule (Flocculation Value)
Flocculation Value ∝ 1(Valency)6
Conditions: For coagulation of colloids by oppositely charged ions
Units: Flocculation value in millimoles per liter (mmol L-1)
⚠️ Important Exceptions & Anomalies
Exception Higher valence ions exhibit significantly higher coagulating power according to Hardy-Schulze rule.

Observation: Transition metal ions with identical charge can exhibit anomalous coagulation values due to complexation or specific adsorption. — Specific chemical interactions and hydration radii alter the effective charge density at the shear plane.

Exception Lyophobic sols are inherently unstable and coagulate readily upon electrolyte addition.

Observation: Extremely dilute lyophobic sols or sols stabilized by high-purity electro-dialysis exhibit apparent kinetic stability. — Absence of counter-ions and extremely low collision frequencies limit aggregation rates despite minimal thermodynamic stability.

Exception Gold number is inversely proportional to the protective power of a lyophilic colloid.

Observation: Protective agents that undergo association or conformational changes at varying pH exhibit non-linear protective behavior. — Altered surface exposure of hydrophilic functional groups changes the steric hindrance efficiency.

💡 Shortcuts & Memory Aids
Hardy-Schulze Valency Mnemonic
Power ∝ Z6 (where Z = Valency: 1, 2, 3 → 1, 64, 729)

Application: For negative sols, look for cations: Al3+ > Ba2+ > Na+. For positive sols, look for anions: [Fe(CN)6]4- > PO43- > SO42- > Cl-.

Gold Number Inverse Rule
Smaller Gold Number = Greater Protective Power

Application: When ranking lyophilic colloids (gelatin, starch, gum arabic) by protective efficiency, pick the one with the minimum milligram value.

Zeta Potential Threshold
|ζ| > 30 mV → Stable; |ζ| < 5 mV → Rapid Coagulation

Application: Quickly determine sol stability regime from electrophoretic mobility data in numerical questions.

🎯 Key Exam Points
  • The stability of lyophobic colloids is primarily kinetic and thermodynamic, maintained by the electrical double layer.
  • According to the Hardy-Schulze rule, coagulating power increases sharply with the valency of the flocculating ion.
  • Gold number is defined as the minimum mass in milligrams of a protective colloid required to prevent coagulation of 10 mL of standard gold sol by 1 mL of 10% NaCl solution.
  • Zeta potential is measured via electro-osmosis, electrophoresis, sedimentation potential, or streaming potential.
  • Mutual coagulation occurs when two oppositely charged sols are mixed in equimolar proportions (e.g., Fe(OH)3 and As2S3).
  • Peptization is the process of converting a freshly precipitated ionic precipitate into a colloidal sol by adding a suitable peptizing electrolyte containing a common ion.
🚫 Common Errors & Confusions
  • Confusing flocculation value with flocculating power; flocculation value is inversely proportional to coagulating power.
  • Assuming lyophilic colloids require electrical charge for stability, whereas their stability depends predominantly on hydration and steric barriers.
  • Forgetting that anions coagulate positive sols and cations coagulate negative sols.
  • Confusing the Stern potential with the Nernst (thermodynamic) potential; Nernst potential is the total potential drop across the entire double layer, while zeta is at the shear plane.

⚡ Quick Revision: Stability of Colloids

Core formulas, rapid recall points, and distinguishing criteria at a glance.

Electrical Double Layer Structure

The particle surface holds a fixed potential-determining ion layer (Nernst potential), followed by a bound Stern layer and a diffuse Gouy-Chapman layer ending at the shear plane with the zeta potential (ζ).

DLVO Potential Energy Profiles

The total interaction energy curve VT(D) features a secondary minimum (weak flocculation), a primary maximum (energy barrier preventing aggregation), and a primary minimum (irreversible coagulation at contact).

Electrolyte Coagulation Kinetics

Electrolytes compress the diffuse double layer, reducing the Debye length (κ-1) and eliminating the electrostatic energy barrier, allowing van der Waals attractive forces to dominate.

Steric and Protective Stabilization

Macromolecular polymers or lyophilic colloids adsorbed on particle surfaces provide steric repulsion via osmotic and volume restriction effects, ensuring stability even at high ionic strengths.

📚 Terms & Units Glossary

Zeta Potential (ζ)
Potential at the shear plane of the electrical double layer measured in mV.
Flocculation Value (C)
Minimum millimoles of electrolyte required to coagulate 1 L of sol in 2 hours.
Gold Number (G)
Milligrams of protective colloid preventing coagulation of 10 mL gold sol by 1 mL 10% NaCl.
Hamaker Constant (A)
Material-dependent constant quantifying van der Waals interaction energies between particles.
Peptization (Dispersion)
Conversion of a precipitate into a colloidal sol by adding an electrolyte.
Electro-osmosis (Electro-kinetic)
Movement of dispersion medium relative to a stationary charged surface under an electric field.

🎯 Key Takeaways

  • Hardy-Schulze rule: Power ∝ Z6
  • Gold number is inversely proportional to protective power
  • Lyophilic sols are reversible; lyophobic sols are irreversible
  • Mutual coagulation occurs when mixing Fe(OH)3 (+) and As2S3 (-)
  • High zeta potential magnitude ensures high electrostatic stability

📌 Quick Recall

  • Colloidal stability depends on electrostatic repulsion versus van der Waals attraction.
  • Electrolytes cause coagulation by compressing the diffuse double layer.
  • Valency of counter-ions dictates coagulation efficiency exponentially.
  • Protective agents confer stability via solvation and steric hindrance.

⚖️ Key Differences

Coagulation VS Flocculation

Coagulation often denotes dense precipitate formation via double-layer collapse, whereas flocculation can refer to loose aggregate network formation in secondary minima.

Question 1 of 20

Which of the following ions has the highest coagulating power for an arsenic sulfide (As2S3) sol?

Explanation: As2S3 is a negatively charged sol. According to the Hardy-Schulze rule, cations with the highest valency (Al3+) possess maximum coagulating power.

Question 2 of 20

What is the primary origin of charge on lyophobic colloidal particles in water?

Explanation: Lyophobic colloids acquire charge predominantly by preferential adsorption of ions present in excess or common to the colloidal lattice.

Question 3 of 20

The gold number of four protective colloids A, B, C, and D are 0.005, 0.05, 0.5, and 5.0 respectively. Which has the maximum protective power?

Explanation: Gold number is inversely proportional to protective power. Colloid A has the smallest gold number (0.005), hence maximum protective power.

Question 4 of 20

According to DLVO theory, colloidal stability is determined by the balance between:

Explanation: DLVO theory models colloidal interaction energy as the sum of attractive van der Waals potential and repulsive electrical double layer potential.

Question 5 of 20

Which electrolyte solution will be most effective in coagulating a positively charged ferric hydroxide sol?

Explanation: For a positive sol, coagulating power increases with anion valency. [Fe(CN)6]4- has a valency of 4, providing the highest coagulating power.

Question 6 of 20

The electrokinetic potential is also known as:

Explanation: The potential at the shear plane between the Stern layer and the diffuse layer is defined as the zeta potential or electrokinetic potential.

Question 7 of 20

Process of converting freshly precipitated substance into colloidal particles by adding a suitable electrolyte is called:

Explanation: Peptization is the dispersion of a freshly precipitated solid into a colloidal sol by shaking with a dispersion medium containing a peptizing agent.

Question 8 of 20

Which of the following is a lyophilic colloid?

Explanation: Gelatin is a macromolecular organic substance that readily forms a lyophilic (solvent-loving) sol in water.

Question 9 of 20

Mutual coagulation occurs when:

Explanation: Mixing oppositely charged sols in equivalent proportions leads to mutual neutralization of charges and subsequent co-precipitation or mutual coagulation.

Question 10 of 20

What is the unit of flocculation value?

Explanation: Flocculation value is conventionally expressed in millimoles of electrolyte per liter of colloidal solution.

Question 11 of 20

The Hardy-Schulze rule states that the coagulating power of an ion depends on:

Explanation: Hardy-Schulze formulated that coagulating power is a strong function of the valency (charge) of the active counter-ion.

Question 12 of 20

In the Stern model of the electrical double layer, the potential drops across:

Explanation: The total potential drop spans both the inner tightly bound Stern layer and the outer diffuse layer.

Question 13 of 20

Which property distinguishes lyophilic colloids from lyophobic colloids?

Explanation: Lyophilic colloids are reversible; when dried, they can be easily reconstituted by adding the dispersion medium, unlike lyophobic colloids.

Question 14 of 20

What happens to the zeta potential when an electrolyte is added to a colloidal sol?

Explanation: Adding electrolytes compresses the electrical double layer, decreasing the magnitude of the zeta potential toward the critical coagulation threshold.

Question 15 of 20

Which phenomenon is directly responsible for preventing lyophobic colloidal particles from settling under gravity?

Explanation: Electrostatic repulsion arising from the electrical double layer prevents particles from approaching close enough for van der Waals forces to cause permanent aggregation.

Question 16 of 20

What is the typical size range of colloidal particles?

Explanation: Colloidal dispersion systems are characterized by dispersed phase dimensions ranging between 1 nm and 1000 nm.

Question 17 of 20

Which of the following substances acts as a protective colloid?

Explanation: Starch is a lyophilic macromolecule that functions as a protective colloid when added to lyophobic sols.

Question 18 of 20

The primary minimum in a DLVO interaction energy curve represents:

Explanation: At very short separation distances, van der Waals attraction dominates completely, creating a deep primary minimum where particles coagulate irreversibly.

Question 19 of 20

Electrophoresis is the movement of colloidal particles under the influence of:

Explanation: Electrophoresis involves the migration of charged colloidal particles toward the oppositely charged electrode when an external electric field is applied.

Question 20 of 20

Stabilization of emulsions is commonly achieved by adding:

Explanation: Emulsifiers or surfactants adsorb at the liquid-liquid interface, lowering interfacial tension and providing electrostatic or steric stabilization.

Question 1 of 10
What is the correct order of coagulating power of phosphate, sulfate, and chloride ions for a positive ferric hydroxide sol?

Solution: For positive sols, anion valency dictates coagulating power: PO43- (3-) > SO42- (2-) > Cl- (1-).

Question 2 of 10
In the DLVO theory curve, what prevents particles trapped in the secondary minimum from collapsing into the primary minimum?

Solution: The primary energy barrier (maximum in total potential energy VT) prevents particles from reaching the primary minimum.

Question 3 of 10
If the gold number of starch is 25, it means that:

Solution: Gold number is defined precisely as the number of milligrams of protective colloid required to prevent coagulation of 10 mL of gold sol by 1 mL of 10% NaCl solution.

Question 4 of 10
Which sol is negatively charged among the following?

Solution: Arsenic sulfide (As2S3) sol is a classic example of a negatively charged metallic sulfide sol.

Question 5 of 10
What happens to the electrical double layer when the ionic strength of the medium is increased?

Solution: Increasing electrolyte concentration (ionic strength) compresses the diffuse electrical double layer, reducing the Debye length.

Question 6 of 10
The stabilization of oil-in-water emulsions by soaps is primarily due to:

Solution: Soaps act as emulsifiers, adsorbing at the interface to lower interfacial tension and build a repulsive barrier preventing droplet coalescence.

Question 7 of 10
Which electrokinetic phenomenon involves the migration of the dispersion medium under an applied electric field while particles are immobilized?

Solution: Electro-osmosis describes the movement of liquid through a porous plug or capillary network under an electric field when solid particles are stationary.

Question 8 of 10
Why are lyophilic sols much more difficult to coagulate than lyophobic sols?

Solution: Lyophilic colloids are heavily solvated by the dispersion medium, forming a physical solvent sheath that prevents particle-particle contact even when charge is neutralized.

Question 9 of 10
Which of the following ions is least effective in coagulating a negatively charged silver iodide (AgI) sol?

Solution: AgI sol is negative; cations are active. Na+ has the lowest valency (+1), making it the least effective coagulating ion compared to Ba2+, Al3+, and Th4+.

Question 10 of 10
What is the physical significance of the critical coagulation concentration (CCC)?

Solution: Critical coagulation concentration (CCC) defines the threshold electrolyte concentration needed to induce rapid coagulation of a colloidal sol.
