Physical Chemistry

Liquefaction of Gases

Liquefaction of gases is achieved by lowering the temperature and increasing the pressure to overcome intermolecular forces of attraction. Below the critical temperature, a gas can be liquefied solely by the application of sufficient pressure.

UP PGT Chemistry Subject Code 02 B.Sc. / M.Sc. Level

Andrew's Isotherms and Critical Phenomena

  • Critical Temperature (Tc) is the highest temperature at which a gas can be liquefied by pressure alone.
  • Above Tc, the gas exists as a supercritical fluid and cannot be liquefied regardless of how high the pressure is raised.
  • Critical Pressure (Pc) is the minimum pressure required to liquefy a gas at its critical temperature.
  • At the critical point, the liquid and vapor phases become indistinguishable and merge into a single homogeneous phase.

Methods of Liquefaction

  • Linde's Method utilizes the Joule-Thomson effect, where compressed gas expands through a porous plug, causing rapid cooling.
  • Claude's Method combines adiabatic expansion against a piston (doing external work) with Joule-Thomson cooling for higher efficiency.
  • Adiabatic Demagnetization is employed for achieving ultra-low cryogenic temperatures needed to liquefy permanent gases like helium.
  • Permanent gases have extremely low Tc values and must be pre-cooled below their inversion temperatures before expansion.

Joule-Thomson Effect and Inversion Temperature

  • The Joule-Thomson effect describes the temperature change of a real gas when throttled through an insulated valve from high to low pressure.
  • Inversion Temperature (Ti) is the threshold temperature below which a gas cools upon expansion (μJT > 0) and above which it heats (μJT < 0).
  • The relationship between inversion temperature and Van der Waals constants is given by Ti = 2a / (Rb).
  • At the inversion temperature, the Joule-Thomson coefficient μJT = 0, meaning no temperature change occurs upon expansion.

⚠️ Important Exceptions & Anomalies
Exception Gases cool down upon expansion through a throttle valve (Joule-Thomson effect).

Observation: Hydrogen (H2) and Helium (He) heat up when expanded at ordinary room temperatures. — Their inversion temperatures are very low (approx. 204 K for H2 and 43 K for He), meaning room temperature lies above their inversion temperatures where μJT is negative.

Exception Real gases obey ideal gas behavior at low pressure and high temperature.

Observation: At extremely high pressures, repulsive forces dominate, making the compressibility factor Z greater than 1. — The volume of gas molecules 'b' cannot be neglected compared to the total volume container, dominating over attractive forces 'a'.

🎯 Key Exam Points
  • The critical temperature of water is 647 K, making it very easy to liquefy.
  • Helium has the lowest critical temperature (5.2 K) among all known substances.
  • At inversion temperature, the enthalpy of the gas remains constant during throttling (isenthalpic process).
  • The constant 'b' in Van der Waals equation represents four times the actual volume of the gas molecules per mole.
  • Sulfur dioxide (SO2) and ammonia (NH3) liquefy easily at room temperature under moderate pressure due to strong dipole-dipole or hydrogen bonding forces.
  • Permanent gases include O2, N2, H2, and He, which require specialized cryogenic cooling techniques for liquefaction.
🚫 Common Errors & Confusions
  • Confusing critical temperature with inversion temperature; remember Ti is significantly higher than Tc.
  • Assuming all gases cool upon expansion; hydrogen and helium heat up if expanded above their inversion temperatures.
  • Assuming liquefaction is possible above Tc by applying massive pressure; supercritical fluids cannot form a liquid surface.
  • Forgetting that units of 'a' are L2 bar mol-2 while 'b' is in L mol-1.

⚡ Quick Revision: Liquefaction of Gases

Core formulas, rapid recall points, and distinguishing criteria at a glance.

Critical Constants Derivation

Critical constants Pc, Vc, and Tc are derived by setting the first and second derivatives of pressure with respect to volume equal to zero at the inflection point of the critical isotherm.

Role of Intermolecular Forces

Gases possessing large Van der Waals 'a' parameters exhibit high intermolecular attraction, leading to high critical temperatures and easy liquefaction.

Joule-Thomson Throttling Mechanism

Isenthalpic expansion causes cooling when attractive potential energy increases faster than kinetic energy decreases, which occurs exclusively below the inversion temperature.

Supercritical Fluid Region

Beyond Tc and Pc, phase boundaries vanish, yielding properties intermediate between gases and liquids with high solvating power.

📚 Terms & Units Glossary

Critical Temperature (Tc)
Maximum temperature for gas liquefaction; Unit: Kelvin (K).
Critical Pressure (Pc)
Minimum pressure required for liquefaction at Tc; Unit: bar or atm.
Critical Volume (Vc)
Molar volume of gas at critical point; Unit: L mol-1.
Inversion Temperature (Ti)
Temperature below which Joule-Thomson cooling occurs; Unit: Kelvin (K).
Joule-Thomson Coefficient (μJT)
Rate of temperature change with pressure at constant enthalpy; Unit: K bar-1.
Compressibility Factor (Z)
Ratio of actual molar volume to ideal molar volume; Dimensionless.

🎯 Key Takeaways

  • Tc = 8a / (27Rb)
  • Pc = a / (27b2)
  • Vc = 3b
  • Ti = 2a / (Rb) = 6.75 Tc
  • Zc = 3/8

📌 Quick Recall

  • Liquefaction requires lowering temperature below Tc and applying pressure.
  • Joule-Thomson effect governs gas cooling upon adiabatic expansion.
  • Hydrogen and helium require pre-cooling below their inversion temperatures before expansion.
  • Higher Van der Waals 'a' parameter promotes easier gas liquefaction.

⚖️ Key Differences

Critical Temperature (Tc) VS Inversion Temperature (Ti)

Tc is the limit for liquefaction by pressure, whereas Ti is the limit for Joule-Thomson cooling.

Which of the following gases has the highest critical temperature?

Question 3 of 19

During the Joule-Thomson expansion, which thermodynamic property remains constant?

Explanation: The Joule-Thomson effect is an isenthalpic process where the enthalpy of the system remains constant (H1 = H2).

Question 5 of 19

At temperatures above the inversion temperature, the Joule-Thomson coefficient (μJT) is:

Explanation: Above Ti, the gas heats up upon expansion, meaning μJT = (∂T / ∂P)H is negative.

Question 6 of 19

Which gas heats up when allowed to expand through a porous plug at room temperature (298 K)?

Explanation: Helium has a very low inversion temperature (approx. 43 K), so at 298 K it is well above Ti and heats up upon expansion.

Question 7 of 19

What is the ratio of inversion temperature (Ti) to critical temperature (Tc) for a Van der Waals gas?

Explanation: Ti = 2a / (Rb) and Tc = 8a / (27Rb). Dividing Ti by Tc yields 27/4 = 6.75.

Question 8 of 19

The critical volume (Vc) of a Van der Waals gas in terms of constant 'b' is:

Explanation: The critical molar volume is exactly equal to three times the co-volume, Vc = 3b.

Question 9 of 19

Which method is commonly used for the liquefaction of air?

Explanation: Linde's method utilizes the Joule-Thomson effect for the continuous cooling and liquefaction of air and permanent gases.

Question 10 of 19

Permanent gases are difficult to liquefy because:

Explanation: Permanent gases possess extremely weak van der Waals forces (small 'a'), resulting in very low critical temperatures.

Question 11 of 19

What is the physical state of matter above its critical temperature and critical pressure?

Explanation: Above Tc and Pc, the distinction between liquid and gas disappears, forming a supercritical fluid.

Question 12 of 19

In the Van der Waals equation, the constant 'a' is a measure of:

Explanation: Van der Waals constant 'a' accounts for the magnitude of attractive forces operating between gas molecules.

Question 13 of 19

If the inversion temperature of a gas is 204 K, it will cool upon throttling at:

Explanation: Cooling occurs only when the expansion temperature is below the inversion temperature (150 K < 204 K).

Question 14 of 19

The unit of Van der Waals constant 'a' is:

Explanation: Dimensional analysis of the pressure correction term P + a(n/V)2 gives L2 atm mol-2 or L2 bar mol-2.

Question 15 of 19

Claude's method of liquefaction differs from Linde's method because it involves:

Explanation: Claude's method extracts energy by making the expanding gas perform external work against a piston, enhancing cooling efficiency.

Question 16 of 19

At the critical point, the slope of the PV isotherm (∂P / ∂V)T is:

Explanation: The critical isotherm shows an inflection point at the critical point, where both the first and second derivatives of pressure with respect to volume are zero.

Question 17 of 19

Which of the following conditions favors the liquefaction of a gas?

Explanation: Low temperatures reduce kinetic energy while high pressures force molecules closer together, facilitating liquefaction.

Question 18 of 19

What is the relation between critical pressure Pc, critical temperature Tc, and critical volume Vc?

Explanation: Substituting critical constants into the compressibility factor expression yields Zc = PcVc / RTc = 3/8.

Question 19 of 19

Which gas among the following can be liquefied most easily?

Solution: Ti = 6.75 × Tc = 6.75 × 150 K = 1012.5 K.

During the Joule-Thomson expansion of a real gas below its inversion temperature:
