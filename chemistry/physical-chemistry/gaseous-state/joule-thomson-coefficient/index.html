Physical Chemistry

Joule–Thomson Coefficient

The Joule–Thomson coefficient quantifies the temperature change of a real gas undergoing isenthalpic expansion through a porous plug. It defines whether a gas experiences heating or cooling upon throttling.

UP PGT Chemistry Subject Code 02 B.Sc. / M.Sc. Level

Thermodynamic Basis of Isenthalpic Expansion

  • The Joule–Thomson effect describes the change in temperature of a gas when it is forced through a porous plug or throttling valve from a high-pressure region to a low-pressure region.
  • The process is strictly isententhalpic, meaning the enthalpy of the system remains constant (ΔH = 0) throughout the expansion.
  • The magnitude and sign of the temperature change depend directly on the Joule–Thomson coefficient (μJT).

Mathematical Derivation and van der Waals Behavior

  • Using Maxwell relations, μJT is expressed as μJT = -( ∂H / ∂P )T / Cp.
  • For an ideal gas, internal energy and enthalpy depend only on temperature; hence, (∂V / ∂T)P = R/P, leading to μJT = 0.
  • For a van der Waals gas, intermolecular attractive forces (parameter a) favor cooling, while molecular volume (parameter b) favors heating.

Inversion Temperature and Liquefaction of Gases

  • The temperature at which μJT changes sign is known as the inversion temperature (Ti).
  • Below Ti, μJT > 0, the gas cools upon expansion; above Ti, μJT < 0, the gas heats upon expansion.
  • Hydrogen and helium possess exceptionally low inversion temperatures (around 204 K and 43 K respectively) and must be pre-cooled below their Ti before they can undergo liquefaction via the Linde process.
📐 Key Formulas & Equations
Thermodynamic Equation for μJT
μJT = (1 / Cp) [ T(∂V / ∂T)P - V ]
Conditions: Applicable to any homogeneous fluid
Units: K bar-1
Joule–Thomson Coefficient for van der Waals Gas
μJT = (1 / Cp) [ (2a / RT) - b ]
Conditions: Low to moderate pressures for van der Waals gases
Units: K bar-1
Inversion Temperature
Ti = 2a / (Rb)
Conditions: Point where μJT = 0
Units: Kelvin (K)
Definition of Joule–Thomson Coefficient
μJT = (∂T / ∂P)H
Conditions: Isenthalpic process (constant enthalpy, H)
Units: K bar-1 or K atm-1
⚠️ Important Exceptions & Anomalies
Exception Real gases always cool upon expansion through a throttling valve.

Observation: Hydrogen (H2) and Helium (He) heat up upon adiabatic throttling at room temperature. — Their inversion temperatures are below room temperature (Ti for H2 is ~204 K, for He is ~43 K), making μJT negative at 298 K.

Exception Ideal gases show temperature changes during expansion.

Observation: Ideal gases exhibit zero Joule–Thomson coefficient (μJT = 0). — Absence of intermolecular forces and zero molecular volume eliminate any potential energy contribution to enthalpy changes during expansion.

Exception The Joule–Thomson coefficient is independent of pressure.

Observation: μJT varies significantly with pressure at high pressures. — At high pressures, non-ideal behavior intensifies, and higher-order virial coefficients become non-negligible.

🎯 Key Exam Points
  • Joule-Thomson expansion is an isenthalpic process where enthalpy remains constant (ΔH = 0).
  • The Joule-Thomson coefficient is defined mathematically as μJT = (∂T / ∂P)H.
  • For an ideal gas, the Joule-Thomson coefficient is identically equal to zero.
  • The inversion temperature is related to van der Waals constants by the expression Ti = 2a / (Rb).
  • At the inversion temperature, the Joule-Thomson coefficient is zero and the gas neither heats nor cools upon expansion.
  • Hydrogen and helium show heating upon throttling at room temperature because their inversion temperatures are lower than 298 K.
🚫 Common Errors & Confusions
  • Confusing adiabatic reversible expansion (isntropic, work done) with Joule-Thomson expansion (isenthalpic, no external work done).
  • Assuming all real gases cool upon throttling at any temperature, forgetting the existence of the inversion temperature.
  • Incorrectly stating that the Joule-Thomson coefficient for an ideal gas is positive or negative instead of zero.
  • Mixing up the formulas for Boyle temperature (TB = a / (Rb)) and Inversion temperature (Ti = 2a / (Rb)).

⚡ Quick Revision: Joule–Thomson Coefficient

Core formulas, rapid recall points, and distinguishing criteria at a glance.

Isenthalpic Nature

Joule-Thomson expansion occurs through a porous plug under adiabatic, constant enthalpy conditions where Hinitial = Hfinal.

Mathematical Formulation

The coefficient μJT evaluates temperature change per unit pressure drop at constant enthalpy, governed by thermal expansion and heat capacity.

Role of van der Waals Constants

Attractive forces (a) promote cooling while molecular volume (b) promotes heating, determining the net sign of μJT.

Inversion Temperature Threshold

Ti marks the thermal boundary separating the cooling regime from the heating regime during throttling expansion.

📚 Terms & Units Glossary

Joule-Thomson Coefficient (μJT)
Rate of change of temperature with pressure at constant enthalpy (K bar-1).
Isenthalpic Process (H = constant)
A thermodynamic process in which the enthalpy of the system remains unchanged.
Inversion Temperature (Ti)
The temperature at which the Joule-Thomson coefficient becomes zero (K).
Boyle Temperature (TB)
Temperature at which a real gas behaves like an ideal gas over a reasonable pressure range (K).
Porous Plug (Throttling Valve)
Experimental device used to maintain pressure drop in isenthalpic expansion.
Linde Process (Liquefaction method)
Industrial method for gas liquefaction utilizing the Joule-Thomson cooling effect.

🎯 Key Takeaways

  • μJT = (∂T / ∂P)H
  • For ideal gas: μJT = 0
  • For van der Waals gas: μJT = (1/Cp)[(2a/RT) - b]
  • Inversion Temperature: Ti = 2a / (Rb)
  • At T = Ti, μJT = 0

📌 Quick Recall

  • Joule-Thomson effect describes temperature changes in real gases during throttling.
  • Cooling occurs when T < Ti; heating occurs when T > Ti.
  • Hydrogen and helium require pre-cooling because their inversion temperatures are low.
  • Ideal gases show no Joule-Thomson effect.

⚖️ Key Differences

Inversion Temperature (Ti) VS Boyle Temperature (TB)

Ti = 2a / (Rb) is twice the Boyle temperature TB = a / (Rb).

Question 1 of 19

What is the value of the Joule-Thomson coefficient for an ideal gas?

Explanation: An ideal gas has no intermolecular forces or finite molecular volume, resulting in a Joule-Thomson coefficient of zero.

Question 2 of 19

During a Joule-Thomson expansion, which thermodynamic property remains constant?

Explanation: Joule-Thomson expansion is an isenthalpic process where enthalpy (H) remains constant.

Question 3 of 19

What is the relation between Inversion temperature (Ti) and Boyle temperature (TB) for a van der Waals gas?

Explanation: Ti = 2a/(Rb) and TB = a/(Rb), therefore Ti = 2TB.

Question 4 of 19

Which of the following gases shows heating upon Joule-Thomson expansion at room temperature (298 K)?

Explanation: Hydrogen has an inversion temperature of ~204 K, so at 298 K it is above Ti and heats up upon expansion.

Question 5 of 19

The units of the Joule-Thomson coefficient are:

Explanation: μJT = (∂T / ∂P)H, which has units of temperature over pressure (K atm-1 or K bar-1).

Question 6 of 19

At the inversion temperature, the Joule-Thomson coefficient is:

Explanation: By definition, the inversion temperature is the temperature at which μJT changes sign and is equal to zero.

Question 7 of 19

Which factor is responsible for the cooling effect in a van der Waals gas during Joule-Thomson expansion?

Explanation: Attractive forces (parameter a) cause potential energy to increase as molecules pull apart, leading to a drop in kinetic energy and temperature.

Question 8 of 19

Which gas has the highest inversion temperature among common gases?

Explanation: Gases with stronger intermolecular forces, like carbon dioxide, have higher inversion temperatures.

Question 9 of 19

The Joule-Thomson expansion is:

Explanation: Throttling through a porous plug is a spontaneous, dissipative, and highly irreversible process.

Question 10 of 19

For a gas where μJT < 0, expansion through a valve results in:

Explanation: A negative Joule-Thomson coefficient means ΔT is positive when ΔP is negative, causing heating.

Question 11 of 19

What is the expression for μJT derived from van der Waals equation at low pressures?

Explanation: For a van der Waals gas, μJT = (1 / Cp) [ (2a / RT) - b ].

Question 12 of 19

Why must helium be pre-cooled before expansion in the Linde process?

Explanation: Helium's inversion temperature is ~43 K; it must be cooled below this threshold to exhibit cooling upon throttling.

Question 13 of 19

In the relation μJT = (1/Cp)[T(∂V/∂T)P - V], the term T(∂V/∂T)P represents:

Explanation: It reflects the volume change with temperature at constant pressure multiplied by absolute temperature.

Question 14 of 19

Which of the following conditions favors gas cooling during throttling?

Explanation: Operating below the inversion temperature ensures μJT > 0, producing cooling.

Question 15 of 19

The Joule-Thomson effect provides direct evidence of:

Explanation: The non-zero temperature changes confirm the existence of intermolecular forces and finite molecular volumes in real gases.

Question 16 of 19

If a gas expands adiabatically and reversibly, the process is:

Explanation: Reversible adiabatic expansion maintains constant entropy (isentropic), unlike Joule-Thomson expansion which is isenthalpic.

Question 17 of 19

What happens to the Joule-Thomson coefficient as temperature approaches infinity?

Explanation: At very high temperatures, (2a/RT) becomes negligible compared to b, making μJT approach -b/Cp (negative constant).

Question 18 of 19

Which parameter predominantly dictates heating during Joule-Thomson expansion at high temperatures?

Explanation: At high temperatures, repulsive forces and molecular volume (parameter b) dominate, leading to heating.

Question 19 of 19

Can an ideal gas ever liquefy via Joule-Thomson expansion?

Explanation: Since μJT = 0 for an ideal gas, no temperature change occurs, making liquefaction impossible.

Question 1 of 10
Identify the correct thermodynamic condition for Joule-Thomson expansion.

Solution: Joule-Thomson expansion is adiabatic (q = 0) and isenthalpic (ΔH = 0).

Question 2 of 10
For a van der Waals gas, if T > Ti, the Joule-Thomson coefficient is:

Solution: Above the inversion temperature, the repulsive term dominates and μJT becomes negative.

Question 3 of 10
The ratio of inversion temperature to Boyle temperature for a van der Waals gas is:

Solution: Ti = 2a/Rb and TB = a/Rb, making the ratio Ti : TB = 2 : 1.

Question 4 of 10
Which of the following gases has the lowest inversion temperature?

Solution: Helium has extremely weak intermolecular forces (very small 'a'), giving it the lowest inversion temperature (~43 K).

Question 5 of 10
What is the physical significance of μJT > 0?

Solution: A positive Joule-Thomson coefficient means a drop in pressure (ΔP < 0) results in a drop in temperature (ΔT < 0), causing cooling.

Question 6 of 10
In the expression μJT = (1/Cp)[(2a/RT) - b], heating occurs when:

Solution: When 2a/RT < b, the term in brackets is negative, making μJT negative, which corresponds to heating.

Question 7 of 10
Why does Joule-Thomson expansion require a porous plug or throttling valve?

Solution: The porous plug creates a high resistance restricting flow, allowing a controlled, adiabatic pressure drop.

Question 8 of 10
Which of the following statements is incorrect regarding the Joule-Thomson effect?

Solution: Joule-Thomson expansion is irreversible, meaning entropy increases (ΔS > 0), so it is not isentropic.

Question 9 of 10
If a gas expands from 10 atm to 1 atm through a porous plug and its temperature increases, the operating temperature is:

Solution: An increase in temperature upon pressure drop indicates heating, which occurs when the gas is above its inversion temperature.

Question 10 of 10
What happens to the Joule-Thomson cooling effect as the initial pressure before throttling is increased?

Solution: At very high initial pressures, non-ideal gas behavior and high-density effects alter the expected linear cooling trend.
