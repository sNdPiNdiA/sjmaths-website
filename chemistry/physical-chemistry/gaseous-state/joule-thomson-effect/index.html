Joule–Thomson Effect

The Joule–Thomson effect describes the temperature change of a real gas when it expands adiabatically through a porous plug or valve from a high-pressure region to a low-pressure region. This process is isenthalpic, meaning the total enthalpy of the system remains constant during the expansion.

UP PGT Chemistry Subject Code 02 B.Sc. / M.Sc. Level

Thermodynamics of Isenthalpic Expansion

  • The Joule–Thomson expansion is conducted adiabatically under constant enthalpy conditions, meaning dH = 0.
  • The degree of temperature change per unit pressure drop is defined as the Joule–Thomson coefficient (μJT).
  • An ideal gas exhibits zero Joule–Thomson effect because intermolecular forces are absent, yielding μJT = 0.

Cooling, Heating, and Inversion Temperature

  • When μJT > 0, expansion causes cooling, which is observed below the inversion temperature.
  • When μJT < 0, expansion causes heating, which occurs when the gas is above its inversion temperature.
  • The inversion temperature (Ti) represents the threshold where the Joule–Thomson coefficient transitions from positive to negative.

Molecular Interpretation via Van der Waals Constants

  • Attractive forces dominate at lower temperatures, assisting expansion and causing cooling due to potential energy increase.
  • Repulsive forces dominate at higher temperatures, resisting expansion and causing heating due to kinetic energy conversion.
  • The constant 'a' promotes cooling via intermolecular attractions, whereas 'b' promotes heating via excluded volume repulsions.
📐 Key Formulas & Equations
Thermodynamic Equation for Joule–Thomson Coefficient
μJT = (1 / CP) [ T(∂V / ∂T)P - V ]
Conditions: Applicable to any homogeneous fluid
Units: CP in J K-1 mol-1, V in m3 mol-1
Joule–Thomson Coefficient using Van der Waals Equation
μJT = (1 / CP) [ (2a / RT) - b ]
Conditions: Low to moderate pressures for Van der Waals gases
Units: a in Pa m6 mol-2, b in m3 mol-1, R in J K-1 mol-1
Inversion Temperature
Ti = 2a / (Rb)
Conditions: At this temperature, μJT = 0
Units: Ti in Kelvin
Joule–Thomson Coefficient
μJT = (∂T / ∂P)H
Conditions: Constant enthalpy process (isenthalpic expansion)
Units: μJT in K bar-1 or K atm-1
⚠️ Important Exceptions & Anomalies
Exception All real gases cool upon adiabatic expansion at room temperature.

Observation: Hydrogen (H2) and Helium (He) heat up upon expansion at room temperature. — Their inversion temperatures are very low (approx. 204 K for H2 and 43 K for He), so at 298 K they are above their inversion temperatures.

Exception Ideal gases show temperature changes during adiabatic free expansion.

Observation: Ideal gases show zero temperature change (ΔT = 0) in Joule–Thomson expansion. — Internal energy of an ideal gas depends solely on temperature; lack of intermolecular forces results in (∂U / ∂V)T = 0.

🎯 Key Exam Points
  • The Joule–Thomson process is strictly an isenthalpic process.
  • The Joule–Thomson coefficient for an ideal gas is identically zero.
  • The inversion temperature is related to Van der Waals constants by Ti = 2a / (Rb).
  • Hydrogen and helium show a heating effect at ordinary room temperatures because their inversion temperatures are below room temperature.
  • At the inversion temperature, the Joule–Thomson coefficient μJT equals zero.
  • Linde's method for liquefaction of gases relies directly on the Joule–Thomson cooling effect.
🚫 Common Errors & Confusions
  • Confusing Joule expansion (adiabatic free expansion into vacuum, ΔU = 0) with Joule–Thomson expansion (isenthalpic throttling, ΔH = 0).
  • Assuming ideal gases show Joule–Thomson cooling; ideal gases have μJT = 0.
  • Forgetting that Hydrogen and Helium heat up at room temperature instead of cooling.
  • Incorrectly assigning units of the Joule–Thomson coefficient as Kelvin alone instead of K bar-1.

⚡ Quick Revision: Joule–Thomson Effect

Core formulas, rapid recall points, and distinguishing criteria at a glance.

Principle of Throttling

High-pressure gas is forced through a porous plug or fine valve into a region of lower pressure under adiabatic conditions, maintaining constant enthalpy.

Joule-Thomson Coefficient

Mathematically expressed as μJT = (∂T / ∂P)H, determining whether a gas cools, heats, or remains unchanged upon expansion.

Inversion Temperature

The characteristic temperature at which μJT changes sign; above Ti the gas heats upon expansion, below Ti it cools.

Liquefaction of Gases

Gases must be cooled below their inversion temperatures before they can be liquefied using the Joule–Thomson throttling process.

📚 Terms & Units Glossary

Joule-Thomson Coefficient (μJT)
Rate of change of temperature with pressure at constant enthalpy, K bar-1.
Inversion Temperature (Ti)
Temperature at which μJT = 0, Kelvin.
Isenthalpic Process (H = constant)
Thermodynamic process where enthalpy remains unchanged, Joules.
Van der Waals Constant 'a' (a)
Measure of intermolecular attractive forces, Pa m6 mol-2.
Van der Waals Constant 'b' (b)
Measure of molecular exclusion volume, m3 mol-1.
Throttling (Adiabatic expansion)
Expansion of fluid through a restricted passage without heat exchange.

🎯 Key Takeaways

  • μJT = (∂T / ∂P)H
  • For ideal gas: μJT = 0
  • Ti = 2a / (Rb)
  • H2 and He heat up at 298 K
  • Process is isenthalpic (ΔH = 0)

📌 Quick Recall

  • Real gases show temperature change on throttling due to intermolecular forces.
  • Cooling occurs when attractive forces dominate (μJT > 0).
  • Heating occurs when repulsive forces dominate (μJT < 0).
  • Inversion temperature separates cooling and heating zones.

⚖️ Key Differences

Joule Expansion VS Joule-Thomson Expansion

Joule expansion is free expansion into a vacuum (ΔU = 0), whereas Joule-Thomson expansion is throttling against a constant pressure (ΔH = 0).

Question 1 of 18

What is the Joule–Thomson coefficient for an ideal gas?

Explanation: An ideal gas has no intermolecular forces, hence its enthalpy depends only on temperature and its Joule-Thomson coefficient is zero.

Question 2 of 18

Which of the following gases exhibits a heating effect during Joule–Thomson expansion at room temperature (298 K)?

Explanation: Hydrogen has an inversion temperature around 204 K, so at 298 K it is above its inversion temperature and heats up upon expansion.

Question 3 of 18

The Joule–Thomson expansion is characterized by which thermodynamic condition?

Explanation: Joule-Thomson expansion is an adiabatic expansion through a porous plug, which is an isenthalpic process (ΔH = 0).

Question 4 of 18

What is the relation for the inversion temperature (Ti) in terms of Van der Waals constants?

Explanation: Setting the Van der Waals Joule-Thomson coefficient equation to zero yields Ti = 2a / (Rb).

Question 5 of 18

When does a real gas exhibit cooling upon Joule–Thomson expansion?

Explanation: Cooling occurs below the inversion temperature where μJT > 0.

Question 6 of 18

What are the units of the Joule–Thomson coefficient (μJT)?

Explanation: μJT = (∂T / ∂P)H, which has units of temperature divided by pressure, i.e., K bar-1 or K atm-1.

Question 7 of 18

Which gas requires pre-cooling with liquid nitrogen before liquefaction by the Linde method?

Explanation: Helium has a very low inversion temperature (approx. 43 K), so it must be pre-cooled below this temperature to achieve cooling upon expansion.

Question 8 of 18

In the Van der Waals equation-based Joule–Thomson coefficient, what role does constant 'b' play?

Explanation: The term '-b' in the expression μJT = (1/CP)[(2a/RT) - b] contributes to the heating effect due to molecular repulsions.

Question 9 of 18

At the inversion temperature, the Joule–Thomson coefficient is:

Explanation: By definition, the inversion temperature is the temperature at which the Joule-Thomson coefficient changes sign and is equal to zero.

Question 10 of 18

Which of the following statements is correct for Joule expansion into a vacuum?

Explanation: In Joule free expansion into a vacuum, q = 0 and w = 0, so by the first law, internal energy change ΔU = 0.

Question 11 of 18

What happens to the temperature of a gas if it expands above its inversion temperature?

Explanation: Above the inversion temperature, μJT is negative, meaning expansion causes an increase in temperature (heating).

Question 12 of 18

For a real gas obeying Van der Waals equation, the condition for cooling is:

Explanation: Cooling requires μJT > 0, which implies (2a/RT) - b > 0, or 2a/RT > b.

Question 13 of 18

The Joule–Thomson effect is primarily used in which industrial process?

Explanation: Linde's method utilizes continuous Joule-Thomson expansion to liquefy air and other gases.

Question 14 of 18

Which parameter is held constant during a Joule–Thomson experiment?

Explanation: Joule-Thomson expansion is an isenthalpic process, meaning enthalpy (H) is constant.

Question 15 of 18

If a gas expands adiabatically against a vacuum, the process is known as:

Explanation: Expansion into a vacuum (external pressure = 0) is Joule expansion.

Question 16 of 18

In terms of intermolecular forces, cooling during Joule-Thomson expansion is attributed to:

Explanation: Attractive forces cause molecules to pull apart during expansion, increasing potential energy and decreasing kinetic energy (temperature).

Question 17 of 18

What is the maximum inversion temperature in terms of Boyle temperature (TB)?

Explanation: Boyle temperature TB = a / (Rb), while maximum inversion temperature Ti = 2a / (Rb), so Ti = 2 TB.

Question 18 of 18

Which of the following describes the sign of μJT when a gas heats up upon expansion?

Explanation: When a gas heats up (ΔT > 0) upon pressure drop (ΔP < 0), μJT = ΔT / ΔP is negative.

Which condition favors maximum cooling in a Joule–Thomson expansion?

Solution: Operating well below the inversion temperature ensures a large positive Joule-Thomson coefficient and significant cooling.

Question 3 of 10
If the inversion temperature of a gas is 500 K, what will happen to the gas if it expands at 400 K?

Solution: Since 400 K is below the inversion temperature of 500 K, the gas will cool down upon expansion.

Question 4 of 10
What is the primary reason why hydrogen and helium warm up during throttling at room temperature?

Solution: Room temperature is higher than their inversion temperatures, placing them in the heating zone (μJT < 0).

Question 5 of 10
Which of the following thermodynamic relations is correct for μJT?

Solution: Applying thermodynamic Maxwell relations to enthalpy gives μJT = (1/CP)[T(∂V / ∂T)P - V], which matches the negative sign formulation when rearranged.

Question 6 of 10
In Joule expansion into a vacuum, the temperature of an ideal gas:

Solution: For an ideal gas, internal energy depends only on temperature; since ΔU = 0 in free expansion, temperature remains constant.

Question 7 of 10
What is the relation between Boyle temperature (TB) and inversion temperature (Ti) for a Van der Waals gas?

Solution: TB = a / (Rb) and Ti = 2a / (Rb), hence Ti = 2 TB.

Question 8 of 10
Which intermolecular force is responsible for the cooling effect in non-ideal gases at low pressures?

Solution: Attractive Van der Waals forces cause potential energy to increase during expansion at the expense of kinetic energy, leading to cooling.

Why can hydrogen not be liquefied by simple Linde's process at room temperature?

Solution: Hydrogen heats up upon throttling at room temperature because 298 K is above its inversion temperature of 204 K.

Question 10 of 10
What is the physical significance of (∂V / ∂T)P in the Joule–Thomson coefficient expression?

Solution: The term (∂V / ∂T)P represents the isobaric coefficient of thermal expansion of the fluid.
