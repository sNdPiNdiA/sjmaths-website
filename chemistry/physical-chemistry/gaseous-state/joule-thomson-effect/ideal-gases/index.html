Joule-Thomson Effect in Ideal Gases

The Joule-Thomson effect describes the temperature change of a gas when it undergoes throttling expansion through a porous plug from high pressure to low pressure. For a strictly ideal gas, internal energy is solely a function of temperature, leading to a zero temperature change during this isenthalpic process.

UP PGT Chemistry Subject Code 02 B.Sc. / M.Sc. Level

Thermodynamics of Isenthalpic Expansion

  • A throttling process is conducted adiabatically under constant enthalpy conditions, meaning H1 = H2.
  • The expansion occurs across a porous plug or a partially opened valve without any exchange of heat with the surroundings (q = 0) and zero external work done by the system (w = 0).
  • The temperature variation with respect to pressure at constant enthalpy defines the Joule-Thomson coefficient (μJT).

Behavior of Ideal Gases

  • For an ideal gas, internal energy U and enthalpy H depend exclusively on temperature and are independent of pressure or volume.
  • Applying the thermodynamic relation, (∂H/∂P)T = 0 for ideal gases because intermolecular forces are entirely absent (a = 0, b = 0).
  • Consequently, the Joule-Thomson coefficient μJT = 0 for any ideal gas, resulting in no temperature change (ΔT = 0) upon throttling expansion.

Comparison with Real Gases

  • Real gases exhibit nonzero μJT values due to intermolecular attractive forces and finite molecular volumes described by van der Waals parameters a and b.
  • Below the inversion temperature (Ti), attractive forces dominate, giving μJT > 0 and causing the gas to cool upon expansion.
  • Above Ti, repulsive forces dominate, yielding μJT < 0 and causing the gas to heat up upon expansion.

⚠️ Important Exceptions & Anomalies
Exception All gases cool upon throttling expansion at room temperature.

Observation: Hydrogen (H2) and Helium (He) show a temperature increase. — Their inversion temperatures are very low (approx. 204 K for H2 and 43 K for He), so at room temperature they are above their respective inversion temperatures where μJT is negative.

Exception Enthalpy changes during phase transitions and pressure variations.

Observation: Enthalpy remains strictly constant in a Joule-Thomson expansion. — The system is thermally insulated and performs no external mechanical work, making the process strictly isenthalpic.

🎯 Key Exam Points
  • For an ideal gas, μJT = 0 under all conditions of temperature and pressure.
  • The Joule-Thomson process is strictly isenthalpic (ΔH = 0).
  • Hydrogen and Helium heat up during Joule-Thomson expansion at ordinary room temperatures.
  • The inversion temperature is defined as the temperature at which μJT changes sign.
  • At the inversion temperature, the Joule-Thomson coefficient is exactly zero for real gases.
  • van der Waals constant 'a' accounts for attractive forces, while 'b' accounts for excluded volume.
🚫 Common Errors & Confusions
  • Assuming ideal gases cool down during throttling; remember ΔT = 0 for ideal gases.
  • Confusing Joule-Thomson expansion (isenthalpic, ΔH = 0) with adiabatic reversible expansion (isentropic, ΔS = 0).
  • Forgetting that hydrogen and helium have negative Joule-Thomson coefficients at room temperature.
  • Mistaking the inversion temperature for Boyle temperature; Ti = 2a/Rb while TB = a/Rb.

⚡ Quick Revision: Joule-Thomson Effect in Ideal Gases

Core formulas, rapid recall points, and distinguishing criteria at a glance.

Definition of Throttling

Throttling is the steady, adiabatic flow of a fluid through a constricted passage or porous plug resulting in a pressure drop without external work extraction.

Isenthalpic Nature

The application of the first law of thermodynamics to a throttling device establishes that the enthalpy of the system before and after expansion remains identical.

Absence of Effect in Ideality

Because ideal gas molecules lack mutual interactions, changing pressure at constant temperature causes no alteration in potential energy or enthalpy.

Inversion Phenomenon

Real gases transition from cooling to heating behavior when crossing the characteristic inversion temperature corresponding to balanced attractive and repulsive forces.

📚 Terms & Units Glossary

Joule-Thomson Coefficient (μJT = (∂T/∂P)H)
Rate of change of temperature with pressure at constant enthalpy. Unit: K atm-1.
Isenthalpic Process (ΔH = 0)
A thermodynamic process in which the enthalpy of the system remains constant.
Inversion Temperature (Ti = 2a/Rb)
Temperature at which Joule-Thomson coefficient is zero. Unit: K.
Throttling Process (Adiabatic constriction)
Expansion of gas from high to low pressure through a porous plug.
van der Waals 'a' (Attractive constant)
Parameter measuring intermolecular attractive forces. Unit: atm L2 mol-2.
van der Waals 'b' (Excluded volume)
Parameter measuring effective molecular volume. Unit: L mol-1.

🎯 Key Takeaways

  • μJT = 0 for ideal gases.
  • Throttling is an isenthalpic process.
  • Ti = 2a/Rb for van der Waals gases.
  • H2 and He show heating at room temperature.
  • Cooling corresponds to μJT > 0.

📌 Quick Recall

  • Ideal gases do not show the Joule-Thomson effect.
  • Enthalpy remains constant during throttling expansion.
  • Real gases cool below Ti and heat above Ti.
  • Molecular interactions govern real gas thermal behavior.

⚖️ Key Differences

Inversion Temperature (Ti) VS Boyle Temperature (TB)

Ti = 2a/Rb (where μJT = 0), whereas TB = a/Rb (where real gas obeys ideal gas law over a range of pressure).

What is the physical significance of the Joule-Thomson coefficient being zero for an ideal gas?

Solution: Zero μJT for an ideal gas directly reflects the absence of intermolecular forces (a = 0, b = 0).

Question 2 of 10
During a Joule-Thomson expansion of a real gas below its inversion temperature, the internal energy:

Solution: Since enthalpy H = U + PV is constant and pressure decreases (PV changes), internal energy U must increase to keep H constant during cooling.

Question 3 of 10
If a gas expands adiabatically against a vacuum (Free Expansion), what is the temperature change for an ideal gas?

Solution: In free expansion into a vacuum, q = 0 and w = 0, so ΔU = 0. For an ideal gas, U depends only on T, hence ΔT = 0.

Question 4 of 10
The inversion temperature Ti and Boyle temperature TB for a van der Waals gas are related as:

Solution: Ti = 2a/Rb and TB = a/Rb, therefore Ti = 2 TB.

Question 5 of 10
Which condition is essential for observing the Joule-Thomson effect?

Solution: The Joule-Thomson effect is measured by maintaining a steady pressure drop across a porous plug under adiabatic conditions.

Question 6 of 10
For a real gas, the Joule-Thomson expansion is:

Solution: By definition and experimental setup, the enthalpy remains constant throughout the throttling process.

Question 7 of 10
What happens to the kinetic energy of ideal gas molecules during throttling expansion?

Solution: Since temperature does not change for an ideal gas in throttling, the average kinetic energy of the molecules remains constant.

Question 8 of 10
Which of the following gases has the highest inversion temperature, making it easiest to liquefy via throttling?

Solution: Carbon dioxide has stronger intermolecular forces (larger 'a' value), giving it a high inversion temperature well above room temperature.

Question 9 of 10
In the equation μJT = (2a/RT - b)/CP, cooling occurs when:

Solution: Cooling corresponds to μJT > 0, which requires the term 2a/RT to be greater than b.

Question 10 of 10

Solution: Helium's inversion temperature is ~43 K; at room temperature it heats up upon expansion and must be precooled below 43 K to achieve cooling.
