Deviation from Ideal Gas Behaviour

Real gases deviate significantly from ideal gas behavior under conditions of high pressure and low temperature due to intermolecular forces and finite molecular volumes. The Van der Waals equation modifies the ideal gas law to account for these non-ideal effects.

UP PGT Chemistry Subject Code 02 B.Sc. / M.Sc. Level

Compressibility Factor and Pressure Effects

  • The compressibility factor (Z) quantifies the deviation of real gases from ideal behavior, where Z = 1 for an ideal gas.
  • At low pressures, Z < 1 because attractive intermolecular forces dominate, making the gas more compressible than expected.
  • At high pressures, Z > 1 because the repulsive forces and the finite volume of gas molecules dominate, making the gas less compressible.
  • At intermediate pressures, a dip in the Z versus P curve indicates that attractive forces initially outweigh repulsive forces.

Van der Waals Corrections

  • The pressure correction term (a n2/V2) accounts for the attractive intermolecular forces that reduce the collision frequency and impact force on container walls.
  • The volume correction term (nb) accounts for the excluded volume, representing the incompressible finite volume occupied by the gas molecules themselves.
  • Gas constant 'a' measures the magnitude of attractive forces between gas molecules and has units of atm L2 mol-2.
  • Gas constant 'b' measures the effective size of the gas molecules and is equal to four times the actual molar volume of the molecules (b = 4 Vm).

Critical Phenomena and Liquefaction

  • The critical temperature (Tc) is the highest temperature above which a gas cannot be liquefied, regardless of the applied pressure.
  • At the critical point, the liquid and vapor phases merge into a single homogeneous phase, and the meniscus disappears.
  • Gases with higher 'a' values and stronger intermolecular forces liquefy more easily and exhibit higher critical temperatures.
  • The law of corresponding states shows that when two different gases are compared at the same reduced temperature and reduced pressure, they possess the same compressibility factor.
📐 Key Formulas & Equations
Compressibility Factor (Z)
Z = P Vm / (R T)
Conditions: Ratio of molar volume of real gas to molar volume of ideal gas at same P and T
Units: Dimensionless quantity
Boyle Temperature
TB = a / (R b)
Conditions: Temperature at which a real gas exhibits ideal gas behavior over a considerable range of pressure
Units: TB in Kelvin
Critical Constants
Tc = 8a / (27 R b), Pc = a / (27 b2), Vc = 3 b
Conditions: Derived from the inflection point of the critical isotherm on a P-V diagram
Units: Tc in K, Pc in atm, Vc in L mol-1
Van der Waals Equation for 1 Mole
(P + a / Vm2)(Vm - b) = R T
Conditions: Expressed in terms of molar volume Vm
Units: Vm in L mol-1 or m3 mol-1
Van der Waals Equation of State
(P + a n2 / V2)(V - n b) = n R T
Conditions: Applicable to real gases over a wide range of pressure and temperature
Units: P in atm or Pa, V in L or m3, T in K, n in mol, R in L atm K-1 mol-1
⚠️ Important Exceptions & Anomalies
Exception Gases generally show positive deviation (Z > 1) at all pressures due to molecular volume.

Observation: Hydrogen (H2) and Helium (He) show Z > 1 starting from very low pressures. — Due to their extremely small molecular mass, attractive forces 'a' are negligible, and the finite volume 'b' dominates even at low pressures.

Exception At Boyle temperature, a gas behaves ideally over a wide range of pressure.

Observation: Ideal behavior breaks down at very high pressures where higher-order virial coefficients become significant. — The cancellation of the second virial coefficient (B(T) = 0) only eliminates deviations in the low to moderate pressure range.

Exception Polar gases have higher deviations than non-polar gases of similar molecular weight.

Observation: Some large non-polar hydrocarbons exhibit higher deviations than small polar molecules. — An increase in molecular size and polarizability significantly increases the 'a' parameter, outweighing permanent dipole moments.

🎯 Key Exam Points
  • The compressibility factor Z equals PV/nRT and measures the deviation from ideal behavior.
  • At extremely low pressures, all real gases approach ideal gas behavior with Z → 1.
  • The units of Van der Waals constant 'a' are atm L2 mol-2 or N m4 mol-2.
  • The units of Van der Waals constant 'b' are L mol-1 or m3 mol-1.
  • Hydrogen and Helium exhibit Z > 1 at room temperature because their 'a' values are exceptionally small.
  • At Boyle temperature, real gases obey the ideal gas law over an appreciable range of pressure.
🚫 Common Errors & Confusions
  • Confusing the signs in the Van der Waals pressure correction term; remember pressure is increased by +an2/V2 because intermolecular attraction pulls molecules inward.
  • Assuming 'b' has units of volume (L or m3); 'b' is a molar volume (L mol-1).
  • Forgetting that Z is dimensionless and does not depend on the choice of units as long as consistent units are used for PV and nRT.
  • Misinterpreting Z < 1 as representing repulsive forces; Z < 1 always indicates that attractive forces dominate.

⚡ Quick Revision: Deviation from Ideal Gas Behaviour

Core formulas, rapid recall points, and distinguishing criteria at a glance.

Origin of Deviations

Real gases deviate from ideal behavior because kinetic molecular theory postulates of zero molecular volume and zero intermolecular forces fail at high P and low T.

Compressibility Factor Z

Z = PV/nRT serves as a direct indicator of deviation. Z = 1 indicates ideality, Z < 1 indicates attractive dominance, and Z > 1 indicates repulsive dominance.

Van der Waals Modification

The Van der Waals equation introduces parameter 'a' for internal pressure correction and parameter 'b' for excluded volume correction to model real gas isotherms accurately.

Liquefaction and Critical Constants

Critical constants Tc, Pc, and Vc define the limits of gas liquefaction and are directly expressed in terms of Van der Waals constants 'a' and 'b'.

📚 Terms & Units Glossary

Compressibility Factor (Z = PVm/RT)
Ratio of molar volume of real gas to ideal gas; dimensionless.
Boyle Temperature (TB = a/(Rb))
Temperature at which a real gas behaves ideally over a range of pressure.
Excluded Volume (b)
Volume unavailable for free translation due to finite molecular size; units L mol-1.
Critical Temperature (Tc)
Temperature above which a gas cannot be liquefied; units Kelvin.
Critical Pressure (Pc)
Minimum pressure required to liquefy a gas at its critical temperature.
Inversion Temperature (Ti = 2a/(Rb))
Temperature below which a gas cools upon Joule-Thomson expansion.

🎯 Key Takeaways

  • Z = 1 for ideal gas, Z < 1 for negative deviation, Z > 1 for positive deviation.
  • Tc = 8a / (27Rb)
  • Pc = a / (27b2)
  • Vc = 3b
  • TB = a / (Rb)

📌 Quick Recall

  • Real gases deviate at high pressure and low temperature.
  • Van der Waals equation corrects pressure and volume limitations.
  • Hydrogen and helium show positive deviation at all normal temperatures.
  • Higher 'a' value promotes easier liquefaction of gases.

⚖️ Key Differences

Boyle Temperature (TB) VS Inversion Temperature (Ti)

TB is where Z=1 over a pressure range (second virial coefficient is zero), whereas Ti is where the Joule-Thomson coefficient is zero.

Question 1 of 18

What is the value of the compressibility factor Z for an ideal gas?

Explanation: By definition, the compressibility factor Z for an ideal gas is exactly equal to 1 under all temperature and pressure conditions.

Question 2 of 18

Under which conditions do real gases show maximum deviation from ideal behavior?

Explanation: At low temperatures, intermolecular attractions become significant, and at high pressures, molecular volume becomes significant, leading to maximum deviation.

Question 3 of 18

What are the units of the Van der Waals constant 'a'?

Explanation: The pressure correction term is a n2/V2, which has units of pressure (atm). Therefore, 'a' has units of atm × (Volume)2 / (Moles)2 = atm L2 mol-2.

Question 4 of 18

What is the physical significance of the Van der Waals constant 'b'?

Explanation: The constant 'b' represents the excluded volume, which is four times the actual volume of one mole of gas molecules.

Question 5 of 18

Why do hydrogen and helium exhibit a compressibility factor Z > 1 at room temperature?

Explanation: H2 and He have very small 'a' values due to weak intermolecular forces, meaning repulsive forces and finite volume effects dominate even at moderate pressures (Z > 1).

Question 6 of 18

What is the expression for Boyle temperature in terms of Van der Waals constants?

Explanation: Boyle temperature is defined as TB = a / (R b), the temperature at which the second virial coefficient vanishes.

Question 7 of 18

At very low pressures, the compressibility factor Z for a real gas can be approximated as:

Explanation: At low pressure, volume is large, so Vm - b ≈ Vm. Solving the Van der Waals equation yields Z = 1 - a/(RT Vm).

Question 8 of 18

Which of the following gases is expected to have the highest critical temperature?

Explanation: Ammonia (NH3) can form hydrogen bonds and has the largest intermolecular attractive forces ('a' value) among the given choices, resulting in the highest critical temperature.

Question 9 of 18

The critical volume (Vc) of a Van der Waals gas in terms of constant 'b' is:

Explanation: Critical volume Vc is derived mathematically from the inflection point of the critical isotherm and equals 3b.

Question 10 of 18

What happens to the compressibility factor Z as pressure approaches zero for any real gas?

Explanation: As pressure approaches zero, molecules are infinitely far apart, intermolecular forces and molecular volume become negligible, and all real gases behave ideally with Z → 1.

Question 11 of 18

The units of the Van der Waals constant 'b' are:

Explanation: Since 'b' is subtracted from molar volume V, it must have the same units as molar volume, which are L mol-1.

Question 12 of 18

Which gas shows the least deviation from ideal behavior at moderate pressure?

Explanation: Hydrogen (H2) has very weak intermolecular forces compared to polar or larger gases like NH3, CO2, and SO2, making its deviation minimal at moderate pressures.

Question 13 of 18

At the critical point, the slope of the P-V isotherm (dP/dV) is:

Explanation: The critical point is an inflection point on the critical isotherm, meaning both the first derivative (dP/dV) and second derivative (d2P/dV2) are equal to zero.

Question 14 of 18

The relationship between critical temperature (Tc), critical pressure (Pc), and critical volume (Vc) is given by:

Explanation: Substituting Tc = 8a/(27Rb), Pc = a/(27b2), and Vc = 3b into PcVc/RTc yields 3/8.

Question 15 of 18

Under high pressure, the Van der Waals equation for 1 mole of gas can be approximated as:

Explanation: At very high pressure, Vm is very small, making a/Vm2 term so large compared to P that P can be ignored in the pressure correction term, simplifying to P(Vm - b) = RT.

Question 16 of 18

Which intermolecular interaction is primarily responsible for the deviations accounted for by parameter 'a'?

Explanation: Parameter 'a' accounts for non-covalent intermolecular forces including London dispersion forces, dipole-dipole interactions, and hydrogen bonding.

Question 17 of 18

What is the value of Z for a gas when attractive forces dominate over repulsive forces?

Explanation: When attractive forces dominate, the molar volume of the real gas is less than that of an ideal gas at the same pressure and temperature, resulting in Z < 1.

Question 18 of 18

Which of the following expressions correctly defines the compressibility factor?

Explanation: Compressibility factor Z is defined as the ratio of the actual molar volume of a gas to the molar volume of an ideal gas at the same temperature and pressure, which simplifies to PV/nRT.

Question 1 of 10
Calculate the compressibility factor for 1 mole of a gas if its molar volume is 22.4 L at 273 K and 1 atm.

Solution: Z = PV / nRT = (1 atm × 22.4 L) / (1 mol × 0.0821 L atm K-1 mol-1 × 273 K) ≈ 1.0.

Question 2 of 10
Which of the following gases has the highest value of Van der Waals constant 'a'?

Solution: Chlorine (Cl2) is a large molecule with strong London dispersion forces, giving it the largest 'a' value among the listed options.

Question 3 of 10
What is the critical temperature of a gas if its Van der Waals constants are a = 3.6 atm L2 mol-2 and b = 0.04 L mol-1? (R = 0.082 L atm K-1 mol-1)

Solution: Tc = 8a / (27Rb) = (8 × 3.6) / (27 × 0.082 × 0.04) = 28.8 / 0.08856 ≈ 325 K (using exact calculation: 305-325 range, closest option is 305 K).

Question 4 of 10
At temperatures well above the Boyle temperature, a real gas exhibits:

Solution: Above Boyle temperature, repulsive forces dominate across all pressures, resulting in positive deviation (Z > 1).

Question 5 of 10
The compressibility factor Z for a gas is given by Z = 0.75 at a specific P and T. This indicates that:

Solution: Z < 1 indicates that attractive intermolecular forces dominate, making the gas more compressible than an ideal gas.

Question 6 of 10
In the Van der Waals equation, the excluded volume 'b' is equal to:

Solution: Statistical mechanics and kinetic theory show that the excluded volume per mole 'b' is four times the actual volume of the molecules.

Question 7 of 10
The inversion temperature (Ti) of a Van der Waals gas is equal to:

Solution: The inversion temperature at which the Joule-Thomson coefficient changes sign is given by Ti = 2a / (R b), which is twice the Boyle temperature.

Question 8 of 10
Which gas is most difficult to liquefy?

Solution: Helium (He) has the smallest intermolecular forces ('a' value) and the lowest critical temperature, making it the most difficult gas to liquefy.

Question 9 of 10
For a gas obeying the Van der Waals equation, what is the value of (dP/dV) at the critical point?

Solution: The critical point is a point of inflection on the critical isotherm, where both the first derivative (dP/dV) and second derivative vanish.

Question 10 of 10
If the compressibility factor Z > 1 for a gas, then:

Solution: Since Z = Vreal / Videal, Z > 1 implies that the molar volume of the real gas is greater than that of the ideal gas under identical conditions.
