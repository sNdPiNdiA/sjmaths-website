Inversion Temperature

Inversion temperature is the specific temperature below which a real gas undergoes cooling upon adiabatic expansion through a porous plug, known as the Joule-Thomson effect. At this exact threshold, the Joule-Thomson coefficient becomes zero, transitioning the gas from cooling behavior to heating behavior.

UP PGT Chemistry Subject Code 02 B.Sc. / M.Sc. Level

Thermodynamic Basis of Joule-Thomson Effect

  • The Joule-Thomson expansion is an isenthalpic process where enthalpy remains constant during pressure drop across a valve or porous plug.
  • The sign of the Joule-Thomson coefficient (μJT) dictates whether a gas cools (μJT > 0) or heats (μJT < 0) upon expansion.
  • At inversion temperature (Ti), μJT equals zero, meaning neither cooling nor heating occurs during expansion.
  • Below Ti, attractive intermolecular forces dominate, causing potential energy to increase and kinetic energy (temperature) to decrease.

Derivation from Van der Waals Equation

  • Applying the definition of the Joule-Thomson coefficient to the Van der Waals equation yields μJT = (1/Cp)[(2a/RT) - b].
  • Setting μJT = 0 removes the bracketed term, resulting in the standard expression Ti = 2a / (Rb).
  • Because real gases exhibit two inversion temperatures (upper and lower Ti) at extreme pressures, the formula Ti = 2a/(Rb) represents the maximum inversion temperature at zero pressure.
  • The parameter a accounts for intermolecular attractive forces, while b accounts for the finite volume occupied by gas molecules.

Temperature Regimes and Gas Liquefaction

  • If a gas is expanded above its upper inversion temperature, it heats up because repulsive forces dominate over attractive forces.
  • For effective liquefaction via the Linde process, the gas must be pre-cooled below its inversion temperature before throttling.
  • Gases like Hydrogen (H2) and Helium (He) possess very low inversion temperatures (around 204 K and 43 K respectively) and thus heat up at room temperature.
  • Nitrogen (N2) and Oxygen (O2) have inversion temperatures well above room temperature, facilitating standard cryogenic cooling.

⚠️ Important Exceptions & Anomalies
Exception Most gases cool down upon adiabatic expansion from high to low pressure at room temperature.

Observation: Hydrogen and Helium heat up upon adiabatic expansion at room temperature. — Their inversion temperatures are exceptionally low (H2 ~ 204 K, He ~ 43 K); at 298 K they operate above Ti where μJT is negative.

Exception Real gases possess a single constant inversion temperature at all pressures.

Observation: Real gases exhibit two distinct inversion temperatures (upper and lower Ti) at high pressures. — At high pressures, non-ideal volumetric behavior and repulsive forces cause the μJT curve to intersect the zero axis twice.

Exception Ideal gases undergo temperature change during Joule-Thomson expansion.

Observation: Ideal gases show zero temperature change (μJT = 0) at all temperatures. — An ideal gas has zero intermolecular forces (a = 0) and zero molecular volume (b = 0), making enthalpy dependent only on temperature.

💡 Shortcuts & Memory Aids
The 2-Factor Rule for Characteristic Temperatures
TB = a / (Rb) and Ti = 2a / (Rb)

Application: Remember that the inversion temperature is exactly double the Boyle temperature for any Van der Waals gas.

Critical Temperature Multiplier
Ti = (27/8) Tc = 3.375 Tc

Application: Use this direct ratio to calculate the inversion temperature when only the critical temperature is provided in the problem.

Sign of Joule-Thomson Coefficient Mnemonic
T < Ti → Cooling (+μ), T > Ti → Heating (-μ)

Application: Associate 'Low is Cool' to quickly determine that a gas cools when kept below its inversion temperature.

🎯 Key Exam Points
  • The Joule-Thomson process is strictly an isenthalpic process where ΔH = 0.
  • The maximum inversion temperature for a Van der Waals gas is given by Ti = 2a / (Rb).
  • For Hydrogen and Helium, the inversion temperature is lower than room temperature, hence they show heating upon expansion at 298 K.
  • The Joule-Thomson coefficient μJT has units of K atm-1.
  • An ideal gas has a Joule-Thomson coefficient equal to zero under all conditions.
  • The relation between inversion temperature (Ti) and critical temperature (Tc) is Ti = 3.375 Tc.
🚫 Common Errors & Confusions
  • Confusing inversion temperature with Boyle temperature; remember Ti = 2TB.
  • Assuming all gases cool down during throttling; hydrogen and helium heat up at room temperature because 298 K exceeds their inversion temperatures.
  • Treating Joule-Thomson expansion as adiabatic and reversible; it is adiabatic but highly irreversible due to sudden pressure drop across a porous plug.
  • Forgetting that inversion temperature varies with pressure, and the standard formula Ti = 2a/(Rb) applies specifically at zero pressure limit.

⚡ Quick Revision: Inversion Temperature

Core formulas, rapid recall points, and distinguishing criteria at a glance.

Joule-Thomson Effect Fundamentals

The Joule-Thomson effect describes the temperature change of a gas when forced through a porous plug or throttling valve from high to low pressure under constant enthalpy conditions. The magnitude and direction of the temperature change are quantified by the Joule-Thomson coefficient μJT = (∂T/∂P)H.

Definition of Inversion Temperature

Inversion temperature (Ti) is the temperature threshold where the Joule-Thomson coefficient changes sign. Below Ti, μJT > 0 (cooling), while above Ti, μJT < 0 (heating). At Ti, expansion causes no temperature change.

Van der Waals Derivation

By applying the first and second laws of thermodynamics to the Van der Waals equation of state, the inversion temperature is derived as Ti = 2a/(Rb). This relates Ti directly to the molecular parameters 'a' (attractive forces) and 'b' (excluded volume).

Cryogenic Applications and Anomalies

Gases must be pre-cooled below their inversion temperatures for liquefaction via the Linde method. Gases like H2 and He require initial cooling with liquid nitrogen because their inversion temperatures are well below room temperature.

🎯 Key Takeaways

  • Ti = 2 TB
  • Ti = 3.375 Tc
  • μJT > 0 for cooling, μJT < 0 for heating
  • Hydrogen and helium heat up during adiabatic expansion at room temperature
  • Ideal gases exhibit μJT = 0 at all temperatures

📌 Quick Recall

  • Inversion temperature marks the boundary between cooling and heating during isenthalpic expansion.
  • Calculated from Van der Waals constants as Ti = 2a/(Rb).
  • Gases with low inversion temperatures like helium require pre-cooling for liquefaction.
  • At inversion temperature, attractive and repulsive intermolecular forces exactly balance each other.

⚖️ Key Differences

Inversion Temperature VS Boyle Temperature

Inversion temperature is where μJT = 0 (Ti = 2a/Rb), whereas Boyle temperature is where compressibility factor Z = 1 over a pressure range (TB = a/Rb).

How is inversion temperature related to the critical temperature (Tc) for a Van der Waals gas?

What are the units of the Joule-Thomson coefficient (μJT)?

Explanation: μJT is defined as (∂T / ∂P)H, giving units of temperature over pressure, such as K atm-1 or K bar-1.

Question 10 of 18

Why does helium heat up during throttling at room temperature?

Explanation: Helium's inversion temperature is approximately 43 K. At room temperature (298 K), it is well above Ti, so it heats up upon expansion.

Question 11 of 18

Which Van der Waals constant primarily determines the magnitude of the inversion temperature?

Explanation: Ti = 2a / (Rb), so both 'a' (attractive forces) and 'b' (molecular size) determine the inversion temperature.

Question 12 of 18

What process utilizes the Joule-Thomson cooling effect for liquefying gases?

Explanation: The Linde process relies on repeated Joule-Thomson expansion to achieve gas liquefaction.

Question 13 of 18

If a gas expands above its inversion temperature, the sign of μJT is:

Explanation: Above Ti, the gas heats up upon expansion, meaning ΔT is positive while ΔP is negative, resulting in a negative μJT.

Question 14 of 18

Which gas has the lowest inversion temperature among common atmospheric gases?

Explanation: Helium has an extremely low inversion temperature of roughly 43 K due to very weak van der Waals interactions.

Question 15 of 18

In the derivation of μJT = (1/Cp)[(2a/RT) - b], what does Cp represent?

Explanation: Cp represents the molar heat capacity at constant pressure.

Question 16 of 18

At pressures approaching zero, a real gas has how many inversion temperatures?

Explanation: At the zero pressure limit, upper and lower inversion temperatures merge into a single maximum inversion temperature given by Ti = 2a/(Rb).

Question 17 of 18

What happens to the kinetic energy of gas molecules when a gas cools during Joule-Thomson expansion?

Explanation: Cooling corresponds to a drop in temperature, which directly reflects a decrease in the average translational kinetic energy of the gas molecules.

Question 18 of 18

Which intermolecular interaction dominates when a gas cools during Joule-Thomson expansion below Ti?

Explanation: Below Ti, attractive forces dominate; as molecules move apart during expansion, potential energy increases at the expense of kinetic energy, causing cooling.

Question 1 of 10
The Joule-Thomson coefficient is mathematically defined as:

Solution: μJT is defined as the partial derivative of temperature with respect to pressure at constant enthalpy: (∂T / ∂P)H.

Question 2 of 10
For a real gas obeying the Van der Waals equation, if a = 4.0 atm L2 mol-2, b = 0.04 L mol-1, and R = 0.082 L atm K-1 mol-1, what is the approximate inversion temperature?

Solution: Ti = 2a / (Rb) = (2 4.0) / (0.082 0.04) = 8 / 0.00328 = 2439 K.

Question 3 of 10
At temperatures well above the inversion temperature, the Joule-Thomson coefficient is:

Solution: Above Ti, repulsive forces dominate, leading to heating upon expansion, which corresponds to a negative Joule-Thomson coefficient.

Question 4 of 10
Which gas among the following has the highest inversion temperature?

Solution: Carbon dioxide has large Van der Waals constant 'a' due to strong polarizability and intermolecular attractions, resulting in a high inversion temperature.

Question 5 of 10
What happens to the intermolecular potential energy when a gas cools during Joule-Thomson expansion?

Solution: As molecules move apart against attractive forces, potential energy increases, which is drawn from the kinetic energy of the molecules, causing cooling.

Question 6 of 10
The ratio of inversion temperature to critical temperature (Ti / Tc) for a Van der Waals gas is:

Solution: Ti / Tc = [2a/(Rb)] / [8a/(27Rb)] = 2 * (27/8) = 27/4 = 6.75 / 2 = 3.375.

Question 7 of 10
Why is it impossible to liquefy hydrogen or helium at room temperature by the Linde method directly?

Solution: Because room temperature (298 K) exceeds the inversion temperatures of H2 (204 K) and He (43 K), expansion causes heating rather than cooling.

Question 8 of 10
In an isenthalpic expansion, if the pressure decreases (ΔP < 0) and the temperature decreases (ΔT < 0), the sign of μJT is:

Solution: μJT = ΔT / ΔP. Since both ΔT and ΔP are negative, their ratio is positive.

Question 9 of 10
Which condition must be met for a gas to exhibit cooling upon throttling?

Solution: Cooling occurs only when the operating temperature is strictly below the inversion temperature (T < Ti).

Question 10 of 10
Which of the following statements about inversion temperature is incorrect?

Solution: Inversion temperature is not strictly constant at all pressures; it varies with pressure, having upper and lower limits at high pressures.
