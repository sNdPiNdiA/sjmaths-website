Potentiometric Titrations

Potentiometric titrations determine the endpoint of a chemical reaction by measuring the potential of an indicator electrode as a function of titrant volume. The equivalence point is identified by a sharp inflection in the titration curve, corresponding to the maximum value of the first derivative (ΔE/ΔV).

UP PGT Chemistry Subject Code 02 B.Sc. / M.Sc. Level

Principle and Instrumentation of Potentiometry

  • Potentiometric titrations require a working indicator electrode sensitive to the analyte ion activity and a reference electrode with a fixed potential.
  • Common reference electrodes include the saturated calomel electrode (SCE) and the silver-silver chloride electrode.
  • The measurement apparatus uses a high-impedance potentiometer or pH meter to prevent drawing current from the electrochemical cell during potential readings.

Acid-Base and Precipitation Potentiometric Titrations

  • For acid-base titrations, a glass membrane electrode acts as the indicator electrode to track changes in hydronium ion activity ([H3O+]).
  • Precipitation titrations involving halides often utilize a silver wire indicator electrode coupled with a double-junction reference electrode.
  • The equivalence point corresponds to the point of inflection on the sigmoidal titration curve of potential versus volume of titrant.

Redox and Complexometric Titrations

  • In redox titrations, an inert metal electrode such as platinum or gold serves as the indicator electrode to sense the ratio of oxidized to reduced species.
  • Complexometric titrations using EDTA can be monitored using metal ion-selective electrodes or mercury electrodes for specific divalent cations.
  • The equivalence point can be evaluated mathematically without plotting graphs using Second Derivative methods (Δ2E/ΔV2 = 0).

Units: ΔE in volts (V), ΔV in milliliters (mL)
Gran Plot Linearization
Vb 10(±E/s) vs Vb
Conditions: Used for acid-base or precipitation titrations before the equivalence point.
Units: Vb in mL, E in mV, s ≈ 59.16 mV at 25°C
Cell Potential during Redox Titration
Ecell = Eindicator - Ereference
Conditions: Reference electrode potential remains constant throughout the titration.
Units: Ecell in volts (V)
Nernst Equation for Indicator Electrode
E = E° - (RT / nF) ln Q
Conditions: Valid for electrochemical cells at constant temperature.
Units: E in volts (V), R in J K-1 mol-1, T in Kelvin (K), F in C mol-1
⚠️ Important Exceptions & Anomalies
Exception Nernst slope should strictly equal 59.16 mV per decade at 25°C for all monovalent ions.

Observation: Sub-Nernstian or super-Nernstian responses are frequently observed in aged glass electrodes. — Hydration layer degradation, chemical etching of the silicate network, or junction potential drift alter the ideal response.

Exception Indicator electrodes should not participate in chemical side reactions with the analyte solution.

Observation: Silver metal indicator electrodes dissolve in strongly oxidizing media or complexing excess cyanide solutions. — Formation of stable soluble complexes lowers free silver ion activity, shifting the measured potential unpredictably.

🎯 Key Exam Points
  • The pH glass electrode functions via boundary potential developed across hydrated gel layers.
  • Saturated calomel electrode (SCE) has a fixed potential of +0.2415 V versus SHE at 25°C.
  • Potentiometric titrations are highly advantageous for colored or turbid solutions where visual indicators fail.
  • The Gran plot linearizes the titration curve and permits equivalence point determination without reading potentials directly at the steep inflection.
  • In redox titrations involving Ce4+ and Fe2+, a platinum indicator electrode is standard.
  • The equivalence point volume corresponds to the maximum of the first derivative curve (ΔE/ΔV).
🚫 Common Errors & Confusions
  • Failing to account for liquid junction potentials can introduce systematic errors in potential measurements.
  • Using a reference electrode whose filling solution reacts chemically with the analyte solution (e.g., KCl with Ag+ analytes without a salt bridge).
  • Assuming the Nernst slope is always ideal without performing regular multi-point calibration.
  • Reading potential values before the cell has reached thermodynamic equilibrium, leading to sluggish potential drift.

⚡ Quick Revision: Potentiometric Titrations

Core formulas, rapid recall points, and distinguishing criteria at a glance.

Electrochemical Cell Setup

Potentiometric cells consist of a reference electrode of constant potential and an indicator electrode whose potential varies logarithmically with analyte activity according to the Nernst equation.

Equivalence Point Location

The inflection point of the sigmoidal E versus V curve marks the equivalence point, identified analytically by the maximum in the first derivative plot (ΔE/ΔV).

Types of Potentiometric Titrations

Classified into acid-base, precipitation, complexometric, and redox titrations based on the chemical reaction taking place and the corresponding choice of indicator electrode.

Advantages Over Classical Titrations

Provides reliable equivalence point detection in colored, turbid, or non-aqueous media where traditional colorimetric indicators are ineffective.

📚 Terms & Units Glossary

Indicator Electrode (Eind)
An electrode whose potential responds to changes in activity of a specific analyte ion.
Reference Electrode (Eref)
An electrode with a stable, known potential independent of the test solution composition.
Nernst Slope (2.303 RT/nF)
The theoretical potential change per decade of ion activity (59.16 mV for n=1 at 25°C).
Gran Plot (Linearized Titration Function)
A graphical method using transformed pre-equivalence data to yield a straight line pointing to the equivalence point.
Liquid Junction Potential (Ej)
Potential difference developed across the boundary between two electrolyte solutions with different compositions.
Inflection Point ((Δ2E/ΔV2 = 0))
The point on the titration curve where curvature changes sign, corresponding to the equivalence point.

🎯 Key Takeaways

  • SCE potential at 25°C is +0.2415 V vs SHE.
  • Nernst factor at 25°C is 0.0592 / n V.
  • Equivalence point corresponds to maximum of ΔE/ΔV.
  • Glass electrode response is governed by ion exchange at hydrated gel layers.
  • High input impedance instruments prevent polarization of the electrochemical cell.

📌 Quick Recall

  • Potentiometric titrations measure cell potential as a function of titrant volume.
  • Equivalence points are identified through sharp potential inflections or derivative maxima.
  • Electrodes are categorized into reference and indicator types.
  • Methods apply successfully to acid-base, redox, precipitation, and complexometric systems.

⚖️ Key Differences

First Derivative Maximum VS Second Derivative Zero

The maximum of ΔE/ΔV and the zero-crossing of Δ2E/ΔV2 both mathematically pinpoint the equivalence point.

In a potentiometric titration curve of potential versus volume, the equivalence point is characterized by:

What is the primary function of the salt bridge in a potentiometric cell?

Explanation: A salt bridge minimizes liquid junction potential and maintains electrical neutrality between half-cells.

Question 6 of 18

The first derivative curve for a potentiometric titration plots which of the following quantities?

Explanation: The first derivative plot graphs the change in potential per unit volume (ΔE/ΔV) against the average volume (V).

Question 7 of 18

Why must potentiometers used in potentiometric titrations have high input impedance?

Explanation: High input impedance prevents drawing current from the cell, avoiding concentration polarization and potential errors.

Question 8 of 18

Explanation: Gran plots linearize pre-equivalence data, allowing accurate graphical determination of the equivalence point without taking readings near the steep inflection.

Question 9 of 18

Which of the following describes the glass membrane of a pH electrode?

Explanation: The active surface of a glass pH electrode consists of a hydrated gel layer where ion exchange with hydrogen ions occurs.

Question 10 of 18

In a precipitation titration of chloride with silver nitrate, what indicator electrode is used?

Explanation: A silver wire responds directly to silver ion activity, making it ideal for precipitation titrations involving halides.

Question 11 of 18

The second derivative of potential with respect to volume (Δ2E/ΔV2) at the equivalence point equals:

Explanation: At the inflection point where the first derivative is at a maximum, the second derivative passes through zero.

Question 12 of 18

Which condition causes sub-Nernstian response in glass electrodes?

Explanation: Degradation or dehydration of the hydrated gel layer on aged glass electrodes results in a sub-Nernstian potential response.

Question 13 of 18

What is the standard potential of the saturated calomel electrode at 25°C relative to the standard hydrogen electrode?

Explanation: The potential of the SCE containing saturated KCl at 25°C is +0.2415 V versus SHE.

Question 14 of 18

Potentiometric titration is preferred over visual indicator titration when the analyte solution is:

Explanation: Potentiometry is independent of solution optical clarity and functions effectively in colored, turbid, or non-aqueous media.

Question 15 of 18

In complexometric titrations with EDTA, metal ion-selective electrodes respond to changes in:

Explanation: Metal ion-selective electrodes respond directly to the activity of free uncomplexed metal ions in solution.

Question 16 of 18

Which equation relates cell potential to ion activities in potentiometry?

Explanation: The Nernst equation governs the relationship between cell potential and ion activity.

Question 17 of 18

What happens to the measured potential when the analyte ion concentration changes by a factor of 10 for a divalent ion at 25°C?

Explanation: For n = 2, the potential change per decade is 59.16 / 2 = 29.58 mV.

Question 18 of 18

Which reference electrode uses mercury and mercurous sulfate in a potassium sulfate medium?

Explanation: The mercury-mercurous sulfate electrode employs Hg, Hg2SO4, and K2SO4, useful when chloride contamination must be avoided.

In potentiometric measurements, why is a high input impedance voltmeter required?

Solution: Drawing current polarizes the cell and alters the equilibrium potential; high impedance prevents this.

Question 2 of 10
Which of the following is a primary advantage of potentiometric titrations over visual titrations?

Solution: Potentiometry measures electrical potential rather than light absorption, making it immune to solution color or turbidity.

Question 3 of 10
The potential of a glass pH electrode depends primarily on:

Solution: The boundary potential of a glass electrode is determined by the difference in hydrogen ion activity between the internal reference solution and the analyte solution.

Question 4 of 10
For a divalent metal ion M2+, what is the theoretical potential change per ten-fold change in ion activity at 25°C?

Solution: Using 59.16 / n, for n = 2 the change is 59.16 / 2 = 29.58 mV.

Question 5 of 10
What is the role of potassium chloride (KCl) in a saturated calomel electrode?

Solution: Saturated KCl establishes a fixed chloride ion activity, fixing the potential of the reference half-cell.

Question 6 of 10
In Gran plot analysis for potentiometric titrations, the plot of volume-corrected potential function versus titrant volume yields:

Solution: Gran plots linearize the titration data into a straight line whose x-intercept gives the equivalence point volume.

Question 7 of 10
Which metal is typically used as an inert indicator electrode in redox potentiometry?

Solution: Platinum is chemically inert and serves as a stable site for electron transfer in redox systems.

Question 8 of 10
What causes the liquid junction potential at the salt bridge interface?

Solution: Differences in diffusion rates between positive and negative ions create a charge separation and liquid junction potential.

Question 9 of 10
In a precipitation titration of Cl- with Ag+, the potential rises sharply near the equivalence point because:

Solution: Once excess Ag+ is added past the equivalence point, free Ag+ activity rises steeply, causing a sharp potential shift.

Question 10 of 10
Which of the following statements about potentiometric titrations is incorrect?

Solution: Potentiometric titrations do not require visual indicators; the electrode potential replaces the color change.
