Kohlrausch's Law

Kohlrausch's law of independent migration of ions states that the limiting molar conductivity of an electrolyte is the sum of the contributions from its constituent cations and anions. It provides a foundational method for determining the molar conductivity of weak electrolytes at infinite dilution by combining limiting molar conductivities of strong electrolytes.

UP PGT Chemistry Subject Code 02 B.Sc. / M.Sc. Level

Principle of Independent Ion Migration

  • At infinite dilution, the dissociation of both strong and weak electrolytes is complete.
  • Each ion makes a definite contribution to the total molar conductivity of the electrolyte, independent of the nature of the other ion with which it is associated.
  • The total limiting molar conductivity is the sum of stoichiometric coefficients multiplied by individual ionic conductivities.

Determination of Limiting Molar Conductivity for Weak Electrolytes

  • Direct measurement of Λm° for weak electrolytes is impossible via extrapolation due to high degree of association at finite concentrations.
  • Indirect calculation is performed by adding and subtracting limiting molar conductivities of appropriate strong electrolytes.
  • For acetic acid (CH3COOH), Λm°(CH3COOH) = Λm°(CH3COONa) + Λm°(HCl) - Λm°(NaCl).

Applications in Dissociation Constant and Solubility

  • The degree of dissociation (α) is evaluated using the ratio of molar conductivity at concentration c to limiting molar conductivity.
  • For sparingly soluble salts like AgCl or BaSO4, solubility is calculated using measured conductivity where concentration equals solubility (s).
  • The relation for solubility is s = (κ × 1000) / Λm°, assuming saturation concentration approximates infinite dilution.
📐 Key Formulas & Equations
Degree of Dissociation
α = Λm / Λm°
Conditions: Weak electrolyte at concentration c
Units: Dimensionless (α ≤ 1)
Dissociation Constant of Weak Electrolyte
Ka = (cα2) / (1 - α) = (c Λm2) / (Λm°(Λm° - Λm))
Conditions: Weak monobasic acid or monoacidic base
Units: Ka in mol L-1
Molar Conductivity Calculation
Λm = (κ × 1000) / c
Conditions: General definition of molar conductivity
Units: κ in S cm-1, c in mol L-1, Λm in S cm2 mol-1
Kohlrausch's Law of Independent Migration of Ions
Λm° = ν+λ+° + ν-λ-°
Conditions: Infinite dilution (c → 0)
Units: Λm° in S cm2 mol-1; λ° in S cm2 mol-1
⚠️ Important Exceptions & Anomalies
Exception Equivalent ionic conductivity increases smoothly with decreasing concentration for all electrolytes.

Observation: Strong electrolytes follow Debye-Huckel-Onsager equation (Λm = Λm° - A√c), whereas weak electrolytes show a sharp exponential rise near infinite dilution. — Weak electrolytes exhibit a steep increase in degree of dissociation as concentration approaches zero.

Exception Ionic mobilities are strictly constant independent of solvent viscosity.

Observation: H+ and OH- ions possess abnormally high mobilities in aqueous solution. — Proton transfer occurs via the Grotthuss mechanism involving rapid structural diffusion along hydrogen-bonded water networks.

💡 Shortcuts & Memory Aids
Weak Acid Additivity Trick
Λ°(HA) = Λ°(NaA) + Λ°(HCl) - Λ°(NaCl)

Application: Match the cation of salt NaA with Cl- from HCl to cancel out spectator ions (Na+ and Cl-) and yield the weak acid HA.

Solubility Shortcut from Conductivity
Solubility (g/L) = [(κ × 1000) / Λm°] × Molar Mass

Application: Directly convert specific conductivity (κ) in S cm-1 to solubility in grams per liter for sparingly soluble salts.

Ostwald Dilution Integration
Ka = cα2 / (1 - α)

Application: Substitute α = Λm / Λm° directly into the Ostwald expression for fast numerical evaluation.

🎯 Key Exam Points
  • Kohlrausch's law states independent migration of ions at infinite dilution.
  • Limiting molar conductivity unit is S cm2 mol-1.
  • Degree of dissociation α = Λm / Λm°.
  • Dissociation constant Ka = cΛm2 / [Λm°(Λm° - Λm)].
  • Proton (H+) and hydroxyl (OH-) ions have anomalous high mobilities in water.
  • Solubility of sparingly soluble salts is calculated using s = (κ × 1000) / Λm°.
🚫 Common Errors & Confusions
  • Using concentration in mol L-1 directly in conductivity equations without multiplying by 1000 for cm3 conversions.
  • Confusing molar conductivity (Λm) with specific conductivity (κ).
  • Applying Debye-Huckel-Onsager equation directly to weak electrolytes where dissociation varies with concentration.
  • Neglecting stoichiometric coefficients (ν+, ν-) when summing ionic conductivities.

⚡ Quick Revision: Kohlrausch's Law

Core formulas, rapid recall points, and distinguishing criteria at a glance.

Law of Independent Migration

At infinite dilution, each ion contributes independently to the total molar conductivity of an electrolyte.

Indirect Determination

Kohlrausch's law enables calculation of Λm° for weak electrolytes using algebraic summation of strong electrolyte values.

Degree of Dissociation

The ratio of molar conductivity at any concentration to that at infinite dilution gives the fraction of dissociated molecules.

Sparingly Soluble Salts

Conductivity measurements of saturated solutions yield accurate solubility values for insoluble salts like AgCl.

📚 Terms & Units Glossary

Molar Conductivity (Λm)
Conductivity of all ions produced by one mole of electrolyte in solution. Unit: S cm2 mol-1.
Limiting Molar Conductivity (Λm°)
Molar conductivity at zero concentration or infinite dilution. Unit: S cm2 mol-1.
Specific Conductivity (κ)
Conductance of a cube of electrolyte solution of 1 cm edge. Unit: S cm-1.
Ionic Conductivity (λ°)
Contribution of a single ionic species to limiting molar conductivity.
Degree of Dissociation (α)
Fraction of total electrolyte molecules dissociated into ions.
Grotthuss Mechanism (Proton Hopping)
Mechanism by which H+ ions rapidly exchange through hydrogen-bonded water molecules.

🎯 Key Takeaways

  • Λm°(CH3COOH) = Λm°(CH3COONa) + Λm°(HCl) - Λm°(NaCl)
  • α = Λm / Λm°
  • Ka = cα2 / (1 - α)
  • s = (κ × 1000) / Λm°
  • Λm = (κ × 1000) / c

📌 Quick Recall

  • Kohlrausch's law applies strictly at infinite dilution.
  • Allows indirect determination of weak electrolyte parameters.
  • Relates ionic mobilities to molar conductivities.
  • Facilitates solubility calculations for sparingly soluble salts.

⚖️ Key Differences

Molar Conductivity (Λm) VS Specific Conductivity (κ)

Molar conductivity increases with dilution as volume increases, whereas specific conductivity decreases due to decrease in ion concentration per unit volume.

Question 1 of 18

What is the mathematical expression for Kohlrausch's law of independent migration of ions for an electrolyte AxBy?

Explanation: Kohlrausch's law states that the limiting molar conductivity is the sum of stoichiometric coefficients multiplied by the respective ionic conductivities.

Question 2 of 18

Which of the following expressions is used to calculate the degree of dissociation (α) of a weak electrolyte?

Explanation: The degree of dissociation is given by the ratio of molar conductivity at concentration c to the molar conductivity at infinite dilution.

Question 3 of 18

How can the limiting molar conductivity of acetic acid (Λm°(CH3COOH)) be calculated?

Explanation: Summing CH3COONa and HCl provides CH3COO-, Na+, H+, and Cl-. Subtracting NaCl removes Na+ and Cl-, leaving CH3COOH.

Question 4 of 18

What is the unit of molar conductivity in SI conventions?

Explanation: The SI unit for molar conductivity is S m2 mol-1, though S cm2 mol-1 is frequently used in literature.

Question 5 of 18

Why cannot the limiting molar conductivity of weak electrolytes be obtained by direct extrapolation of Λm versus √c curves?

Explanation: Weak electrolytes dissociate very little at higher concentrations, and the curve rises sharply asymptotically near zero concentration, preventing accurate extrapolation.

Question 6 of 18

What is the relationship between solubility (s) and specific conductivity (κ) for a sparingly soluble salt?

Explanation: For sparingly soluble salts, concentration equals solubility, hence c = (κ × 1000) / Λm°.

Question 7 of 18

Which ion possesses the highest ionic mobility in aqueous solution due to the Grotthuss mechanism?

Explanation: The hydrogen ion (H+) exhibits anomalous high mobility via proton hopping along hydrogen bonds in water.

Question 8 of 18

What happens to the molar conductivity of a strong electrolyte upon dilution?

Explanation: Molar conductivity of strong electrolytes increases slightly and gradually upon dilution due to a decrease in interionic electrostatic interactions.

Question 9 of 18

The dissociation constant (Ka) of a weak monobasic acid in terms of molar conductivity is given by:

Explanation: Substituting α = Λm / Λm° into Ka = cα2/(1 - α) yields cΛm2 / [Λm°(Λm° - Λm)].

Question 10 of 18

Which of the following represents Kohlrausch's law mathematically?

Explanation: Λm° = ∑ νiλi° is the standard statement of Kohlrausch's law.

Question 11 of 18

What is the effect of temperature on molar conductivity?

Explanation: Increasing temperature decreases solvent viscosity and increases ionic mobility, thereby increasing molar conductivity.

Question 12 of 18

In the Debye-Huckel-Onsager equation Λm = Λm° - A√c, what does constant 'A' depend on?

Explanation: The constant A depends on the type of electrolyte (e.g., 1:1, 2:1), temperature, viscosity, and dielectric constant of the medium.

Question 13 of 18

What is the limiting molar conductivity of BaCl2 if ionic conductivities are λ°(Ba2+) and λ°(Cl-)?

Explanation: BaCl2 dissociates into 1 Ba2+ and 2 Cl- ions, hence Λm° = λ°(Ba2+) + 2λ°(Cl-).

Question 14 of 18

Why does specific conductivity decrease on dilution?

Explanation: Specific conductivity is the conductance of unit volume of solution; dilution decreases the number of ions present in 1 cm3 of solution.

Question 15 of 18

Which law forms the basis for determining dissociation constants of weak acids conductometrically?

Explanation: Kohlrausch's law provides Λm° for weak acids, which is required to calculate α and Ka.

Question 16 of 18

If the molar conductivity of a weak acid at concentration c is 10 S cm2 mol-1 and its limiting molar conductivity is 400 S cm2 mol-1, what is its degree of dissociation?

Explanation: α = Λm / Λm° = 10 / 400 = 0.025.

Question 17 of 18

Which of the following is a strong electrolyte whose limiting molar conductivity can be obtained directly by extrapolation?

Explanation: KCl is a strong electrolyte and shows a linear plot of Λm versus √c, allowing direct extrapolation to zero concentration.

Question 18 of 18

What is the dimension of specific conductivity (κ)?

Explanation: Specific conductivity is conductance (ohm-1) divided by length (cm), resulting in ohm-1 cm-1 or S cm-1.

Question 1 of 10
State Kohlrausch's law of independent migration of ions and identify its primary utility.

Solution: Kohlrausch's law is primarily used to determine limiting molar conductivities of weak electrolytes which cannot be obtained by direct extrapolation.

Question 2 of 10
Calculate the dissociation constant of 0.01 M CH3COOH if its molar conductivity is 16.5 S cm2 mol-1 and limiting molar conductivity is 390.5 S cm2 mol-1.

Solution: α = 16.5 / 390.5 = 0.0422. Ka = cα2 / (1 - α) = 0.01 × (0.0422)2 / 0.9578 ≈ 1.85 × 10-5 mol L-1.

Question 3 of 10
If the specific conductivity of a saturated solution of AgCl at 298 K is 1.82 × 10-6 S cm-1 and its limiting molar conductivity is 138.3 S cm2 mol-1, what is the solubility of AgCl in mol L-1?

Solution: s = (κ × 1000) / Λm° = (1.82 × 10-6 × 1000) / 138.3 = 1.32 × 10-5 mol L-1.

Question 4 of 10
Which graphical extrapolation is employed for strong electrolytes to find Λm°?

Solution: Plotting Λm against the square root of concentration (√c) yields a straight line that intercepts the y-axis at Λm°.

Question 5 of 10
What is the contribution of Na+ and SO42- to the limiting molar conductivity of Na2SO4?

Solution: Sodium sulfate dissociates into two Na+ ions and one SO42- ion, hence Λm° = 2λ°(Na+) + λ°(SO42-).

Question 6 of 10
Why does the molar conductivity of a weak electrolyte increase steeply on dilution compared to a strong electrolyte?

Solution: Weak electrolytes dissociate more completely as concentration decreases, causing a rapid increase in the number of conducting ions.

Question 7 of 10
Calculate Λm° for CaSO4 given λ°(Ca2+) = 119.0 S cm2 mol-1 and λ°(SO42-) = 160.0 S cm2 mol-1.

Solution: Λm°(CaSO4) = λ°(Ca2+) + λ°(SO42-) = 119.0 + 160.0 = 279.0 S cm2 mol-1.

Question 8 of 10
Which of the following factors does NOT affect the ionic mobility of an ion in solution?

Solution: Ionic mobility is an intrinsic property at a given temperature and solvent; the applied potential gradient determines current flow but does not alter inherent ionic mobility.

Question 9 of 10
What is the relationship between equivalent conductivity (Λeq) and molar conductivity (Λm) for Al2(SO4)3?

Solution: For Al2(SO4)3, total positive charge (n-factor) is 6. Therefore, Λm = 6 × Λeq (since Λm = n-factor × Λeq).

Question 10 of 10
In conductivity measurements, water used to prepare solutions must be conductivity water (purest form) to prevent:

Solution: Dissolved impurities such as atmospheric CO2 form carbonic acid, producing H+ and HCO3- ions that distort low-concentration conductivity readings.
