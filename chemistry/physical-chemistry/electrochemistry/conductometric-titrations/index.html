Conductometric Titrations

Conductometric titrations involve measuring the change in electrical conductance of an electrolytic solution as a titrant is added. The equivalence point is determined graphically by locating the intersection of two linear branches with different slopes.

UP PGT Chemistry Subject Code 02 B.Sc. / M.Sc. Level

Principle of Conductometric Titrations

  • The conductance of a solution is directly proportional to the mobility and concentration of ions present in the medium.
  • Substitution of ions with vastly different ionic mobilities during a chemical reaction causes a distinct change in slope of the titration curve.
  • Fast-moving ions such as H+ and OH- contribute significantly higher conductance compared to heavier counter-ions like Na+ or Cl-.
  • Using an AC source prevents polarization effects and localized concentration changes at the platinum electrodes.

Titration Curve Profiles for Acid-Base Systems

  • Titration of a strong acid with a strong base (e.g., HCl vs NaOH) yields a V-shaped curve due to the rapid replacement of fast H+ by slower Na+ followed by the addition of fast OH-.
  • Titration of a weak acid with a strong base (e.g., CH3COOH vs NaOH) initially shows a sharp rise due to buffer formation, followed by a minimum and a steep increase post-equivalence.
  • Titration of a mixture of a strong and weak acid against a strong base yields a curve with two distinct inflection points representing sequential neutralization.
  • Precipitation titrations such as KCl vs AgNO3 exhibit a nearly constant or slightly rising conductance until the equivalence point, followed by a sharp increase due to excess Ag+ and NO3- ions.

Experimental Advantages and Limitations

  • Conductometric titrations are highly effective for colored or turbid solutions where visual indicators fail to show a distinct color change.
  • The method yields accurate results even for very dilute solutions and weak acids or bases that exhibit poorly defined end-points in standard volumetric analysis.
  • Extrapolation of lines to find the equivalence point eliminates errors arising from hydrolysis or incomplete reactions near the stoichiometric point.
  • High concentrations of foreign inert electrolytes in the solution swamp the conductance changes, rendering the method unsuitable for solutions with high ionic strength.
📐 Key Formulas & Equations
Molar Conductance
Λm = (κ × 1000) / C
Conditions: For solutions where concentration C is in mol L-1
Units: Λm in S cm2 mol-1
Cell Constant
G* = l / A
Conditions: Depends on the physical geometry of the conductivity cell
Units: G* in cm-1 or m-1
Observed Conductance Relation
G = 1 / R = (κ × A) / l
Conditions: At constant temperature using alternating current (AC)
Units: Resistance R in Ohms (Ω), Conductance G in Siemens (S)
Specific Conductance
κ = G × (l / A)
Conditions: Applicable to any electrolytic cell of uniform cross-section
Units: κ in S m-1 or S cm-1, G in Siemens (S)
⚠️ Important Exceptions & Anomalies
Exception Conductance generally increases linearly with the addition of titrant ions.

Observation: Initial decrease in conductance during strong acid-strong base titration — Fast-moving hydrogen ions (H+) are consumed and replaced by slower sodium ions (Na+), causing a net drop in total conductance.

Exception Weak acid titration curves show a steady decrease in conductance initially.

Observation: Initial sharp drop followed by a rise in weak acid-strong base titration — Initial common-ion suppression of weak acid ionization is overridden by the accumulation of highly conducting salt ions.

💡 Shortcuts & Memory Aids
Volume Correction Rule
Gcorrected = Gobs × [(Vinitial + Vtitrant) / Vinitial]

Application: Always apply this correction factor when large volumes of titrant are added to prevent significant dilution errors from distorting the linear plot.

Mobility Order Mnemonic
H+ > OH- >> Other Monatomic Ions

Application: Instantly predict whether a titration branch will slope upward or downward by identifying whether protons or hydroxide ions are being added or removed.

End-Point Intersection
Extrapolating Linear Branches

Application: Do not read the titration end-point from a single minimum or maximum; always extrapolate the linear segments well before and after the equivalence point to locate the exact intersection.

🎯 Key Exam Points
  • The SI unit of specific conductance (κ) is Siemens per meter (S m-1).
  • Molar conductance increases significantly upon dilution for weak electrolytes due to increased degree of dissociation.
  • Conductometric titrations do not require chemical indicators.
  • Precipitation titrations like NaCl with AgNO3 rely on the replacement of Cl- by NO3- ions.
  • The cell constant (l/A) has units of cm-1 or m-1.
  • An alternating current (AC) is utilized in conductance measurements to prevent electrolysis of the solution.
🚫 Common Errors & Confusions
  • Neglecting volume correction when large volumes of titrant are added, leading to curved instead of straight graphical branches.
  • Assuming conductance always increases upon adding an electrolyte.
  • Confusing specific conductance units (S cm-1) with molar conductance units (S cm2 mol-1).
  • Failing to maintain a constant temperature, as ionic mobility and conductance vary significantly with temperature.

⚡ Quick Revision: Conductometric Titrations

Core formulas, rapid recall points, and distinguishing criteria at a glance.

Conductance Fundamentals

Conductance is the reciprocal of resistance and serves as a measure of current-carrying capacity governed by Ohm's Law.

Ionic Mobilities

Hydrogen and hydroxide ions possess abnormally high mobilities due to the Grotthuss proton-jump mechanism in aqueous media.

Graphical End-Point Detection

Equivalence points are determined graphically by extrapolating linear segments before and after the stoichiometric point.

Application Scope

The technique is exceptionally useful for colorful, turbid, or very dilute acid-base and precipitation systems.

📚 Terms & Units Glossary

Specific Conductance (κ)
Conductance of a 1 cm cube of electrolyte solution. Unit: S cm-1.
Molar Conductance (Λm)
Conductance of a solution containing 1 mole of electrolyte placed between electrodes 1 cm apart. Unit: S cm2 mol-1.
Cell Constant (G*)
Ratio of distance between electrodes (l) to effective cross-sectional area (A). Unit: cm-1.
Equivalent Conductance (Λeq)
Conductance of a solution containing 1 gram equivalent of electrolyte. Unit: S cm2 equiv-1.
Resistance (R)
Opposition to current flow. Measured in Ohms (Ω).
Siemens (S)
SI unit of electrical conductance, equivalent to ohm-1 or mho.

🎯 Key Takeaways

  • Λm = (κ × 1000) / C
  • G* = R × κ
  • H+ and OH- have the highest ionic mobilities in water.
  • Volume correction is essential for precise graphical end-point location.
  • AC bridge measurements eliminate polarization errors.

📌 Quick Recall

  • Conductometric titrations monitor changes in solution conductance during chemical reactions.
  • Curves consist of intersecting linear branches whose intersection denotes the equivalence point.
  • Strong acid-strong base titrations display a characteristic V-shape.
  • No chemical indicator is required, making it ideal for colored or dilute systems.

⚖️ Key Differences

Specific Conductance VS Molar Conductance

Specific conductance decreases with dilution, whereas molar conductance increases with dilution.

Which ion possesses the highest ionic mobility in aqueous solution?

What is the unit of specific conductance?

Explanation: Specific conductance (κ) is expressed in S cm-1 or S m-1.

Question 3 of 19

Why is an alternating current (AC) used in conductometric measurements?

Explanation: AC prevents concentration polarization and decomposition of the electrolyte at the electrode surface.

Question 4 of 19

What shape is typical for the conductometric titration curve of a strong acid with a strong base?

Explanation: The curve drops as fast H+ is replaced by slower Na+ and then rises as excess OH- is added, forming a V-shape.

Question 5 of 19

The cell constant is defined as:

Explanation: Cell constant G* = l / A, where l is distance between electrodes and A is cross-sectional area.

Question 6 of 19

How does molar conductance vary with dilution for a weak electrolyte?

Explanation: Dilution increases the degree of dissociation (α) of weak electrolytes, sharply increasing molar conductance.

Question 7 of 19

In the titration of acetic acid against sodium hydroxide, the initial conductance:

Explanation: Initial addition of NaOH neutralizes CH3COOH to form CH3COONa, suppressing acid ionization and decreasing conductance initially.

Question 8 of 19

Which titration does NOT require an indicator when monitored conductometrically?

Explanation: All conductometric titrations rely on physical conductance changes rather than chemical indicators.

Question 9 of 19

What causes the sharp rise in conductance after the equivalence point in a strong acid-strong base titration?

Explanation: Beyond the equivalence point, excess OH- ions are added, which possess very high ionic mobility.

Question 10 of 19

The relationship between molar conductance (Λm), specific conductance (κ), and concentration (C in mol L-1) is:

Explanation: Converting units from liters to cubic centimeters yields Λm = (κ × 1000) / C.

Question 11 of 19

What is the primary purpose of volume correction in conductometric titrations?

Explanation: Adding titrant volume dilutes the solution, which must be corrected to maintain linear plot extrapolation.

Question 12 of 19

Conductometric titration of a mixture of HCl and CH3COOH against NaOH gives how many inflection points?

Explanation: Two inflection points appear: the first when strong acid (HCl) is neutralized, and the second when weak acid (CH3COOH) is neutralized.

Question 13 of 19

Which of the following salts is typically used to calibrate a conductivity cell?

Explanation: Potassium chloride (KCl) solutions of known concentration are standard primary references for cell calibration.

Question 14 of 19

Precipitation titrations like BaCl2 against MgSO4 involve monitoring conductance changes due to:

Explanation: Precipitation removes ions from solution, altering the total ionic conductance until the equivalence point is reached.

Question 15 of 19

Why are conductometric titrations unsuitable for solutions containing high concentrations of inert electrolytes?

Explanation: High background electrolyte concentration masks the conductance changes due to the added titrant.

Question 16 of 19

The dimension of conductance is:

Explanation: Conductance is the inverse of resistance, measured in Ohm-1 or Siemens.

Question 17 of 19

Which physical property is plotted against the volume of titrant in conductometric titrations?

Explanation: Conductance or corrected conductance is plotted on the y-axis against titrant volume on the x-axis.

Question 18 of 19

In a titration of a weak base against a strong acid, the initial conductance is:

Explanation: Weak bases are feebly ionized, resulting in low initial concentration of ions and low initial conductance.

Question 19 of 19

Which of the following conditions is necessary for accurate conductometric measurements?

Explanation: Conductance varies significantly with temperature due to changes in ionic mobility and solution viscosity.

Question 1 of 10
What is the primary reason why conductometric titrations are not suitable for solutions with high foreign electrolyte concentrations?

Solution: High background ionic strength swamps the conductivity variations produced by the reaction.

Question 2 of 10
Calculate the cell constant if the resistance of a 0.1 M KCl solution (specific conductance = 0.0129 S cm-1) is 100 ohms.

Solution: Cell constant G* = R × κ = 100 Ω × 0.0129 S cm-1 = 1.29 cm-1.

Question 3 of 10
Which ion has a disproportionately large effect on the slope of a conductometric titration curve?

Solution: H+ possesses exceptionally high ionic mobility and dominates solution conductance.

Question 4 of 10
In a precipitation titration of KCl against AgNO3, the conductance after the equivalence point:

Solution: Post-equivalence, excess Ag+ and NO3- ions are added, causing a sharp increase in conductance.

Question 5 of 10
The equivalent conductance of an electrolyte is related to specific conductance (κ ) and normality (N) by:

Solution: Equivalent conductance definition yields Λeq = (κ × 1000) / N.

Question 6 of 10
What type of curve is obtained when titrating a weak base with a strong acid?

Solution: Adding strong acid adds fast H+ ions, but they are consumed to form weakly dissociated salt, then excess acid increases conductance, resulting in a minimum followed by a rise (or continuous drop depending on coordinates).

Question 7 of 10
Which frequency range of alternating current is typically used in conductivity bridges to avoid polarization?

Solution: Audio frequencies between 500 Hz and 3000 Hz prevent polarization effects at the platinum electrodes.

Question 8 of 10
How does temperature affect the conductance of an electrolytic solution?

Solution: Higher temperature lowers solvent viscosity and increases ionic mobility, thereby increasing conductance.

Question 9 of 10
In the conductometric titration of a weak acid with a weak base, why is the end-point often poorly defined?

Solution: Hydrolysis of the resulting weak salt rounds off the intersection point, making the end-point difficult to locate accurately.

Question 10 of 10
The molar conductance of strong electrolytes obeys which empirical equation with concentration (C)?

Solution: The Debye-Huckel-Onsager equation describes the variation of molar conductance with concentration for strong electrolytes: Λm = Λm° - bC1/2.
