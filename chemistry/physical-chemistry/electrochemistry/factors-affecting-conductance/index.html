Factors Affecting Conductance

Electrolytic conductance depends on the mobility of ions, their degree of dissociation, and the viscosity of the medium. Temperature, concentration, and ionic solvation collectively dictate both molar and equivalent conductivity.

UP PGT Chemistry Subject Code 02 B.Sc. / M.Sc. Level

Nature of Electrolyte and Interionic Forces

  • Strong electrolytes dissociate completely, showing high conductance due to a high concentration of free ions.
  • Weak electrolytes exhibit low degrees of dissociation (α), yielding low conductance values in solution.
  • Strong interionic electrostatic forces at high concentrations restrict independent ionic movement, reducing equivalent conductance.
  • Asymmetrical effects and electrophilic retardation slow down ionic mobility in concentrated ionic solutions.

Solvation of Ions and Viscosity of Medium

  • Smaller ions possess higher charge densities, resulting in extensive solvation and increased effective hydrodynamic radii.
  • Larger solvated ions move sluggishly through solution, yielding lower molar conductivities despite high initial charge density.
  • Solvent viscosity (η) is inversely proportional to ionic mobility; higher viscosity impedes transport.
  • Decreased solvent dielectric constant promotes ion-pair formation, reducing the number of free current carriers.

Effect of Temperature and Dilution

  • Elevating temperature decreases solvent viscosity and weakens interionic forces, increasing both conductance and ionic mobility.
  • Dilution increases molar conductance (Λm) for both strong and weak electrolytes due to enhanced dissociation or reduced interionic crowding.
  • Specific conductance (κ) always decreases upon dilution because the number of ions per unit volume decreases.
  • Limiting molar conductance (Λm∞) represents the maximum theoretical conductance at infinite dilution.

⚠️ Important Exceptions & Anomalies
Exception Smaller sized ions have higher mobility and higher conductance.

Observation: Li+ has a lower ionic conductance than Cs+ in aqueous solution. — Li+ has the highest charge density, causing extensive hydration (solvation), making its effective hydrated radius much larger than Cs+.

Exception Conductance decreases with increase in concentration for all electrolytes.

Observation: Very high temperature and specific molten salts show complex viscosity-driven conduction anomalies. — Extreme thermal agitation disrupts normal solvation shells and alters classical hydrodynamic flow models.

Exception Specific conductance increases with concentration.

Observation: At extreme saturation limits, ion-pair and triple-ion formation can cause non-linear deviations in κ vs C plots. — Formation of neutral ion aggregates reduces the effective concentration of charge-carrying species.

💡 Shortcuts & Memory Aids
The Dilution Rule
κ drops, Λm rises

Application: Remember that specific conductance always decreases on dilution, while molar conductance always increases.

Hydrated Radius Order (Group 1)
Li+(aq) > Na+(aq) > K+(aq) > Rb+(aq) > Cs+(aq)

Application: Hydration energy and hydrated size follow the reverse order of bare ionic radii, directly predicting mobility trends.

Proton and Hydroxide Anomaly
H+ and OH- use Grotthuss mechanism

Application: H+ and OH- show abnormally high limiting equivalent conductances due to proton-jump mechanism rather than physical migration.

🎯 Key Exam Points
  • Specific conductance (κ) has SI units of Siemens per meter (S·m-1).
  • Molar conductance (Λm) is expressed in S·cm2·mol-1 or S·m2·mol-1.
  • The Debye-Hückel-Onsager equation applies linearly to strong electrolytes at low concentrations.
  • Weak electrolytes show a sharp increase in molar conductance near infinite dilution due to increased degree of dissociation (α).
  • Temperature coefficient of electrolytic conductance is positive, roughly 2% per degree Celsius rise.
  • Cell constant (G*) is defined as the ratio of distance between electrodes to cross-sectional area (l / A).
🚫 Common Errors & Confusions
  • Confusing specific conductance with molar conductance; remember specific conductance decreases on dilution while molar conductance increases.
  • Assuming bare ionic radius determines mobility without considering hydration sphere size in aqueous solutions.
  • Forgetting unit conversion factors (like multiplying by 1000) when calculating molar conductance from cm3 to L.
  • Applying Kohlrausch's law directly to strong electrolytes without accounting for interionic interactions at higher concentrations.

⚡ Quick Revision: Factors Affecting Conductance

Core formulas, rapid recall points, and distinguishing criteria at a glance.

Electrolytic Resistance and Conductance

Conductance (G) is the reciprocal of resistance (R) and is measured in Siemens (S). It depends directly on the geometry of the electrolytic cell and the nature of the ions present.

Specific Conductance and Cell Constant

Specific conductance (κ) links resistance to solution dimensions via the cell constant (G* = l/A). It measures the conducting capacity of a unit volume of electrolyte.

Molar and Equivalent Conductance

Molar conductance normalizes specific conductance per mole of solute, reflecting the collective conducting power of all ions produced by one mole of the electrolyte.

Factors Governing Ionic Mobility

Viscosity of the solvent, ionic charge, temperature, and degree of solvation directly control the drift speed of ions under an applied electrical potential gradient.

📚 Terms & Units Glossary

Conductance (G = 1 / R)
Ease of flow of electric current through a conductor; SI unit is Siemens (S).
Specific Conductance (κ (Kappa))
Conductance of a unit cube (1 cm3) of electrolyte solution; unit is S·cm-1.
Molar Conductance (Λm)
Conductance of solution containing 1 mole of electrolyte placed between electrodes 1 cm apart.
Cell Constant (G* = l / A)
Ratio of distance between electrodes to their cross-sectional area; unit is cm-1.
Degree of Dissociation (α = Λm / Λm∞)
Fraction of total electrolyte molecules dissociated into ions.
Viscosity (η (Eta))
Internal friction of a fluid medium opposing ionic migration.

🎯 Key Takeaways

  • Λm increases with dilution and reaches a maximum limiting value (Λm∞).
  • κ decreases upon dilution due to a decrease in ion concentration per unit volume.
  • Hydrated ionic radius is inversely proportional to bare ionic radius for alkali metals.
  • Proton (H+) and hydroxide (OH-) possess exceptionally high mobility due to the Grotthuss chain mechanism.
  • Temperature rise increases conductance by lowering solvent viscosity and breaking hydration shells.

📌 Quick Recall

  • Electrolytic conductance is governed by ion concentration, ionic mobility, solvation, and temperature.
  • Strong electrolytes exhibit high conductance due to complete dissociation, modified by interionic attraction.
  • Weak electrolytes show strong concentration-dependent dissociation behavior.
  • Dilution increases molar conductance while decreasing specific conductance.

⚖️ Key Differences

Specific Conductance (κ) VS Molar Conductance (Λm)

κ decreases on dilution because ion density drops, whereas Λm increases because total volume holding one mole expands.

Question 1 of 18

What happens to the specific conductance of an electrolyte solution upon dilution?

Explanation: Specific conductance is the conductance of unit volume of solution. On dilution, the number of ions per unit volume decreases, thereby decreasing specific conductance.

Question 2 of 18

Which of the following alkali metal ions has the lowest mobility in aqueous solution?

Explanation: Li+ has the smallest bare size and highest charge density, resulting in maximum hydration (solvation). The large hydrated radius reduces its mobility.

Question 3 of 18

The SI unit of specific conductance is:

Explanation: Specific conductance (κ) is expressed in S·m-1 in SI units (or Ω-1·m-1).

Question 4 of 18

Molar conductance of a strong electrolyte increases with dilution mainly because:

Explanation: For strong electrolytes, dilution separates ions further apart, reducing interionic electrostatic forces of retardation and increasing ionic mobility.

Question 5 of 18

The Debye-Hückel-Onsager equation relates molar conductance to:

Explanation: The Debye-Hückel-Onsager equation is Λm = Λm∞ - (A + BΛm∞)√C, linking conductance directly to √C.

Question 6 of 18

What is the effect of increasing temperature on electrolytic conductance?

Explanation: Increasing temperature decreases solvent viscosity and weakens ionic solvation, thereby increasing kinetic energy and ionic conductance.

Question 7 of 18

Which ions exhibit abnormally high molar ionic conductance due to a proton-jump mechanism?

Explanation: H+ and OH- migrate via the Grotthuss mechanism involving rapid proton hopping through hydrogen-bonded water networks.

Question 8 of 18

The cell constant G* is expressed by the formula:

Explanation: Cell constant G* = l / A, where l is the distance between electrodes and A is the cross-sectional area.

Question 9 of 18

For a weak electrolyte, the degree of dissociation (α) is calculated as:

Explanation: According to Arrhenius theory of electrolytic dissociation, α = Λm / Λm∞.

Question 10 of 18

Which factor does NOT affect the equivalent conductance of a solution?

Explanation: The material of the container holding the solution does not affect electrolytic conductance, unlike temperature, concentration, and electrolyte nature.

Question 11 of 18

Units of molar conductance in CGS system are typically:

Explanation: In CGS units, molar conductance is expressed as ohm-1·cm2·mol-1 (or S·cm2·mol-1).

Question 12 of 18

Why does a weak electrolyte show a sharp rise in molar conductance near infinite dilution?

Explanation: Weak electrolytes approach 100% dissociation (α → 1) at infinite dilution, causing a steep rise in molar conductance.

Question 13 of 18

The resistance of a solution measured in a conductivity cell is 50 ohms. If the cell constant is 0.5 cm-1, what is the specific conductance?

Explanation: κ = (1 / R) × (l / A) = (1 / 50) × 0.5 = 0.01 S·cm-1.

Question 14 of 18

Which of the following has the highest ionic conductance at infinite dilution in water?

Explanation: H+ possesses the highest limiting ionic conductance due to its exceptionally small size and the Grotthuss hopping mechanism.

Question 15 of 18

When sodium chloride is added to water, how does the specific conductance change initially?

Explanation: Adding NaCl introduces mobile Na+ and Cl- ions into pure water, rapidly increasing its specific conductance.

Question 16 of 18

Which term represents the reciprocal of resistivity?

Explanation: Specific conductance (κ) is the reciprocal of specific resistance (resistivity, ρ).

Question 17 of 18

At infinite dilution, the dissociation constant K of a weak electrolyte can be expressed using molar conductance as:

Explanation: Substituting α = Λm / Λm∞ into Ostwald's dilution law expression K = cα2 / (1 - α) yields this relationship.

Question 18 of 18

The variation of molar conductance with concentration for a strong electrolyte is represented by:

Explanation: According to the Debye-Hückel-Onsager equation, Λm vs √C is linear with a negative slope.

Question 1 of 10
Which of the following plots is linear for strong electrolytes according to Debye-Hückel-Onsager theory?

Question 2 of 10
The specific conductance of a 0.1 N KCl solution is 0.0129 S·cm-1. What is its equivalent conductance?

Solution: Λeq = (κ × 1000) / N = (0.0129 × 1000) / 0.1 = 129 S·cm2·eq-1.

Question 3 of 10
What is the primary reason for the decrease in specific conductance upon dilution?

Solution: Specific conductance measures conducting power per unit volume; dilution reduces the number of ions present per unit volume.

Question 4 of 10
Which of the following pairs has the reverse order of mobility in water compared to their gas-phase radii?

Solution: Li+ is smaller than Cs+ in gas phase, but in water Li+ is heavily hydrated and moves slower than Cs+.

Question 5 of 10
The unit of cell constant is:

Solution: Cell constant G* = l / A, where l is in cm and A is in cm2, giving cm-1.

Question 6 of 10
Why does the molar conductance of CH3COOH increase steeply near infinite dilution?

Solution: Weak electrolytes like acetic acid dissociate nearly completely at very high dilutions (α → 1), causing a steep rise in Λm.

Question 7 of 10
If resistance of a cell containing 0.1 M KCl is 100 Ω and specific conductance is 0.0129 S·cm-1, the cell constant is:

Solution: G* = R × κ = 100 Ω × 0.0129 S·cm-1 = 1.29 cm-1.

Question 8 of 10
In electrolytic conduction, current is carried by:

Solution: Current in electrolytic solutions is carried by the physical migration of both positively charged cations and negatively charged anions.

Question 9 of 10
Temperature coefficient of electrolytic conductance typically corresponds to an increase of how much per degree Celsius?

Solution: Electrolytic conductance generally increases by about 2% for every 1°C rise in temperature due to decreased solvent viscosity.

Question 10 of 10
Which of the following solutions will have the lowest molar conductance at 0.01 M concentration?

Solution: CH3COOH is a weak electrolyte with low degree of dissociation at 0.01 M, resulting in significantly lower molar conductance than strong electrolytes (HCl, NaCl, KCl).
