Physical Chemistry

Liquid-junction potential

Liquid-junction potential is the electrical potential difference generated at the boundary between two electrolyte solutions of different concentrations or compositions. It arises due to the unequal rates of diffusion of cations and anions across the phase boundary.

UP PGT Chemistry Subject Code 02 B.Sc. / M.Sc. Level

Origin and Mechanism of Liquid-Junction Potential

  • A liquid-junction potential develops whenever two solutions of different ionic activities are brought into direct contact.
  • The potential originates from the differential rates of diffusion (ionic mobilities) of positive and negative ions across the boundary.
  • Fast-moving ions like H+ and OH- outpace counter-ions, creating a transient charge separation and local electrostatic field.
  • The steady-state potential gradient persists as long as the concentration gradient across the junction is maintained.

Mathematical Treatment and Boundary Models

  • The Henderson model assumes a continuous mixture region where concentration gradients vary linearly from solution 1 to solution 2.
  • The magnitude of Ej depends directly on the transport numbers (ti) and valencies (zi) of the diffusing ions.
  • When cationic and anionic mobilities are identical (u+ = u-), the net liquid-junction potential theoretically approaches zero.
  • The Planck model applies to constrained diffusion boundaries where ion mixing follows restricted geometry.

Minimization and Elimination Techniques

  • A salt bridge containing a concentrated electrolyte (typically saturated KCl) effectively minimizes liquid-junction potentials.
  • KCl is chosen because the ionic mobilities of K+ and Cl- are nearly identical (approx. 76.2 vs 76.3 S cm2 mol-1).
  • By equalizing opposing diffusion rates, the salt bridge reduces Ej to values typically less than 1 mV.
  • Electrochemical cells requiring high thermodynamic accuracy must mathematically account for or experimentally eliminate Ej.
📐 Key Formulas & Equations
Simplified Henderson Equation for 1:1 Electrolytes
Ej = RTF u_+ - u_-u_+ + u_- ln c_2c_1
Conditions: Dilute solutions of a single 1:1 electrolyte with concentrations c1 and c2
Units: u+ and u- are ionic mobilities, concentrations in mol L-1
Planck Equation for Constrained Diffusion
Ej = RTF ln c_2 (u_+ + v_-)c_1 (u_+ + v_-)
Conditions: Static continuous boundary where ionic concentrations vary linearly
Units: u+, v- are mobilities, c1, c2 are total concentrations
Total Cell Potential with Junction Potential
Ecell = Eright - Eleft + Ej
Conditions: Potentiometric cells containing a liquid junction without a salt bridge
Units: All potentials in Volts
Henderson Equation for Liquid-Junction Potential
Ej = RTF ∫ ∑ (ti / zi) d(ln ai)
Conditions: Continuous mixture boundary between two electrolyte solutions
Units: Ej in Volts, R in J K-1 mol-1, T in Kelvin, F in C mol-1
⚠️ Important Exceptions & Anomalies
Exception Liquid-junction potentials increase with greater concentration differences across the boundary.

Observation: Very high concentrations can lead to ionic association and complexation. — Ion-pairing reduces the effective activity gradients and alters individual ionic mobilities.

Exception Saturated KCl salt bridge eliminates junction potentials completely.

Observation: Fails when interacting with solutions containing perchlorate or protein samples. — Precipitation of potassium perchlorate (KClO4) or protein clogging blocks the liquid junction, causing erratic potentials.

💡 Shortcuts & Memory Aids
Salt Bridge Mobility Match Rule
λ+(K+) ≈ λ-(Cl-)

Application: Instantly identify KCl as the ideal salt bridge electrolyte due to near-equal cation and anion molar conductivities.

Sign of Liquid-Junction Potential
Ej sign follows the faster ion sign

Application: If the faster ion diffuses into the dilute side, the dilute side acquires the charge sign of that faster ion.

Henderson Equation Shortcut for Dilute 1:1 Systems
Ej ∝ u_+ - u_-u_+ + u_-

Application: If u+ = u-, numerator becomes zero, making Ej = 0 regardless of concentration ratio.

🎯 Key Exam Points
  • Liquid-junction potential arises due to unequal rates of ionic diffusion across a phase boundary.
  • The Henderson equation calculates liquid-junction potential using ionic mobilities and activities.
  • A saturated KCl salt bridge minimizes Ej because K+ and Cl- have nearly identical transport numbers.
  • If ionic mobilities of cation and anion are equal, the liquid-junction potential is zero.
  • Liquid-junction potentials add directly to the measured electromotive force of an electrochemical cell: Ecell = Eright - Eleft + Ej.
  • High concentrations of KCl in salt bridges suppress diffusion potentials in precise potentiometric measurements.
🚫 Common Errors & Confusions
  • Assuming liquid-junction potential is always zero in standard cell representations without salt bridges.
  • Confusing thermodynamic standard cell potential with measured cell potential containing an uncorrected Ej.
  • Using a salt bridge with unequal ionic mobilities when trying to eliminate junction potentials.
  • Neglecting the sign convention of ionic mobilities in the Henderson equation.

⚡ Quick Revision: Liquid-junction potential

Core formulas, rapid recall points, and distinguishing criteria at a glance.

Diffusion and Ionic Mobilities

Unequal diffusion rates of cations and anions across boundaries create charge separation and potential differences known as liquid-junction potentials.

Henderson and Planck Formulations

Mathematical models like the Henderson and Planck equations quantify Ej based on ionic transport numbers, mobilities, and activity ratios.

Salt Bridge Elimination

Saturated potassium chloride salt bridges eliminate liquid-junction potentials by utilizing ions with virtually identical transference numbers.

Cell Potential Correction

Accurate thermodynamic measurements require accounting for Ej as an additive term in total cell electromotive force equations.

📚 Terms & Units Glossary

Liquid-Junction Potential (Ej)
Potential difference at the boundary of two dissimilar electrolyte solutions in Volts.
Transport Number (ti)
Fraction of total current carried by a specific ionic species.
Ionic Mobility (ui)
Speed of an ion under unit electric field in cm2 s-1 V-1.
Henderson Equation (Ej expression)
Calculates junction potential for continuous mixture boundaries.
Salt Bridge (Electrolyte bridge)
U-tube containing high-concentration electrolyte to suppress Ej.
Planck Equation (Constrained diffusion)
Calculates junction potential across restricted boundary layers.

🎯 Key Takeaways

  • Ej is caused by differential ionic diffusion rates.
  • KCl salt bridge minimizes Ej due to equal K+ and Cl- mobilities.
  • Henderson equation relates Ej to ionic mobilities and concentrations.
  • Total cell potential includes Ej when no salt bridge is present.
  • If u+ = u-, Ej = 0.

📌 Quick Recall

  • Boundary potentials distort measured cell voltages unless minimized.
  • Henderson model integrates continuous concentration gradients.
  • Saturated KCl is the universal salt bridge electrolyte.
  • Proper cell design eliminates liquid-junction errors in potentiometry.

⚖️ Key Differences

Liquid-Junction Potential VS Electrode Potential

Liquid-junction potential occurs at solution-solution boundaries, whereas electrode potential occurs at metal-solution interfaces.

What is the primary origin of liquid-junction potential?

Explanation: Liquid-junction potential arises from the different diffusion rates (mobilities) of cations and anions across a solution boundary.

Question 2 of 18

Why is saturated KCl commonly used in salt bridges?

Explanation: KCl is chosen because the ionic mobilities of K+ and Cl- are nearly identical, which minimizes the liquid-junction potential.

Question 3 of 18

Which equation is most commonly used to calculate liquid-junction potential for continuous mixtures?

Explanation: The Henderson equation is derived for continuous mixture liquid junctions based on ionic mobilities and concentration gradients.

Question 4 of 18

Under what condition is the liquid-junction potential theoretically zero?

Explanation: When u+ = u-, the terms in the Henderson equation cancel out, making Ej equal to zero.

Question 5 of 18

How does a liquid-junction potential affect the measured cell potential?

Explanation: The measured cell potential includes the junction potential as an additional term: Ecell = Eright - Eleft + Ej.

Question 6 of 18

Which ion pair has the most similar ionic mobilities in aqueous solution?

Explanation: K+ and Cl- possess very close transport numbers and mobilities in water, making KCl ideal for salt bridges.

Question 7 of 18

What type of boundary does the Planck equation evaluate?

Explanation: The Planck equation is formulated for restricted or constrained diffusion boundaries where concentration varies linearly.

Question 8 of 18

In the Henderson equation, what variables are integrated across the boundary?

Explanation: The Henderson equation integrates the contribution of ionic transport numbers and differential logarithms of ionic activities.

Question 9 of 18

Which of the following can cause failure of a KCl salt bridge?

Explanation: Perchlorate ions react with potassium to form insoluble KClO4, clogging the junction and causing erratic potentials.

Question 10 of 18

What is the typical magnitude of a well-suppressed liquid-junction potential using a salt bridge?

Explanation: A salt bridge reduces liquid-junction potentials to very small values, typically under 1 mV.

Question 11 of 18

Which ion exhibits exceptionally high mobility in aqueous solution due to a proton-jump mechanism?

Explanation: The hydrogen ion (H+) has unusually high mobility via the Grotthuss mechanism.

Question 12 of 18

If the concentration of an electrolyte increases across a junction, how does Ej respond?

Explanation: Greater concentration gradients lead to larger differences in diffusion rates, increasing the magnitude of Ej.

Question 13 of 18

Which scientist derived the equation for liquid-junction potential based on continuous mixtures?

Explanation: Lawrence Henderson derived the continuous mixture equation for liquid-junction potentials in 1907.

Question 14 of 18

Why are liquid-junction potentials problematic in precise thermodynamic measurements?

Explanation: Ej introduces an indeterminate potential component, making precise calculation of standard cell Gibbs free energy difficult.

Question 15 of 18

What is the role of agar-agar in a salt bridge?

Explanation: Agar-agar forms a semi-solid gel matrix that holds the concentrated KCl solution while allowing ion migration.

Question 16 of 18

In dilute 1:1 electrolyte solutions, what parameter dictates the sign of the liquid-junction potential?

Explanation: The sign of Ej is determined by whether the cation or the anion has a higher mobility.

Question 17 of 18

Can liquid-junction potential be measured directly with absolute certainty?

Explanation: Absolute single-ion values and junction potentials cannot be measured directly without thermodynamic conventions.

Question 18 of 18

What happens to the liquid-junction potential over a long period if the boundary is left unstirred?

Explanation: Over time, diffusion equalizes the concentrations on both sides of the boundary, eliminating the concentration gradient and Ej.
