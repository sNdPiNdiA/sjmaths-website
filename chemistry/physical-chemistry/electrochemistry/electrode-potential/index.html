Physical Chemistry

Electrode Potential

Electrode potential is the measure of potential development of a metal strip when it is contacted with its own ion solution. It quantifies the tendency of an electrode to gain or lose electrons in a redox half-cell reaction.

UP PGT Chemistry Subject Code 02 B.Sc. / M.Sc. Level

Origin and Measurement of Electrode Potential

  • Electrode potential arises due to the phase boundary potential established between a metal surface and its solvated ions.
  • Absolute electrode potential cannot be measured directly; it is always referenced against the Standard Hydrogen Electrode (SHE).
  • The standard hydrogen electrode has an arbitrarily assigned potential of 0.00 V at all temperatures.
  • By IUPAC convention, Standard Reduction Potentials are universally used to describe electrode tendencies.

Factors Affecting Electrode Potential

  • Electrode potential depends directly on the nature of the metal and its respective metal ions in solution.
  • An increase in the concentration of metal ions shifts the reduction potential to more positive values according to the Nernst equation.
  • Rise in temperature alters the logarithmic term and overall thermal coefficient of the half-cell reaction.
  • Formation of complexing agents or precipitates alters the free metal ion concentration, directly modifying the formal potential.

Thermodynamics of Electrochemical Cells

  • A spontaneous cell reaction requires a positive cell potential (Ecell > 0) and a negative Gibbs free energy change (ΔG < 0).
  • Standard cell potential is related to the equilibrium constant via the relation ΔG° = -RT ln K.
  • Concentration cells generate electricity solely due to the difference in concentration of the same species in two compartments.
  • The temperature coefficient of an electrochemical cell is given by (∂E / ∂T)p = ΔS / nF.
📐 Key Formulas & Equations
Nernst Equation at 298 K
E = E° - (0.0591 / n) log([Mn+] / [M])
Conditions: Standard temperature T = 298 K, aqueous solutions
Units: E in Volts, concentrations in mol L-1
Gibbs Free Energy and Electrode Potential
ΔG = -nFE
Conditions: Constant temperature and pressure
Units: ΔG in Joules, F in C mol-1, E in Volts
Standard Cell Potential and Equilibrium Constant
E°cell = (0.0591 / n) log Keq
Conditions: At 298 K under standard equilibrium conditions
Units: E° in Volts, Keq is dimensionless
Standard Cell Potential
E°cell = E°cathode - E°anode
Conditions: Standard reduction potentials used for both electrodes
Units: E° in Volts
Nernst Equation for Single Electrode
E = E° - (RT / nF) ln([Reduced] / [Oxidized])
Conditions: Applicable for any temperature T, non-standard concentrations
Units: E in Volts, R in J K-1 mol-1, T in Kelvin, F in C mol-1
⚠️ Important Exceptions & Anomalies
Exception Elements with more negative reduction potentials act as stronger reducing agents.

Observation: Lithium (Li) exhibits the most negative reduction potential in aqueous solution (E° = -3.05 V). — Exception is governed by exceptionally high heat of hydration of Li+ ions overriding its high ionization enthalpy.

Exception Standard hydrogen electrode operates at unit activity of hydrogen ions.

Observation: Real solutions deviate from ideal behavior at high concentrations. — Activity coefficients depart from unity due to ionic atmosphere interactions described by the Debye-Hückel theory.

Exception Reduction potential increases monotonically across a transition series.

Observation: Irregular trends appear in the first transition series, such as Mn2+/Mn having an unusually negative potential. — Extra stability of the half-filled 3d5 configuration in Mn2+.

🎯 Key Exam Points
  • Standard hydrogen electrode uses platinized platinum foil immersed in 1 M HCl purged with H2 gas at 1 atm.
  • Standard reduction potential of fluorine is the highest (+2.87 V), making it the strongest oxidizing agent.
  • Standard reduction potential of lithium is the lowest (-3.05 V), making it the strongest reducing agent in aqueous media.
  • A negative value of standard reduction potential indicates that the metal is more reactive than hydrogen.
  • The Gibbs free energy change (ΔG) is an extensive property, but cell potential (E) is an intensive property.
  • When a half-cell reaction equation is multiplied by a factor, E° value remains unchanged while ΔG° gets multiplied by that factor.
🚫 Common Errors & Confusions
  • Adding half-cell potentials directly when multiplying the half-reactions by stoichiometric coefficients; remember E is an intensive property.
  • Confusing anode and cathode polarities between galvanic and electrolytic cells.
  • Omitting the conversion factor from natural log to base-10 log (2.303) when calculating Nernst potentials.
  • Neglecting activity coefficients in concentrated electrolyte solutions when exact potentials are required.

⚡ Quick Revision: Electrode Potential

Core formulas, rapid recall points, and distinguishing criteria at a glance.

Electrode Potential Fundamentals

Electrode potential is the potential difference between a metal and its solution of ions. Standard reduction potential (SRP) is the universal IUPAC standard.

The Nernst Equation

Relates actual cell or electrode potential to standard potential, temperature, and reaction quotient Q.

Electrochemical Series

Arrangement of elements in order of increasing standard reduction potentials, predicting spontaneity and displacement reactions.

Thermodynamic Functions

Establishes quantitative links between cell potential, free energy change, and equilibrium constants.

📚 Terms & Units Glossary

Standard Reduction Potential (E°)
Potential of a half-cell under standard conditions (1 M, 1 bar, 298 K) for reduction.
Standard Hydrogen Electrode (SHE)
Primary reference electrode with an assigned potential of 0.00 V.
Faraday Constant (F)
Charge carried by one mole of electrons, approximately 96485 C mol-1.
Nernst Equation (E = E° - ...)
Mathematical relation between electrode potential and ionic concentration.
Cell Potential (Ecell)
Difference in potential between cathode and anode in volts.
Concentration Cell (E°cell = 0)
Cell where electrodes are identical but concentrations differ.

🎯 Key Takeaways

  • SHE potential is 0.00 V at all temperatures.
  • F = 96485 C mol-1.
  • E°cell = E°red(cathode) - E°red(anode).
  • ΔG° = -nFE°cell.
  • At chemical equilibrium, Ecell = 0.

📌 Quick Recall

  • Higher SRP indicates greater tendency to get reduced.
  • Metals with negative SRP displace hydrogen from acids.
  • Intensive property E° does not depend on stoichiometric coefficients.
  • Spontaneous reactions have positive E°cell.

⚖️ Key Differences

Oxidation Potential VS Reduction Potential

Oxidation potential is the exact negative of reduction potential for the same half-reaction.

Question 1 of 18

What is the standard reduction potential of the Standard Hydrogen Electrode?

Explanation: By international convention, the standard hydrogen electrode is assigned an arbitrary potential of 0.00 V at all temperatures.

Question 2 of 18

Which of the following represents the correct Nernst equation for a metal ion reduction Mn+ + ne- → M?

Explanation: The reduced solid metal has unit activity, leading to E = E° - (RT/nF) ln(1/[Mn+]) which is equivalent to E = E° + (RT/nF) ln([Mn+]).

Question 3 of 18

If the standard cell potential is positive, what can be deduced about the cell reaction?

Explanation: A positive E°cell results in a negative ΔG°, signifying a spontaneous electrochemical reaction.

Question 4 of 18

Which of the following is an intensive property?

Explanation: Electrode potential and standard cell potential depend on the nature of the reaction and concentration, not on the total quantity of matter, making them intensive properties.

Question 5 of 18

What is the relationship between standard free energy change and standard cell potential?

Explanation: The thermodynamic equation linking free energy change and cell potential is ΔG° = -nFE°cell.

Question 6 of 18

Which metal has the highest standard reduction potential in aqueous solution?

Explanation: Fluorine has the highest standard reduction potential (+2.87 V) among all elements, making it the strongest oxidizing agent.

Question 7 of 18

In a galvanic cell, oxidation occurs at which electrode?

Explanation: By universal convention, oxidation always occurs at the anode in any electrochemical cell.

Question 8 of 18

What happens to the reduction potential of a half-cell if the concentration of the metal ion is increased?

Explanation: According to the Nernst equation, increasing the reactant concentration (metal ions) increases the reduction potential.

Question 9 of 18

Which of the following ions will be reduced most easily if all have equal concentration?

Explanation: The species with the highest standard reduction potential (+0.80 V for Ag+) is reduced most easily.

Question 10 of 18

What is the unit of electrode potential?

Explanation: Electrode potential is measured in Volts (V), representing potential energy per unit charge.

Question 11 of 18

Which of the following can liberate hydrogen gas when reacted with dilute HCl?

Explanation: Metals with negative standard reduction potentials relative to hydrogen (such as zinc, E° = -0.76 V) can liberate hydrogen gas from acids.

Question 12 of 18

What is the value of n in the Nernst equation for the reaction: Cu2+ + 2e- → Cu?

Explanation: Two electrons are transferred in the reduction of Cu2+ to Cu, so n = 2.

Question 13 of 18

At chemical equilibrium in a galvanic cell, what is the value of Ecell?

Explanation: At equilibrium, the net cell potential becomes zero because the forward and reverse reaction rates equalize.

Question 14 of 18

Which salt is commonly used in a salt bridge due to similar transport numbers of its ions?

Explanation: Potassium chloride (KCl) is widely used in salt bridges because K+ and Cl- have nearly identical ionic mobilities.

Question 15 of 18

If E°cell for a reaction is negative, the equilibrium constant K for the reaction is:

Explanation: Since E° = (0.0591/n) log K, a negative E°cell implies log K is negative, meaning K < 1.

Question 16 of 18

The tendency of an electrode to lose electrons is called:

Explanation: Loss of electrons is oxidation, so the corresponding potential is the oxidation potential.

Question 17 of 18

What is the role of platinum foil in the standard hydrogen electrode?

Explanation: Platinized platinum is chemically inert and provides a catalytic surface for the redox equilibrium of hydrogen gas and hydrogen ions.

Question 18 of 18

How does temperature affect the standard electrode potential?

Explanation: The temperature dependence of electrode potential is governed by the temperature coefficient, which relates to entropy change via ΔS = nF(∂E/∂T).

UP PGT 2013
Which of the following expressions correctly relates the equilibrium constant K and standard cell potential E° at 298 K?
Answer: Option D (Both A and C) Both log K = (n E°) / 0.0591 and K = exp(nFE° / RT) are valid mathematical expressions derived from ΔG° = -nFE° and ΔG° = -RT ln K.

Model Question
If the concentration of Ag+ ions in the half-cell Ag+/Ag is decreased by a factor of 10, what will be the change in the electrode potential at 298 K?
Answer: Option A (Decreases by 0.0591 V) According to E = E° - (0.0591/1) log(1/[Ag+]), decreasing [Ag+] by a factor of 10 decreases the reduction potential by 0.0591 V.

Question 1 of 10
Calculate the standard cell potential for a cell constructed from nickel and silver half-cells, given E°(Ni2+/Ni) = -0.25 V and E°(Ag+/Ag) = +0.80 V.

Solution: E°cell = E°cathode - E°anode = +0.80 V - (-0.25 V) = +1.05 V.

Question 2 of 10
Which species acts as an oxidizing agent in the Daniell cell?

Solution: Cu2+ gets reduced to Cu by gaining electrons, meaning it acts as the oxidizing agent.

Question 3 of 10
What is the effect of doubling the stoichiometric coefficients of a half-cell reaction on its standard reduction potential E°?

Solution: Electrode potential is an intensive property and does not depend on the stoichiometric coefficients of the balanced half-reaction.

Question 4 of 10
For the redox reaction involving 2 moles of electrons, ΔG° was found to be -212 kJ mol-1. What is the standard cell potential?

Solution: E° = -ΔG° / (nF) = -(-212000 J) / (2 × 96485 C) ≈ 1.10 V.

Question 5 of 10
Under which conditions does the Nernst equation simplify to E = E°?

Solution: When Q = 1 (all ionic concentrations equal to unity), log(1) = 0, so E = E°.

Question 6 of 10
Which of the following metals cannot displace copper from copper sulfate solution?

Solution: Silver has a higher reduction potential (+0.80 V) than copper (+0.34 V), so it cannot displace copper.

Question 7 of 10
The standard reduction potential of Fe3+/Fe2+ is +0.77 V. This implies that:

Solution: A positive standard reduction potential of +0.77 V indicates that Fe3+ readily accepts an electron to become Fe2+, acting as an oxidizing agent.

Question 8 of 10
What is the equilibrium constant for a cell where E°cell = 0.00 V at 298 K?

Solution: If E° = 0, then log K = 0, which means K = 1.

Question 9 of 10
Which electrode reaction occurs at the cathode during the electrolysis of aqueous NaCl using inert electrodes?

Solution: Due to the more positive reduction potential of water compared to Na+ under aqueous conditions, water gets reduced at the cathode.

Question 10 of 10
What is the unit of the Faraday constant F?

Solution: Faraday constant represents the charge per mole of electrons, expressed in Coulombs per mole (C mol-1).
