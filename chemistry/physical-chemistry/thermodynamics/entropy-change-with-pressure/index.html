Entropy Change with Pressure

Entropy change with pressure defines the variation of system entropy as a function of external or internal pressure at constant temperature. For an ideal gas, isothermal compression or expansion relates entropy directly to the natural logarithm of the pressure ratio.

UP PGT Chemistry Subject Code 02 B.Sc. / M.Sc. Level

Thermodynamic Derivation for Ideal Gases

  • Combining the first and second laws gives dS = (dqrev) / T = (dU + PdV) / T for a closed system.
  • Substituting dU = n Cv dT and P = nRT / V yields dS = n Cv (dT / T) + nR (dV / V).
  • Using the ideal gas law in differential form, dV / V = -dP / P + dT / T, replaces volume with pressure terms.
  • Integrating at constant temperature (dT = 0) yields the isothermal pressure-entropy relation ΔS = -nR ln(P2 / P1).

Physical Interpretation of Pressure Effects

  • Increasing the pressure of a gas at constant temperature restricts its spatial volume and decreases available microstates.
  • A decrease in spatial volume corresponds to a negative entropy change during isothermal compression (P2 > P1 ⇒ ΔS < 0).
  • Conversely, isothermal expansion lowers the pressure, increases the volume, and results in a positive entropy change (ΔS > 0).
  • In a vacuum expansion, work is zero, temperature remains constant for an ideal gas, and entropy strictly increases due to greater volume dispersion.

Maxwell Relations and Condensed Phases

  • The Maxwell relation (∂S / ∂P)T = -(∂V / ∂T)P directly connects entropy variation with pressure to thermal expansion.
  • Since the coefficient of thermal expansion α = (1/V)(∂V / ∂T)P is generally positive, entropy decreases with increasing pressure.
  • For liquids and solids, compressibility and thermal expansion are exceptionally small, making ΔS across moderate pressure changes near-zero.
  • High-pressure geochemistry and deep-Earth thermodynamics account for small condensed-phase volume changes using molar volume and expansivity.

Units: ΔS in J K-1, P in atm or Pa, R = 8.314 J K-1 mol-1
⚠️ Important Exceptions & Anomalies
Exception Entropy decreases when pressure increases isothermally for all standard fluids.

Observation: Water between 0°C and 4°C at atmospheric pressure. — Liquid water has a negative coefficient of thermal expansion (α < 0) below 4°C, meaning (∂S / ∂P)T becomes positive in this narrow regime.

Exception Condensed phases show negligible entropy change with pressure.

Observation: Extreme high-pressure regimes (gigapascals) in geology and materials science. — At ultra-high pressures, phase transitions (polymorphic shifts) cause abrupt volume collapses, leading to significant local entropy changes.

Exception Ideal gas entropy depends logarithmically on pressure changes.

Observation: Real gases at high pressures and low temperatures. — Intermolecular forces and compressibility factors (Z ≠ 1) require replacing pressure with fugacity in the logarithmic expression.

🎯 Key Exam Points
  • The isothermal entropy change of an ideal gas depends solely on the pressure ratio, not on the absolute pressure values.
  • For an isothermal compression of an ideal gas from 1 atm to 10 atm, the entropy change is negative: ΔS = -nR ln(10).
  • The Maxwell relation linking pressure and entropy is (∂S / ∂P)T = -(∂V / ∂T)P.
  • The entropy of a real gas at high pressure is evaluated using fugacity (f) in place of pressure (P).
  • For solids and liquids, the pressure coefficient of entropy is directly proportional to the molar volume and thermal expansion coefficient.
  • In a reversible adiabatic process, pressure changes occur with zero entropy change (isntropic process).
🚫 Common Errors & Confusions
  • Confusing the sign of the pressure log term: remember that ΔS = nR ln(P1 / P2), so higher final pressure gives negative ΔS.
  • Applying the ideal gas formula ΔS = -nR ln(P2/P1) to non-isothermal processes without including the temperature-dependent heat capacity term.
  • Assuming solids and liquids have exactly zero entropy change under all pressure variations regardless of magnitude.
  • Neglecting the conversion factor (2.303) when calculating entropy using common logarithms (log10) instead of natural logarithms (ln).

⚡ Quick Revision: Entropy Change with Pressure

Core formulas, rapid recall points, and distinguishing criteria at a glance.

Mathematical Formulation

The entropy change for an ideal gas undergoing isothermal pressure change is derived from fundamental thermodynamic identities as ΔS = nR ln(P1 / P2).

Microscopic Viewpoint

Pressure constriction reduces the volume accessible to gas molecules, lowering translational microstates and decreasing macroscopic entropy.

Maxwell Relations

Cross-differentiation of Gibbs and Helmholtz free energies yields Maxwell relations that quantify how entropy responds to mechanical pressure variations.

Real Gases and Condensed Phases

Deviations from ideality require fugacity corrections, while condensed phases exhibit minimal entropy shifts due to low compressibility and small expansivity.

📚 Terms & Units Glossary

Isothermal Entropy Change (ΔST)
Entropy variation at constant temperature driven by pressure or volume shifts, measured in J K-1.
Thermal Expansion Coefficient (α = (1/V)(∂V / ∂T)P)
Fractional change in volume per unit change in temperature at constant pressure, measured in K-1.
Fugacity (f)
Effective pressure corrected for non-ideal gas intermolecular interactions, measured in units of pressure.
Maxwell Relation ((∂S / ∂P)T = -(∂V / ∂T)P)
Fundamental differential identity linking entropy, pressure, volume, and temperature.
Reversible Process (dqrev / T)
A quasistatic process operating along a continuous succession of equilibrium states.
Isentropic Process (dS = 0)
A constant entropy process, typical of reversible adiabatic expansions or compressions.

🎯 Key Takeaways

  • ΔS = nR ln(P1 / P2) for isothermal ideal gas expansion/compression.
  • (∂S / ∂P)T = -(∂V / ∂T)P is the core Maxwell relation for pressure-entropy dependence.
  • Isothermal compression of a gas always results in a negative entropy change (ΔS < 0).
  • For condensed phases, ΔS is proportional to negative volume, expansion coefficient, and pressure change.
  • Real gases substitute fugacity (f) for pressure (P) at high densities.

📌 Quick Recall

  • Pressure directly alters the spatial distribution and microstate availability of gas molecules.
  • Isothermal expansion increases entropy, whereas isothermal compression decreases entropy.
  • Maxwell relations provide rigorous mathematical linkage between thermal and mechanical variables.
  • Condensed phases show negligible entropy variation with pressure under normal conditions.

⚖️ Key Differences

Isothermal vs Adiabatic Pressure Change VS Entropy Behavior

Isothermal pressure changes alter entropy (ΔS ≠ 0), whereas reversible adiabatic pressure changes maintain constant entropy (ΔS = 0).

Question 2 of 20

Which of the following Maxwell relations correctly expresses the variation of entropy with pressure at constant temperature?

Explanation: From the Maxwell relation derived from the Gibbs free energy differential (dG = VdP - SdT), cross differentiation yields (∂S / ∂P)T = -(∂V / ∂T)P.

Question 3 of 20

What happens to the entropy of an ideal gas during isothermal expansion?

Explanation: During isothermal expansion, the volume increases and pressure decreases, leading to a greater number of available microstates and a positive entropy change (ΔS > 0).

Question 4 of 20

For a condensed phase (solid or liquid), the entropy change with pressure at constant temperature is:

Explanation: Condensed phases have very small molar volumes and low thermal expansion coefficients, making their entropy change with moderate pressure changes practically negligible.

Question 5 of 20

In an isothermal compression of an ideal gas, the sign of ΔS is:

Explanation: Compression decreases volume and increases pressure, reducing spatial randomness and resulting in a negative entropy change (ΔS < 0).

Question 6 of 20

How does the entropy change scale with the number of moles (n) for an ideal gas undergoing isothermal pressure change?

Explanation: The formula ΔS = nR ln(P1 / P2) shows that entropy change is directly proportional to the number of moles (n).

Question 7 of 20

Which property replaces pressure in the entropy equation when dealing with real gases at very high pressures?

Explanation: Real gases depart from ideal behavior at high pressures; thermodynamic expressions substitute fugacity (f) for pressure (P) to account for intermolecular forces.

Question 8 of 20

What is the entropy change for 1 mole of an ideal gas if its pressure is doubled isothermally?

Explanation: ΔS = 1 mol × 8.314 J K-1 mol-1 × ln(1 / 2) = 8.314 × (-0.693) = -5.76 J K-1.

Question 9 of 20

Which thermodynamic potential's natural variables are temperature and pressure?

Explanation: The Gibbs free energy has natural variables of temperature and pressure (dG = VdP - SdT), making it the primary potential for deriving pressure-entropy relations.

Question 10 of 20

If a gas expands isothermally and reversibly, what is the entropy change of the surroundings?

Explanation: For any reversible isothermal process, the total entropy change of the universe is zero, meaning ΔSsurr = -ΔSsys.

Question 11 of 20

What is the value of (∂S / ∂P)T for an ideal gas?

Explanation: From ΔS = -nR ln(P2 / P1), differentiating with respect to pressure gives (∂S / ∂P)T = -nR / P (since V/T = nR/P).

Question 12 of 20

Under what condition is the entropy change of a system equal to zero during a pressure change?

Explanation: A reversible adiabatic process involves no heat exchange (dqrev = 0), yielding zero entropy change (dS = 0).

Question 13 of 20

Which of the following units is correct for molar entropy change (ΔSm)?

Explanation: Molar entropy is expressed in Joules per Kelvin per mole (J K-1 mol-1).

Question 14 of 20

Why does liquid water between 0°C and 4°C exhibit an anomalous positive (∂S / ∂P)T?

Explanation: Water has a negative coefficient of thermal expansion (α < 0) between 0°C and 4°C; since (∂S / ∂P)T = -Vα, a negative α results in a positive value.

Question 15 of 20

What is the entropy change when the pressure of an ideal gas is halved isothermally?

Explanation: ΔS = nR ln(P1 / P2). If P2 = P1 / 2, then P1 / P2 = 2, giving +nR ln 2.

Question 16 of 20

Which law of thermodynamics establishes the absolute scale of entropy used in pressure dependence calculations?

Explanation: The Third Law of Thermodynamics establishes that the entropy of a pure crystalline substance is zero at absolute zero (0 K), providing a baseline for absolute entropy values.

Question 17 of 20

In terms of molecular chaos, why does decreasing pressure increase entropy?

Explanation: Lowering pressure expands the volume, which increases the positional probability distribution and randomness of gas molecules.

Question 18 of 20

What is the dimensional formula of entropy (S)?

Explanation: Entropy has units of Energy / Temperature = (Force × Distance) / Temperature = ([M L T-2] [L]) / [K] = [M L2 T-2 K-1].

Question 19 of 20

Calculate ΔS when 1 mole of an ideal gas expands isothermally to triple its initial volume (which corresponds to 1/3 of its initial pressure).

Explanation: ΔS = nR ln(V2 / V1) = 1 × 8.314 × ln(3) = 8.314 × 1.0986 = +9.13 J K-1.

Question 20 of 20

Which relation correctly links entropy change to volume change at constant temperature for an ideal gas?

Explanation: Since P1 / P2 = V2 / V1 for isothermal ideal gas changes, ΔS = nR ln(V2 / V1).

Question 1 of 10
Calculate the entropy change for 3 moles of an ideal gas undergoing isothermal expansion from 2 atm to 0.5 atm at 300 K.

Solution: ΔS = nR ln(P1 / P2) = 3 × 8.314 × ln(2 / 0.5) = 3 × 8.314 × ln(4) = 3 × 8.314 × 1.3863 = +34.57 J K-1.

Question 2 of 10
Which of the following statements is true for the pressure dependence of entropy in condensed phases?

Solution: Liquids and solids have very small molar volumes and thermal expansivities, making (∂S / ∂P)T extremely small.

Question 3 of 10
What is the differential entropy change (dS) for an ideal gas when both temperature and pressure change simultaneously?

Solution: In terms of temperature and pressure, dS = n Cp,m(dT/T) - nR(dP/P) for one mole of an ideal gas.

Question 4 of 10
If the pressure of an ideal gas is increased by a factor of e (Euler's number) at constant temperature, what is the molar entropy change?

Solution: ΔS = R ln(P1 / P2) = R ln(1 / e) = R ln(e-1) = -R.

Question 5 of 10
Which thermodynamic identity is the starting point for deriving the pressure coefficient of entropy?

Solution: dG = VdP - SdT is the fundamental equation for Gibbs free energy, whose cross-derivative yields (∂S / ∂P)T = -(∂V / ∂T)P.

Question 6 of 10
In an isothermal process, if pressure decreases from 5 atm to 1 atm for 2 moles of an ideal gas, the entropy change is:

Solution: Decrease in pressure means expansion, leading to an increase in volume and a positive entropy change (ΔS > 0).

Question 7 of 10
What is the physical meaning of the Maxwell relation (∂S / ∂P)T = -(∂V / ∂T)P?

Solution: The relation states that the rate of change of entropy with pressure at constant temperature equals the negative rate of change of volume with temperature at constant pressure.

Question 8 of 10
For a van der Waals gas, how does the isothermal entropy change with pressure compare to an ideal gas?

Solution: Real gases governed by the van der Waals equation require corrections to account for molecular interactions (parameter a) and excluded volume (parameter b).

Question 9 of 10
What is the entropy change when 0.5 moles of an ideal gas undergo isothermal compression, reducing volume from 10 L to 2 L?

Solution: ΔS = nR ln(V2 / V1) = 0.5 × 8.314 × ln(2 / 10) = 0.5 × 8.314 × (-1.6094) = -6.69 J K-1.

Question 10 of 10
Which condition ensures that the entropy of a system remains constant during a pressure variation?

Solution: A reversible adiabatic process is isentropic (dS = 0), meaning entropy does not change even when pressure and temperature vary.
