Variation of Chemical Potential with Temperature

Chemical potential represents the partial molar Gibbs free energy of a component in a system. Its variation with temperature is governed by the negative partial molar entropy of that component.

UP PGT Chemistry Subject Code 02 B.Sc. / M.Sc. Level

Fundamental Temperature Derivative

  • The first derivative of chemical potential (μi) with respect to temperature at constant pressure is equal to negative partial molar entropy (-S¯i).
  • Since the absolute entropy of any substance is always positive (third law of thermodynamics), the chemical potential of a substance always decreases with an increase in temperature.
  • The slope of the μ vs T plot is negative for all substances across all phases under standard conditions.

Gibbs-Helmholtz Formulation

  • The temperature dependence of μi / T is directly proportional to the partial molar enthalpy (H¯i) divided by T2.
  • This relation mirrors the standard Gibbs-Helmholtz equation used for macroscopic Gibbs free energy changes in closed systems.
  • It allows the integration of chemical potential across varying temperature ranges when partial molar enthalpy is assumed constant.

Phase Transitions and Equilibrium

  • At a phase transition temperature (e.g., melting or boiling point), the chemical potentials of the two coexisting phases are equal (μalpha = μbeta).
  • The slope of the phase boundary in a P-T phase diagram is given by the Clapeyron equation, incorporating the entropy and volume changes of transition.
  • An inflection or abrupt change in the slope of the μ vs T curve indicates a phase transition.

Units: G in J, ni in mol
Temperature Dependence of Chemical Potential
(∂μi / ∂T)P,n = -S¯i
Conditions: Constant pressure and composition
Units: μi in J mol-1, T in K, S¯i in J K-1 mol-1
⚠️ Important Exceptions & Anomalies
Exception Chemical potential universally decreases with increasing temperature

Observation: Cryogenic systems near absolute zero (T → 0 K) — By the Nernst Heat Theorem (Third Law of Thermodynamics), entropy approaches zero as temperature approaches zero, making the temperature derivative of chemical potential vanish (∂μ/∂T → 0).

Exception Partial molar quantities remain independent of composition in pure states

Observation: Electrolyte solutions at extreme dilutions — Long-range Coulombic interactions cause deviations governed by Debye-Hückel limiting law, making chemical potential highly sensitive to ionic strength.

💡 Shortcuts & Memory Aids
Slope Rule for μ vs T
Slope = -S¯

Application: Remember that entropy is always positive, so the chemical potential curve always slopes downward from left to right on a μ versus T graph.

Phase Stability Mnemonic
Lowest μ Wins

Application: At any given temperature and pressure, the phase with the lowest chemical potential is the thermodynamically stable phase.

Gibbs-Helmholtz Sign Flip
Δ(μ/T) / ΔT = -H/T2

Application: Use this form when integrating between two temperatures T1 and T2 to eliminate entropy terms.

🎯 Key Exam Points
  • The partial molar Gibbs free energy is synonymous with chemical potential (μ).
  • The derivative (∂μi / ∂T)P equals -S¯i.
  • Chemical potential decreases with an increase in temperature because partial molar entropy is positive.
  • At phase equilibrium, chemical potentials of the phases in contact are identical.
  • The slope of chemical potential versus temperature is steepest for gases due to high molar entropy.
  • The Gibbs-Helmholtz relation connects μ/T to the partial molar enthalpy H¯.
🚫 Common Errors & Confusions
  • Assuming chemical potential increases with temperature because free energy terms often do; remember the negative sign in (∂μ/∂T) = -S.
  • Confusing partial molar entropy with standard molar entropy in multicomponent mixtures.
  • Applying ideal gas chemical potential formulas directly to condensed phases without activity corrections.
  • Neglecting the pressure term when evaluating total temperature derivatives in open systems.

⚡ Quick Revision: Variation of Chemical Potential with Temperature

Core formulas, rapid recall points, and distinguishing criteria at a glance.

Definition and Physical Significance

Chemical potential measures the escaping tendency of a component. Its temperature dependence is dictated entirely by the partial molar entropy.

Mathematical Derivation

Starting from dG = -SdT + VdP + ∑μidni, cross-differentiation yields the temperature coefficient of chemical potential.

Phase Equilibrium Application

Phase transitions occur where chemical potential curves of different phases intersect as temperature changes.

Effect of Enthalpy

The Gibbs-Helmholtz reformulation allows calculation of chemical potential at different temperatures using enthalpy data.

🎯 Key Takeaways

  • (∂μi / ∂T)P = -S¯i
  • (∂(μ/T) / ∂T) = -H¯ / T2
  • Chemical potential always decreases with rising temperature.
  • At equilibrium between phases, μ1 = μ2.
  • Gas phase chemical potential has the steepest negative temperature slope.

📌 Quick Recall

  • Temperature variation of chemical potential is governed by entropy.
  • Negative sign in derivative equation reflects thermodynamic stability criteria.
  • Phase boundaries are defined by intersections of chemical potential curves.
  • Useful in deriving Clausius-Clapeyron and boiling point elevation relations.

⚖️ Key Differences

Total Gibbs Free Energy (G) VS Chemical Potential (μ)

G is an extensive property of the total system, whereas μ is an intensive partial molar property.

For an ideal gas, how does chemical potential depend on temperature explicitly through its standard term?

Explanation: Processes spontaneously occur from regions of higher chemical potential to lower chemical potential until equilibrium (μ1 = μ2) is reached.

Question 1 of 10
Calculate the change in chemical potential when 1 mole of a substance with partial molar entropy 40 J K-1 mol-1 is heated from 300 K to 310 K at constant pressure.

Solution: dμ = -S¯ dT = -40 × (310 - 300) = -40 × 10 = -400 J mol-1.

Question 2 of 10
The slope of the chemical potential versus temperature curve for a gas is generally ________ than that for a solid.

Solution: Gases have much higher molar entropy than solids, resulting in a steeper negative slope (-S¯).

Question 3 of 10
What is the value of (∂μ / ∂T) as T → 0 K for any pure crystalline substance?

Solution: By the Third Law of Thermodynamics, S¯ → 0 as T → 0 K, so (∂μ / ∂T) = -S¯ → 0.

Question 4 of 10
For a multicomponent ideal solution, the temperature dependence of the chemical potential of component i includes:

Solution: Since μi = μi° + RT ln xi, differentiating with respect to T gives contributions from both μ° and mole fraction if composition varies with temperature.

Question 5 of 10
If the partial molar enthalpy of a component is 20 kJ mol-1, what is the value of (∂(μ/T) / ∂T)P?

Solution: Using Gibbs-Helmholtz, (∂(μ/T) / ∂T) = -H¯ / T2 = -20000 / T2 J K-1 mol-1.

Question 6 of 10
At temperatures below the freezing point of water, the chemical potential of ice is ________ than that of liquid water.

Solution: Ice is the stable phase below 0°C, meaning its chemical potential is lower than that of liquid water.

Question 7 of 10
The partial molar Gibbs free energy of a substance in a mixture is another name for:

Solution: By definition, chemical potential is the partial molar Gibbs free energy.

Question 8 of 10
In a phase diagram of a pure substance, triple point is a point where:

Solution: At the triple point, solid, liquid, and gas phases coexist in equilibrium, so μsolid = μliquid = μgas.

Question 9 of 10
How does an increase in temperature affect the escaping tendency of molecules from the condensed phase into the vapor phase?

Solution: Chemical potential decreases with temperature, lowering the stability of the condensed phase and increasing the vapor pressure (escaping tendency).

Question 10 of 10
Which Maxwell relation directly yields the temperature dependence of chemical potential?

Solution: Differentiating the total differential dG = -SdT + VdP with respect to T at constant P gives (∂G / ∂T)P = -S, which extends to partial molar quantities for chemical potential.
