Chemical Potential

Chemical potential is the partial molar Gibbs free energy representing the capacity of a substance to undergo a chemical reaction or spatial transport. It serves as the fundamental driving force for phase transitions, chemical equilibria, and diffusion processes in multicomponent systems.

UP PGT Chemistry Subject Code 02 B.Sc. / M.Sc. Level

Thermodynamic Definition and Partial Molar Quantities

  • Chemical potential μi is defined as the partial derivative of any extensive thermodynamic property (U, H, A, G) with respect to moles of component i under specified constant constraints.
  • For Gibbs free energy, μi = (∂G / ∂ni)T, P, nj, establishing it as the molar Gibbs energy for a pure substance.
  • Total Gibbs energy of a multicomponent homogeneous phase is expressed as G = ∑ niμi.
  • At equilibrium between two phases (α and β), the chemical potential of any component i must be identical in both phases: μi(α) = μi(β).

Dependence on Composition, Pressure, and Temperature

  • The chemical potential of an ideal gas component depends logarithmically on its partial pressure via μi = μi° + RT ln Pi.
  • In real gases, partial pressure is replaced by fugacity fi to maintain rigorous thermodynamic validity: μi = μi° + RT ln(fi / P°).
  • For real liquid solutions, activity ai replaces mole fraction xi such that μi = μi* + RT ln ai.
  • Since partial molar entropy Sm,i is positive, chemical potential invariably decreases with an increase in temperature at constant pressure.

Criteria for Phase and Chemical Equilibrium

  • Spontaneous material transport occurs from regions of higher chemical potential to regions of lower chemical potential (Δμ < 0).
  • Chemical equilibrium in a generalized reaction ∑ νiAi = 0 is satisfied when ∑ νiμi = 0.
  • Combining the reaction chemical potential with ideal gas activities yields the standard van't Hoff reaction isotherm: ΔG = ΔG° + RT ln Q.
  • Phase equilibrium requires equality of chemical potentials across all coexisting phases, forming the theoretical basis of the Clapeyron and Clausius-Clapeyron equations.

⚠️ Important Exceptions & Anomalies
Exception Chemical potential increases with increasing concentration or mole fraction in a mixture.

Observation: Non-ideal solutions exhibiting extreme negative or positive deviations from Raoult's law. — Strong intermolecular solute-solvent associations or repulsions alter the activity coefficient γi such that activity ai diverges significantly from mole fraction xi.

Exception Chemical potential of a pure solid or liquid is independent of pressure in condensed phase approximations.

Observation: High-pressure systems (e.g., deep crustal geology or high-pressure synthesis). — Although molar volumes Vm of condensed phases are small, large pressure increments (ΔP) yield measurable changes via Δμ = VmΔP.

Exception Standard chemical potential μ° is a constant independent of concentration.

Observation: Choice of different standard state conventions (e.g., molal vs. molar scales, or different reference states for solutes). — The numerical value of μ° depends entirely on the chosen reference standard state (pure liquid, infinite dilution, or 1 mol kg-1).

🎯 Key Exam Points
  • Chemical potential is an intensive thermodynamic property with units of J mol-1.
  • At absolute zero (0 K), the chemical potential equals the partial molar internal energy or enthalpy depending on definition constraints.
  • The chemical potential of a pure substance under standard conditions is equal to its standard molar Gibbs free energy (μ° = Gm°).
  • Osmotic pressure equations are derived directly by setting the chemical potential of the solvent in pure phase equal to that in the solution phase.
  • Partial molar quantities like chemical potential obey the Gibbs-Duhem equation: ∑ ni dμi = 0.
  • In a binary mixture, if the chemical potential of component 1 increases with mole fraction, the chemical potential of component 2 must decrease according to the Gibbs-Duhem relation.
🚫 Common Errors & Confusions
  • Assuming chemical potential depends on the total system size; it is intensive and independent of total mass or volume.
  • Confusing standard chemical potential (μ°) with standard Gibbs free energy of reaction (ΔG°); μ° is for a single component, while ΔG° applies to a complete stoichiometric reaction.
  • Using concentrations (ci) directly instead of activities (ai) or fugacities (fi) when dealing with non-ideal systems.
  • Neglecting the temperature dependence of standard chemical potential when calculating equilibrium constants at varied temperatures.

⚡ Quick Revision: Chemical Potential

Core formulas, rapid recall points, and distinguishing criteria at a glance.

Partial Molar Property Formulation

Partial molar properties quantify the change in an extensive system property per mole of component added at constant T, P, and remaining composition. Chemical potential is specifically the partial molar Gibbs energy.

Chemical Potential and Equilibrium

Equilibrium across phases or chemical reactions requires that the chemical potential of every mobile component is uniform throughout all interacting phases (Δμ = 0).

Fugacity and Activity Corrections

Real gases and solutions deviate from idealized logarithmic laws. Replacing pressure and concentration with fugacity and activity restores thermodynamic rigor to chemical potential equations.

The Gibbs-Duhem Equation

The relation ∑ ni dμi = 0 dictates that chemical potentials of components in a mixture cannot change independently of one another.

📚 Terms & Units Glossary

Partial molar Gibbs free energy; J mol-1.
Fugacity (fi)
Effective pressure corrected for non-ideal gas behavior; Pa or atm.
Activity (ai)
Effective concentration in thermodynamic calculations; dimensionless.
Activity Coefficient (γi)
Ratio of activity to concentration (γ = a / x); dimensionless.
Standard State (μ°)
Defined reference state at 1 bar pressure and specified temperature.
Gibbs-Duhem Equation (∑ ni dμi = 0)
Relates compositional changes in chemical potentials within a phase.

🎯 Key Takeaways

  • μi = (∂G / ∂ni)T, P, nj
  • G = ∑ niμi
  • μi(real) = μi° + RT ln ai
  • ∑ ni dμi = 0 (Gibbs-Duhem relation)
  • At phase equilibrium: μi(α) = μi(β)

📌 Quick Recall

  • Chemical potential drives mass transport and chemical reactions.
  • It measures escaping tendency of a component from a phase.
  • Ideal systems use mole fractions or partial pressures; real systems require activities or fugacities.
  • All components in equilibrium share identical chemical potentials.

⚖️ Key Differences

Chemical Potential (μ) VS Gibbs Free Energy (G)

G is an extensive total property of the system, whereas μ is an intensive partial molar property.

Question 2 of 18

What are the SI units of chemical potential?

Explanation: Chemical potential is an energy per mole, giving units of J mol-1.

Question 3 of 18

For a pure substance, what does the chemical potential equal?

Explanation: For a pure substance, ni = n, so (∂G / ∂n) = Gm, the molar Gibbs free energy.

Question 4 of 18

What is the condition for phase equilibrium between phase α and phase β for component i?

Explanation: Phase equilibrium requires that the chemical potential of any mobile component is equal in all coexisting phases.

Question 5 of 18

How does chemical potential vary with temperature at constant pressure for a pure substance?

Explanation: Since G = H - TS, differentiating with respect to temperature at constant pressure yields the partial derivative equal to negative molar entropy (-Sm).

Question 6 of 18

How does chemical potential vary with pressure at constant temperature for a pure substance?

Explanation: The derivative of Gibbs energy with respect to pressure at constant temperature is volume, making (∂μ / ∂P)T equal to molar volume Vm.

Question 7 of 18

Which mathematical relation represents the Gibbs-Duhem equation for a binary mixture?

Explanation: The Gibbs-Duhem equation states ∑ ni dμi = 0, which for two components expands to n1 dμ1 + n2 dμ2 = 0.

Question 8 of 18

For an ideal gas component in a mixture, the chemical potential is expressed as:

Explanation: Ideal gas chemical potential depends logarithmically on its partial pressure Pi relative to the standard pressure.

Question 9 of 18

What is substituted for partial pressure in the chemical potential expression when dealing with real gases?

Explanation: Fugacity is thermodynamic effective pressure introduced to correct non-ideal gas behavior in chemical potential formulas.

Question 10 of 18

What is substituted for mole fraction in non-ideal liquid solutions?

Explanation: Activity is used in place of concentration/mole fraction for non-ideal solutions to account for solute-solvent interactions.

Question 11 of 18

If a chemical reaction is at equilibrium, what is the sum of stoichiometric coefficients multiplied by respective chemical potentials (∑ νiμi)?

Explanation: At chemical equilibrium, ∑ νiμi = 0, meaning the affinity of the reaction is zero.

Question 12 of 18

Spontaneous transfer of matter occurs from a region of:

Explanation: Chemical potential acts as the escaping tendency; matter naturally flows from high μ to low μ.

Question 13 of 18

What does the activity coefficient (γi) approach as a solution becomes infinitely dilute?

Explanation: At infinite dilution, solute molecules are isolated from each other, behaving ideally, so γi → 1.

Question 14 of 18

Which thermodynamic property is extensive?

Explanation: Total Gibbs free energy is extensive, whereas chemical potential and temperature are intensive properties.

Question 15 of 18

What is the relation between standard chemical potential and standard Gibbs free energy of formation (ΔfG°) for a pure element in its reference state?

Explanation: By convention, the standard Gibbs energy of formation of an element in its reference state is zero, making its standard chemical potential also zero relative to standard states.

Question 16 of 18

How does the chemical potential of a solvent change when a non-volatile solute is added at constant T and P?

Explanation: Addition of a solute lowers the mole fraction of the solvent, reducing its chemical potential (μsolvent = μ* + RT ln xsolvent).

Question 17 of 18

Which equation relates chemical potential to the equilibrium constant K of a reaction?

Explanation: The standard reaction Gibbs energy relates to the equilibrium constant through ΔG° = -RT ln K.

Question 18 of 18

Why is chemical potential considered a partial molar property?

Explanation: By definition, any property differentiated with respect to moles of one component at constant T, P, and other moles is a partial molar property.

Question 3 of 10
What condition must hold true for a chemical reaction to be at equilibrium in terms of chemical potentials?

Solution: At chemical equilibrium, the sum of stoichiometric coefficients times chemical potentials equals zero.

Question 4 of 10
Which law or equation restricts independent variations of chemical potentials in a mixture?

Solution: The Gibbs-Duhem equation links compositional changes of all chemical potentials within a phase.

Question 5 of 10
What happens to the chemical potential of a solvent when a solute is dissolved in it?

Solution: Since xsolvent < 1, ln(xsolvent) is negative, causing μsolvent to decrease relative to pure solvent.

Question 6 of 10
What is the physical significance of chemical potential?

Solution: Chemical potential dictates the thermodynamic driving force for matter to move or react.

Question 7 of 10
For a pure gas behaving ideally, if the pressure is doubled at constant temperature, how does its chemical potential change?

Solution: Δμ = RT ln(P2 / P1) = RT ln(2P / P) = RT ln 2.

Question 8 of 10
Which of the following expressions correctly defines fugacity coefficient (φ)?

Solution: The fugacity coefficient is the ratio of fugacity f to the actual pressure P.

Question 9 of 10
What is the value of chemical potential for a pure substance at its standard state (μ°)?

Solution: By definition, for a pure substance, standard chemical potential equals standard molar Gibbs free energy.

Question 10 of 10
