Limitations of the First Law of Thermodynamics

The first law of thermodynamics relies on the principle of conservation of energy but fails to explain the direction of spontaneous processes. It does not account for why heat cannot spontaneously flow from a colder body to a hotter body without external work.

UP PGT Chemistry Subject Code 02 B.Sc. / M.Sc. Level

Direction of Spontaneous Processes

  • The first law establishes energy conservation between heat and work but provides no criterion for process spontaneity.
  • It fails to explain why natural processes such as heat flow or gas diffusion proceed unidirectionally in time.
  • Energy changes alone (ΔU = 0 for cyclic processes) cannot predict whether a given chemical reaction will proceed forward.

Inability to Explain Heat Engine Efficiency

  • The first law treats heat and work as mutually convertible in equivalent quantities without imposing conversion limits.
  • It cannot account for why 100% conversion of heat into work is impossible in a cyclic process, a limitation resolved by the second law.
  • It fails to define the maximum theoretical efficiency limit set by Carnot's theorem for thermal engines.

Absence of Equilibrium Criteria

  • The law gives no metric to determine the state of chemical equilibrium in reversible reactions.
  • State functions like enthalpy and internal energy do not dictate whether a system has attained maximum stability or randomness.
  • Introduction of the second law and entropy (ΔS) is necessary to resolve the directional limitations of the first law.

⚠️ Important Exceptions & Anomalies
Exception Heat flows spontaneously from hot to cold bodies

Observation: Refrigeration and heat pump cycles — External electrical work is supplied to drive heat against the natural temperature gradient, complying with the second law.

Exception First law equates work and heat as exact energy forms

Observation: Path dependence of work (w) and heat (q) — Internal energy (ΔU) is a state function, whereas q and w are path functions dependent on the process specifics.

🎯 Key Exam Points
  • The first law of thermodynamics is simply the law of conservation of energy applied to thermodynamic systems.
  • Internal energy (ΔU) is a state function, whereas heat (q) and work (w) are path functions.
  • The first law cannot explain why water flows downward or why gases expand into a vacuum spontaneously.
  • Conversion of complete heat into work in a single reservoir is restricted by the second law, not the first.
  • Joule's mechanical equivalent of heat proves work and heat are quantitatively interconvertible.
  • Cyclic processes operating on ideal gases maintain ΔU = 0, leading directly to q = -w.
🚫 Common Errors & Confusions
  • Assuming that because energy is conserved (ΔU = 0), a chemical reaction or physical process must occur spontaneously.
  • Confusing path functions (q, w) with state functions (ΔU, ΔH) during cyclic integrations.
  • Believing that the first law restricts the direction of heat transfer between bodies at different temperatures.
  • Neglecting the sign conventions for work done by the system (-w) versus work done on the system (+w).

⚡ Quick Revision: Limitations of the First Law of Thermodynamics

Core formulas, rapid recall points, and distinguishing criteria at a glance.

Energy Conservation Principle

Energy can neither be created nor destroyed, only transformed from one form to another, expressed mathematically as ΔU = q + w.

The Directional Blind Spot

The primary limitation is its inability to specify whether a given physical or chemical process will occur spontaneously in a particular direction.

State vs Path Functions

Internal energy is independent of the path taken, whereas heat and work are strictly dependent on the specific pathway of the process.

Transition to Second Law

Limitations of the energy balance approach necessitate the introduction of entropy and the second law to evaluate feasibility and equilibrium.

📚 Terms & Units Glossary

Internal Energy (ΔU)
Total energy contained within a thermodynamic system in Joules.
Heat (q)
Energy transferred due to a temperature difference in Joules.
Work (w)
Energy transfer through organized macroscopic displacement in Joules.
State Function (Property)
A property whose value depends only on the current state, not the path.
Path Function (Process Variable)
A property dependent on the specific sequence of steps taken.
Entropy (S)
Measure of thermal energy unavailability per unit temperature in J K-1.

🎯 Key Takeaways

  • First law equation: ΔU = q + w
  • For an isolated system: ΔU = 0
  • For a cyclic process: ΔU = 0 and q = -w
  • The first law fails to indicate process spontaneity
  • State functions depend on initial and final states only

📌 Quick Recall

  • The first law is based on energy conservation.
  • It equates heat and work quantitatively.
  • It cannot determine the direction of spontaneous reactions.
  • It requires the second law to explain thermodynamic efficiency and equilibrium.

⚖️ Key Differences

First Law Limitations VS First Law Validity

The first law is always valid for energy conservation; its 'limitation' is its silence on process direction and spontaneity.

Question 1 of 19

What is the primary limitation of the first law of thermodynamics?

Explanation: The first law accounts for energy conservation but provides no criterion regarding the direction of spontaneous changes.

Question 2 of 19

For a cyclic process involving an ideal gas, what is the net change in internal energy (ΔU)?

Explanation: Since internal energy is a state function and the system returns to its initial state in a cycle, ΔU = 0.

Question 3 of 19

Which of the following is a path function?

Explanation: Work (w) and heat (q) depend on the path taken between states, making them path functions.

Question 4 of 19

The first law of thermodynamics is mathematically expressed as:

Explanation: The standard IUPAC formulation of the first law is ΔU = q + w.

Question 5 of 19

Why can heat not spontaneously flow from a cold body to a hot body according to thermodynamics?

Explanation: The first law allows energy transfer in either direction as long as energy is conserved, but the second law restricts spontaneous heat flow to hot-to-cold.

Question 6 of 19

Which thermodynamic property is a state function?

Explanation: Internal energy depends only on the state of the system, not how that state was reached.

Question 7 of 19

In an isolated system, the change in internal energy (ΔU) is always:

Explanation: An isolated system cannot exchange heat or work with its surroundings, so its internal energy remains constant (ΔU = 0).

Question 8 of 19

Joule's experiment established the equivalence between:

Explanation: Joule demonstrated that mechanical work can be converted into heat in fixed proportional amounts.

Question 9 of 19

Which law must be invoked to determine if a chemical reaction is feasible?

Explanation: The second law introduces entropy, which determines process feasibility and spontaneity.

Question 10 of 19

For a cyclic process where q = +500 J, what is the value of w?

Explanation: Since ΔU = 0 for a cyclic process, q + w = 0, which means w = -q = -500 J.

Question 11 of 19

State functions depend on:

Explanation: State functions are macroscopic properties determined solely by the current state of the system.

Question 12 of 19

Which of the following processes is a limitation of the first law regarding heat engines?

Explanation: The first law places no restriction on converting 100% of heat into work, which contradicts real-world heat engine behavior.

Question 13 of 19

If work is done on a system adiabatically, how does its internal energy change?

Explanation: For an adiabatic process (q = 0), ΔU = w. Work done on the system is positive (+w), so internal energy increases.

Question 14 of 19

Which property is NOT an extensive property?

Explanation: Temperature is an intensive property because it does not depend on the quantity of matter in the system.

Question 15 of 19

What does the symbol 'q' represent in thermodynamics?

Explanation: q represents heat transferred into or out of the system.

Question 16 of 19

Why does a gas expanding into a vacuum perform no work?

Explanation: Work is defined as w = -PextΔV. Since expansion occurs against a vacuum (Pext = 0), work done is zero.

Question 17 of 19

Which branch of thermodynamics deals with the rates of reactions?

Explanation: Reaction rates are studied under chemical kinetics, whereas thermodynamics deals with energy states and equilibrium.

Question 18 of 19

Can internal energy be measured directly in an absolute sense?

Explanation: Absolute internal energy cannot be experimentally measured; only changes between states can be quantified.

Question 19 of 19

What is the condition for a process to be adiabatic?

Explanation: An adiabatic process is defined as one where no heat is transferred between the system and surroundings (q = 0).

Question 5 of 10

Question 6 of 10
What is the net change in internal energy for an isothermal expansion of an ideal gas?

Solution: For an isothermal process, temperature is constant, and for an ideal gas, internal energy depends only on temperature (ΔU = 0).

Solution: Kelvin-Planck statement of the second law prohibits complete conversion of heat into work in a cyclic engine.

Solution: State functions are independent of history and depend solely on the current state.

Question 9 of 10
In the expansion of a gas against a constant external pressure, work is:

Solution: Work done by the system during expansion is negative by IUPAC convention (w = -PΔV).

Solution: Entropy was introduced as a state function to quantify process directionality and overcome first law limitations.
