Physical Chemistry

Enthalpy of formation

The standard enthalpy of formation (ΔHf°) is the enthalpy change accompanying the formation of one mole of a substance from its constituent elements in their standard states at 1 bar pressure and specified temperature. By IUPAC convention, the standard enthalpy of formation of any element in its most stable reference state is defined as exactly zero.

UP PGT Chemistry Subject Code 02 B.Sc. / M.Sc. Level

Standard States and Reference States

  • The standard state of an element or compound is its most stable form at a pressure of 1 bar and at a specified temperature (usually 298.15 K).
  • For carbon, graphite is the reference state with ΔH°f = 0 kJ mol-1, whereas diamond has a non-zero positive value.
  • For diatomic gases, H2(g), O2(g), and N2(g) represent the standard reference states at 1 bar.
  • For liquid elements, Hg(l) and Br2(l) are the standard reference states at 298.15 K.

Thermochemical Equations and Hess's Law

  • A thermochemical equation specifies the physical states of all reactants and products alongside the reaction enthalpy (ΔH).
  • Reversing a chemical equation inverts the sign of the enthalpy change (ΔH becomes -ΔH).
  • Multiplying a thermochemical equation by a stoichiometric factor scales the enthalpy change by the same factor.
  • Hess's Law of Constant Heat Summation dictates that the overall enthalpy change is independent of the reaction pathway, depending solely on initial and final states.

Applications and Calculation Protocols

  • Reaction enthalpies are calculated by subtracting the sum of standard formation enthalpies of reactants from those of products.
  • Negative values of ΔH°f indicate an exothermic formation process and thermodynamic stability relative to elements.
  • Positive values of ΔH°f indicate an endothermic formation process, often signifying thermodynamic instability towards decomposition into elements.
  • Combustion data combined with formation enthalpies allow determination of standard enthalpies of atomization and lattice energies via Born-Haber cycles.
📐 Key Formulas & Equations
Temperature Dependence (Kirchhoff's Law)
ΔH°(T2) = ΔH°(T1) + ∫T1T2 ΔCp dT
Conditions: Constant pressure, over moderate temperature ranges where ΔCp is constant
Units: J mol-1 K-1 for heat capacities
Integral Form of Kirchhoff's Law (Constant ΔCp)
ΔH°(T2) - ΔH°(T1) = ΔCp(T2 - T1)
Conditions: Assuming ΔCp is independent of temperature
Units: Joules or kJ
Bond Enthalpy Estimation of Enthalpy of Formation
ΔH°f ≈ ∑ BE(Reactant bonds broken) - ∑ BE(Product bonds formed)
Conditions: Gas phase species, average bond dissociation enthalpies
Units: kJ mol-1
Standard Enthalpy of Reaction from Enthalpies of Formation
ΔH°rxn = ∑ νp ΔH°f(Products) - ∑ νr ΔH°f(Reactants)
Conditions: Standard conditions (298.15 K, 1 bar)
Units: kJ mol-1
⚠️ Important Exceptions & Anomalies
Exception All elements in their standard states have zero enthalpy of formation.

Observation: Allotrope selections matter; diamond has ΔH°f = +1.9 kJ mol-1, not 0. — Graphite is the thermodynamically most stable allotrope of carbon at 1 bar and 298.15 K, making it the true reference state.

Exception Enthalpy of formation refers strictly to the formation of one mole of compound.

Observation: Fractional stoichiometric coefficients are permitted and standard in formation equations. — To maintain exactly one mole of product, reactant elements must often be written with fractional coefficients (e.g., H2(g) + 0.5O2(g) → H2O(l)).

💡 Shortcuts & Memory Aids
Products Minus Reactants Rule
ΔH = ∑ ΔHf(Prod) - ∑ ΔHf(Reac)

Application: Always list formation values vertically and subtract reactant sums from product sums to avoid sign errors.

Combustion to Formation Conversion
ΔHf = ∑ ΔHc(Elements) - ΔHc(Compound)

Application: When combustion data is provided instead of formation data, use the reverse Hess cycle formula.

Element Zero Check
ΔHf(element in standard state) = 0

Application: Instantly cross out any elemental reactant or product (like O2(g), Na(s)) in tabulated sums to save calculation time.

🎯 Key Exam Points
  • Standard enthalpy of formation of an element in its standard state is zero.
  • Units of standard enthalpy of formation are typically kJ mol-1.
  • Hess's law is a direct consequence of enthalpy being a state function.
  • Kirchhoff's law accounts for the variation of reaction enthalpy with temperature using heat capacity differences (ΔCp).
  • Diamond is not the reference state of carbon; graphite is.
  • A negative ΔH°f signifies that the compound is more stable than its constituent elements.
🚫 Common Errors & Confusions
  • Assuming oxygen gas in combustion problems has a non-zero formation enthalpy; O2(g) is an element in its standard state, so its ΔH°f = 0.
  • Forgetting to multiply the standard enthalpy of formation by the stoichiometric coefficient from the balanced equation.
  • Confusing the standard state of carbon as diamond instead of graphite.
  • Reversing the subtraction order in products minus reactants calculation (ΔHf(Reactants) - ΔHf(Products)).

⚡ Quick Revision: Enthalpy of formation

Core formulas, rapid recall points, and distinguishing criteria at a glance.

Definition and Reference States

Enthalpy of formation measures heat change for 1 mole of substance formation from elements in standard reference states where elemental enthalpies are set to zero.

Calculation via Hess's Law

ΔH°rxn is evaluated by subtracting reactant formation enthalpies from product formation enthalpies, leveraging state function properties.

Temperature Dependence

Kirchhoff's equation integrates heat capacity differences to calculate standard enthalpies at non-standard temperatures.

Stability Criteria

Large negative formation enthalpies reflect high thermodynamic stability toward decomposition into elements.

🎯 Key Takeaways

  • ΔH°f of all stable elemental forms = 0
  • ΔH°rxn = ∑ ΔH°f(Products) - ∑ ΔH°f(Reactants)
  • Graphite is the reference state of carbon, not diamond
  • Kirchhoff's equation: ΔH(T2) - ΔH(T1) = ΔCp(T2 - T1)
  • Enthalpy is an extensive property and a state function

📌 Quick Recall

  • Formation enthalpies gauge relative thermodynamic stability.
  • Stoichiometric coefficients must be factored into all sums.
  • State functions ensure path independence in thermochemical cycles.
  • Reference states are strictly defined by 1 bar and stable allotropes.

⚖️ Key Differences

Enthalpy of Formation VS Enthalpy of Combustion

Formation builds 1 mole of compound from elements; combustion burns 1 mole of substance in oxygen.

Question 1 of 20

What is the standard enthalpy of formation of liquid bromine, Br2(l), at 298.15 K?

Explanation: Bromine in its liquid state is the stable reference state of the element at 298.15 K and 1 bar, hence its standard enthalpy of formation is defined as zero.

Question 2 of 20

Which of the following represents a standard enthalpy of formation reaction?

Explanation: Formation of 1 mole of H_2O(l) from its constituent elements in their standard states (H_2(g) and O_2(g)) defines the standard enthalpy of formation of water.

Question 3 of 20

The standard enthalpy of formation of graphite is 0 kJ mol^-1. What is the standard enthalpy of formation of diamond?

Explanation: Graphite is the reference state of carbon (0 kJ mol^-1). Diamond is less stable and has a small positive standard enthalpy of formation (+1.9 kJ mol^-1).

Question 4 of 20

According to Kirchhoff's law, if ΔC_p for a reaction is zero, how does the enthalpy of reaction vary with temperature?

Explanation: Kirchhoff's law states that d(ΔH)/dT = ΔC_p. If ΔC_p = 0, ΔH does not change with temperature.

Question 5 of 20

For which of the following substances is ΔH_f° equal to zero at 298 K and 1 bar?

Explanation: Cl_2(g) is the stable reference state of chlorine at 298 K and 1 bar. I_2 is solid, H_2O is a compound, and white phosphorus is the standard state of phosphorus, not red.

Question 6 of 20

If the enthalpies of formation of A, B, and C are -50 kJ mol^-1, -100 kJ mol^-1, and -200 kJ mol^-1 respectively, which is the most stable?

Explanation: Substance C has the most negative enthalpy of formation (-200 kJ mol^-1), indicating it is the most stable relative to its constituent elements.

Question 7 of 20

What is the physical significance of a negative standard enthalpy of formation?

Explanation: A negative ΔH_f° means heat is released when the compound is formed from its elements, making it lower in energy and more stable than the separate elements.

Question 8 of 20

Hess's Law is a direct mathematical consequence of which thermodynamic property?

Explanation: Because enthalpy is a state function, its change depends only on the initial and final states, permitting summation of thermochemical equations.

Question 9 of 20

In calculating ΔH°_rxn using standard enthalpies of formation, why are reactant values subtracted from product values?

Explanation: Any thermodynamic property change (ΔX) is calculated as final state minus initial state. Products correspond to the final state and reactants to the initial state.

Question 10 of 20

Which of the following allotropes of sulfur is taken as the standard reference state with ΔH_f° = 0?

Explanation: Rhombic (or alpha) sulfur is the most stable allotrope of sulfur at 298.15 K and 1 bar, serving as its standard reference state.

Question 11 of 20

Calculate ΔH° for the reaction: CO(g) + 0.5O_2(g) → CO_2(g), given ΔH_f°(CO) = -110.5 kJ mol^-1 and ΔH_f°(CO_2) = -393.5 kJ mol^-1.

Explanation: ΔH° = ΔH_f°(CO_2) - [ΔH_f°(CO) + 0.5 ΔH_f°(O_2)] = -393.5 - (-110.5 + 0) = -283.0 kJ mol^-1.

Question 12 of 20

What is the standard state of phosphorus at 298.15 K?

Explanation: White phosphorus is accepted as the standard reference state for elemental phosphorus, despite being less thermodynamically stable than red or black phosphorus.

Question 13 of 20

Which of the following is true regarding standard enthalpies of formation?

Explanation: Enthalpies of formation establish a quantitative scale enabling comparison of relative thermodynamic stabilities of compounds.

Question 14 of 20

Why is fractional stoichiometry used in defining standard enthalpy of formation equations?

Explanation: By definition, standard enthalpy of formation corresponds to the formation of exactly one mole of a single substance, requiring fractional coefficients for reactants when necessary.

Question 15 of 20

If ΔH° for a reaction is negative, what happens to the equilibrium constant K when temperature is increased?

Explanation: According to the Van 't Hoff equation, for an exothermic reaction (ΔH < 0), increasing temperature decreases the equilibrium constant K.

Question 16 of 20

Which thermodynamic property is equal to enthalpy change at constant pressure?

Explanation: At constant pressure and with only pressure-volume work, the heat absorbed or released equals the enthalpy change (ΔH = q_p).

Question 17 of 20

What is the standard state of carbon for thermochemical measurements?

Explanation: Graphite is the standard reference state of carbon because it is the most thermodynamically stable allotrope at 1 bar and 298.15 K.

Question 18 of 20

Which of the following has a standard enthalpy of formation equal to zero?

Explanation: F_2(g) is an element in its standard state at 298 K and 1 bar, so its ΔH_f° = 0. Br_2 standard state is liquid, O_3 is an allotrope, and H_2O_2 is a compound.

Question 19 of 20

How does an increase in temperature affect the standard enthalpy of formation of a substance, assuming ΔC_p is positive?

Explanation: By Kirchhoff's law, if ΔC_p > 0, then ΔH increases with an increase in temperature.

Question 20 of 20

Which instrument is used experimentally to determine enthalpies of combustion and indirect formation?

Explanation: A bomb calorimeter is the standard experimental apparatus used to measure combustion energies at constant volume, which are then converted to enthalpies.

Question 1 of 10
What is the standard enthalpy of formation of an element in its reference state?

Solution: By international IUPAC convention, the standard enthalpy of formation of any element in its most stable reference state at 1 bar is exactly zero.

Question 2 of 10
Calculate the standard enthalpy of formation of liquid ethanol, C_2H_5OH(l), given that the standard enthalpies of combustion of C_2H_5OH(l), C(graphite), and H_2(g) are -1368, -393.5, and -285.8 kJ mol^-1 respectively.

Solution: 2C + 3H_2 + 0.5O_2 → C_2H_5OH. ΔH_f = 2(ΔH_c(C)) + 3(ΔH_c(H_2)) - ΔH_c(C_2H_5OH) = 2(-393.5) + 3(-285.8) - (-1368) = -787 - 857.4 + 1368 = -277.4 kJ mol^-1 (approx -277.7).

Question 3 of 10
Which of the following pairs has both species in their standard reference states at 298 K and 1 bar?

Solution: Graphite is the standard reference state for carbon, and liquid mercury Hg(l) is the standard reference state for mercury at 298 K and 1 bar.

Question 4 of 10
If ΔH_f° of H_2O(l) is -285.8 kJ mol^-1, what is the enthalpy change for the decomposition of 2 moles of liquid water into its elements?

Solution: Decomposition is the exact reverse of formation, changing the sign from negative to positive, and scaled by 2 moles: +285.8 × 2 = +571.6 kJ.

Question 5 of 10
Which law states that the enthalpy change of a reaction is independent of the path taken?

Solution: Hess's Law of Constant Heat Summation establishes that reaction enthalpy is path-independent because enthalpy is a state function.

Question 6 of 10
What is Δn_g for the formation reaction of 1 mole of ammonia: 0.5N_2(g) + 1.5H_2(g) → NH_3(g)?

Solution: Δn_g = (moles of gaseous product) - (moles of gaseous reactants) = 1 - (0.5 + 1.5) = 1 - 2 = -1.

Question 7 of 10
Why is the standard enthalpy of formation of diamond not zero?

Solution: The zero enthalpy reference state is assigned only to the most stable allotrope of an element under standard conditions, which for carbon is graphite.

Question 8 of 10
In Kirchhoff's equation, ΔC_p represents:

Solution: ΔC_p = ∑ C_p(Products) - ∑ C_p(Reactants) at constant pressure.

Question 9 of 10
The standard enthalpy of formation of a compound is generally determined experimentally by measuring its:

Solution: Enthalpies of formation are frequently determined indirectly via heats of combustion measured in a bomb calorimeter using Hess's Law.

Question 10 of 10
Which substance has a standard enthalpy of formation of zero?

Solution: Cl_2(g) is an element in its standard state, whereas H_2O, NaCl, and CO are chemical compounds with non-zero formation enthalpies.
