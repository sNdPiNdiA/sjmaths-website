Heat capacity at constant pressure

Heat capacity at constant pressure (Cp) is defined as the rate of change of enthalpy with respect to temperature at a constant pressure. It quantifies the amount of heat required to raise the temperature of a substance by one degree Celsius under isobaric conditions.

UP PGT Chemistry Subject Code 02 B.Sc. / M.Sc. Level

Thermodynamic Definition and Enthalpy Link

  • Cp is defined as the partial derivative of enthalpy H with respect to temperature T at constant pressure p.
  • At constant pressure, the heat absorbed by a system equals the change in enthalpy: qp = ΔH.
  • For any substance, Cp is an extensive property, while specific heat capacity (s) and molar heat capacity (Cm) are intensive properties.

Mayer Relation for Ideal Gases

  • For an ideal gas, the difference between molar heat capacities at constant pressure and constant volume is the universal gas constant: Cp,m - Cv,m = R.
  • For a monoatomic ideal gas, Cv,m = (3/2)R and Cp,m = (5/2)R, yielding a heat capacity ratio γ = 1.67.
  • For a diatomic ideal gas at room temperature, Cv,m = (5/2)R and Cp,m = (7/2)R, yielding γ = 1.40.

Temperature Dependence and Measurement

  • According to Kirchhoff's equation, the variation of reaction enthalpy with temperature is given by ΔCp = (∂ΔH / ∂T)p.
  • At very low temperatures near absolute zero, both Cp and Cv approach zero in accordance with the Third Law of Thermodynamics.
  • For solids, Cp increases with temperature and can be represented empirically by the power series Cp = a + bT + cT-2.

⚠️ Important Exceptions & Anomalies
Exception Cp - Cv = R for ideal gases

Observation: Real gases deviate significantly at high pressures and low temperatures — Intermolecular forces and the finite volume of gas molecules introduce additional potential energy terms dependent on volume and pressure.

Exception Cp is always greater than Cv

Observation: Liquid water between 0°C and 4°C, and certain condensed matter under extreme conditions — Anomalous thermal expansion coefficient (α) where volume decreases with increasing temperature, making (∂V/∂T)p negative.

Exception Heat capacity is continuous with temperature

Observation: Phase transitions and lambda (λ) transitions — Discontinuities occur due to abrupt absorption or release of latent heat or cooperative phenomena like order-disorder transitions in alloys.

💡 Shortcuts & Memory Aids
Degree of Freedom Rule for Cv and Cp
Cv = (f/2)R and Cp = ((f+2)/2)R

Application: Use translational (3), rotational (2 for linear, 3 for non-linear), and vibrational degrees of freedom (f) to quickly find heat capacities.

Poisson's Ratio Shortcut
γ = 1 + (2/f)

Application: Directly calculate the adiabatic index γ from the total active degrees of freedom f without calculating individual Cp and Cv values.

Enthalpy Change Integration Trick
ΔH = Cp,avg ΔT

Application: When Cp varies linearly with temperature over a narrow range, use the arithmetic mean of Cp at initial and final temperatures.

🎯 Key Exam Points
  • Cp is always greater than Cv for both ideal and real gases under normal conditions.
  • For a monoatomic ideal gas, Cp = 20.78 J mol-1 K-1 (5/2 R).
  • The difference Cp - Cv represents the external work done by one mole of an ideal gas when heated by 1 Kelvin at constant pressure.
  • Kirchhoff's law states that the rate of change of reaction enthalpy with temperature equals ΔCp.
  • At absolute zero (T = 0 K), the heat capacity Cp of a pure crystalline solid approaches zero.
  • For diatomic gases like O2 or N2 at room temperature, Cp = 7/2 R.
🚫 Common Errors & Confusions
  • Assuming Cp - Cv = R holds true for liquids and solids; for condensed phases, Cp and Cv are approximately equal.
  • Confusing extensive Cp (J K-1) with molar heat capacity Cp,m (J mol-1 K-1).
  • Forgetting that Cp varies with temperature, treating it as a constant over large temperature intervals.
  • Applying ideal gas heat capacity formulas directly to gases undergoing strong intermolecular association like HF or water vapor at high density.

⚡ Quick Revision: Heat capacity at constant pressure

Core formulas, rapid recall points, and distinguishing criteria at a glance.

Definition and Physical Meaning of Cp

Cp measures the capacity of a system to store thermal energy as enthalpy under isobaric conditions. It is experimentally measured as the ratio of heat supplied to the resulting temperature rise at constant pressure.

Mayer's Relation Derivation

Combining the first law of thermodynamics with the definition of enthalpy yields Cp,m - Cv,m = [P + (∂U/∂V)T](∂V/∂T)p, which simplifies to R for ideal gases.

Temperature Dependence via Kirchhoff's Law

The temperature dependence of reaction enthalpy is governed by ΔCp. Integrating this relation allows calculation of reaction enthalpies at non-standard temperatures.

Equipartition Theorem

Classical statistical mechanics attributes a thermal energy contribution of (1/2)RT per degree of freedom to molar heat capacity, explaining atomic and molecular heat capacity values.

📚 Terms & Units Glossary

Heat Capacity (Cp) (Cp = (∂H / ∂T)p)
Quantity of heat required to change the temperature of a system by 1 K at constant pressure. SI unit: J K-1.
Molar Heat Capacity (Cp,m)
Heat capacity of one mole of a substance at constant pressure. SI unit: J mol-1 K-1.
Specific Heat Capacity (s or c)
Heat capacity per unit mass of a substance. SI unit: J g-1 K-1 or J kg-1 K-1.
Adiabatic Index (γ) (γ = Cp / Cv)
Ratio of heat capacity at constant pressure to that at constant volume. Dimensionless.
Kirchhoff's Equation (d(ΔH)/dT = ΔCp)
Relation showing the variation of reaction enthalpy with temperature.
Equipartition Theorem (E = (f/2)RT)
Principle stating that energy is shared equally among all active degrees of freedom.

🎯 Key Takeaways

  • Cp - Cv = R (for 1 mole of an ideal gas)
  • qp = ΔH (at constant pressure)
  • Cp,m for monoatomic ideal gas = (5/2)R
  • Cp,m for diatomic ideal gas = (7/2)R
  • ΔH = ∫ Cp dT

📌 Quick Recall

  • Cp represents enthalpy change per unit temperature change at constant pressure.
  • Mayer's relation links Cp and Cv through the gas constant R for ideal gases.
  • Degrees of freedom directly determine the molar heat capacity values of gases.
  • Kirchhoff's equation utilizes ΔCp to determine enthalpy changes at different temperatures.

⚖️ Key Differences

Cp VS Cv

Cp measures enthalpy change at constant pressure, whereas Cv measures internal energy change at constant volume.

Question 1 of 18

What is the relation between Cp and Cv for one mole of an ideal gas?

Explanation: For an ideal gas, Mayer's relation states that Cp,m - Cv,m = R.

Question 2 of 18

For a monoatomic ideal gas, what is the value of Cp,m?

Explanation: A monoatomic gas has 3 translational degrees of freedom, so Cv = 1.5 R and Cp = Cv + R = 2.5 R.

Question 3 of 18

The heat capacity at constant pressure Cp is defined mathematically as:

Explanation: By definition, Cp = (∂H / ∂T)p.

Question 4 of 18

What is the ratio Cp / Cv (γ) for a rigid diatomic ideal gas?

Explanation: A rigid diatomic gas has f = 5 (3 translational, 2 rotational). Thus Cv = 2.5R, Cp = 3.5R, and γ = 3.5 / 2.5 = 1.40.

Question 5 of 18

Which of the following conditions makes qp equal to ΔH?

Explanation: At constant pressure and in the absence of non-expansion work, the heat absorbed equals the enthalpy change (ΔH = qp).

Question 6 of 18

Kirchhoff's equation relates the variation of reaction enthalpy with temperature to:

Explanation: Kirchhoff's law states that d(ΔH)/dT = ΔCp.

Question 7 of 18

What is the unit of molar heat capacity Cp,m?

Explanation: Molar heat capacity is expressed per mole per Kelvin, with units J mol-1 K-1.

Question 8 of 18

For a real gas obeying van der Waals equation, Cp - Cv is generally:

Explanation: For real gases, intermolecular forces and finite volume corrections usually make Cp - Cv slightly greater than R.

Question 9 of 18

According to the Third Law of Thermodynamics, what happens to Cp as T approaches 0 K?

Explanation: The Third Law dictates that the heat capacity Cp of a pure crystalline substance approaches zero as temperature approaches absolute zero.

Question 10 of 18

If 2 moles of an ideal gas are heated by 10 K at constant pressure, what is the heat absorbed if Cp,m = 29.1 J mol-1 K-1?

Explanation: qp = n Cp,m ΔT = 2 mol × 29.1 J mol-1 K-1 × 10 K = 582 J.

Question 11 of 18

Which property type is heat capacity (Cp)?

Explanation: Heat capacity depends on the quantity of substance present, making it an extensive property.

Question 12 of 18

What is the value of Cp,m - Cv,m for an incompressible liquid?

Explanation: For an incompressible liquid, the volume expansion coefficient is nearly zero, making Cp equal to Cv (Cp - Cv ≈ 0).

Question 13 of 18

In an isobaric expansion of an ideal gas, the heat supplied is used for:

Explanation: At constant pressure, heat supplied increases both the internal energy (raising temperature) and performs expansion work (PΔV).

Question 14 of 18

Which of the following has the highest molar heat capacity Cp,m at room temperature?

Explanation: Water vapor is a non-linear polyatomic molecule with high vibrational and rotational degrees of freedom, giving it a higher Cp,m than monoatomic or simple diatomic gases.

Question 15 of 18

What does the area under the Cp vs T curve between T1 and T2 represent?

Explanation: Since dH = Cp dT, integrating Cp with respect to T gives the enthalpy change ΔH.

Question 16 of 18

For a polyatomic gas with 'n' atoms, the total number of degrees of freedom is:

Explanation: Each atom has 3 spatial coordinates (degrees of freedom), giving a total of 3n degrees of freedom for an n-atom molecule.

Question 17 of 18

Why is Cp greater than Cv for gases?

Explanation: At constant pressure, heating causes expansion, requiring additional thermal energy to perform work against the external pressure.

Question 18 of 18

What is the dimension of molar heat capacity?

Explanation: Heat capacity has units of Energy / (Amount × Temperature), corresponding to dimensions [ML2T-2K-1mol-1].

Question 1 of 10
Calculate Cv,m for a gas if its Cp,m is 20.78 J mol-1 K-1 and R = 8.314 J mol-1 K-1.

Solution: Cv,m = Cp,m - R = 20.78 - 8.314 = 12.466 J mol-1 K-1.

Question 2 of 10
What is the value of γ (Cp/Cv) for a triatomic linear gas like CO2 (ignoring vibrational contribution)?

Solution: A linear triatomic gas has f = 7 (3 translational, 2 rotational, 2 vibrational modes active at high T, or rigid f = 5 giving 1.4, non-rigid 1.28). For standard rigid linear molecule, Cv = 3.5R, Cp = 4.5R, γ = 4.5/3.5 = 1.28.

Question 3 of 10
The enthalpy change when 3 moles of an ideal gas are heated from 25°C to 55°C with Cp,m = 28.8 J mol-1 K-1 is:

Solution: ΔH = n Cp,m ΔT = 3 mol × 28.8 J mol-1 K-1 × 30 K = 2592 J.

Question 4 of 10
In an isobaric process, if q = 500 J and work done by the system w = 100 J, what is the change in internal energy (ΔU)?

Solution: From the first law: ΔU = q - w = 500 J - 100 J = 400 J.

Question 5 of 10
Which thermodynamic parameter is kept constant during the measurement of Cp?

Solution: By definition, heat capacity at constant pressure Cp is measured under isobaric (constant pressure) conditions.

Question 6 of 10
What is the temperature derivative of enthalpy at constant pressure?

Solution: The definition of Cp is (∂H / ∂T)p.

Question 7 of 10
For a real gas, the Joule-Thomson coefficient is zero at:

Solution: The inversion temperature is the temperature at which the Joule-Thomson coefficient changes sign and is equal to zero.

Question 8 of 10
How does Cp vary with temperature for most solids at moderate temperatures?

Solution: For solids, Cp increases with temperature, following Debye's T3 law at very low temperatures and approaching the Dulong-Petit limit at high temperatures.

Question 9 of 10
If Cp for a gas is given by 25 + 0.01 T (J K-1), the heat required to raise its temperature from 300 K to 400 K at constant pressure is:

Solution: ΔH = ∫ (25 + 0.01 T) dT from 300 to 400 = 25(100) + 0.01(4002 - 3002)/2 = 2500 + 0.01(70000)/2 = 2500 + 350 = 2850 J. Recalculating: (400-300)25 = 2500; 0.01/2 (160000-90000) = 0.005 * 70000 = 350; total = 2850 J.

Question 10 of 10
Which of the following expressions represents Mayer's relation in terms of specific heat capacities (sp and sv)?

Solution: Since specific heat is per unit mass, sp - sv = R / M where M is the molar mass.
