Physical Chemistry

van't Hoff Equation

The van't Hoff equation quantitatively expresses the temperature dependence of the chemical equilibrium constant. It relates the standard enthalpy change of a reaction to the change in equilibrium constant with respect to temperature.

UP PGT Chemistry Subject Code 02 B.Sc. / M.Sc. Level

Thermodynamic Derivation and Temperature Dependence

  • The relation combines the Gibbs-Helmholtz equation with the standard free energy isotherm: ΔG° = -RT ln K.
  • Differentiating ΔG°/T with respect to T at constant pressure yields the fundamental differential van't Hoff relation.
  • For an endothermic reaction (ΔH° > 0), the equilibrium constant increases with a rise in temperature.
  • For an exothermic reaction (ΔH° < 0), the equilibrium constant decreases as the temperature rises.

Integrated Form and Graphical Analysis

  • Integrating the differential van't Hoff equation between T1 and T2 allows calculation of K at any temperature if ΔH° is known.
  • A plot of ln K versus 1/T yields a straight line with a slope equal to -ΔH°/R and an intercept equal to ΔS°/R.
  • The slope directly yields the experimental standard enthalpy change of the reaction without calorimetry measurements.
  • Non-linear Arrhenius-type plots indicate that ΔH° varies significantly with temperature due to non-zero heat capacity change (ΔCp ≠ 0).

Van't Hoff Factor in Colligative Properties

  • The van't Hoff factor i accounts for the extent of solute association or dissociation in solution.
  • It is defined as the ratio of the observed colligative property to the normal calculated colligative property.
  • For complete dissociation into n ions, i = n; for complete association of n molecules into a dimer/polymer, i = 1/n.
  • The modified osmotic pressure equation incorporates the factor as Π = iCRT to accurately predict colligative behavior of electrolytes.
📐 Key Formulas & Equations
Integrated van't Hoff Equation (Two Temperatures)
ln(K2/K1) = -ΔH°/R [1/T2 - 1/T1]
Conditions: Assuming standard enthalpy of reaction (ΔH°) remains constant between T1 and T2.
Units: K1, K2 dimensionless; T in Kelvin
van't Hoff Isochore in Terms of Base-10 Logarithm
log(K2/K1) = -ΔH°/(2.303 R) [ (T2 - T1)/(T1T2) ]
Conditions: Constant enthalpy change over the temperature interval T1 to T2.
Units: Log values dimensionless, temperatures in Kelvin
Van't Hoff Osmotic Pressure Equation
Π = iCRT
Conditions: Dilute solutions exhibiting ideal behavior
Units: Π in atm or Pa, C in mol L-1, R in L atm K-1 mol-1, T in K
Differential van't Hoff Equation
d(ln K)/dT = ΔH°/(RT2)
Conditions: Standard state, constant pressure, assuming ΔH° is independent of temperature over the range.
Units: K in Kelvin, ΔH° in J mol-1, R in J K-1 mol-1
⚠️ Important Exceptions & Anomalies
Exception Equilibrium constant always increases with increasing temperature for all chemical systems.

Observation: Exothermic reactions show a decrease in equilibrium constant with increasing temperature. — Derived from Le Chatelier's principle and the negative sign of ΔH° in the van't Hoff equation.

Exception Van't Hoff factor (i) for strong electrolytes equals the stoichiometric number of ions formed.

Observation: Experimental 'i' values for concentrated electrolyte solutions are lower than stoichiometric values. — Interionic attractions and formation of ion pairs in concentrated solutions reduce the effective number of independent particles.

Exception A plot of ln K versus 1/T is strictly linear over wide temperature ranges.

Observation: Curvature is observed over very wide temperature intervals for many real reactions. — Standard enthalpy change (ΔH°) is temperature-dependent when the heat capacity change (ΔCp) is non-zero.

💡 Shortcuts & Memory Aids
Sign of Slope Mnemonic
Slope = -ΔH° / R

Application: Remember 'Endo = Positive Delta H, Negative Slope'. If the line goes down from left to right on a ln K vs 1/T plot, the reaction is endothermic.

Quick Temperature Ratio Rule
ln(K2/K1) = (ΔH°/R) × (ΔT / (T1T2))

Application: Use this expanded form to solve numerical problems requiring rapid calculation of K2 at a new temperature T2.

Dissociation Fraction Shortcut
i = 1 + α(n - 1)

Application: Calculate the van't Hoff factor directly from the degree of dissociation (α) and number of ions (n) without setting up ICE tables.

🎯 Key Exam Points
  • The differential van't Hoff equation is d(ln K)/dT = ΔH°/(RT2).
  • A negative slope in a ln K versus 1/T graph indicates an endothermic reaction.
  • For an exothermic reaction, the equilibrium constant decreases with an increase in temperature.
  • The van't Hoff factor i for acetic acid in benzene is less than 1 due to dimerization.
  • Integration of the van't Hoff equation assumes ΔH° is constant over the temperature range.
  • The unit of the universal gas constant R in these calculations is typically J K-1 mol-1.
🚫 Common Errors & Confusions
  • Forgetting the negative sign in the integrated van't Hoff equation: ln(K2/K1) = -ΔH°/R (1/T2 - 1/T1).
  • Using Celsius instead of Kelvin temperature in the denominator of the integrated equation.
  • Confusing the sign of ΔH° when interpreting the direction of equilibrium shift upon heating.
  • Assuming the van't Hoff factor i is always equal to the total number of ions produced without considering association or incomplete dissociation.

⚡ Quick Revision: van't Hoff Equation

Core formulas, rapid recall points, and distinguishing criteria at a glance.

Equilibrium Temperature Dependence

The van't Hoff equation provides the exact mathematical framework for predicting how chemical equilibria respond to thermal shifts based on reaction enthalpy.

Enthalpy Determination via Slope

Experimental measurement of equilibrium constants at multiple temperatures allows graphical extraction of standard reaction enthalpy via van't Hoff plots.

Colligative Corrections

The van't Hoff factor bridges ideal thermodynamic equations with real electrolytic solution behavior by correcting for particle multiplicity.

Le Chatelier Correlation

The equation mathematically validates Le Chatelier's qualitative prediction regarding thermal perturbations on dynamic chemical equilibria.

📚 Terms & Units Glossary

van't Hoff Equation (d(ln K)/dT = ΔH°/RT2)
Equation relating equilibrium constant temperature derivative to enthalpy change.
Van't Hoff Factor (i)
Ratio of actual colligative property to normal calculated value.
Standard Enthalpy (ΔH°)
Heat change of a reaction at standard state conditions in J mol-1.
Equilibrium Constant (K)
Ratio of product activities to reactant activities at equilibrium (dimensionless).
Osmotic Pressure (Π)
Pressure required to stop osmosis in atm or Pa.
Degree of Dissociation (α)
Fraction of total moles of a substance that dissociates.

🎯 Key Takeaways

  • Differential form: d(ln K)/dT = ΔH°/(RT2)
  • Integrated form: ln(K2/K1) = -ΔH°/R (1/T2 - 1/T1)
  • Slope of ln K vs 1/T = -ΔH°/R
  • Endothermic: K increases with T
  • Exothermic: K decreases with T

📌 Quick Recall

  • The van't Hoff equation quantifies temperature effects on chemical equilibrium.
  • Enthalpy sign dictates whether equilibrium constants rise or fall with heat.
  • Graphical plots of ln K vs 1/T enable rapid determination of ΔH°.
  • The van't Hoff factor accounts for solute association and dissociation in solutions.

⚖️ Key Differences

Differential van't Hoff equation VS Arrhenius equation

Van't Hoff relates equilibrium constant K to enthalpy ΔH°; Arrhenius relates rate constant k to activation energy Ea.

Question 1 of 20

What is the slope of the linear plot of ln K versus 1/T?

Explanation: From the integrated van't Hoff equation, ln K = -ΔH°/R(1/T) + constant, making the slope equal to -ΔH°/R.

Question 2 of 20

For an endothermic reaction, what happens to the equilibrium constant K when temperature is increased?

Explanation: For an endothermic reaction (ΔH° > 0), the differential van't Hoff equation shows that d(ln K)/dT is positive, so K increases with temperature.

Question 3 of 20

What is the van't Hoff factor (i) for a dilute aqueous solution of BaCl2 assuming complete dissociation?

Explanation: BaCl2 dissociates into one Ba2+ ion and two Cl- ions, giving a total of 3 ions (i = 3).

Question 4 of 20

Which form of the van't Hoff equation is used to calculate the equilibrium constant at two different temperatures?

Explanation: The integrated two-temperature form allows calculation of K2 at T2 knowing K1, T1, and ΔH°.

Question 5 of 20

If an exothermic reaction is heated, the equilibrium constant:

Explanation: For an exothermic reaction (ΔH° < 0), increasing temperature causes a decrease in the equilibrium constant according to the van't Hoff equation.

Question 6 of 20

What is the van't Hoff factor for benzoic acid when it undergoes complete dimerization in benzene?

Explanation: Dimerization means 2 molecules combine to form 1 particle, so i = 1/n = 1/2 = 0.5.

Question 7 of 20

In the van't Hoff isochore, the standard enthalpy change ΔH° is assumed to be:

Explanation: Simple integration of the van't Hoff equation assumes that ΔH° is independent of temperature (ΔCp = 0).

Question 8 of 20

The osmotic pressure of a solution is given by which van't Hoff relation?

Explanation: The modified van't Hoff equation for osmotic pressure incorporating the dissociation/association factor is Π = iCRT.

Question 9 of 20

A plot of log K versus 1/T has a slope equal to:

Explanation: Converting natural logarithm to base-10 logarithm introduces the factor 2.303, making the slope equal to -ΔH° / (2.303 R).

Question 10 of 20

Which of the following conditions favors the formation of products in an exothermic equilibrium reaction?

Explanation: For exothermic reactions, lower temperatures increase K, thus favoring the forward reaction to produce more products.

Question 11 of 20

If ΔH° for a reaction is zero, how does the equilibrium constant vary with temperature?

Explanation: When ΔH° = 0, d(ln K)/dT = 0, meaning the equilibrium constant is independent of temperature.

Question 12 of 20

Calculate the van't Hoff factor for a solute that is 50% dissociated into 2 ions.

Explanation: Using i = 1 + α(n - 1), where α = 0.5 and n = 2, i = 1 + 0.5(2 - 1) = 1.5.

Question 13 of 20

What are the standard units of the universal gas constant R when substituted into the van't Hoff equation with enthalpy in Joules?

Explanation: J K-1 mol-1 is the SI unit compatible with enthalpy expressed in Joules per mole.

Question 14 of 20

The integration constant obtained during the integration of the differential van't Hoff equation represents:

Explanation: Integrating d(ln K)/dT yields ln K = -ΔH°/(RT) + ΔS°/R, where the integration constant is ΔS°/R.

Question 15 of 20

When is the van't Hoff equation strictly applicable without approximation?

Explanation: The standard integrated form assumes ΔH° is constant, which strictly requires ΔCp = 0.

Question 16 of 20

What is the physical significance of a positive sign for ΔH° in the van't Hoff context?

Explanation: A positive ΔH° denotes an endothermic process where heat is absorbed.

Question 17 of 20

If K2 > K1 for a reaction when T2 > T1, what can be deduced about the reaction?

Explanation: An increase in equilibrium constant with an increase in temperature signifies an endothermic reaction.

Question 18 of 20

Which of the following compounds has a van't Hoff factor close to 1 in dilute aqueous solution?

Explanation: Glucose is a non-electrolyte and does not dissociate, so its van't Hoff factor i is approximately 1.

Question 19 of 20

The differential form of the van't Hoff equation can be derived directly by combining the Gibbs-Helmholtz equation and:

Explanation: Combining the relation ΔG° = -RT ln K with the Gibbs-Helmholtz equation gives the differential van't Hoff equation.

Question 20 of 20

If the van't Hoff factor 'i' of an electrolyte is less than its stoichiometric number of ions 'n', it indicates:

Explanation: An 'i' value less than 'n' indicates that ions associate or form ion pairs in solution, reducing the effective particle count.

Question 1 of 10
If the equilibrium constant of a reaction doubles when temperature changes from 300 K to 340 K, what is the standard enthalpy change (ΔH°)? (Given R = 8.314 J K-1 mol-1)

Solution: Using ln(2) = -ΔH°/8.314 × (1/340 - 1/300), solving yields ΔH° ≈ 15.8 kJ mol-1.

Question 2 of 10
The standard enthalpy of a reaction is -40 kJ mol-1. If K1 is the equilibrium constant at 400 K, what happens to K at 500 K?

Solution: Since ΔH° is negative (exothermic), increasing temperature from 400 K to 500 K decreases the equilibrium constant.

Question 3 of 10
What is the van't Hoff factor for a solute exhibiting 80% dissociation in water into 3 ions?

Solution: i = 1 + α(n - 1) = 1 + 0.8(3 - 1) = 1 + 0.8(2) = 2.6.

Question 4 of 10
A straight-line graph is obtained by plotting log K versus 1/T. The intercept on the y-axis corresponds to:

Solution: In log K vs 1/T form, log K = -ΔH°/(2.303 RT) + ΔS°/(2.303 R), making the intercept equal to ΔS° / (2.303 R).

Question 5 of 10
For which of the following reactions will the equilibrium constant decrease with increasing temperature?

Solution: The oxidation of sulfur dioxide is exothermic (ΔH° < 0), so its equilibrium constant decreases with rising temperature.

Question 6 of 10
Calculate ΔH° if the slope of the ln K versus 1/T graph is +2500 K.

Solution: Slope = -ΔH°/R = 2500 → ΔH° = -2500 × 8.314 = -20785 J mol-1 = -20.78 kJ mol-1.

Question 7 of 10
Which solute in aqueous solution will have a van't Hoff factor closest to 2?

Solution: NaCl dissociates into 2 ions (Na+ and Cl-), giving an ideal van't Hoff factor of 2.

Question 8 of 10
If d(ln K)/dT = 2000 / T2, what is the standard enthalpy of the reaction (ΔH°)?

Solution: Comparing with d(ln K)/dT = ΔH°/(RT2), ΔH°/R = 2000 → ΔH° = 2000 × 8.314 = 16628 J mol-1 = 16.63 kJ mol-1.

Question 9 of 10
The osmotic pressure of a 0.1 M NaCl solution at 300 K compared to a 0.1 M glucose solution at 300 K is approximately:

Solution: NaCl has i ≈ 2 while glucose has i = 1, so Π for NaCl is twice that of glucose at the same molar concentration.

Question 10 of 10
In the derivation of the van't Hoff equation, which thermodynamic relation for standard free energy change is substituted?

Solution: The core substitution linking equilibrium constant K to temperature is ΔG° = -RT ln K combined with the Gibbs-Helmholtz relation.
