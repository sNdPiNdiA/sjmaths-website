Variation of Chemical Potential with Pressure

The chemical potential of a pure substance increases monotonically with an increase in applied pressure at constant temperature. This partial molar Gibbs free energy change is directly determined by the molar volume of the given thermodynamic phase.

UP PGT Chemistry Subject Code 02 B.Sc. / M.Sc. Level

Thermodynamic Derivation from Gibbs Free Energy

  • The chemical potential (μ) of a component in a pure system is identical to its molar Gibbs free energy (Gm).
  • Starting from the total differential dG = -S dT + V dP, taking the partial derivative with respect to pressure at constant temperature yields (∂G / ∂P)T = V.
  • For one mole of a pure substance, this establishes the fundamental relation (∂μ / ∂P)T = Vm.
  • Since the molar volume Vm is strictly positive for all stable matter, the chemical potential must always increase as pressure increases.

Behavior in Condensed Phases (Liquids and Solids)

  • Liquids and solids exhibit very small molar volumes (Vm), making their chemical potential weakly dependent on moderate pressure changes.
  • Assuming liquids and solids are incompressible, integrating the volume derivative yields μ(P2) = μ(P1) + Vm(P2 - P1).
  • Large pressure increments are required to effect a noticeable change in the chemical potential of condensed phases compared to gaseous systems.
  • This slight pressure dependence dictates the Le Chatelier principle shifts in solid-liquid phase boundaries, such as the melting point variation with pressure.

Behavior in Gaseous Systems and Fugacity

  • For an ideal gas, substituting the equation of state Vm = RT / P into the pressure derivative yields (∂μ / ∂P)T = RT / P.
  • Integration from a standard pressure P° to a final pressure P gives the logarithmic dependence μ(P) = μ° + RT ln(P / P°).
  • At very high pressures, deviations from ideal gas behavior require the substitution of real pressure with fugacity (f) to correct for intermolecular forces.
  • The fugacity coefficient φ = f / P accounts for these non-ideal interactions when calculating absolute chemical potential values.
📐 Key Formulas & Equations
Integrated Form for Incompressible Condensed Phases
μ(P) = μ° + Vm(P - P°)
Conditions: Condensed phases (liquids and solids) where Vm is approximately constant
Units: μ in J mol-1, Vm in m3 mol-1, P in Pa
Integrated Form for Ideal Gases
μ(P) = μ° + RT ln(P / P°)
Conditions: Ideal gas behavior at constant temperature
Units: μ in J mol-1, R in J K-1 mol-1, T in K, P in Pa
Integrated Form for Real Gases (Fugacity)
μ(P) = μ° + RT ln(f / P°)
Conditions: Real gas systems replacing pressure with fugacity f
Units: μ in J mol-1, f in Pa
Pressure Derivative of Chemical Potential
(∂μ / ∂P)T = Vm
Conditions: Pure substance, single phase
Units: μ in J mol-1, P in Pa, Vm in m3 mol-1
⚠️ Important Exceptions & Anomalies
Exception Chemical potential always increases with increasing pressure.

Observation: Phase transitions and thermodynamic triple points — At a phase transition boundary, two phases coexist in equilibrium having identical chemical potentials, causing discontinuities in derivatives when crossing phases.

Exception Condensed phase chemical potential is nearly independent of pressure.

Observation: Extreme megabar high-pressure geophysics and diamond anvil cells — Under extreme pressures (GPa range), even solids and liquids undergo significant volume compression, altering Vm.

💡 Shortcuts & Memory Aids
Volume Sign Check for Pressure Dependence
(∂μ / ∂P)T = Vm > 0

Application: Always remember that because molar volume is positive, increasing pressure moves chemical potential uphill on a free energy diagram.

Ideal Gas Logarithmic Rule
Δμ = RT ln(P2 / P1)

Application: Use this direct ratio formula for isothermal compression or expansion of ideal gases without evaluating integrals.

Condensed Phase Shortcut
Δμ ≈ Vm ΔP

Application: Apply this linear approximation for liquids and solids where volume changes are negligible over moderate pressure intervals.

🎯 Key Exam Points
  • The partial derivative of chemical potential with respect to pressure at constant temperature equals the molar volume: (∂μ / ∂P)T = Vm.
  • For an ideal gas, the chemical potential variation is given by μ(P) = μ° + RT ln(P / P°).
  • Because molar volume is always positive, chemical potential increases with an increase in pressure.
  • Liquids and solids show negligible change in chemical potential under moderate pressure due to their small molar volumes.
  • Fugacity replaces pressure in the chemical potential expression when dealing with non-ideal real gases.
  • At a phase equilibrium boundary, the chemical potentials of all coexisting phases are equal regardless of pressure.
🚫 Common Errors & Confusions
  • Ignoring the temperature constraint: (∂μ / ∂P)T strictly applies only under isothermal conditions.
  • Confusing standard pressure P° (1 bar or 1 atm) inside logarithmic terms with system pressure variables.
  • Assuming liquids have zero volume change under pressure rather than treating them as incompressible approximations.
  • Applying the ideal gas logarithmic equation directly to condensed matter systems.

⚡ Quick Revision: Variation of Chemical Potential with Pressure

Core formulas, rapid recall points, and distinguishing criteria at a glance.

Fundamental Definition

Chemical potential equals molar Gibbs free energy, and its pressure derivative is the molar volume Vm.

Gas Phase Integration

Integration for ideal gases results in a logarithmic dependence on pressure relative to standard state pressure.

Condensed Phase Approximation

Liquids and solids exhibit linear dependence on pressure scaled by their very small molar volumes.

Real System Corrections

Non-ideal gases replace pressure with fugacity to maintain exact thermodynamic relations for chemical potential.

📚 Terms & Units Glossary

Molar Volume (Vm)
Volume occupied by one mole of substance in m3 mol-1.
Fugacity (f)
Effective pressure corrected for non-ideality in Pa.
Standard Pressure (P°)
Reference pressure typically set to 1 bar.
Isothermal (Constant T)

Incompressible (dV ≈ 0)
Condition where volume does not change with pressure.

🎯 Key Takeaways

  • (∂μ / ∂P)T = Vm
  • μ = μ° + RT ln(P / P°) for ideal gases
  • μ = μ° + Vm(P - P°) for condensed phases
  • Molar volume is always positive
  • Fugacity replaces pressure for real gases

📌 Quick Recall

  • Chemical potential always rises with pressure.
  • Gases show large logarithmic pressure dependence.
  • Liquids and solids show minimal pressure dependence.
  • Thermodynamic equilibrium requires equal chemical potential across phases.

⚖️ Key Differences

Ideal Gas Pressure Dependence VS Condensed Phase Pressure Dependence

Ideal gases scale logarithmically with pressure, whereas liquids and solids scale linearly with a very small molar volume coefficient.

Question 1 of 19

What is the exact partial derivative of chemical potential with respect to pressure at constant temperature?

Explanation: From the fundamental thermodynamic relation dG = -S dT + V dP, dividing by amount gives dGm = -Sm dT + Vm dP, yielding (∂μ / ∂P)T = Vm.

Question 2 of 19

How does the chemical potential of a pure ideal gas change when the pressure is increased isothermally?

Explanation: According to μ = μ° + RT ln(P / P°), increasing pressure P increases the logarithmic term, hence chemical potential increases.

Question 3 of 19

For an incompressible condensed phase, the variation of chemical potential with pressure is given by:

Explanation: Integrating (∂μ / ∂P)T = Vm with constant Vm for condensed phases yields Δμ = VmΔP.

Question 4 of 19

Why is the pressure dependence of chemical potential much smaller for liquids than for gases?

Explanation: Because Vm for liquids is orders of magnitude smaller than for gases, the product VmΔP results in a very small change in μ.

Question 5 of 19

What term replaces pressure in the chemical potential expression when dealing with real, non-ideal gases?

Explanation: Fugacity (f) is defined to correct for non-ideal intermolecular interactions in real gases for chemical potential equations.

Question 6 of 19

If the pressure of an ideal gas is doubled at constant temperature, what is the change in its chemical potential?

Explanation: Δμ = RT ln(P2 / P1) = RT ln(2P / P) = RT ln 2.

Question 7 of 19

The slope of chemical potential versus pressure at constant temperature is always:

Explanation: Since molar volume Vm is a physically positive quantity for all stable states of matter, the slope (∂μ / ∂P)T is always positive.

Question 8 of 19

At a phase transition temperature and pressure between solid and liquid, what is the relationship between their chemical potentials?

Explanation: Phase equilibrium requires that the chemical potential of the substance in both coexisting phases be identical.

Question 9 of 19

Which of the following conditions is required for the relation (∂μ / ∂P)T = Vm to hold true?

Explanation: The partial derivative is taken with respect to pressure while keeping temperature constant.

Question 10 of 19

What is the unit of chemical potential in the SI system?

Explanation: Chemical potential is a molar quantity representing Gibbs free energy per mole, with units of J mol-1.

Question 11 of 19

In the integrated expression μ(P) = μ° + RT ln(P / P°), what does P° represent?

Explanation: P° denotes the standard state pressure, conventionally chosen as 1 bar.

Question 12 of 19

How does an increase in pressure affect the escaping tendency of molecules from a liquid phase?

Explanation: Increasing pressure increases the chemical potential, which corresponds to an increased escaping tendency or chemical activity.

Question 13 of 19

For a mixture of ideal gases, the chemical potential of component i depends on its partial pressure Pi as:

Explanation: Each component in an ideal gas mixture behaves independently with respect to its partial pressure in the chemical potential formula.

Question 14 of 19

What happens to the chemical potential curve of a gas on a μ versus P plot as pressure approaches zero?

Explanation: As P approaches zero, ln(P) approaches negative infinity, causing μ to approach negative infinity.

Question 15 of 19

Which phase has the largest molar volume Vm under normal laboratory conditions?

Explanation: Gases have molar volumes on the order of liters per mole, whereas condensed phases are on the order of milliliters per mole.

Question 16 of 19

The fugacity coefficient φ for an ideal gas is equal to:

Explanation: For an ideal gas, fugacity equals pressure (f = P), so the fugacity coefficient φ = f / P equals 1.

Question 17 of 19

Which thermodynamic property is equivalent to chemical potential for a pure substance?

Explanation: Chemical potential μ for a pure component is defined as its molar Gibbs free energy Gm.

Question 18 of 19

When solid water (ice) melts under high pressure, the melting point decreases. This is consistent with:

Explanation: Ice has a larger molar volume than liquid water (Vm(ice) > Vm(liquid)), causing the melting point to decrease with increased pressure per Le Chatelier's principle.

Question 19 of 19

If pressure is expressed in bars and standard pressure is 1 bar, the argument of the logarithm in the ideal gas chemical potential equation is:

Explanation: Arguments of logarithmic functions must be dimensionless, so P is divided by P° (1 bar), leaving a dimensionless ratio.

Question 1 of 10
Calculate the change in chemical potential when 1 mole of an ideal gas is compressed isothermally from 1 bar to 10 bar at 298 K.

Solution: Δμ = RT ln(P2 / P1) = (8.314 J K-1 mol-1)(298 K) ln(10 / 1) = 8.314 × 298 × 2.303 = 5705 J mol-1 ≈ 5.71 kJ mol-1.

Question 2 of 10
Which of the following derivatives represents the volume change per mole with respect to pressure?

Solution: (∂G / ∂P)T = V, and dividing by amount gives molar volume Vm, which equals (∂μ / ∂P)T.

Question 3 of 10
For a condensed substance with a molar volume of 50 cm3 mol-1, what is the increase in chemical potential when pressure is increased by 100 bar?

Solution: Δμ = VmΔP = (50 × 10-6 m3 mol-1) × (100 × 105 Pa) = 500 J mol-1? Wait, 50 × 10-6 × 107 = 500? Let's check: 50 cm3 = 50 × 10-6 m3; 100 bar = 107 Pa. Product = 500 J mol-1. Correct index is 0.

Question 4 of 10
The chemical potential of a gas increases with pressure because:

Solution: The fundamental relation (∂μ / ∂P)T = Vm dictates that a positive molar volume guarantees an increase in μ with P.

Question 5 of 10
If fugacity f replaces pressure P for a real gas, the exact definition of fugacity is obtained by integrating:

Solution: For real gases, dμ = RT d(ln f) defines fugacity in direct analogy to the ideal gas expression dμ = RT d(ln P).

Question 6 of 10
At constant temperature, a plot of chemical potential versus ln P for an ideal gas is a straight line with a slope equal to:

Solution: Since μ = μ° + RT ln(P / P°), plotting μ against ln(P / P°) yields a straight line with slope RT.

Question 7 of 10
In a multiphase system at equilibrium, the effect of applying an external hydrostatic pressure equally to all phases is that:

Solution: Applying equal pressure changes the chemical potential of each phase by VmΔP; equilibrium is maintained if all phases experience the same pressure change.

Question 8 of 10
What is the sign of (∂μ / ∂P)T for a substance near its critical point?

Solution: Molar volume remains positive even near the critical point, so the pressure derivative of chemical potential remains positive.

Question 9 of 10
Which phase transition follows the rule that an increase in pressure favors the phase with the smaller molar volume?

Solution: Le Chatelier's principle states that a system under increased pressure shifts to minimize volume, favoring the phase with smaller Vm.

Question 10 of 10
If the pressure of an ideal gas is decreased by a factor of 4 isothermally, the chemical potential changes by:

Solution: Δμ = RT ln(P2 / P1) = RT ln(1/4) = -RT ln 4.
