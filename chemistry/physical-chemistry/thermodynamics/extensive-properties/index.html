Physical Chemistry

Extensive Properties

Extensive properties are thermodynamic properties whose magnitude depends directly upon the total quantity of matter present in the system. Examples include mass, volume, internal energy, enthalpy, entropy, and Gibbs free energy.

UP PGT Chemistry Subject Code 02 B.Sc. / M.Sc. Level

Mathematical Definition and Homogeneity

  • An extensive property scales linearly with the system size, satisfying the homogeneity relation f(λn1, λn2) = λf(n1, n2).
  • Partial derivatives of an extensive property with respect to component moles yield intensive partial molar quantities (e.g., chemical potential μ).
  • The ratio of any two extensive properties of the same system yields an intensive property (e.g., mass / volume = density).

Thermodynamic Functions Classification

  • Classic extensive thermodynamic state functions include Internal Energy (U), Enthalpy (H), Helmholtz Energy (A), and Gibbs Energy (G).
  • Capacity properties such as Heat Capacity (Cp and Cv) are strictly extensive because they scale directly with system mass.
  • Entropy (S) and Volume (V) are fundamental extensive properties that dictate the limits of spontaneous change in closed systems.

Additivity and System Combination

  • When two independent subsystems are merged, their respective extensive properties are strictly additive without cross-terms.
  • Surface area and total charge are also categorized as extensive properties because they scale with system dimensions.
  • Extensive properties fail to serve as criteria for chemical equilibrium; intensive properties like temperature and pressure are required.
📐 Key Formulas & Equations
Euler's Theorem for Homogeneous Functions
X(dn1, dn2, …) = n · X(n1, n2, …)
Conditions: Extensive property as a homogeneous function of degree 1 in the number of moles ni
Units: ni in moles, X in its respective unit
Conversion to Intensive Property
x = X / n   (or   x = X / m)
Conditions: Dividing an extensive property (X) by total moles (n) or total mass (m)
Units: x is intensive (e.g., J mol-1, L mol-1)
Total Volume Expression
V = ∑ (ni · Vi,m)
Conditions: Mixture of components where Vi,m is partial molar volume
Units: V in liters or m3, Vi,m in L mol-1
Additivity Rule for Extensive Properties
Xtotal = X1 + X2 + X3 + …
Conditions: For any extensive property X when subsystems are combined
Units: Units depend on the specific property (e.g., Joules, moles, liters)
⚠️ Important Exceptions & Anomalies
Exception Ratio of two extensive properties is always intensive.

Observation: Ratio of two extensive properties measured relative to different independent systems — Dimensional consistency breaks down unless both properties pertain to the exact same homogenous system boundary.

Exception Extensive properties are completely additive upon mixing.

Observation: Partial molar volumes or excess thermodynamic properties in non-ideal solutions — Intermolecular interactions and volume contraction or expansion upon mixing cause non-linear deviations from strict additivity.

💡 Shortcuts & Memory Aids
The Division Rule
Extensive / Extensive = Intensive

Application: Quickly identify property types in MCQs; e.g., Mass (Extensive) / Volume (Extensive) = Density (Intensive).

Size Scaling Test
Double system → Check property value

Application: If the numerical value doubles when the system is partitioned or doubled, the property is extensive.

Molar Prefix Rule
Add 'Molar' → Becomes Intensive

Application: Enthalpy (H, Extensive) becomes Molar Enthalpy (Hm, Intensive).

🎯 Key Exam Points
  • Internal energy (U) and enthalpy (H) are extensive thermodynamic properties.
  • Entropy (S) is an extensive property; standard molar entropy is intensive.
  • Heat capacity (Cp, Cv) scales with system size and is extensive.
  • Specific heat capacity is intensive, whereas total heat capacity is extensive.
  • Chemical potential (μ) is an intensive property despite being a derivative of extensive properties.
  • The quotient of mass and volume gives density, which is an intensive property.
🚫 Common Errors & Confusions
  • Confusing molar heat capacity (intensive) with total heat capacity (extensive).
  • Assuming temperature is extensive because thermal energy is extensive.
  • Forgetting that specific properties (per unit mass) are intensive.
  • Treating partial molar properties as strictly additive in non-ideal multi-component systems.

⚡ Quick Revision: Extensive Properties

Core formulas, rapid recall points, and distinguishing criteria at a glance.

Definition and Scaling

Extensive properties depend on the total quantity of matter and scale linearly with system size.

Additivity Principle

When subsystems combine, extensive properties are obtained by direct summation.

Mathematical Formulation

Extensive functions satisfy Euler's theorem for homogeneous functions of degree one.

Conversion to Intensive

Dividing an extensive property by mass or moles yields an intensive property.

📚 Terms & Units Glossary

Extensive Property (X)
Property dependent on system size, SI unit varies.
Intensive Property (x)
Property independent of system size, SI unit independent of mass.
Internal Energy (U)
Total energy contained within a thermodynamic system, Joules.
Enthalpy (H)
Heat content of a system at constant pressure, Joules.
Entropy (S)
Measure of thermal energy unavailability per unit temperature, J K-1.
Gibbs Energy (G)
Maximum reversible work obtainable at constant T and P, Joules.

🎯 Key Takeaways

  • Extensive properties are additive.
  • Ratio of two extensive properties is intensive.
  • Specific properties are intensive.
  • Molar properties are intensive.
  • Temperature and pressure are intensive.

📌 Quick Recall

  • Matter dependence defines extensive properties.
  • Homogeneity of degree 1 defines mathematical extent.
  • Division by moles or mass creates intensive counterparts.
  • Equilibrium criteria rely entirely on intensive variables.

⚖️ Key Differences

Heat Capacity VS Specific Heat Capacity

Heat capacity is extensive (J K-1); specific heat capacity is intensive (J g-1 K-1).
