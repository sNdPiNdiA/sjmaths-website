Physical Chemistry

Enthalpy of Neutralization

Enthalpy of neutralization is the standard enthalpy change that occurs when one gram-equivalent of an acid and a base undergo neutralization to form one mole of water. For strong acids and strong bases in dilute aqueous solutions, the constant value is approximately -57.1 kJ mol-1.

UP PGT Chemistry Subject Code 02 B.Sc. / M.Sc. Level

Thermochemistry of Strong Acid-Base Reactions

  • The enthalpy of neutralization of a strong acid and a strong base is constant at approximately -57.1 kJ mol-1 (or -13.7 kcal mol-1) at 298 K.
  • The net ionic equation involves the combination of hydronium ions and hydroxide ions to form undissociated water: H+(aq) + OH-(aq) → H2O(l).
  • Spectator ions such as Na+, K+, Cl-, and NO3- do not participate in bond formation and remain hydrated in solution.

Energy Profiles of Weak Acids and Bases

  • Neutralization involving a weak acid (e.g., CH3COOH) or weak base (e.g., NH4OH) releases less heat because a portion of the energy is consumed in ionizing the weak electrolyte.
  • The enthalpy of ionization of a weak acid is calculated by subtracting the experimental neutralization enthalpy from the standard strong-strong value (-57.1 kJ mol-1).
  • Polybasic acids like H3PO4 or H2SO4 release varying amounts of heat per mole of protons neutralized depending on step-wise dissociation enthalpies.

Calorimetric Determination and Factors

  • Enthalpy of neutralization is measured using a coffee-cup calorimeter or bomb calorimeter operating under constant pressure conditions.
  • The temperature rise (ΔT) is inversely proportional to the total heat capacity of the resulting solution and calorimeter assembly.
  • Enthalpy values increase slightly in magnitude with increasing temperature due to the temperature dependence of heat capacities.

⚠️ Important Exceptions & Anomalies
Exception Enthalpy of neutralization of strong acids and bases is constant (-57.1 kJ mol-1).

Observation: Hydrofluoric acid (HF) neutralization yields a higher enthalpy magnitude (~ -68.6 kJ mol-1). — Strong hydration energy of fluoride ion (F-) and formation of stable [HF2]- hydrogen-bonded species.

Exception Neutralization of weak acids releases less heat than strong acids.

Observation: Neutralization of certain strong acids with strong bases in very concentrated non-ideal solutions shows deviations. — Heat of dilution and strong interionic attractions override standard infinite dilution assumptions.

Exception Enthalpy of neutralization represents only water formation.

Observation: Neutralization of boric acid (H3BO3) with NaOH shows an abnormally low heat release. — Boric acid acts as a Lewis acid, absorbing hydroxyl ions and undergoing complex step-wise structural reorganizations.

💡 Shortcuts & Memory Aids
Weak Acid Ionization Shortcut
ΔHion = -57.1 - (ΔHobserved)

Application: Directly calculate the heat absorbed during weak acid ionization by taking the algebraic difference from the standard strong acid value.

Gram-Equivalent Rule
n = normality × volume (in L)

Application: Always calculate equivalents rather than moles when dealing with polyprotic acids (like H2SO4) to find correct heat output.

Calorimeter Heat Capacity Sign Convention
qreleased = -qabsorbed

Application: Remember that exothermic neutralization reactions yield a negative ΔH value because heat flows from system to surroundings.

🎯 Key Exam Points
  • The standard enthalpy of neutralization of any strong acid by any strong base is always constant at -57.1 kJ mol-1.
  • The heat of neutralization of HF is higher than -57.1 kJ mol-1 due to high hydration energy of F- ions.
  • Neutralization of CH3COOH with NaOH gives less heat because energy is consumed in ionizing acetic acid.
  • Enthalpy of neutralization is an exothermic process, hence ΔH is always negative.
  • The neutralization of a strong acid by a weak base also releases less heat due to the ionization energy of the weak base.
  • Calorimetric measurements of neutralization use the formula q = mcΔT under constant pressure.
🚫 Common Errors & Confusions
  • Forgetting that sulfuric acid (H2SO4) provides two moles of H+ ions per mole of acid.
  • Confusing the sign of enthalpy change; neutralization is exothermic, so ΔH must carry a negative sign.
  • Assuming weak acids have a fixed enthalpy of neutralization regardless of their degree of dissociation.
  • Ignoring the heat capacity of the calorimeter vessel when precise experimental calculations are required.

⚡ Quick Revision: Enthalpy of Neutralization

Core formulas, rapid recall points, and distinguishing criteria at a glance.

Principle of Constant Neutralization Enthalpy

Strong acids and strong bases are completely ionized in dilute aqueous solutions. Thus, neutralization simply involves the combination of H+ and OH- ions to form water, yielding a constant -57.1 kJ mol-1.

Effect of Weak Electrolytes

When a weak acid or weak base is neutralized, part of the evolved heat is utilized to dissociate the un-ionized molecules. Consequently, the observed enthalpy of neutralization is lower in magnitude.

Calorimetric Equations

The heat released in solution is calculated using q = m c ΔT, which equals the enthalpy change at constant pressure divided by the number of moles of water formed.

Exceptions Involving Anomalous Hydration

Hydrofluoric acid exhibits an anomalously high enthalpy of neutralization due to exceptionally strong hydration of fluoride ions and hydrogen bonding effects.

📚 Terms & Units Glossary

Enthalpy of Neutralization (ΔHneut)
Heat released when 1 gram-equivalent of acid is neutralized by base in dilute solution (kJ mol-1).
Heat Capacity (C)
Amount of heat required to raise the temperature of a substance by 1 K (J K-1).
Specific Heat (c)
Heat capacity per unit mass of a substance (J g-1 K-1).
Calorimeter Constant (Ccal)
Heat capacity of the calorimeter apparatus itself (J K-1).
Strong Electrolyte (α ≈ 1)
Substance that dissociates completely into ions in aqueous solution.
Enthalpy of Ionization (ΔHion)
Enthalpy change accompanying the complete dissociation of one mole of weak electrolyte.

🎯 Key Takeaways

  • Standard strong acid-base enthalpy of neutralization = -57.1 kJ mol-1.
  • Net ionic reaction for strong acid-strong base: H+ + OH- → H2O.
  • Neutralization is always an exothermic process (ΔH < 0).
  • HF exhibits anomalous higher neutralization heat due to strong F- hydration.
  • Calorimeter heat equation: q = m × c × ΔT.

📌 Quick Recall

  • Enthalpy of neutralization measures heat evolved during salt and water formation.
  • Strong-strong systems maintain a constant value because spectator ions do not bond.
  • Weak electrolyte systems absorb energy for dissociation, reducing net heat release.
  • Experimental determination requires accurate mass, specific heat, and temperature change measurement.

⚖️ Key Differences

Enthalpy of Formation VS Enthalpy of Neutralization

Formation is from constituent elements, whereas neutralization is the reaction between an acid and a base.

Question 2 of 18

Why is the enthalpy of neutralization of a weak acid with a strong base lower than -57.1 kJ mol-1?

Explanation: Part of the heat evolved during neutralization is consumed in dissociating the un-ionized weak acid molecules.

Question 3 of 18

Explanation: HF has a higher enthalpy of neutralization due to the extraordinarily high hydration energy of fluoride ions.

Question 4 of 18

The net ionic equation for the neutralization of HCl with NaOH is:

Explanation: Spectator ions (Na+ and Cl-) cancel out, leaving H+(aq) + OH-(aq) → H2O(l).

Question 5 of 18

What is the enthalpy of neutralization when 1 mole of CH3COOH is neutralized by NaOH, given its ionization enthalpy is +2.2 kJ mol-1?

Explanation: ΔHneut = -57.1 + (+2.2) = -54.9 kJ mol-1.

Question 6 of 18

Which apparatus is commonly used to measure the enthalpy of neutralization in a laboratory?

Explanation: A coffee-cup calorimeter provides a simple constant-pressure environment for measuring heat changes in solution.

Question 7 of 18

Neutralization reactions are always:

Explanation: Bond formation between H+ and OH- releases energy, making neutralization exothermic (ΔH < 0).

Question 8 of 18

Calculate the heat released when 100 mL of 0.5 M NaOH is mixed with 100 mL of 0.5 M HCl.

Explanation: Moles = 0.1 L × 0.5 M = 0.05 moles. Heat released = 0.05 mol × 57.1 kJ mol-1 = 2.855 kJ.

Question 9 of 18

What is the enthalpy of neutralization of a strong monoprotic acid with a strong monoacidic base?

Explanation: Per mole of water formed, strong acid-base neutralization is always -57.1 kJ mol-1.

Question 10 of 18

When 1 mole of H2SO4 is completely neutralized by excess NaOH, the heat released is approximately:

Explanation: H2SO4 is diprotic, releasing 2 moles of H+ per mole of acid. 2 × (-57.1 kJ) = -114.2 kJ.

Question 11 of 18

Which pair will have the lowest enthalpy of neutralization in magnitude?

Explanation: Both the weak acid and weak base require energy for ionization, resulting in the lowest net heat release.

Question 12 of 18

In the relation q = m c ΔT, 'c' stands for:

Explanation: 'c' is the specific heat capacity of the solution (J g-1 K-1).

Question 13 of 18

Why does a strong acid-strong base neutralization have a constant enthalpy value?

Explanation: Since strong acids and bases dissociate completely, the only chemical change is the formation of water from H+ and OH-.

Question 14 of 18

What is the value of enthalpy of neutralization in kcal mol-1 for strong acids and bases?

Explanation: Converting -57.1 kJ mol-1 by dividing by 4.184 gives approximately -13.7 kcal mol-1.

Question 15 of 18

Which factor does NOT affect the enthalpy of neutralization of a weak acid?

Explanation: Container volume does not directly affect thermodynamic enthalpy as long as solutions remain dilute.

Question 16 of 18

What happens to the enthalpy of neutralization when temperature increases slightly?

Explanation: According to Kirchhoff's law, heat of reaction varies with temperature based on the difference in heat capacities (ΔCp).

Question 17 of 18

The enthalpy of neutralization of HCN by NaOH is -12.1 kJ mol-1. What is the enthalpy of ionization of HCN?

Explanation: ΔHion = -57.1 - (-12.1) = +45.0 kJ mol-1 (absorbed energy).

Question 18 of 18

Which of the following is true for the neutralization of NaOH with HCl?

Explanation: Neutralization is exothermic (ΔH < 0) and formation of ordered water molecules from free ions leads to a decrease in entropy (ΔS < 0).

Calculate the heat of ionization of HCN given that its neutralization enthalpy with NaOH is -11.7 kJ mol-1.

Solution: ΔHion = -57.1 - (-11.7) = +45.4 kJ mol-1.

Question 2 of 10
Which of the following neutralization reactions will release the maximum amount of heat?

Solution: Strong acid and strong base release the maximum heat (-57.1 kJ mol-1) without any energy loss toward ionization.

Question 3 of 10
What is the enthalpy change when 2 moles of HNO3 are neutralized by 2 moles of KOH in dilute solution?

Solution: 2 moles of water formed × -57.1 kJ mol-1 = -114.2 kJ.

Question 4 of 10
If the temperature of a calorimeter rises by 2.5 K upon mixing 50 mL of 1 M HCl and 50 mL of 1 M NaOH (total mass = 100 g, c = 4.2 J g-1 K-1), what is the heat released?

Solution: q = m × c × ΔT = 100 g × 4.2 J g-1 K-1 × 2.5 K = 1050 J.

Question 5 of 10
In the neutralization of a weak acid by a strong base, the net enthalpy change is a result of:

Solution: The overall enthalpy is the sum of endothermic acid ionization and exothermic water formation.

Question 6 of 10
Which combination yields a neutralization enthalpy closest to -57.1 kJ mol-1?

Solution: Both HClO4 and KOH are strong electrolytes, giving the standard strong-strong neutralization enthalpy.

Question 7 of 10
What is the enthalpy of neutralization when 0.5 mole of H2SO4 is neutralized by KOH?

Solution: 0.5 mole of H2SO4 provides 1 mole of H+ ions. 1 mole of water formed releases 57.1 kJ.

Question 8 of 10
Why does NH4OH + HCl have a lower heat of neutralization than NaOH + HCl?

Solution: Energy is absorbed to dissociate the weak base NH4OH into ammonium and hydroxide ions.

Question 9 of 10
The enthalpy of neutralization of formic acid (HCOOH) by NaOH is -56.0 kJ mol-1. Calculate its ionization enthalpy.

Solution: ΔHion = -57.1 - (-56.0) = +1.1 kJ mol-1.

Question 10 of 10
Which statement is incorrect regarding enthalpy of neutralization?

Solution: Enthalpy of neutralization is always negative (exothermic), never positive for strong acid-base reactions.
