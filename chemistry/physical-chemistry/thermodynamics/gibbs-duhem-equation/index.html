Gibbs–Duhem Equation

The Gibbs–Duhem equation relates the changes in chemical potentials of the various components in a thermodynamic system at constant temperature and pressure. It establishes that the intensive properties of a multicomponent solution are not completely independent of one another.

UP PGT Chemistry Subject Code 02 B.Sc. / M.Sc. Level

Derivation and Fundamental Principles

  • The total Gibbs energy G of a multicomponent system is an extensive property expressed as G = ∑ μini.
  • Differentiating the total Gibbs energy yields dG = ∑ μi dni + ∑ ni dμi.
  • Comparing with the exact differential dG = -S dT + V dP + ∑ μi dni gives the fundamental relation -S dT + V dP = ∑ ni dμi.
  • Under conditions of constant temperature and pressure (dT = 0, dP = 0), this reduces to the classical Gibbs–Duhem equation ∑ ni dμi = 0.

Application to Binary Solutions and Partial Molar Quantities

  • For a binary solution containing components 1 and 2, the equation takes the form x1 dμ1 + x2 dμ2 = 0.
  • If the chemical potential or activity coefficient of one component is known as a function of composition, the property of the second component can be determined via integration.
  • Any partial molar property Ẏi satisfies a corresponding relation: ∑ xi dẎi = 0 at constant T and P.
  • The chemical potential of a volatile component is related to its vapor pressure via μi = μi° + RT ln Pi.

Physical Significance and Limitations

  • The equation proves that the chemical potentials of components in a mixture are not independent; changing the composition alters all chemical potentials.
  • It provides a rigorous test for the thermodynamic consistency of experimental vapor-liquid equilibrium (VLE) data.
  • The relation applies strictly to systems in internal thermodynamic equilibrium.
  • It fails if macroscopic fluctuations or external non-chemical fields alter the local intensive state of the system.

⚠️ Important Exceptions & Anomalies
Exception Gibbs–Duhem equation maintains constant T and P during compositional variations.

Observation: Non-isothermal or non-isobaric open systems. — When dT ≠ 0 or dP ≠ 0, the entropy and volume terms (-S dT + V dP) must be retained in the summation.

Exception Partial molar properties vary continuously across all mole fractions in ideal solutions.

Observation: Phase separation boundaries in strongly non-ideal liquid mixtures (mushy zones or immiscibility gaps). — Discontinuities in partial molar volumes or chemical potentials occur when a system splits into two coexisting phases.

Exception Chemical potential approaches negative infinity smoothly as mole fraction approaches zero.

Observation: Pure crystalline solids in heterogeneous multicomponent mixtures. — Pure solids maintain fixed standard states where activity equals unity and compositional derivatives vanish.

🎯 Key Exam Points
  • The Gibbs–Duhem equation relates changes in chemical potentials of components at constant temperature and pressure.
  • Mathematical expression for a binary system: n1 dμ1 + n2 dμ2 = 0.
  • The equation demonstrates that intensive properties in a mixture are interdependent.
  • It is derived by combining the total differential of Gibbs free energy with the Euler theorem for homogeneous functions.
  • Used extensively to test thermodynamic consistency of vapor-liquid equilibrium data.
  • Applies to any partial molar property: ∑ ni dẎi = 0.
🚫 Common Errors & Confusions
  • Assuming the Gibbs–Duhem equation holds when temperature and pressure are allowed to vary without including dT and dP terms.
  • Confusing total molar Gibbs energy with partial molar Gibbs energy (chemical potential).
  • Applying the binary relation to systems with ongoing chemical reactions without accounting for stoichiometric constraints.
  • Ignoring units when integrating chemical potential changes with respect to mole fractions.

⚡ Quick Revision: Gibbs–Duhem Equation

Core formulas, rapid recall points, and distinguishing criteria at a glance.

Thermodynamic Foundation

The Gibbs-Duhem equation stems from the exact differential of Gibbs energy in open systems, linking compositional changes to intensive variables.

Intensive Interdependence

It proves that in any mixture, the intensive properties cannot all be varied independently at fixed temperature and pressure.

Binary Solution Form

For binary mixtures, knowing the variation of one component's chemical potential allows exact calculation of the other component's potential.

Practical Testing

The equation provides the ultimate mathematical criterion for verifying experimental solution thermodynamics and phase equilibrium data.

📚 Terms & Units Glossary

Chemical Potential (μi)
Partial molar Gibbs energy of component i, J mol-1.
Mole Fraction (xi)
Ratio of moles of component i to total moles, dimensionless.
Activity (ai)
Effective concentration of a component in a non-ideal mixture, dimensionless.
Activity Coefficient (γi)
Ratio of activity to mole fraction (ai / xi), dimensionless.
Partial Molar Volume (V̇i)
Contribution of a component to the total volume of a mixture, m3 mol-1.
Gibbs Energy (G)
Thermodynamic potential measuring maximum non-expansion work, Joules.

  • ∑ ni dμi = 0 (at constant T, P)

  • x1 d(ln a1) + x2 d(ln a2) = 0
  • G = ∑ ni μi
  • dG = -S dT + V dP + ∑ μi dni

📌 Quick Recall

  • Gibbs–Duhem equation connects chemical potentials in multicomponent systems.
  • Requires constant temperature and pressure for its standard simplified form.
  • Proves intensive properties in solutions are mathematically bound.
  • Essential for validating vapor-liquid equilibrium data.

⚖️ Key Differences

Total Gibbs Energy (G) VS Chemical Potential (μi)

G is an extensive property for the whole system, whereas μi is an intensive partial molar property.

Question 3 of 18

Which thermodynamic property is equal to the chemical potential μi in a single-component system?

Explanation: For a pure substance, the chemical potential is simply the Gibbs free energy per mole (Gm).

Question 4 of 18

What does the Gibbs–Duhem equation imply about the intensive properties of a multicomponent mixture?

Explanation: Because the Gibbs–Duhem equation relates all chemical potentials, changing the concentration of one component forces adjustments in the chemical potentials of all other components.

Question 6 of 18

If the chemical potential of component 1 in a binary mixture increases at constant T and P, what must happen to the chemical potential of component 2?

Explanation: Since x1 dμ1 + x2 dμ2 = 0 and mole fractions are positive, if dμ1 is positive, dμ2 must be negative.

Question 7 of 18

Which of the following is the correct expression for the total differential of Gibbs energy in an open system?

Explanation: The fundamental thermodynamic equation for an open system with variable composition is dG = -S dT + V dP + ∑ μi dni.

Question 8 of 18

What is the primary practical application of the Gibbs–Duhem equation in chemical engineering and physical chemistry?

Explanation: Experimental vapor-liquid equilibrium data are routinely checked for thermodynamic consistency using the Gibbs–Duhem equation.

Question 9 of 18

For a partial molar property Ẏi, what is the Gibbs–Duhem type relation at constant T and P?

Explanation: Any partial molar property satisfies the Gibbs–Duhem relation ∑ xi dẎi = 0 at constant temperature and pressure.

Question 10 of 18

What is the value of ∑ ni dμi when a system is subjected to arbitrary changes in temperature and pressure?

Explanation: When T and P are not constant, the complete Gibbs–Duhem relation is -S dT + V dP = ∑ ni dμi.

Question 11 of 18

In a binary solution, if the mole fraction of component 1 is 0.7, what is the ratio dμ1 / dμ2?

Explanation: Using x1 dμ1 + x2 dμ2 = 0, the ratio dμ1 / dμ2 = -x2 / x1 = -0.3 / 0.7 = -2.33.

Question 12 of 18

Which quantity is classified as an intensive property among the following?

Explanation: Chemical potential is a partial molar property, which is inherently an intensive property independent of system size.

Question 13 of 18

What does Euler's theorem applied to homogeneous functions yield for the total Gibbs energy G?

Explanation: Since G is a homogeneous function of first degree in the mole numbers ni, Euler's theorem gives G = ∑ ni μi.

Question 14 of 18

Why cannot a binary liquid mixture have one component's chemical potential change arbitrarily while the other remains completely unaffected at constant T and P?

Explanation: The relation x1 dμ1 + x2 dμ2 = 0 strictly ties the variation of dμ1 to dμ2.

Question 15 of 18

What is the relationship between activity ai and chemical potential μi?

Explanation: The standard thermodynamic definition linking chemical potential to activity is μi = μi° + RT ln ai.

Question 16 of 18

If a system consists of 3 components, how many independent chemical potential variations exist at constant T and P?

Explanation: The Gibbs–Duhem equation provides one constraint among the 3 chemical potentials, leaving 3 - 1 = 2 independent variations.

Question 17 of 18

Which of the following describes a partial molar volume V̇i?

Explanation: Partial molar volume is defined as the rate of change of total volume with respect to the moles of component i at constant T, P, and other mole numbers.

Question 18 of 18

In an ideal binary solution, the activity ai is equal to:

Explanation: By definition, in an ideal solution, the activity of a component equals its mole fraction (ai = xi).

Question 1 of 10
Which differential equation correctly represents the Gibbs–Duhem relation when temperature and pressure are allowed to vary?

Solution: The full derivation equating the open-system Gibbs energy differential with Euler's expansion yields -S dT + V dP = ∑ ni dμi.

Question 2 of 10
What is the physical meaning of the chemical potential μi in a multicomponent phase equilibrium?

Solution: Chemical potential acts as the thermodynamic driving force or escaping tendency for mass transfer between phases.

Question 3 of 10
In a ternary solution (3 components), how many partial molar properties can be independently varied at constant T and P?

Solution: The Gibbs–Duhem constraint reduces the number of independent intensive property variations by 1, leaving 2 degrees of freedom for compositional changes.

Question 4 of 10
Which of the following is true for an ideal binary solution regarding activity coefficients (γi)?

Solution: By definition, ideal solutions follow Raoult's law across all concentrations, meaning activity coefficients are identically equal to 1.

Question 5 of 10
What does the equation x1 d(ln a1) + x2 d(ln a2) = 0 signify?

Solution: This is the logarithmic activity form of the Gibbs–Duhem equation used to test experimental phase equilibrium data.

Question 6 of 10
If dμ2 = 0 in a binary mixture at constant T and P, what must be true for dμ1, assuming non-zero mole fractions?

Solution: From x1 dμ1 + x2 dμ2 = 0, if x1 ≠ 0 and dμ2 = 0, then dμ1 must equal 0.

Question 7 of 10
Euler's theorem applies to which type of thermodynamic functions to yield relations like G = ∑ ni μi?

Solution: Euler's theorem applies strictly to homogeneous functions of first degree, such as extensive thermodynamic properties like G, V, H, and U.

Question 8 of 10
How does the Gibbs–Duhem equation assist in determining the partial molar volume V̇2 of solute when V̇1 is known across all concentrations?

Solution: Partial molar volumes obey the Gibbs–Duhem relation x1 dV̇1 + x2 dV̇2 = 0, allowing integration to find V̇2.

Question 9 of 10
What is the standard state chemical potential μi°?

Solution: The standard state chemical potential μi° is a reference value corresponding to the pure substance or standard state at the temperature of interest and standard pressure.

Question 10 of 10
Which limitation prevents the direct application of the Gibbs–Duhem equation to non-equilibrium systems?

Solution: The Gibbs–Duhem equation relies on state functions and exact differentials which require the system to be in internal thermodynamic equilibrium.
