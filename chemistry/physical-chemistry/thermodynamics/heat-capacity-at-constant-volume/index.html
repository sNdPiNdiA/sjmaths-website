Heat capacity at constant volume

Heat capacity at constant volume (Cv) is defined as the rate of change of internal energy with respect to temperature at constant volume. It measures the amount of heat required to raise the temperature of a given substance by one degree Celsius at a fixed volume.

UP PGT Chemistry Subject Code 02 B.Sc. / M.Sc. Level

Thermodynamic Definition and First Law Connection

  • At constant volume, the work done dW is zero since dV = 0.
  • According to the first law of thermodynamics, dU = dqv + dW, which simplifies to dqv = dU.
  • Consequently, the heat absorbed at constant volume is equal to the change in internal energy of the system.
  • Cv is a state function property derived from the exact differential of internal energy U with respect to temperature T.

Equipartition Theorem and Degrees of Freedom

  • According to the equipartition theorem, each active degree of freedom contributes (1/2)R to the molar heat capacity.
  • Monatomic gases possess 3 translational degrees of freedom, giving Cv,m = (3/2)R.
  • Rigid diatomic gases possess 3 translational and 2 rotational degrees of freedom, yielding Cv,m = (5/2)R.
  • Non-linear polyatomic gases possess 3 translational and 3 rotational degrees of freedom, giving Cv,m = 3R for rigid structures.

Temperature Dependence and Condensed Phases

  • Heat capacity approaches zero as the absolute temperature approaches zero, in accordance with the Third Law of Thermodynamics.
  • At extremely high temperatures, vibrational degrees of freedom become fully active, increasing the molar heat capacity of polyatomic gases.
  • For solids at very low temperatures, Debye's T3 law applies, where Cv is proportional to T3.
  • At high temperatures, solid heat capacities approach the constant limiting value of 3R according to the Dulong-Petit law.

⚠️ Important Exceptions & Anomalies
Exception Dulong-Petit law stating Cv ≈ 3R for all solid elements at room temperature

Observation: Diamond, beryllium, and boron show significantly lower heat capacities at room temperature — High vibrational frequencies due to strong covalent bonding and low atomic masses mean characteristic Debye temperatures are well above room temperature.

Exception Cv increases monotonically with temperature for all gases

Observation: H2 and D2 exhibit anomalous Cv steps at cryogenic temperatures — Quantum mechanical constraints on nuclear spin isomers (ortho and para hydrogen) restrict rotational energy level transitions at low temperatures.

Exception Mayer's relation (Cp - Cv = R) is universally applicable to all fluids

Observation: Fails for liquids and solids under standard conditions — Liquids and solids have very small coefficients of thermal expansion and are virtually incompressible, making volume work negligible.

💡 Shortcuts & Memory Aids
Equipartition Quick Count
Cv,m = [(3ftrans + 2frot + fvib) / 2] R

Application: Sum active translational (ftrans = 3), rotational (frot = 3 for non-linear, 2 for linear), and vibrational modes to instantly find Cv.

Adiabatic Index from Cv
γ = Cp / Cv = (Cv + R) / Cv

Application: Use this relation to compute the Poisson adiabatic constant directly from the constant volume heat capacity.

Internal Energy Increment
ΔU = n Cv ΔT

Application: Applies for any ideal gas process (isothermal, isobaric, or isochoric) because internal energy is strictly a state function of temperature.

🎯 Key Exam Points
  • Cv is defined as the partial derivative of internal energy with respect to temperature at constant volume.
  • For an ideal gas, internal energy and heat capacity at constant volume are strictly functions of temperature alone.
  • Mayer's relation states that Cp - Cv = R for one mole of an ideal gas.
  • The equipartition theorem assigns (1/2)R to each quadratic term in the energy expression.
  • At absolute zero (T = 0 K), the heat capacity of all substances approaches zero.
  • According to Dulong-Petit law, the molar heat capacity of solid elements at high temperatures is approximately 25 J K-1 mol-1.
🚫 Common Errors & Confusions
  • Assuming Cv applies to constant pressure processes; heat added at constant pressure equals enthalpy change, not internal energy change.
  • Using Cp instead of Cv in expressions for internal energy change (ΔU = n Cv ΔT).
  • Forgetting that vibrational degrees of freedom contribute 2 terms (kinetic and potential) per normal mode to energy, thus contributing R per mode to Cv.
  • Applying Mayer's relation (Cp - Cv = R) directly to condensed phases without accounting for thermal expansion coefficients and compressibility.

⚡ Quick Revision: Heat capacity at constant volume

Core formulas, rapid recall points, and distinguishing criteria at a glance.

Definition and Physical Significance

Heat capacity at constant volume measures the sensitivity of internal energy to temperature changes in an isochoric process. It equals the heat absorbed per unit temperature rise when volume is held fixed.

Molecular Interpretation

Statistical mechanics relates Cv to the distribution of thermal energy across translational, rotational, and vibrational degrees of freedom via the equipartition theorem.

Ideal Gas Relations

For ideal gases, Cv depends exclusively on atomicity and temperature. The difference between Cp and Cv is precisely the universal gas constant R.

Temperature Limits

At cryogenic temperatures, quantum effects dominate, causing Cv to vanish at absolute zero in agreement with the Third Law of Thermodynamics.

📚 Terms & Units Glossary

Heat Capacity at Constant Volume (Cv)
Rate of change of internal energy with temperature at constant volume; J K-1.
Molar Heat Capacity (Cv,m)
Heat capacity of one mole of a substance at constant volume; J mol-1 K-1.
Internal Energy (U)
Total microscopic energy contained within a thermodynamic system; Joules.
Equipartition Theorem (Energy Partition)
Principle stating that thermal energy is equally shared among active degrees of freedom.
Adiabatic Index (γ)
Ratio of heat capacities Cp / Cv; dimensionless.
Mayer's Relation (Cp - Cv = R)
Difference between molar heat capacities for an ideal gas.

🎯 Key Takeaways

  • Cv = (∂U / ∂T)v
  • dqv = dU
  • Cv,m (monatomic) = (3/2)R
  • Cv,m (diatomic) = (5/2)R
  • Cp - Cv = R

📌 Quick Recall

  • Cv measures internal energy variation with temperature at constant volume.
  • Energy is partitioned equally among active molecular degrees of freedom.
  • Ideal gas heat capacity depends strictly on molecular structure and temperature.
  • Quantum statistical mechanics dictates the temperature freeze-out of vibrational and rotational modes.

⚖️ Key Differences

Cv VS Cp

Cv measures energy change at constant volume (dq = dU), while Cp measures enthalpy change at constant pressure (dq = dH).

Question 1 of 18

What is the thermodynamic definition of heat capacity at constant volume (Cv)?

Explanation: Cv is fundamentally defined as the partial derivative of internal energy U with respect to temperature T at constant volume.

Question 2 of 18

For a monoatomic ideal gas, what is the theoretical value of molar heat capacity at constant volume (Cv,m)?

Explanation: A monoatomic ideal gas has 3 translational degrees of freedom, each contributing (1/2)R, resulting in 3 * (1/2)R = 1.5R.

Question 3 of 18

Which thermodynamic property is equal to heat absorbed at constant volume (dqv)?

Explanation: From the first law dU = dq + dW, at constant volume dW = 0, so dqv = dU.

Question 4 of 18

What is the value of Cp - Cv for one mole of an ideal gas?

Explanation: Mayer's relation establishes that for any ideal gas, Cp - Cv = R.

Question 5 of 18

According to the equipartition theorem, how much energy does each rotational degree of freedom contribute to molar heat capacity?

Explanation: Each quadratic term (translational or rotational kinetic energy) contributes (1/2)R to the molar heat capacity.

Question 6 of 18

What is the molar heat capacity at constant volume (Cv,m) for a rigid diatomic gas at moderate temperature?

Explanation: A rigid diatomic gas possesses 3 translational and 2 rotational degrees of freedom, giving a total of 5 active modes, leading to Cv,m = (5/2)R.

Question 7 of 18

At absolute zero (T = 0 K), what does the heat capacity of a pure crystalline solid approach?

Explanation: In accordance with the Third Law of Thermodynamics, the heat capacity of all pure crystalline substances approaches zero as temperature approaches absolute zero.

Question 8 of 18

What does Dulong-Petit law predict for the molar heat capacity of solid elements at high temperatures?

Explanation: Dulong-Petit law states that classical molar heat capacities of solid elements approach 3R (~25 J K-1 mol-1) at high temperatures.

Question 9 of 18

Why does diamond show a lower heat capacity than expected by Dulong-Petit law at room temperature?

Explanation: Strong carbon-carbon single bonds and low atomic mass result in high characteristic Debye temperatures, meaning vibrational modes are frozen out at room temperature.

Question 10 of 18

What is the relation between adiabatic index (γ), Cp, and Cv?

Explanation: The adiabatic index (γ) is defined as the ratio of heat capacity at constant pressure to that at constant volume (γ = Cp / Cv).

Question 11 of 18

For a non-linear polyatomic gas molecule with N atoms, how many vibrational degrees of freedom exist?

Explanation: A non-linear molecule has 3N total degrees of freedom, minus 3 translational and 3 rotational, leaving 3N - 6 vibrational modes.

Question 12 of 18

For a linear polyatomic gas molecule with N atoms, how many vibrational degrees of freedom exist?

Explanation: A linear molecule has 3 translational and only 2 rotational degrees of freedom, leaving 3N - 5 vibrational modes.

Question 13 of 18

How does internal energy change with temperature for an ideal gas at constant volume?

Explanation: For any ideal gas process, the internal energy change is given by ΔU = n Cv ΔT.

Question 14 of 18

Which law describes the low-temperature heat capacity behavior of crystalline solids proportional to T3?

Explanation: At low temperatures, lattice vibration contributions to heat capacity follow Debye's T3 law.

Question 15 of 18

Which of the following is a state function?

Explanation: Heat capacity is a state function property, whereas heat and work are path functions.

Question 16 of 18

What is the value of Cv,m for a non-linear polyatomic ideal gas at high temperatures when all vibrational modes are active?

Explanation: Translational (3/2 R) + rotational (3/2 R) = 3R. Each of the 3N-6 vibrational modes contributes R (kinetic + potential), giving Cv,m = 3R + (3N-6)R.

Question 17 of 18

Why is Cp always greater than Cv for gases?

Explanation: At constant pressure, heat added must both raise internal energy and perform expansion work (P ΔV), requiring more heat than at constant volume.

Question 18 of 18

What are the SI units of heat capacity at constant volume (Cv)?

Explanation: Extensive heat capacity has units of Joules per Kelvin (J K-1).

Question 2 of 10
Calculate the change in internal energy when 2 moles of a monatomic ideal gas are heated from 200 K to 300 K at constant volume. (R = 8.314 J mol-1 K-1)

Solution: ΔU = n Cv,m ΔT = 2 (1.5 8.314) (300 - 200) = 2 12.471 * 100 = 2494.2 J.

Question 3 of 10
What is the molar heat capacity at constant volume for a rigid diatomic gas?

Solution: Rigid diatomic gases possess 3 translational and 2 rotational degrees of freedom, giving Cv,m = 2.5R.

Question 4 of 10
Which law states that the molar heat capacity of solid elements at high temperatures is approximately 3R?

Solution: Dulong-Petit law establishes the classical limit of 3R for solid elemental heat capacities.

Question 5 of 10
What is the value of (∂U / ∂V)T for an ideal gas?

Solution: Internal energy of an ideal gas is independent of volume at constant temperature; hence (∂U / ∂V)T = 0.

Question 6 of 10
In an isochoric heating of a system, what is the work done by the system?

Solution: Since volume remains constant (dV = 0), expansion work P dV is identically zero.

Question 7 of 10
What is the adiabatic index (γ) for a monatomic ideal gas?

Solution: γ = Cp / Cv = (2.5R) / (1.5R) = 5/3 ≈ 1.67.

Question 8 of 10
Why does the molar heat capacity of gases generally increase with an increase in temperature?

Solution: Higher temperatures impart sufficient thermal energy to excite vibrational modes that were frozen out at lower temperatures.

Question 9 of 10
What does the Third Law of Thermodynamics state regarding Cv at absolute zero?

Solution: The entropy change of isothermal processes approaches zero as T → 0, which requires that heat capacities like Cv also approach zero.

Question 10 of 10
If Cp for a gas is 20.78 J mol-1 K-1 and R is 8.314 J mol-1 K-1, what is Cv?

Solution: Cv = Cp - R = 20.78 - 8.314 = 12.466 J mol-1 K-1.
