Physical Chemistry

Chemical Thermodynamics

Thermodynamics describes the macroscopic properties of matter and the energy transformations accompanying physical and chemical changes. It establishes the criteria for spontaneity and equilibrium in closed and open systems.

UP PGT Chemistry Subject Code 02 B.Sc. / M.Sc. Level

State Functions and Path Functions

  • State functions (e.g., U, H, G, S, P, V, T) depend only on the initial and final states, not the path taken.
  • Path functions (e.g., q, w) depend on the specific process path between two states.
  • For any cyclic process, the change in a state function is exactly zero (ΔX = 0).

Criteria for Spontaneity

  • A process is spontaneous if ΔG < 0 at constant T and P.
  • At equilibrium, ΔG = 0 and ΔG° = -RT ln K.
  • Entropy of the universe (ΔSuniv = ΔSsys + ΔSsurr) must be > 0 for spontaneous processes.

Thermochemistry Principles

  • Hess's Law states that the total enthalpy change for a reaction is the same regardless of the number of steps.
  • Kirchhoff's Law relates the change in enthalpy of a reaction to the heat capacity difference: ΔHT2 = ΔHT1 + ∫ ΔCp dT.
  • Standard enthalpy of formation (ΔHf°) of an element in its most stable form is defined as zero.

⚠️ Important Exceptions & Anomalies
Exception Entropy increases in all spontaneous processes

Observation: Formation of ordered crystals from solution — The entropy of the surroundings increases significantly due to the release of heat (exothermic process), making ΔStotal > 0.

Exception Heat capacity is always positive

Observation: Negative heat capacity in self-gravitating systems — In stellar thermodynamics, increasing energy leads to expansion and cooling, resulting in a negative effective heat capacity.

Exception Ideal gas internal energy depends only on temperature

Observation: Real gases — Intermolecular forces and finite molecular volume cause U to depend on volume/pressure in real gases.

Work done BY system = Negative; Work done ON system = Positive

If ΔH is (-) and ΔS is (+), always spontaneous.

🎯 Key Exam Points
  • For an ideal gas, ΔH = ΔU + Δ(PV) = ΔU + ΔnRT.
  • Joule-Thomson coefficient μ = (∂T/∂P)H.
  • Third Law of Thermodynamics: Entropy of a perfect crystal at 0 K is zero.
  • Work done in vacuum (free expansion) is always zero.
  • For adiabatic processes, q = 0, so ΔU = w.
  • Enthalpy of combustion is always negative.
🚫 Common Errors & Confusions
  • Confusing ΔH and ΔU; remember ΔH = ΔU + PΔV only at constant pressure.
  • Forgetting to convert temperature to Kelvin in gas law calculations.
  • Assuming ΔH = 0 for isothermal processes in real gases (it is only 0 for ideal gases).
  • Using the wrong units for R (8.314 J/mol·K vs 0.0821 L·atm/mol·K).

⚡ Quick Revision: Chemical Thermodynamics

Core formulas, rapid recall points, and distinguishing criteria at a glance.

First Law

Conservation of energy principle: ΔU = q + w. Energy cannot be created or destroyed, only transferred.

Second Law

Entropy of the universe increases for any spontaneous process. Defines the direction of energy flow.

Third Law

Absolute zero cannot be reached in a finite number of steps. Defines the zero-point entropy of crystals.

Gibbs Energy

Combines enthalpy and entropy to predict spontaneity. ΔG = ΔH - TΔS.

📚 Terms & Units Glossary

Enthalpy (H)
Heat content of a system at constant pressure.
Entropy (S)
Measure of molecular disorder or randomness.
Gibbs Energy (G)
Maximum non-expansion work available.
Isothermal (dT = 0)
Process occurring at constant temperature.
Adiabatic (dq = 0)
Process with no heat exchange.
Isobaric (dP = 0)
Process occurring at constant pressure.

🎯 Key Takeaways

  • ΔG° = -nFE°cell
  • ΔG = ΔG° + RT ln Q
  • Cp - Cv = R
  • ΔU = nCvΔT
  • ΔH = nCpΔT

📌 Quick Recall

  • State functions are path-independent.
  • Spontaneity requires ΔG < 0.
  • Work is path-dependent.
  • Heat capacity relates heat to temperature change.

⚖️ Key Differences

Extensive Property VS Intensive Property

Extensive depends on mass (e.g., V, H); Intensive is independent (e.g., T, P, density).
