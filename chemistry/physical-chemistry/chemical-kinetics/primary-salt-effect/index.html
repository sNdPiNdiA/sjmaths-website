Primary Salt Effect in Ionic Reactions

The primary salt effect describes the alteration of reaction rates between ions in solution due to the presence of added inert electrolytes that modify the ionic atmosphere. It is quantitatively governed by the Debye-Hückel limiting law through changes in the activity coefficients of the reacting ions and the activated complex.

UP PGT Chemistry Subject Code 02 B.Sc. / M.Sc. Level

Debye-Hückel Theory of Ionic Atmospheres and Activity

  • The addition of an inert electrolyte increases the ionic strength (I) of the solution, compacting the ionic atmosphere surrounding reacting ions.
  • According to the Debye-Hückel limiting law, the activity coefficient (γ) of an ion of charge z decreases as ionic strength increases according to log γi = -0.509 zi2 √I.

Brønsted-Bjerrum Kinetics Formulation

  • For a reaction between ions AzA and BzB, the activated complex has a charge of (zA + zB).
  • When the charges of reacting ions have the same sign (zAzB > 0), increasing ionic strength increases the reaction rate.
  • When the charges have opposite signs (zAzB < 0), increasing ionic strength decreases the reaction rate.
  • When one reactant is neutral (zA = 0 or zB = 0), the primary salt effect is zero at low ionic strengths because zAzB = 0.

Experimental Verification and Limitations

  • Plots of log k versus √I yield straight lines with slopes equal to 1.02 zA zB in dilute aqueous solutions at 298 K.
  • Deviations from linearity occur at higher ionic strengths where the extended Debye-Hückel equation must be used instead of the limiting law.
  • The primary salt effect is distinct from the secondary salt effect, which involves changes in concentration of reacting ions via equilibria such as weak acid dissociation.
📐 Key Formulas & Equations
Ionic Strength Definition
I = 0.5 ∑ ci zi2
Conditions: Applicable to all dilute electrolyte solutions containing multiple ionic species.
Units: ci in mol L-1, zi is dimensionless charge number
Rate Constant Dependence on Activity Coefficients
k = k0 (γA γB) / γ‡
Conditions: General relationship for reactions between ions A and B forming activated complex ‡.
Units: γ represents activity coefficients (dimensionless)
Logarithmic Rate Form with Activity Coefficients
log k = log k0 + log γA + log γB - log γ‡
Conditions: Valid for elementary ionic reactions in solution.
Units: Rate constants and activity coefficients are dimensionless in logarithmic ratios
Brønsted-Bjerrum Equation for Primary Salt Effect
log k = log k0 + 1.02 zA zB √I
Conditions: Dilute aqueous solutions at 298 K, where Debye-Hückel limiting law applies.
Units: k and k0 are rate constants (concentration1-n time-1), I is ionic strength (mol kg-1)
⚠️ Important Exceptions & Anomalies
Exception Reactions between ions of the same sign always accelerate with added inert electrolyte.

Observation: High ionic strength regimes where ion pairing occurs. — At high concentrations, ion-pair formation effectively reduces the net charge and alters the distance of closest approach, causing deviations from the limiting slope.

Exception Uncharged reactant reactions show zero primary salt effect.

Observation: Dipolar or solvent-dependent reactions at high salt concentrations. — High electrolyte concentrations alter the dielectric constant of the medium, indirectly affecting rates through solvent restructuring.

💡 Shortcuts & Memory Aids
Sign Rule for Slope
Slope = 1.02 × (zA × zB)

Application: Multiply reactant charges to instantly determine if log k vs √I has a positive or negative slope.

Neutral Reactant Shortcut
If zA = 0 or zB = 0 ⇒ Δlog k = 0

Application: Immediately identify that reactions involving at least one neutral molecule are independent of ionic strength in the dilute region.

Ionic Strength Calculation Hack
I = 0.5 ∑ (ci × zi2)

Application: For 1:1 electrolytes like NaCl, I equals the molar concentration. For 1:2 electrolytes like Na2SO4, I = 3 × c.

  • Addition of KNO3 to a reaction between two negatively charged ions increases the rate constant.

🚫 Common Errors & Confusions
  • Confusing primary salt effect (which alters rate constants) with secondary salt effect (which alters reactant concentrations).
  • Dropping the factor of 0.5 when calculating ionic strength for unsymmetrical electrolytes.
  • Assuming neutral reactants show salt effects at low concentrations.
  • Incorrectly assigning charges to the activated complex when calculating the Brønsted-Bjerrum slope.

Core formulas, rapid recall points, and distinguishing criteria at a glance.

Relates single-ion activity coefficients to ionic strength: log γi = -AZi2√I.

Expresses rate constant variation with ionic strength: log k = log k0 + 1.02 zA zB √I.

Charge Product Criterion

📚 Terms & Units Glossary

Ionic Strength (I)
A measure of the electrical intensity of a solution taking into account all ions present.
Activity Coefficient (γ)
Dimensionless factor relating effective concentration to actual concentration.
Activated Complex (‡)
High-energy transition state species formed during the rate-determining step.
Brønsted-Bjerrum Equation (log k = log k0 + 1.02 zA zB √I)
Mathematical formula describing primary salt effect.
Primary Salt Effect (Kinetic salt effect)
Influence of inert electrolytes on the rate constants of ionic reactions.
Secondary Salt Effect (Equilibrium salt effect)
Influence of inert electrolytes on reactant concentrations via equilibria.

🎯 Key Takeaways

  • Slope of log k vs √I equals 1.02 zA zB at 298 K in water.
  • I = 0.5 ∑ ci zi2
  • For oppositely charged ions, rate decreases with increasing ionic strength.
  • For like-charged ions, rate increases with increasing ionic strength.
  • Reactions involving uncharged species show no primary salt effect.

📌 Quick Recall

  • Primary salt effect alters reaction rate constants via activity coefficient modifications.
  • Governed by Debye-Hückel limiting law and Brønsted-Bjerrum kinetics.
  • Depends entirely on the charges of the reacting ions and ionic strength.
  • Valid primarily in dilute solutions where Debye-Hückel limiting law holds.

⚖️ Key Differences

Primary Salt Effect VS Secondary Salt Effect

Primary affects rate constants via activity coefficients; secondary affects reactant concentrations via chemical equilibria.

Question 1 of 19

What is the primary salt effect on the rate constant of a reaction between two negatively charged ions?

Explanation: Like-charged ions (zAzB > 0) form an activated complex with a higher magnitude charge, stabilizing it more at higher ionic strength, thus increasing the rate constant.

Question 2 of 19

For a reaction between a cation and an anion, the slope of log k versus √I is:

Explanation: Since one ion is positive and the other negative, zAzB < 0, making the slope negative.

Question 3 of 19

What is the ionic strength of a 0.05 M solution of BaCl2?

Explanation: I = 0.5 × (0.05 × (+2)2 + 0.10 × (-1)2) = 0.5 × (0.20 + 0.10) = 0.15 M.

Question 4 of 19

In the reaction between H2O2 and I- in acidic medium, what is the primary salt effect if the rate-determining step involves H+, I-, and H2O2 (neutral)?

Explanation: The rate-determining step involves H+ and I- (both charged), so zAzB = (+1)(-1) = -1, leading to a negative salt effect.

Question 5 of 19

The Debye-Hückel limiting law is valid only in:

Explanation: The limiting law relies on simplifying assumptions applicable strictly to dilute electrolyte solutions (I ≤ 0.01 M).

Question 6 of 19

What is the slope of log k vs √I for a reaction between two uncharged molecules?

Explanation: When zA = 0 or zB = 0, zAzB = 0, making the slope zero.

Question 7 of 19

Which equation relates the rate constant k to ionic strength I?

Explanation: The Brønsted-Bjerrum equation explicitly links the rate constant to ionic strength and ion charges.

Question 8 of 19

If the ionic strength of a solution is quadrupled, the value of √I is:

Explanation: The square root of 4I is 2√I, meaning it is doubled.

Question 9 of 19

In the Brønsted-Bjerrum equation, what does z‡ represent?

Explanation: z‡ represents the electrical charge of the activated transition state complex.

Question 10 of 19

Adding an inert salt like NaCl to a kinetic system affects:

Explanation: The primary salt effect operates by altering the activity coefficients of the ions via the ionic atmosphere.

Question 11 of 19

What is the ionic strength of a 0.1 M NaCl solution?

Explanation: For a 1:1 electrolyte like NaCl, ionic strength equals the molar concentration (0.1 M).

Question 12 of 19

For a reaction where zA = +2 and zB = +2, the slope of log k vs √I at 298 K is approximately:

Explanation: Slope = 1.02 × (+2) × (+2) = 1.02 × 4 = 4.08.

Question 13 of 19

The primary salt effect is independent of:

Explanation: As long as the electrolyte is inert and contributes to the ionic strength I, its specific chemical identity does not matter in the limiting law.

Question 14 of 19

Which of the following reactions will show a positive primary salt effect?

Explanation: S2O82- and I- are both negatively charged (like-charged), so zAzB > 0, yielding a positive salt effect.

Question 15 of 19

The units of ionic strength I are:

Explanation: Ionic strength can be expressed in molarity (mol L-1) or molality (mol kg-1).

Question 16 of 19

What happens to the activity coefficient of an ion as the ionic strength of the solution increases?

Explanation: According to the Debye-Hückel law, log γi is negative, meaning activity coefficients decrease as ionic strength increases in dilute solutions.

Question 17 of 19

In the Brønsted-Bjerrum rate equation, k0 represents:

Explanation: k0 is the rate constant extrapolated to zero ionic strength (infinite dilution where activity coefficients equal unity).

Question 18 of 19

Which factor primarily causes deviations from the Debye-Hückel limiting law at higher ionic strengths?

Explanation: At higher concentrations, finite ion sizes cannot be neglected, requiring the extended Debye-Hückel equation.

Question 19 of 19

If zAzB = -2, an increase in ionic strength will:

Explanation: A negative product of charges results in a negative slope in the log k vs √I plot, meaning the rate decreases as ionic strength increases.
