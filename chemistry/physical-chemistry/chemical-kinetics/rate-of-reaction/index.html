Physical Chemistry

Rate of Reaction

The rate of a chemical reaction is defined as the change in concentration of a reactant or product per unit time. Mathematically, it is expressed as the differential change in molar concentration divided by the stoichiometric coefficient.

UP PGT Chemistry Subject Code 02 B.Sc. / M.Sc. Level

Expressing Reaction Rates

  • Reactants show a negative sign in the rate expression to denote consumption and decreasing concentration over time.
  • Products show a positive sign in the rate expression to denote formation and increasing concentration over time.
  • Stoichiometric coefficients are used as reciprocal divisors to equate rates of disappearance of reactants to rates of appearance of products.

Factors Affecting Reaction Rate

  • According to the collision theory, increasing reactant concentration increases the frequency of effective binary collisions per unit volume.
  • Raising the temperature increases the fraction of molecules possessing kinetic energy equal to or greater than the activation energy (Ea).
  • A catalyst alters the reaction pathway, providing an alternative mechanism with a lower activation energy without changing overall enthalpy (ΔH°).

Differential and Integrated Rate Laws

  • The rate law expresses the experimental rate as a direct proportionality to the product of molar concentrations of reactants raised to their respective partial orders.
  • Molecularity is a theoretical, integer-only property of an elementary step, whereas order of reaction is an experimental value that can be zero, fractional, or negative.
  • The half-life (t1/2) of a zero-order reaction is directly proportional to initial concentration, while for a first-order reaction, it is entirely independent of initial concentration.
📐 Key Formulas & Equations
Instantaneous Rate of Reaction
rinst = ± d[C] / dt
Conditions: At a specific instant t
Units: mol L-1 s-1
Rate for Stoichiometric Equation aA + bB → cC + dD
r = -(1/a)(d[A]/dt) = -(1/b)(d[B]/dt) = +(1/c)(d[C]/dt) = +(1/d)(d[D]/dt)
Conditions: General balanced chemical equation
Units: mol L-1 s-1
Arrhenius Equation
k = A e-Ea / RT
Conditions: Temperature dependence of rate constant
Units: k in appropriate order units, Ea in J mol-1
Logarithmic Arrhenius Equation
log(k2 / k1) = (Ea / 2.303 R) [(T2 - T1) / (T1 T2)]
Conditions: Between two distinct temperatures T1 and T2
Units: T in Kelvin, R in J K-1 mol-1
Average Rate of Reaction
ravg = ± Δ[C] / Δt
Conditions: Finite interval of time Δt
Units: mol L-1 s-1
⚠️ Important Exceptions & Anomalies
Exception Reaction rate generally increases uniformly with an increase in reactant concentration.

Observation: Zero-order reactions — The rate is completely independent of the concentration of reactants and depends solely on external factors like light intensity or surface adsorption.

Exception Molecularity and order of a reaction are identical for elementary reactions.

Observation: Complex reactions involving chain mechanisms or intermediates — The overall stoichiometric order is determined by the slowest elementary step (rate-determining step) and may differ completely from the molecularity of the overall equation.

Exception Temperature rise always accelerates reaction rates by increasing kinetic energy.

Observation: Exomolecular explosive reactions and certain enzyme-catalyzed reactions at high temperature — Thermal denaturation of protein-based enzymes or complex chain termination mechanisms can cause a sudden decline in rate at elevated temperatures.

💡 Shortcuts & Memory Aids
Temperature Coefficient Rule
k(T+10) / kT ≈ 2 to 3

Application: Use this factor to quickly estimate how much a reaction rate multiplies when the absolute temperature is raised by 10 Kelvin.

Fractional Life Time Formula for nth Order
tf ∝ 1 / [A0]n-1

Application: Quickly determine the dependence of any fractional life (e.g., 75% completion) on initial concentration for nth order kinetics.

Unit Determination of Rate Constant (k)
Units of k = (mol L-1)1-n s-1

🎯 Key Exam Points
  • Rate of a reaction has units of mol L-1 time-1, whereas zero-order rate constant has the same unit.
  • First-order rate constant has units of s-1 or min-1, independent of concentration units.
  • The slope of the Arrhenius plot of ln k versus 1/T yields -Ea / R.
  • Photochemical reactions like H2 + Cl2 → 2HCl are generally zero-order with respect to reactants.
  • Hydrolysis of an ester in the presence of an excess of water is an example of a pseudo first-order reaction.
  • A negative activation energy is observed in certain radical recombination gas-phase reactions where no potential energy barrier exists.
🚫 Common Errors & Confusions
  • Confusing molecularity with order of reaction; remember molecularity is strictly theoretical, order is experimental.
  • Omitting stoichiometric coefficients in denominator when expressing overall rate for reactions with unbalanced or non-unity coefficients.
  • Using natural log (ln) instead of common log (log 10) in the integrated Arrhenius equation without accounting for the 2.303 multiplier.
  • Assuming reaction rate remains constant throughout a run; rate continuously decreases as reactants are consumed (except for zero-order).

⚡ Quick Revision: Rate of Reaction

Core formulas, rapid recall points, and distinguishing criteria at a glance.

Definition and Expression

Reaction rate measures the speed of chemical transformation via concentration changes over time, formalized through differential expressions with stoichiometric coefficients.

Rate Law and Order

The rate law correlates reaction rate directly to reactant concentrations raised to experimental powers termed reaction orders.

Temperature Dependence

The Arrhenius equation mathematically relates the exponential temperature dependence of the rate constant to activation energy.

Collision Theory

Chemical reaction rates depend on effective collisions meeting proper spatial orientation and threshold activation energy criteria.

📚 Terms & Units Glossary

Reaction Rate (r = d[C]/dt)
Change in concentration per unit time. Unit: mol L-1 s-1.
Rate Constant (k)
Proportionality constant in rate law equal to rate when concentrations are unity.
Order of Reaction (n)
Sum of concentration exponents in the experimental rate law.
Molecularity (M)
Number of reacting particles colliding in an elementary step.
Activation Energy (Ea)
Minimum kinetic energy barrier required for reactants to react. Unit: J mol-1.
Frequency Factor (A)
Pre-exponential constant related to collision frequency and spatial orientation.

🎯 Key Takeaways

  • Rate = -(1/a) d[A]/dt = +(1/c) d[C]/dt
  • log(k2/k1) = (Ea/2.303R) [(T2-T1)/(T1T2)]
  • t1/2 for first order = 0.693 / k
  • Units of zero order rate constant: mol L-1 s-1
  • Units of first order rate constant: s-1

📌 Quick Recall

  • Rates quantify reactant consumption and product formation speed.
  • Rate law equations are strictly determined by experiment, not stoichiometry.
  • Temperature elevation increases rate constants by lowering fraction below Ea.
  • Catalysts accelerate forward and backward rates equally by lowering Ea.

⚖️ Key Differences

Order VS Molecularity

Order is experimental and can be fractional/zero; molecularity is theoretical and must be a positive integer.

Question 1 of 20

What are the units of the rate constant for a zero-order reaction?

Explanation: For a zero-order reaction, Rate = k[A]0 = k, hence units of k are identical to rate: mol L-1 s-1.

Question 2 of 20

Which of the following parameters can have a fractional value?

Explanation: Order of a reaction is determined experimentally and can take zero, fractional, or negative values, unlike molecularity.

Question 3 of 20

In the reaction 2A + B → 3C, the rate of disappearance of A is related to the rate of formation of C by:

Explanation: Rate = -(1/2)d[A]/dt = +(1/3)d[C]/dt, which rearranges to d[C]/dt = +(3/2)(-d[A]/dt).

Question 4 of 20

The temperature coefficient for most chemical reactions lies between:

Explanation: For most chemical reactions, raising the temperature by 10 K doubles or triples the rate constant, giving a coefficient of 2 to 3.

Question 5 of 20

What is the order of a reaction whose rate constant has the unit L mol-1 s-1?

Explanation: Using (mol L-1)1-n s-1, equating to L mol-1 s-1 yields 1 - n = -1, so n = 2.

Question 6 of 20

Which factor decreases the activation energy of a chemical reaction?

Explanation: A catalyst provides an alternative reaction mechanism with a lower activation energy.

Question 7 of 20

Hydrolysis of methyl acetate in aqueous hydrochloric acid is an example of:

Explanation: Water is present in large excess, making its concentration effectively constant, turning second-order kinetics into pseudo first-order.

Question 8 of 20

The slope of the straight line obtained by plotting ln k versus 1/T in the Arrhenius plot is equal to:

Explanation: From ln k = ln A - (Ea / R)(1/T), the slope of ln k vs 1/T is -Ea / R.

Question 9 of 20

What happens to the half-life of a first-order reaction when the initial concentration of the reactant is doubled?

Explanation: The half-life of a first-order reaction is t1/2 = 0.693 / k, which is independent of initial concentration.

Question 10 of 20

Collision theory takes into account which of the following factors for an effective collision?

Explanation: An effective collision requires molecules to possess threshold energy (Ea) and proper steric orientation.

Question 11 of 20

The rate law for a reaction is Rate = k[A]1/2 [B]3/2. What is the overall order of the reaction?

Explanation: Overall order is the sum of exponents: 1/2 + 3/2 = 4/2 = 2.

Question 12 of 20

Which of the following reactions is an example of a zero-order reaction?

Explanation: Decomposition of gases on solid surfaces at high pressure becomes zero-order because active sites are fully saturated.

Question 13 of 20

If the activation energy of a reaction is zero, the rate constant is:

Explanation: When Ea = 0, k = A e0 = A, meaning the rate constant equals the pre-exponential factor.

Question 14 of 20

Which of the following parameters does not affect the rate of a chemical reaction?

Explanation: Overall enthalpy change (ΔH) is a thermodynamic state function and does not determine kinetic reaction rates.

Question 15 of 20

For a second-order reaction with reactant A, the unit of rate constant k is:

Explanation: For n = 2, units are (mol L-1)1-2 s-1 = L mol-1 s-1 (or mol-1 L s-1).

Question 16 of 20

In a complex reaction, the overall rate of reaction is governed by:

Explanation: The rate-determining step is the slowest elementary step which dictates the overall rate of the reaction.

Question 17 of 20

What is the molecularity of a termolecular elementary reaction?

Explanation: Termolecular reactions involve three reacting particles colliding simultaneously, giving a molecularity of 3.

Question 18 of 20

If the rate expression for a reaction is Rate = k[A]2[B], what is the order with respect to B?

Explanation: The power of the concentration term [B] in the rate law is 1, so the order with respect to B is 1.

Question 19 of 20

Which mathematical relationship represents the Arrhenius activation energy effect?

Explanation: k = A e-Ea / RT shows that k decreases exponentially as activation energy Ea increases.

Question 20 of 20

The time required for 99.9% completion of a first-order reaction is equal to how many half-lives?

Explanation: 99.9% completion corresponds to approximately 10 half-lives (t99.9% = 10 × t1/2).

UP PGT 2013
Which of the following is true for the elementary reaction A + 2B → Products?
Answer: Option C (Order can be fractional, Molecularity = 3) For an elementary reaction, molecularity is fixed by stoichiometry (1 + 2 = 3), whereas order is experimental and can be fractional under modified conditions.
UP PGT 2009
In the reaction N2(g) + 3H2(g) → 2NH3(g), the rate of formation of NH3 is 2 × 10-4 mol L-1 s-1. What is the rate of consumption of H2?
Answer: Option A (3 × 10-4 mol L-1 s-1) Rate = -(1/3)(d[H2]/dt) = +(1/2)(d[NH3]/dt). Thus, -(d[H2]/dt) = (3/2) × (2 × 10-4) = 3 × 10-4 mol L-1 s-1.

Question 1 of 10
For a first-order reaction, the time required for 99% completion is how many times the time required for 90% completion?

Solution: t99% = (2.303/k) log(100/1) = (2.303/k)(2). t90% = (2.303/k) log(100/10) = (2.303/k)(1). Thus, t99% / t90% = 2/1 = 2.

Question 2 of 10
If the rate constant of a reaction is k = 3 × 10-4 s-1, the order of the reaction is:

Solution: The unit s-1 corresponds uniquely to a first-order rate constant.

Question 3 of 10
Which of the following statements about activation energy is correct?

Solution: Activation energy is a characteristic constant independent of temperature and is reduced in the presence of a positive catalyst.

Question 4 of 10
In a zero-order reaction, if the initial concentration is doubled, the half-life of the reaction:

Solution: For zero-order, t1/2 = [A0] / 2k, showing that half-life is directly proportional to initial concentration.

Question 5 of 10
The rate of a reaction quadruples when temperature increases from 290 K to 310 K. What is the approximate activation energy?

Solution: Using log(k2/k1) = (Ea/2.303R)[(ΔT)/(T1T2)], log(4) = (Ea/2.303 × 8.314)[20 / (290 × 310)], yielding Ea ≈ 52.8 kJ mol-1.

Question 6 of 10
Which term in the Arrhenius equation represents the maximum fraction of collisions that have energy exceeding activation energy at temperature T?

Solution: The exponential term e-Ea/RT represents the Boltzmann fraction of molecules with kinetic energy ≥ Ea.

Question 7 of 10
For the reaction 2NO(g) + O2(g) → 2NO2(g), rate = k[NO]2[O2]. If the volume of the reaction vessel is suddenly reduced to half of its initial volume, the rate of reaction will:

Solution: Halving the volume doubles concentrations ([NO] → 2[NO], [O2] → 2[O2]). Rate ∝ (2)2(2)1 = 8 times increase.

Question 8 of 10
The rate constant of a reaction depends upon:

Solution: Rate constant k is dependent on temperature and presence of catalysts, but independent of reactant concentrations and time.

Question 9 of 10
What is the order of reaction for radioactive decay of unstable nuclides?

Solution: Radioactive decay processes strictly follow first-order kinetics.

Question 10 of 10
Which of the following conditions is mandatory for a collision to be effective?

Solution: Effective collisions require both threshold energy (energy barrier) and appropriate steric orientation between reacting molecules.
