Theories of Catalysis

Catalysis is the acceleration of a chemical reaction by means of a substance known as a catalyst that remains chemically unaltered at the end of the reaction. The mechanisms are fundamentally classified into intermediate compound formation theory and the modern adsorption theory.

UP PGT Chemistry Subject Code 02 B.Sc. / M.Sc. Level

Intermediate Compound Formation Theory

  • Proposes that the catalyst reacts with one of the reactants to form an unstable intermediate complex.
  • The intermediate subsequently reacts with the other reactant to yield the final product and regenerate the original catalyst.
  • Explains homogeneous catalysis effectively, such as the catalytic role of nitric oxide in the lead chamber process.
  • Fails to explain heterogeneous surface catalysis and the action of catalytic promoters or poisons.

Modern Adsorption Theory of Heterogeneous Catalysis

  • Combines the intermediate compound theory with physical and chemical adsorption on solid catalyst surfaces.
  • Step 1 involves diffusion of reactants to the catalyst surface followed by chemisorption at active centers with unsatisfied valencies.
  • Step 2 involves lowering of activation energy due to bond weakening in adsorbed reactant molecules.
  • Step 3 involves surface reaction to form products, followed by desorption and diffusion of products away from the surface.

Enzyme Catalysis and Kinetic Theories

  • Enzymes are highly specific protein catalysts functioning via lock-and-key or induced-fit mechanisms.
  • The Michaelis constant (Km) represents the substrate concentration at which the reaction velocity is half of its maximum value (Vmax).
  • Small values of Km indicate high affinity between the enzyme and the substrate.
  • Enzyme activity exhibits sharp optima at specific pH and temperature ranges due to protein denaturation.
📐 Key Formulas & Equations
Langmuir Adsorption Isotherm
θ = KP1 + KP
Conditions: Unimolecular surface adsorption at constant temperature
Units: θ = fractional coverage, P = pressure in atm, K = adsorption coefficient
Rate Law for Surface Catalyzed Bimolecular Reaction (Langmuir-Hinshelwood)
Rate = k KA PA KB PB(1 + KAPA + KBPB)2
Conditions: Reaction between two adsorbed gases A and B
Units: Rate in mol L-1 s-1, P in atm
Rate Law for Desorption-Controlled Surface Reaction
Rate = k θ = k K P1 + KP
Conditions: When surface product desorption is the rate-determining step
Units: Rate in mol L-1 s-1
Enzyme Catalysis Rate (Michaelis-Menten Equation)
v = Vmax [S]Km + [S]
Conditions: Single-substrate enzyme-catalyzed reactions under steady-state approximation
Units: v, Vmax in mol L-1 s-1, [S], Km in mol L-1
Arrhenius Equation with Catalyst
k = A e-(Ea - ΔEa)/RT
Conditions: Presence of catalyst lowering activation energy by ΔEa
Units: k in s-1 or L mol-1 s-1, Ea in J mol-1
⚠️ Important Exceptions & Anomalies
Exception Catalysts increase the rate of both forward and reverse reactions equally

Observation: Chemically altered catalytic media in open systems — If a reaction product permanently binds to the catalyst active site (poisoning), the reverse reaction rate is selectively suppressed.

Exception Rate of heterogeneous catalytic reactions increases indefinitely with reactant pressure

Observation: Zero-order kinetics at high pressures — At very high pressures, the catalyst surface becomes completely saturated (θ ≈ 1), making the rate independent of reactant concentration.

Exception Catalysts do not alter the position of chemical equilibrium

Observation: Apparent equilibrium shifts in coupled systems — If the catalyst preferentially side-tracks one product into an irreversible secondary reaction, the overall yield ratio shifts, though the primary equilibrium constant remains unchanged.

💡 Shortcuts & Memory Aids
Michaelis-Menten Limiting Cases
If [S] << Km, Rate ≈ (Vmax/Km)[S] (First order); If [S] >> Km, Rate ≈ Vmax (Zero order)

Application: Quickly determine the apparent reaction order in enzyme kinetics based on substrate concentration relative to Km.

🎯 Key Exam Points
  • A catalyst alters the reaction path by providing an alternative mechanism with a lower activation energy (ΔEa).
  • Catalysts do not change the thermodynamic parameters such as standard Gibbs free energy change (ΔG°) and enthalpy change (ΔH°).
  • Promoters (e.g., Mo in Haber's process for ammonia) enhance the activity of the catalyst by increasing surface irregularities or active centers.
  • Catalytic poisons (e.g., CO or H2S in platinum catalysts) act by preferential strong chemisorption, blocking active sites.
  • Autocatalysis occurs when one of the reaction products acts as a catalyst for the reaction, exhibiting an initial slow rate followed by rapid acceleration.
  • Negative catalysts (inhibitors) decrease the reaction rate by destroying positive catalysts or breaking chain propagation steps.
🚫 Common Errors & Confusions
  • Assuming a catalyst changes the equilibrium constant (Kc or Kp); remember it only accelerates the attainment of equilibrium.
  • Confusing physical adsorption (multilayer, weak van der Waals) with chemisorption (monolayer, strong chemical bonds) in heterogeneous catalysis theories.
  • Believing a catalyst is completely consumed; while it participates in intermediate steps, it is quantitatively regenerated.
  • Applying high-pressure zero-order Langmuir kinetics when the surface coverage is far from saturation.

⚡ Quick Revision: Theories of Catalysis

Core formulas, rapid recall points, and distinguishing criteria at a glance.

Thermodynamic vs Kinetic Role

Catalysts modify only the pre-exponential factor or the activation energy in kinetics, leaving overall reaction thermodynamics (ΔG, ΔH, Keq) untouched.

Intermediate Compound Formation

Involves formation of distinct covalent or coordination intermediates between the catalyst and reagents, prominent in solution-phase reactions.

Adsorption Theory Mechanics

Relies on surface chemisorption, bond weakening, surface migration, reaction, and product desorption on solid heterogeneous surfaces.

Enzyme Kinetics and Specificity

Governed by enzyme-substrate complexes and characterized by the Michaelis-Menten constant Km reflecting binding affinity and maximum turnover.

📚 Terms & Units Glossary

Catalyst (C)
Substance altering reaction rate without being consumed.
Promoter (P)
Substance that increases the efficiency of a catalyst.
Catalytic Poison (Poison)
Substance that inactivates catalyst active sites.
Active Center (Active Site)
Unsaturated surface atoms where chemisorption occurs.
Turnover Number (TON)
Number of reactant moles converted per mole of catalyst per unit time.
Michaelis Constant (Km)
Substrate concentration at half-maximal enzyme velocity (mol L-1).

🎯 Key Takeaways

  • Catalysts lower activation energy (ΔEa)
  • Thermodynamic equilibrium constant remains unaffected
  • Chemisorption is specific and often activated
  • Langmuir-Hinshelwood model accounts for bimolecular surface reactions
  • Km is inversely proportional to enzyme-substrate affinity

📌 Quick Recall

  • Theories of catalysis bridge microscopic surface interactions and macroscopic kinetic rate laws.
  • Homogeneous reactions proceed via intermediate complexes.
  • Heterogeneous reactions proceed via chemisorption and surface reaction steps.
  • Enzyme systems exhibit saturation kinetics modeled by Michaelis-Menten equations.

⚖️ Key Differences

Promoter VS Catalyst

A promoter enhances a catalyst's activity but cannot catalyze the reaction by itself.

Question 1 of 10
Which theory best accounts for the catalytic activity of transition metal surfaces possessing variable oxidation states?

Solution: Transition metals utilize d-orbitals and variable oxidation states for chemisorption in modern adsorption theory.

Question 2 of 10
If the activation energy of a reaction is lowered by 20 kJ mol-1 by a catalyst at 300 K, approximately by what factor does the rate constant increase (R = 8.314 J K-1 mol-1)?

Solution: Using kcat/kuncat = exp(ΔEa/RT), exp(20000 / (8.314 x 300)) ≈ exp(8.02) ≈ 3040 ≈ 3.0 x 103.

Question 3 of 10
In zero-order surface catalytic reactions, the rate of reaction is:

Solution: When surface sites are fully saturated, changing reactant pressure or concentration does not alter the rate.

Question 4 of 10
Which of the following is an example of homogeneous catalysis?

Solution: Sucrose, water, and HCl are all in the liquid solution phase (homogeneous).

Question 5 of 10
What is the kinetic order of an enzyme-catalyzed reaction when substrate concentration [S] is extremely large compared to Km?

Solution: When [S] >> Km, rate ≈ Vmax, which is independent of [S] (zero order).

Question 6 of 10
Arsenic acts as a poison for platinum catalyst in the contact process because:

Solution: Arsenic impurities block platinum active sites via strong chemisorption poisoning.

Question 7 of 10
The intermediate compound formation theory fails to satisfactorily explain:

Solution: Heterogeneous catalysis involves surface phenomena requiring adsorption theory rather than simple solution-phase molecule intermediates.

Question 8 of 10
Which parameter remains completely unaltered upon the addition of a catalyst?

Solution: Thermodynamic functions like ΔH° and ΔG° depend only on initial and final states, not on the reaction path.

Question 9 of 10
In the Langmuir-Hinshelwood mechanism for bimolecular surface reactions between A and B, the rate is proportional to:

Solution: The reaction rate depends on the frequency of collisions between adjacent adsorbed molecules A and B, proportional to θA θB.

Question 10 of 10
Which of the following represents an autocatalytic reaction?

Solution: The acetic acid produced during methyl acetate hydrolysis acts as an acid catalyst for the reaction (autocatalysis).
