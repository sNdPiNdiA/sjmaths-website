Physical Chemistry

Criteria of Catalysis

Catalysis alters the reaction rate by providing an alternative reaction pathway with a lower activation energy without being consumed in the overall stoichiometry. A catalyst does not alter the position of chemical equilibrium or the thermodynamic free energy change (ΔG°) of the reaction.

UP PGT Chemistry Subject Code 02 B.Sc. / M.Sc. Level

General Characteristics and Thermodynamics of Catalysis

  • A catalyst remains chemically unchanged and quantitatively recovered at the end of the reaction.
  • The presence of a catalyst does not alter the equilibrium constant Keq, but it allows the system to attain equilibrium more rapidly.
  • Catalysis strictly alters the path of the reaction, thereby decreasing the activation energy Ea.
  • Catalysts are highly specific in their action; a given catalyst may catalyze only one specific reaction.

Homogeneous and Heterogeneous Catalysis

  • In homogeneous catalysis, reactants and the catalyst exist in the same phase, exemplified by ester hydrolysis catalyzed by aqueous acids.
  • In heterogeneous catalysis, the catalyst exists in a separate phase from the reactants, typically solid catalysts acting on gaseous or liquid reactants.
  • Heterogeneous catalysis involves adsorption of reactants onto active sites on the catalyst surface, surface reaction, and desorption of products.
  • Promoters enhance the activity of a catalyst (e.g., Mo in the Haber process for ammonia synthesis), while poisons decrease catalytic efficiency.

Autocatalysis and Enzyme Catalysis

  • Autocatalysis occurs when one of the reaction products acts as a catalyst for the reaction, resulting in an initial slow rate that accelerates over time.
  • Enzymes are proteinaceous biochemical catalysts that operate with extreme efficiency and substrate specificity under mild physiological conditions.
  • The lock-and-key model and induced-fit model explain the high specificity of enzyme-catalyzed reactions.
  • Enzyme kinetics typically follow saturation kinetics described by the Michaelis-Menten mechanism at varying substrate concentrations.

Conditions: For unimolecular surface catalysis with gas adsorption

Enzymatic Catalysis (Michaelis-Menten Equation)
v = Vmax[S]K_m + [S]
Conditions: Steady-state approximation applied to enzyme-substrate kinetics
Units: v and Vmax in mol L-1 s-1, [S] and Km in mol L-1
Arrhenius Equation with Catalyst
k = A e-(Ea - ΔEa)/RT
Conditions: Valid for elementary and complex reactions where activation energy is lowered by ΔEa
Units: k in s-1 or mol L-1 s-1, Ea in J mol-1, R in J K-1 mol-1, T in K
⚠️ Important Exceptions & Anomalies
Exception Catalysts increase the rate of chemical reactions.

Observation: Negative Catalysis (Inhibitors) — Certain substances decrease reaction rates by destroying positive catalysts or intercepting active chain carriers in radical mechanisms.

Exception Catalysts remain chemically unchanged at the end of a reaction.

Observation: Temporary Intermediate Formation — Catalysts undergo temporary chemical transformation to form unstable reaction intermediates that subsequently decompose to regenerate the original catalyst.

Exception Solid heterogeneous catalysts have uniform surface reactivity.

Observation: Active Sites and Surface Heterogeneity — Crystal edges, corners, and atomic defects possess different electronic unsaturation, creating localized high-activity catalytic sites.

💡 Shortcuts & Memory Aids
Equilibrium Invariance Rule
Keq (catalyzed) = Keq (uncatalyzed)

Application: Directly eliminate any MCQ option stating that a catalyst changes the equilibrium yield or position.

Activation Energy Drop Shortcut
ΔEa = Ea(uncat) - Ea(cat)

Application: Use the exponential dependence in the Arrhenius equation to calculate rate enhancement factors upon lowering Ea.

Michaelis-Menten Low Substrate Limit
v ≈ (Vmax / Km)[S] when [S] << Km

Application: Treat enzymatic reactions as first-order kinetics with respect to substrate concentration at low substrate regimes.

🎯 Key Exam Points
  • A catalyst alters only the rate of a reaction, not the Gibbs free energy change (ΔG°).
  • Transition metal catalysts exhibit high catalytic activity due to variable oxidation states and vacant d-orbitals.
  • Promoters such as Al2O3 or K2O improve the structural stability and efficiency of iron catalysts in the Haber process.
  • Catalytic poison works by chemisorption onto active sites, rendering them permanently unavailable to reactants.
  • Acid-base catalysis involves proton transfer steps in the rate-determining pathway.
  • Autocatalytic reactions show a characteristic sigmoidal (S-shaped) conversion-time curve.
🚫 Common Errors & Confusions
  • Assuming a catalyst alters the thermodynamic equilibrium constant; remember it only speeds up the attainment of equilibrium.
  • Confusing catalytic promoters with catalysts; promoters do not catalyze the reaction by themselves.
  • Neglecting the desorption step in heterogeneous catalysis, which can be the rate-limiting step if binding is too strong.
  • Assuming all reactions can be catalyzed by any transition metal; catalysis requires specific electronic orbital matching.

⚡ Quick Revision: Criteria of Catalysis

Core formulas, rapid recall points, and distinguishing criteria at a glance.

Thermodynamic Principles

Catalysts decrease activation energy symmetrically for forward and reverse reactions, leaving equilibrium constants unchanged.

Adsorption Theories

Heterogeneous catalysis proceeds through physical adsorption followed by chemical adsorption (chemisorption) onto active surface centers.

Enzyme Kinetics

Enzymes exhibit saturation kinetics governed by the Michaelis constant Km, reflecting substrate affinity.

Catalytic Poisoning

Poisons bind preferentially to active catalytic centers, inhibiting turnover and diminishing reaction rates.

📚 Terms & Units Glossary

Catalyst (Substance altering rate)
A chemical species that increases reaction rate without being consumed.
Promoter (Activity Enhancer)
A substance that enhances the efficiency of a catalyst.
Catalytic Poison (Inhibitor)
A substance that deactivates a catalyst by binding to active sites.
Turnover Number (TON)
Number of moles of reactant converted per mole of catalyst.
Active Center (Surface Site)
Specific locus on a heterogeneous catalyst where reaction occurs.
Autocatalysis (Self-Catalysis)
A reaction where a product acts as the catalyst.

🎯 Key Takeaways

  • Catalysts do not change ΔG°, ΔH°, or Keq.
  • Activation energy is lowered via intermediate complex formation.
  • Enzymes operate optimally within narrow pH and temperature ranges.
  • Heterogeneous catalysis depends heavily on surface area and porosity.
  • The Arrhenius pre-exponential factor A remains largely unaffected by simple catalysts.

📌 Quick Recall

  • Catalysis provides alternative low-energy pathways.
  • Homogeneous and heterogeneous systems differ primarily in phase boundaries.
  • Surface adsorption is critical in solid-gas catalytic systems.
  • Enzymes provide the pinnacle of catalytic efficiency and specificity.

⚖️ Key Differences

Catalyst VS Reactant

Catalysts are regenerated unchanged at the end of the reaction, whereas reactants are consumed.

Which of the following parameters is altered by a positive catalyst?

Explanation: A catalyst lowers the activation energy of the reaction pathway without affecting thermodynamic state functions like equilibrium constant, enthalpy, or free energy.

Question 2 of 19

In the contact process for sulfuric acid manufacturing, the catalyst used is:

Explanation: Vanadium pentoxide (V2O5) is the standard heterogeneous catalyst used for the oxidation of sulfur dioxide to sulfur trioxide.

Question 3 of 19

What role does molybdenum play in Haber's process for ammonia synthesis?

Explanation: Molybdenum (Mo) or alumina acts as a promoter to enhance the catalytic activity of iron in the Haber process.

Question 4 of 19

Which kinetic order is observed in enzyme catalysis at very high substrate concentrations ([S] >> K_m)?

Explanation: At very high substrate concentrations, enzyme active sites are fully saturated, making the rate independent of substrate concentration (zero-order kinetics).

Question 5 of 19

Which of the following is an example of homogeneous catalysis?

Explanation: Both the ester and the aqueous HCl catalyst are in the same liquid phase, making it a homogeneous catalytic system.

Question 6 of 19

The efficiency of a solid catalyst can be increased by:

Explanation: Finely dividing or increasing the porosity of a solid catalyst increases its surface area and the number of active sites available.

Question 7 of 19

What is the primary function of catalytic promoters?

Explanation: Promoters are substances added to increase the chemical activity or efficiency of the primary catalyst.

Question 8 of 19

Autocatalysis is characterized by a reaction rate that initially:

Explanation: In autocatalysis, the reaction starts slowly because catalyst concentration is low, then accelerates as product (catalyst) accumulates.

Question 9 of 19

Which of the following equations represents the Michaelis-Menten kinetics?

Explanation: The Michaelis-Menten equation relating enzyme reaction velocity to substrate concentration is v = (V_max[S]) / (K_m + [S]).

Question 10 of 19

In heterogeneous catalysis, the first step in the mechanism is usually:

Explanation: Heterogeneous catalysis initiates with the transport and adsorption of reactant molecules onto the active sites of the catalyst surface.

Question 11 of 19

Arsenic acts as a poison in the catalytic hydrogenation of alkenes using platinum because:

Explanation: Arsenic chemisorbs strongly onto active metal catalyst sites, blocking reactant adsorption and causing catalyst deactivation.

Question 12 of 19

Which of the following statements about enzyme catalysis is incorrect?

Explanation: Enzymes accelerate reaction rates by lowering activation energy but never alter the thermodynamic equilibrium constant.

Question 13 of 19

What does the Turnover Number (TON) measure?

Explanation: Turnover number quantifies the catalytic capacity by defining the number of moles of reactant transformed per mole of catalyst.

Question 14 of 19

Which catalyst is used in the hydrogenation of oils to fats (Vanaspati ghee)?

Explanation: Nickel (Ni) powder is conventionally used as a heterogeneous catalyst for the catalytic hydrogenation of vegetable oils.

Question 15 of 19

According to intermediate compound formation theory, what is formed in the first step?

Explanation: The catalyst reacts with one of the reactants to form an unstable intermediate complex that subsequently reacts further to yield the product and regenerate the catalyst.

Question 16 of 19

Which factor does NOT affect heterogeneous catalytic reactions?

Explanation: The optical color of a solid catalyst has no direct bearing on its catalytic activity or reaction kinetics.

Question 17 of 19

In acid-base catalysis, the reaction rate is proportional to:

Explanation: Specific acid-base catalysis rates depend directly on the activity or concentration of lyonium (H3O+) or lyate (OH-) ions.

Question 18 of 19

Negative catalysis is useful in:

Explanation: Negative catalysts or inhibitors are added to suppress unwanted side reactions, such as preventing the decomposition of hydrogen peroxide.

Question 19 of 19

The term 'active centers' on a catalyst surface refers to:

Explanation: Active centers are localized surface sites possessing unsatisfied valency forces that facilitate strong chemisorption of reactants.

Question 1 of 10
What is the effect of a catalyst on the enthalpy change (ΔH) of a reaction?

Solution: Enthalpy change is a state function determined solely by initial and final states; catalysts only alter reaction pathways, leaving ΔH unchanged.

Question 2 of 10
Which of the following processes represents heterogeneous catalysis?

Solution: Colloidal platinum is a solid phase dispersed in liquid hydrogen peroxide, representing a heterogeneous catalytic system.

Question 3 of 10
In enzyme kinetics, the Michaelis constant (K_m) represents:

Solution: K_m is defined as the substrate concentration at which the reaction velocity reaches half of its maximum value (V_max / 2).

Question 4 of 10
Why does finely divided nickel have higher catalytic activity than a solid block of nickel?

Solution: Fine subdivision increases surface area per unit mass, exposing a greater number of active surface atoms for reactant adsorption.

Question 5 of 10
Which theory of catalysis accounts for the ability of solid surfaces to form activated complexes via residual valency forces?

Solution: The adsorption theory of heterogeneous catalysis proposes that reactant molecules are held to the catalyst surface by residual valencies.

Question 6 of 10
What is the role of promoters in catalytic reactions?

Solution: Promoters are substances that enhance the chemical activity, structural stability, or selectivity of the primary catalyst.

Question 7 of 10
Which of the following statements regarding autocatalysis is correct?

Solution: In autocatalysis, the reaction rate accelerates as the reaction proceeds because the product acts as a catalyst.

Question 8 of 10
The phenomenon where a small amount of foreign substance destroys catalyst activity is called:

Solution: Catalytic poisoning refers to the deactivation of a catalyst caused by the chemisorption of trace impurities onto active sites.

Question 9 of 10
How does an increase in temperature affect a catalyzed reaction compared to an uncatalyzed reaction?

Solution: Both catalyzed and uncatalyzed reactions obey the Arrhenius equation, though the catalyzed reaction has a lower activation energy.

Question 10 of 10
Which enzyme catalyzes the conversion of glucose into ethanol and carbon dioxide?

Solution: Zymase is the enzyme complex present in yeast that catalyzes the fermentation of glucose into ethanol and carbon dioxide.
