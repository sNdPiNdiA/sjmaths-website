Physical Chemistry

Order of Reaction

The order of reaction is defined as the sum of the powers of the concentration terms of the reactants in the experimentally determined rate law expression. It can be an integer, zero, or a fractional value, and it is determined solely by experiment rather than stoichiometric coefficients.

UP PGT Chemistry Subject Code 02 B.Sc. / M.Sc. Level

Experimental Determination of Rate Law and Order

  • The rate law is an empirical expression relating reaction rate directly to reactant molar concentrations.
  • Overall order equals the mathematical sum of concentration exponents in the differential rate equation: Rate = k[A]x[B]y.
  • Stoichiometric coefficients do not dictate reaction order; elementary steps follow stoichiometry, but complex reactions require experimental determination.
  • Units of the rate constant (k) vary with order according to mol1-n Ln-1 s-1.

Characteristics of Zero, First, and Second-Order Kinetics

  • Zero-order reactions proceed at a constant rate unaffected by decreasing reactant concentration, yielding a linear plot of [A]t versus t.
  • First-order kinetics exhibit an exponential decay of concentration, with a constant half-life independent of initial reactant loading.
  • Pseudo-first-order reactions occur when a reactant in high excess maintains a virtually constant concentration, shifting apparent bimolecular kinetics to first order.
  • Second-order integrated rate dependencies yield a linear plot of 1/[A]t versus time with slope equal to k.

Fractional and Negative Order Reactions

  • Fractional order reactions often denote complex mechanisms involving rapid pre-equilibria with intermediate radical formation.
  • The thermal decomposition of acetaldehyde (CH3CHO) proceeds with an overall order of 1.5 due to radical chain steps.
  • Negative orders arise when a product or intermediate inhibits the reaction rate, appearing in the denominator of the rate law.
  • Ozone decomposition or specific photochemical reactions display fractional or variable orders depending on light intensity and pressure domains.
📐 Key Formulas & Equations
First-Order Integrated Rate Equation
k = (2.303 / t) log([A]0 / [A]t)
Conditions: For unimolecular or specific multi-molecular reactions acting as first order
Units: k in s-1
Second-Order Integrated Rate Equation
1 / [A]t - 1 / [A]0 = kt
Conditions: For bimolecular reactions dependent on second-power concentration terms
Units: k in L mol-1 s-1
Half-Life Expression for nth Order
t1/2 ∝ 1 / ([A]0n-1)
Conditions: Applicable for any order n except n = 1
Units: t1/2 in seconds, n is dimensionless
First-Order Half-Life Expression
t1/2 = 0.693 / k
Conditions: Independent of initial concentration for first-order reactions
Units: t1/2 in seconds, k in s-1
Zero-Order Integrated Rate Equation
[A]t = -kt + [A]0
Conditions: For reactions where rate is independent of reactant concentration
Units: k in mol L-1 s-1
⚠️ Important Exceptions & Anomalies
Exception Reaction rate always increases with an increase in reactant concentration.

Observation: Zero-order reactions and reactions with negative order kinetics. — In zero-order processes, active sites on catalysts are saturated; in negative order, products inhibit the rate.

Exception Stoichiometric coefficients determine the order of a chemical reaction.

Observation: Complex multi-step reactions. — The rate-determining step dictates the overall rate law, which rarely matches the overall balanced stoichiometric equation.

Exception Half-life of a reaction depends on the initial concentration of reactants.

Observation: First-order reactions. — Mathematical integration of first-order kinetics causes the initial concentration term [A]0 to cancel out in the half-life equation.

🎯 Key Exam Points
  • Order of a reaction is an experimental quantity determined from the rate law.
  • Molecularity is a theoretical concept applicable only to elementary steps, whereas order applies to both elementary and complex reactions.
  • Order can be zero, fractional, integer, or even negative, whereas molecularity is a positive integer from 1 to 3.
  • For a first-order reaction, the time required for 99.9% completion is approximately 10 times its half-life (t1/2).
  • Hydrolysis of an ester in acidic medium is pseudo-first-order because water is present in large excess.
  • Radioactive decay strictly follows first-order kinetics.
🚫 Common Errors & Confusions
  • Confusing order with molecularity; remember order is experimental while molecularity is theoretical.
  • Assuming stoichiometric coefficients equal reaction orders in overall balanced equations.
  • Failing to convert time units (minutes to seconds) when calculating rate constants.
  • Incorrectly stating that zero-order reactions have a half-life independent of initial concentration.

⚡ Quick Revision: Order of Reaction

Core formulas, rapid recall points, and distinguishing criteria at a glance.

Definition and Rate Law

The order of a reaction represents the sum of concentration exponents in the experimentally derived rate law expression.

Zero and First Order Kinetics

Zero-order rates are concentration-independent, whereas first-order rates exhibit exponential decay governed by rate constant k.

Pseudo-Unimolecular Reactions

High excess of a reactant shifts higher-order kinetics to apparent first-order behavior.

Experimental Determination

Graphical methods, initial rate methods, and half-life variations serve as primary experimental techniques to evaluate reaction order.

📚 Terms & Units Glossary

Rate Law (Rate = k[A]x)
Algebraic expression relating rate to reactant concentrations with SI unit mol L-1 s-1 for rate.
Rate Constant (k)
Proportionality constant representing specific reaction rate at unit concentration.
Molecularity (M)
Number of reacting species colliding simultaneously in an elementary step.
Half-Life (t1/2)
Time required for reactant concentration to decrease to half its initial value.
Pseudo-Order (Pseudo-n)
Higher-order reaction behaving as lower-order due to excess reactant.
Activation Energy (Ea)
Energy barrier threshold required for reactants to form products in Joules per mole.

🎯 Key Takeaways

  • Order is strictly experimental; molecularity is theoretical.
  • First-order half-life is completely independent of initial concentration.
  • Zero-order rate constant unit matches the rate unit: mol L-1 s-1.
  • Radioactive disintegrations obey first-order kinetics.
  • Inversion of cane sugar is a classic example of a pseudo-first-order reaction.

📌 Quick Recall

  • Reaction order sums concentration exponents in rate laws.
  • Calculations utilize integrated rate equations and half-life relations.
  • Complex reactions involve rate-determining steps.
  • Anomalous orders (fractional/negative) stem from complex reaction mechanisms.

⚖️ Key Differences

Order of Reaction VS Molecularity

Order is experimentally determined and can be fractional or zero; molecularity is a theoretical integer counting colliding molecules in an elementary step.

Question 1 of 19

What is the overall order of a reaction if its rate law is given by Rate = k[A]1/2[B]3/2?

Explanation: Overall order is the sum of exponents: 1/2 + 3/2 = 4/2 = 2.

Question 2 of 19

The unit of the rate constant for a zero-order reaction is:

Explanation: For zero order (n=0), units of k are mol1-0 L0-1 s-1 = mol L-1 s-1.

Question 3 of 19

Which of the following reactions is an example of a pseudo-first-order reaction?

Explanation: Acid hydrolysis of ethyl acetate involves water in large excess, making the apparent order equal to one.

Question 4 of 19

For a first-order reaction, the half-life period is:

Explanation: The half-life equation for first-order is t1/2 = 0.693 / k, which contains no concentration term.

Question 5 of 19

What is the unit of the rate constant for a first-order reaction?

Explanation: For n = 1, unit is s-1 as concentration terms cancel out.

Question 6 of 19

The thermal decomposition of acetaldehyde (CH3CHO) has an order of:

Explanation: Acetaldehyde decomposition proceeds via a chain mechanism resulting in an overall fractional order of 1.5.

Question 7 of 19

If the concentration of a reactant is doubled and the rate increases by a factor of 4, the order with respect to that reactant is:

Explanation: Rate ∝ [A]n. Since 2n = 4, n must equal 2.

Question 8 of 19

Can the order of a reaction be negative?

Explanation: Negative orders occur when a product or intermediate appears in the denominator of the rate law, inhibiting the reaction rate.

Question 9 of 19

What is the slope of the plot of ln[A]t versus time (t) for a first-order reaction?

Explanation: From ln([A]t / [A]0) = -kt, ln[A]t = -kt + ln[A]0, giving a slope of -k.

Question 10 of 19

In a zero-order reaction, the time required for 100% completion (t100%) is equal to:

Explanation: For zero order, [A]t = [A]0 - kt. Setting [A]t = 0 yields t = [A]0 / k.

Question 11 of 19

Molecularity of a reaction can never be:

Explanation: Molecularity represents the number of colliding molecules in an elementary step and must be a positive integer (1, 2, or 3).

Question 12 of 19

Which integrated rate equation represents a second-order reaction involving a single reactant 2A → Products?

Explanation: The standard second-order integrated rate law for 2A → Products is 1/[A]t - 1/[A]0 = kt.

Question 13 of 19

Radioactive disintegration follows which order of kinetics?

Explanation: Radioactive decay is a spontaneous nuclear process independent of external physical conditions, following strict first-order kinetics.

Question 14 of 19

If the units of rate constant are L2 mol-2 s-1, the reaction order is:

Explanation: Using mol1-n Ln-1 s-1, setting Ln-1 = L2 gives n - 1 = 2, so n = 3.

Question 15 of 19

Which factor affects the rate constant (k) of a reaction?

Explanation: Rate constant k is independent of concentration and time, varying only with temperature and catalysts.

Question 16 of 19

For a zero-order reaction, the half-life is directly proportional to:

Explanation: t1/2 = [A]0 / (2k), showing direct proportionality to initial concentration.

Question 17 of 19

What is the order of the reaction for the photochemical combination of H2 and Cl2 to form HCl?

Explanation: The photochemical reaction between H2 and Cl2 is zero order with respect to reactants and depends on light intensity.

Question 18 of 19

Which method is best suited to determine the order of a reaction when multiple reactants are present?

Explanation: Ostwald's isolation method isolates the effect of one reactant by taking all other reactants in large excess.

Question 19 of 19

The time taken for 75% completion of a first-order reaction is equal to:

Explanation: 75% completion corresponds to 2 half-lives (50% in 1 t1/2, another 50% of the remaining in the 2nd t1/2).

Question 1 of 10
Which of the following expressions correctly represents the half-life of a zero-order reaction?

Solution: For zero order, setting [A]t = [A]0 / 2 in [A]t = -kt + [A]0 yields t1/2 = [A]0 / (2k).

Question 2 of 10
The rate law for a reaction is Rate = k[A]2[B]. What happens to the reaction rate if the concentration of A is halved and B is doubled?

Solution: New rate = k([A]/2)2(2[B]) = k([A]2 / 4)(2[B]) = 0.5 × (k[A]2[B]), meaning the rate is halved.

Question 3 of 10
For a first-order reaction, the time required for 99.9% completion is approximately equal to:

Solution: t99.9% = (2.303 / k) log(100 / 0.1) = (2.303 / k) × 3 = 6.909 / k ≈ 10 × (0.693 / k) = 10 t1/2.

Question 4 of 10
Ostwald's isolation method is used to determine:

Solution: Ostwald's isolation method isolates each reactant's concentration effect by keeping others in large excess to determine order.

Question 5 of 10
What is the order of reaction if the half-life is inversely proportional to the initial concentration?

Solution: For second-order reactions, t1/2 = 1 / (k[A]0), which is inversely proportional to initial concentration.

Question 6 of 10
In a first-order reaction, concentration decreases from 0.8 mol L-1 to 0.1 mol L-1 in 60 minutes. What is the half-life?

Solution: 0.8 → 0.4 (1 t1/2) → 0.2 (2 t1/2) → 0.1 (3 t1/2). Total time = 3 t1/2 = 60 min, so t1/2 = 20 minutes.

Question 7 of 10
Which of the following statements is incorrect regarding the order of a reaction?

Solution: Order does not necessarily depend on stoichiometric coefficients and must be determined via experimental rate laws.

Question 8 of 10
What is the unit of rate for a zero-order reaction?

Solution: Rate is always concentration per unit time: mol L-1 s-1.

Question 9 of 10
For a reaction with rate law Rate = k[A]3/2[B]-1/2, the overall order is:

Solution: Overall order = 3/2 + (-1/2) = 2/2 = 1.

Question 10 of 10
Why does the rate of a chemical reaction generally decrease as the reaction proceeds?

Solution: As reactants are consumed, their active concentration decreases, which directly lowers the reaction rate according to the rate law.
