Physical Chemistry

Second-Order Reaction

A second-order reaction is a chemical process whose rate is proportional to the concentration of two reactant species raised to the first power or the square of a single reactant's concentration. The differential rate law is expressed as d[A]/dt = -k[A]2 for a single reactant or d[A]/dt = -k[A][B] for two distinct reactants.

UP PGT Chemistry Subject Code 02 B.Sc. / M.Sc. Level

Mathematical Derivation and Integrated Rate Equations

  • The differential form -d[A]/dt = k[A]2 separates into -d[A]/[A]2 = k dt upon rearrangement.
  • Integration between limits [A]0 at t = 0 and [A] at time t yields the reciprocal concentration relation 1/[A] - 1/[A]0 = kt.
  • Plotting 1/[A] versus time t yields a straight line with a positive slope equal to the rate constant k and an y-intercept of 1/[A]0.
  • For second-order reactions involving two different reactants A and B with initial concentrations a and b, the integrated rate equation incorporates logarithmic terms of remaining concentrations.

Half-Life Characteristics and Concentration Dependency

  • The half-life (t1/2) of a second-order reaction is inversely proportional to the initial concentration [A]0.
  • Successive half-lives double as the reaction proceeds and concentration decreases by half, unlike first-order reactions where t1/2 remains constant.
  • The time required for the reaction to reach a specific fraction of completion depends explicitly on the initial concentration value.
  • Initial rate methods can determine reaction order by measuring initial rates at varying initial concentrations and evaluating log(rate) versus log([A]0).

Pseudo-First-Order Reactions and Experimental Verification

  • A second-order reaction can be converted into a pseudo-first-order reaction by taking one reactant in large excess.
  • Under excess conditions, the concentration of the excess reagent remains virtually constant, merging its value into the pseudo rate constant k' = k[B]0.
  • Acid-catalyzed ester hydrolysis or sucrose inversion are classic pseudo-first-order systems where water is taken in vast excess.
  • Experimental validation of second-order kinetics involves confirming constancy of calculated k values from the integrated rate equation at various time intervals.

⚠️ Important Exceptions & Anomalies
Exception Second-order half-life depends on initial concentration

Observation: Pseudo-first-order reactions exhibit constant half-life — One reactant is present in massive excess, rendering its concentration effectively constant throughout the reaction course.

Exception Elementary bimolecular reactions follow second-order kinetics

Observation: Gas-phase termolecular reactions follow third-order kinetics, while some bimolecular reactions exhibit complex non-integer or fractional orders via chain mechanisms. — Presence of rapid pre-equilibriums or consecutive elementary steps alters the overall rate law expression derived from steady-state approximations.

t75% = 3 × t1/2

Linear 1/[A] vs t plot

🎯 Key Exam Points
  • The unit of second-order rate constant is L mol-1 s-1 or dm3 mol-1 s-1.
  • A plot of 1/[A] versus time for a second-order reaction produces a straight line with a positive slope equal to k.
  • The half-life of a second-order reaction is inversely proportional to the initial concentration of the reactant.
  • If the initial concentration of a second-order reactant is doubled, its half-life is halved.
  • Acid hydrolysis of an ester behaves as a pseudo-first-order reaction when water is present in large excess.
  • The integrated rate equation for 2A → Products is 1/[A] - 1/[A]0 = kt.
🚫 Common Errors & Confusions
  • Confusing the unit of second-order rate constant with first-order (remember: mol-1 L s-1 vs s-1).
  • Assuming half-life is independent of initial concentration for second-order reactions.
  • Failing to account for stoichiometric coefficients when setting up integrated rate laws for reactions like 2A → B.
  • Applying the pseudo-first-order integrated rate law without confirming that one reactant is in large excess.

⚡ Quick Revision: Second-Order Reaction

Core formulas, rapid recall points, and distinguishing criteria at a glance.

Differential and Integrated Rate Laws

Second-order reactions have rates proportional to the square of a single reactant concentration or the product of two reactant concentrations. The integrated equation 1/[A] - 1/[A]0 = kt enables determination of rate constants from concentration-time data.

Graphical Analysis

Linear transformation requires plotting the reciprocal of concentration (1/[A]) against time (t). The slope of this straight line gives the rate constant directly.

Half-Life Characteristics

Unlike first-order processes, second-order half-life depends on initial concentration via t1/2 = 1/(k[A]0), meaning each successive half-life is twice as long as the preceding one.

Pseudo-First-Order Kinetics

Reactions that are kinetically second-order can be analyzed as first-order systems by maintaining one reactant in massive excess, simplifying mathematical treatment.

📚 Terms & Units Glossary

Rate Constant (k) (L mol-1 s-1)
Proportionality constant relating reaction rate to concentration terms.
Half-Life (t1/2) (1/(k[A]0))
Time required for reactant concentration to decrease to half its initial value.
Initial Concentration ([A]0) (mol L-1)
Concentration of reactant at time t = 0.
Pseudo-Order (Apparent order)
Higher-order reaction behaving as lower order due to excess reagent.
Differential Rate Law (-d[A]/dt = k[A]2)
Rate expression as a function of instantaneous concentration.
Integrated Rate Law (1/[A] - 1/[A]0 = kt)
Equation relating concentration to elapsed time.

🎯 Key Takeaways

  • Integrated rate equation: 1/[A] - 1/[A]0 = kt
  • Half-life expression: t1/2 = 1/(k[A]0)
  • Unit of k: L mol-1 s-1
  • Linear plot: 1/[A] vs t
  • Pseudo-first-order condition: One reactant in large excess

📌 Quick Recall

  • Second-order rate depends on [A]2 or [A][B].
  • Half-life is inversely proportional to initial concentration.
  • Reciprocal concentration plot yields a straight line with slope k.
  • Excess reagent converts second-order kinetics to pseudo-first-order.

⚖️ Key Differences

First-Order Half-Life VS Second-Order Half-Life

First-order half-life is constant and independent of [A]0; second-order half-life depends inversely on [A]0.

Question 1 of 19

What is the unit of the rate constant for a second-order reaction?

Explanation: The unit of rate constant is (mol L-1)1-n s-1. For n = 2, unit is L mol-1 s-1.

Question 2 of 19

Which of the following plots gives a straight line for a second-order reaction (2A → Products)?

Explanation: The integrated rate law 1/[A] - 1/[A]0 = kt rearranges to 1/[A] = kt + 1/[A]0, which is linear for 1/[A] versus t.

Question 3 of 19

How does the half-life of a second-order reaction change if the initial concentration of the reactant is doubled?

Explanation: Since t1/2 = 1/(k[A]0), half-life is inversely proportional to initial concentration. Doubling [A]0 halves the half-life.

Question 4 of 19

In a second-order reaction 2A → Products, if [A]0 = 0.1 mol L-1 and k = 0.05 L mol-1 s-1, what is the half-life?

Explanation: t1/2 = 1 / (0.05 × 0.1) = 1 / 0.005 = 200 seconds.

Question 5 of 19

Which reaction condition transforms a second-order reaction into a pseudo-first-order reaction?

Explanation: When one reactant is in large excess, its concentration remains essentially constant, making the reaction pseudo-first-order.

Question 6 of 19

What is the slope of the linear plot of 1/[A] versus t for a second-order reaction?

Explanation: From 1/[A] = kt + 1/[A]0, the slope of the line is equal to the rate constant k.

Question 7 of 19

If the initial concentration of a reactant in a second-order reaction is tripled, the initial rate of the reaction increases by a factor of:

Explanation: Rate = k[A]2. If [A] is multiplied by 3, rate is multiplied by 32 = 9.

Question 8 of 19

The hydrolysis of an ester in acidic medium is an example of:

Explanation: Ester hydrolysis is bimolecular (second-order overall), but because water is in vast excess, it exhibits pseudo-first-order kinetics.

Question 9 of 19

For a second-order reaction, if the concentration drops from 1.0 M to 0.5 M in 50 seconds, what is the next half-life (from 0.5 M to 0.25 M)?

Explanation: Second-order half-life is inversely proportional to concentration. Halving the concentration from 0.5 M to 0.25 M doubles the half-life to 100 seconds.

Question 10 of 19

What are the units of the rate constant for a gas-phase second-order reaction where pressure is used instead of concentration?

Explanation: Rate in pressure units is atm s-1, and rate = kP2, so k has units of atm-1 s-1.

Question 11 of 19

Which of the following expressions represents the integrated rate law for a second-order reaction of type A + B → Products with initial concentrations a and b?

Explanation: The standard integrated rate law for unequal initial concentrations a and b is k = [2.303/t(a-b)] log[b(a-x)/a(b-x)].

Question 12 of 19

In a second-order reaction, the time t75% for 75% completion is related to the initial half-life t1/2 as:

Explanation: At 75% completion, [A] = [A]0/4. Substituting into 1/[A] - 1/[A]0 = kt gives 4/[A]0 - 1/[A]0 = 3/[A]0 = kt, which equals 3 t1/2.

Question 13 of 19

Which method is best suited to determine the order of a reaction when multiple reactants are involved?

Explanation: Ostwald's Isolation Method isolates each reactant by taking others in large excess to determine individual orders.

Question 14 of 19

If a reaction has a rate law Rate = k[A][B], the overall order of the reaction is:

Explanation: The overall order is the sum of exponents of concentration terms: 1 + 1 = 2.

Question 15 of 19

What is the dimension of the second-order rate constant k in terms of concentration (C) and time (t)?

Explanation: Rate has dimensions C t-1, and Rate = k C2, so k has dimensions C-1 t-1.

Question 16 of 19

In the saponification of an ester (ester + NaOH → salt + alcohol), the reaction is:

Explanation: Saponification follows a second-order rate law: Rate = k[Ester][NaOH].

Question 17 of 19

Which factor affects the value of the second-order rate constant k?

Explanation: Rate constants depend primarily on temperature (Arrhenius equation) and catalysts, but not on reactant concentrations.

Question 18 of 19

For a second-order reaction 2A → Products, if 50% reaction takes 100 seconds, how long will the next 25% take (from 50% to 75% completion)?

Explanation: From 50% to 75% completion, concentration drops from [A]0/2 to [A]0/4, which takes exactly one more half-life (100 seconds).

Question 19 of 19

What is the intercept on the y-axis when plotting 1/[A] versus t for a second-order reaction?

Explanation: From 1/[A] = kt + 1/[A]0, setting t = 0 gives the y-intercept as 1/[A]0.

Question 1 of 10
Calculate the rate constant of a second-order reaction if 40% of a reactant with initial concentration 0.1 mol L-1 reacts in 200 seconds.

Solution: [A]0 = 0.1, [A] = 0.06 (since 40% reacted). Using kt = 1/[A] - 1/[A]0 = (1/0.06) - (1/0.1) = 16.67 - 10 = 6.67. Thus k = 6.67 / 200 = 0.0333... wait, 16.67 - 10 = 6.67; 6.67 / 200 = 0.0333? Let's check: 1/0.06 = 16.667; 1/0.1 = 10; difference = 6.667. k = 6.667/200 = 0.0333? Let's re-evaluate options. Correct calculation: k = x / [t(a)(a-x)] = 0.04 / [200 × 0.1 × 0.06] = 0.04 / 1.2 = 0.0333. Option A is 0.0333 (adjusted for format).

Question 2 of 10
What is the unit of rate for a second-order reaction?

Solution: Reaction rate always has units of concentration per unit time (mol L-1 s-1), regardless of reaction order.

Question 3 of 10
If the rate constant for a second-order reaction is 0.5 L mol-1 min-1 and initial concentration is 0.2 mol L-1, what is the half-life?

Solution: t1/2 = 1 / (0.5 × 0.2) = 1 / 0.1 = 10 minutes.

Question 4 of 10
Which parameter has no effect on the rate constant of a second-order reaction?

Solution: Rate constants are independent of reactant concentrations; they depend only on temperature, activation energy, and catalysts.

Question 5 of 10
For a second-order reaction, the slope of the 1/[A] versus t graph has dimensions of:

Solution: The slope equals the second-order rate constant k, which has units of L mol-1 s-1.

Question 6 of 10
In a second-order reaction, if concentration [A] changes from 0.4 M to 0.1 M, how many half-lives have elapsed?

Solution: From 0.4 M to 0.2 M is 1 half-life. From 0.2 M to 0.1 M is a second half-life (since t1/2 doubles). Total elapsed = 2 half-lives.

Question 7 of 10
What is the integrated rate law form when expressed in terms of fraction reacted x for 2A → Products?

Solution: From 1/[A] - 1/[A]0 = kt with [A] = a(1-x), rearranging gives x / [a(a-x)] = kt.

Question 8 of 10
Which type of molecularity strictly corresponds to a second-order overall rate law?

Solution: Bimolecular elementary reactions follow second-order kinetics, but complex multi-step reactions can also exhibit overall second-order kinetics.

Question 9 of 10
If the units of k are L2 mol-2 s-1, what is the order of the reaction?

Solution: Using (mol L-1)1-n s-1 = L2 mol-2 s-1 gives 1-n = -2, so n = 3 (third-order).

Question 10 of 10
Why do termolecular reactions occur less frequently than bimolecular second-order reactions?

Solution: The simultaneous effective collision of three independent reactant particles in the correct orientation has an extremely low statistical probability.
