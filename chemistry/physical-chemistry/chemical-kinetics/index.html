Physical Chemistry

Chemical Kinetics

Chemical kinetics investigates the rates of chemical transformations, the experimental factors influencing reaction velocities, and the microscopic mechanisms involved. It correlates differential concentration changes with time through empirical rate laws and temperature-dependent rate constants.

UP PGT Chemistry Subject Code 02 B.Sc. / M.Sc. Level

Reaction Rates, Rate Laws, and Reaction Order

  • The rate of reaction for aA + bB → cC is expressed as -(1/a)(d[A]/dt) = -(1/b)(d[B]/dt) = +(1/c)(d[C]/dt).
  • Order of reaction is the sum of exponents of concentration terms in the experimentally determined differential rate law; it may be zero, fractional, integer, or negative.
  • Molecularity represents the number of colliding species in an elementary step and must be a positive integer (1, 2, or 3); it is never zero or fractional.
  • Pseudo-first-order reactions occur when one reactant is present in large stoichiometric excess, rendering its concentration effectively constant (e.g., acid-catalyzed sucrose inversion).

Kinetic Theories and Activation Parameters

  • Arrhenius theory postulation: Only molecules with energy ≥ threshold energy (Eth = Ereactants + Ea) undergo reactive collisions.
  • Collision theory defines rate as ZAB × ρ × e-Ea/(RT), where ZAB is collision frequency and ρ is the steric (orientation) factor.
  • Transition State Theory (Eyring): Reaction proceeds via an activated complex; thermodynamic parameters relate by Ea = ΔH‡ + RT (for unimolecular solution reactions) and Ea = ΔH‡ + 2RT (for bimolecular gas reactions).
  • A large negative entropy of activation (ΔS‡) indicates a highly ordered, sterically constrained transition state (e.g., associative mechanisms).

Complex Mechanisms and Catalysis

  • The rate-determining step (RDS) is the slowest elementary step in a multi-step pathway, controlling the overall reaction velocity.
  • The Steady-State Approximation (SSA) assumes the rate of formation of an unstable intermediate equals its rate of destruction: d[Intermediate]/dt = 0.
  • Homogeneous catalysts alter the reaction path by providing an alternative mechanism with lower Ea, accelerating forward and reverse rates equally without shifting equilibrium position (Keq).
  • In enzyme kinetics (Michaelis-Menten), at low substrate concentration [S] ≪ Km, the reaction is first-order; at high substrate concentration [S] ≫ Km, the reaction becomes zero-order (rate = Vmax).
📐 Key Formulas & Equations
Integrated Rate Law: Zero-Order
[A]t = [A]0 - kt  |  t1/2 = [A]0 / (2k)
Conditions: Surface-catalyzed reactions, photochemical reactions at saturated intensity.
Units: [A] in mol L-1, k in mol L-1 s-1, t in s
Integrated Rate Law: First-Order
k = (2.303 / t) log([A]0 / [A]t)  |  t1/2 = 0.693 / k
Conditions: Decay rates proportional solely to the first power of a single reactant concentration; independent of initial concentration.
Units: [A] in mol L-1, k in s-1, t in s
General Half-Life Relation
t1/2 ∝ 1 / [A]0n-1
Conditions: Single reactant reaction of order n (where n ≠ 1).
Units: t1/2 in s, [A]0 in mol L-1, n = order of reaction
Arrhenius Equation
log(k2 / k1) = (Ea / 2.303R) [(T2 - T1) / (T1T2)]
Conditions: Temperature dependence of rate constant over moderate temperature intervals.
Units: Ea in J mol-1, R = 8.314 J K-1 mol-1, T in K
Eyring Transition State Equation
k = (kBT / h) eΔS‡ / R e-ΔH‡ / (RT)
Conditions: Activated complex in quasi-equilibrium with reactants.
Units: kB = 1.38 × 10-23 J K-1, h = 6.626 × 10-34 J s, ΔS‡ in J K-1 mol-1, ΔH‡ in J mol-1
General Rate Constant Units
Units of k = (mol L-1)1-n s-1 = M1-n s-1
Conditions: Applicable to an nth-order reaction where rate is expressed in mol L-1 s-1.
Units: mol = moles, L = liters, s = seconds, n = overall order
⚠️ Important Exceptions & Anomalies
Exception Reaction rate typically increases with an increase in temperature (Ea > 0).

Observation: Reactions with negative apparent activation energy (e.g., 2NO + O2 → 2NO2). — The reaction mechanism involves a rapid pre-equilibrium step with an exothermic intermediate (N2O2); an increase in temperature shifts the equilibrium backward, decreasing the overall rate.

Exception The order of a reaction corresponds to simple integral stoichiometry.

Observation: Pyrolysis of acetaldehyde has an order of 1.5 (rate = k[CH3CHO]3/2); formation of HBr has a fractional/composite rate law. — Chain reactions involving free-radical initiation, propagation, and bimolecular termination steps yield non-integral orders upon applying the steady-state approximation.

Exception Higher molecularity increases collision probability and rate.

Observation: Molecularity greater than 3 is practically non-existent. — The simultaneous collision of four or more distinct chemical species with requisite kinetic energy and proper spatial orientation is statistically improbable.

💡 Shortcuts & Memory Aids
Half-Life Ratio Method for Order
(t1/2)1 / (t1/2)2 = ([A]0,2 / [A]0,1)n-1

Application: To determine reaction order n directly from two half-life values obtained at two different initial concentrations without integrating rate expressions.

First-Order Decay Fractions
t75% = 2 × t50% | t87.5% = 3 × t50% | t99.9% ≈ 10 × t50%

Application: Instantly solve multi-step radioactive decay or first-order chemical degradation MCQs without evaluating logarithms.

Arrhenius Temperature Coefficient Rule
kT+10 / kT ≈ μ ≈ 2 to 3

Application: For a temperature rise of ΔT, rate factor increases by μΔT/10. If μ = 2 and temperature rises from 300 K to 340 K, rate increases by 24 = 16 times.

🎯 Key Exam Points
  • Units of rate of reaction are always mol L-1 s-1, irrespective of reaction order.
  • For a zero-order reaction, the half-life is directly proportional to initial concentration: t1/2 = [A]0 / (2k); a plot of [A] versus t is linear with slope = -k.
  • For a first-order reaction, the half-life is independent of initial concentration: t1/2 = 0.693 / k; a plot of ln[A] versus t is linear with slope = -k.
  • A plot of 1/[A] versus t is linear with slope = +k exclusively for second-order reactions obeying rate = k[A]2.
  • A catalyst decreases activation energy (Ea) by an equal amount for both the forward and reverse reactions, leaving ΔH, ΔG, and Keq unaltered.
  • According to the Arrhenius plot (ln k vs 1/T), the slope equals -Ea/R and the y-intercept equals ln A.
🚫 Common Errors & Confusions
  • Mistaking molecularity for reaction order by reading the stoichiometric coefficients of an overall unbalanced or balanced complex reaction.
  • Using inconsistent units for activation energy (Ea in kJ mol-1 vs R in 8.314 J K-1 mol-1); always convert Ea to J mol-1 before calculating.
  • Assuming catalyst shifts the position of equilibrium; it only accelerates the approach to equilibrium by lowering activation energy.
  • Confusing the half-life dependence: t1/2 ∝ 1/[A]0 represents a second-order reaction, whereas t1/2 ∝ [A]0 represents zero-order.

⚡ Quick Revision: Chemical Kinetics

Core formulas, rapid recall points, and distinguishing criteria at a glance.

Differential vs Integrated Rate Equations

Differential rate expressions define instantaneous rate in terms of concentration gradients (-d[A]/dt), whereas integrated rate equations correlate absolute concentration with time, permitting determination of rate constants and half-lives.

Temperature Dependence and Activation Energy

Reaction rates increase exponentially with temperature as defined by the Arrhenius relation. Activation energy represents the minimum energy barrier reactants must overcome to form the transition state intermediate.

Collision and Transition State Theories

Collision theory bases reactivity on collision frequency, energy barriers, and spatial orientation factors. Activated Complex Theory quantifies rate via the thermodynamic parameters of enthalpy and entropy of activation.

Mechanism and Kinetic Approximations

Complex reactions proceed via multi-step pathways where the slowest elementary step dictates overall velocity. The steady-state approximation simplifies kinetic analysis by assuming intermediate concentrations remain quasi-stationary.

📚 Terms & Units Glossary

Rate of Reaction (r = -(1/v) d[C]/dt)
Change in concentration of a reactant or product per unit time divided by its stoichiometric coefficient (mol L-1 s-1).
Specific Rate Constant (k)
Rate of reaction when the concentration of each reactant is unity.
Activation Energy (Ea)
The excess energy over the ground state that reactant molecules must acquire to form the activated complex (J mol-1).
Steric Factor (ρ = P)
The fraction of collisions having the correct mutual spatial orientation required for reaction.
Half-Life (t1/2)
Time required for the concentration of a reactant to decrease to half of its initial value.
Threshold Energy (Eth)
Minimum kinetic energy colliding particles must possess for a collision to result in chemical reaction (Eth = Einitial + Ea).

🎯 Key Takeaways

  • Rate constant unit formula: M1-n s-1 (where n is the overall reaction order).
  • For first-order kinetics: t99% = 2 × t90%.
  • Catalysts lower Ea without altering ΔH, ΔS, or ΔG of the overall reaction.
  • Slope of Arrhenius plot (ln k vs 1/T) is -Ea/R; slope of (log10 k vs 1/T) is -Ea / (2.303R).
  • Zero-order reactions proceed to completion in finite time: t100% = [A]0 / k = 2 × t1/2.

📌 Quick Recall

  • Reaction rates are governed by concentration exponents defined empirically as the reaction order.
  • Integrated rate laws yield characteristic straight-line graphs: [A] vs t (zero-order), ln[A] vs t (first-order), 1/[A] vs t (second-order).
  • Arrhenius equation quantitatively describes how thermal energy increases the fraction of molecules with E ≥ Ea.
  • Complex reactions are analyzed using the rate-determining step concept or the steady-state approximation on reactive intermediates.

⚖️ Key Differences

Reaction Order VS Molecularity

Order is an empirical sum of concentration exponents that can be fractional or zero; molecularity is a theoretical count of colliding particles in an elementary step and is strictly a non-zero positive integer.

Question 1 of 19

What are the units of the rate constant for a third-order reaction?

Explanation: Units of k = (mol L-1)1-n s-1. For n = 3, units = (mol L-1)-2 s-1 = mol-2 L2 s-1 = L2 mol-2 s-1.

Question 2 of 19

For a first-order reaction, the time required for 99.9% completion is approximately how many times the half-life?

Explanation: t = (2.303/k) log(100/0.1) = (2.303/k) log(103) = 3 × 2.303 / k. Since t1/2 = 0.693 / k = 2.303 log(2) / k ≈ 0.693/k, t99.9% / t1/2 = (3 × 2.303) / 0.693 ≈ 6.909 / 0.693 ≈ 10.

Question 3 of 19

If the initial concentration of a reactant is doubled and the half-life remains unchanged, what is the order of the reaction?

Explanation: Half-life relates to initial concentration via t1/2 ∝ [A]01-n. If t1/2 is independent of [A]0, 1 - n = 0 ⇒ n = 1 (First-order).

Question 4 of 19

A plot of 1/[A] versus time t gives a straight line with a positive slope. What is the order of the reaction?

Explanation: The integrated rate equation for a second-order reaction is 1/[A]t = 1/[A]0 + kt. Plotting 1/[A] vs t yields a straight line with slope = +k.

Question 5 of 19

What is the slope of the linear plot of log10 k versus 1/T in the Arrhenius equation?

Explanation: From log10 k = log10 A - Ea / (2.303RT), plotting log10 k versus 1/T yields a linear slope equal to -Ea / (2.303R).

Question 6 of 19

The rate of a gaseous reaction is halved when the volume of the reaction vessel is doubled at constant temperature. What is the order of the reaction?

Explanation: Doubling the volume halves the concentration ([A] → [A]/2). Rate' = k([A]/2)n = Rate × (1/2)n. Since Rate' = (1/2) Rate, (1/2)n = (1/2)1 ⇒ n = 1.

Question 7 of 19

Which of the following statements regarding a catalyst is scientifically correct?

Explanation: A catalyst provides an alternate transition state with lower activation energy (Ea); it does not alter thermodynamic properties (ΔH, ΔG, Keq) or change molecular kinetic energies.

Question 8 of 19

The inversion of cane sugar in an aqueous acidic solution is an example of which type of reaction?

Explanation: C12H22O11 + H2O → C6H12O6 + C6H12O6. Because H2O is the solvent and present in large excess, its concentration remains constant, rendering the reaction pseudo-first-order.

Question 9 of 19

The decomposition of NH3 on a hot tungsten or platinum surface at very high pressure follows which reaction order?

Explanation: At high pressure, the catalytic metal surface is completely saturated with adsorbed ammonia molecules; the rate of decomposition becomes independent of ammonia concentration, exhibiting zero-order kinetics.

Question 10 of 19

In the Lindemann-Hinshelwood mechanism for unimolecular reactions, what is the apparent reaction order at very low gas pressures?

Explanation: At low pressure, bimolecular collision to form activated molecules (A + A → A* + A) is the rate-determining step, making the overall reaction second-order.

Question 11 of 19

If the rate constant of a reaction is numerically equal to the rate of the reaction at all times, what is the order of the reaction?

Explanation: Rate = k[A]n. If Rate = k regardless of concentration [A], then [A]n = 1, which requires n = 0.

Question 12 of 19

For a reaction A → Products, when [A] changes from 0.4 M to 0.2 M, t1/2 is 20 min. When [A] changes from 0.8 M to 0.4 M, t1/2 is 40 min. What is the order of the reaction?

Explanation: When [A]0 doubles from 0.2 M to 0.4 M (or 0.4 M to 0.8 M), t1/2 doubles from 20 to 40 min. Since t1/2 ∝ [A]0, (1 - n) = 1 ⇒ n = 0.

Question 13 of 19

According to Transition State Theory, the relation between the Arrhenius activation energy Ea and enthalpy of activation ΔH‡ for a unimolecular liquid-phase reaction is:

Explanation: For liquid-phase or unimolecular processes, Δn‡ is zero (or solution phase work term Δ(PV) ≈ 0), leading to Ea = ΔH‡ + RT.

Question 14 of 19

For an endothermic reaction with enthalpy change ΔH, the activation energy of the forward reaction (Ea,f) must be:

Explanation: For any reaction, ΔH = Ea,f - Ea,b. Since the reverse reaction activation energy Ea,b must be positive (> 0), Ea,f = ΔH + Ea,b > ΔH.

Question 15 of 19

The steady-state approximation applied to a reaction intermediate assumes that:

Explanation: The steady-state approximation assumes that reactive intermediates are consumed as rapidly as they are formed, hence d[Intermediate]/dt ≈ 0.

Question 16 of 19

When the temperature of a reaction is raised from 300 K to 310 K, the rate doubles. What is the approximate activation energy in kJ mol-1? (R = 8.314 J K-1 mol-1)

Explanation: log(2) = (Ea / (2.303 × 8.314)) × [(310 - 300) / (300 × 310)]. 0.3010 = (Ea / 19.147) × (10 / 93000). Ea = 0.3010 × 19.147 × 9300 ≈ 53,600 J mol-1 = 53.6 kJ mol-1.

Question 17 of 19

In a zero-order reaction, the time required for 100% completion of the reaction is:

Explanation: From [A]t = [A]0 - kt, setting [A]t = 0 gives t100% = [A]0 / k.

Question 18 of 19

For the elementary reaction 2A + B → Products, if the concentration of A is doubled and that of B is halved, the rate of reaction will:

Explanation: For an elementary step, Rate = k[A]2[B]. New Rate = k(2[A])2(0.5[B]) = k × 4[A]2 × 0.5[B] = 2 × Rate.

Question 19 of 19

The rate constant k for a reaction is 0.0693 min-1. If the starting concentration is 0.1 M, what is the rate of the reaction after 20 minutes?

Explanation: The unit of k is min-1 ⇒ first-order. t1/2 = 0.693 / 0.0693 = 10 min. In 20 minutes (2 half-lives), [A] = 0.1 / 4 = 0.025 M. Rate = k[A] = 0.0693 × 0.025 = 0.0017325 M min-1.

Question 1 of 10
For a reaction A + B → Products, doubling the concentration of A increases the rate by a factor of 4, while doubling the concentration of B has no effect. The differential rate equation is:

Solution: Rate ∝ [A]x[B]y. 2x = 4 ⇒ x = 2. 2y = 1 ⇒ y = 0. Thus, Rate = k[A]2[B]0.

Question 2 of 10
A substance decomposes according to first-order kinetics. If its rate constant is 0.0005 s-1, what is its half-life?

Solution: t1/2 = 0.693 / k = 0.693 / 0.0005 s-1 = 1386 s.

Question 3 of 10
The rate constant of a reaction at 300 K is k. At what temperature will the rate constant become 2k if the activation energy is 57.6 kJ mol-1? (R = 8.314 J K-1 mol-1, log 2 = 0.3010)

Solution: log(2) = (Ea / 2.303R) [(T2 - 300)/(300T2)]. 0.3010 = (57600 / 19.147) [(T2 - 300)/(300T2)] ⇒ 0.3010 = 3008.3 × [(T2 - 300)/(300T2)]. (T2 - 300)/(300T2) = 1.0 × 10-4 ⇒ T2 - 300 = 0.03 T2 ⇒ 0.97 T2 = 300 ⇒ T2 ≈ 309.3 K ≈ 310 K.

Question 4 of 10
For a second-order reaction where both reactants have equal initial concentrations, the time required for 75% completion is:

Solution: For second-order: t = (1/k) [1/[A]t - 1/[A]0]. At 50%: t1/2 = 1/(k[A]0). At 75%: [A]t = [A]0/4, so t75% = (1/k) [4/[A]0 - 1/[A]0] = 3/(k[A]0) = 3 × t1/2.

Question 5 of 10
According to the collision theory of bimolecular gaseous reactions, the rate constant is proportional to:

Solution: Collision frequency Z is proportional to average molecular velocity, which varies as T1/2. Hence, pre-exponential collision factor A ∝ T1/2.

Question 6 of 10
For the complex reaction mechanism: Step 1: 2NO ⇄ N2O2 (fast equilibrium, K); Step 2: N2O2 + O2 → 2NO2 (slow, k2). The overall rate law is:

Solution: The slow step governs the rate: Rate = k2[N2O2][O2]. From the pre-equilibrium step, K = [N2O2] / [NO]2 ⇒ [N2O2] = K[NO]2. Substituting gives Rate = k2K[NO]2[O2] = k'[NO]2[O2].

Question 7 of 10
In a reaction A → Products, the concentration of A falls from 0.8 M to 0.4 M in 15 minutes, and from 0.4 M to 0.1 M in 30 minutes. What is the order of the reaction?

Solution: 0.8 M → 0.4 M is 1 half-life (15 min). 0.4 M → 0.2 M → 0.1 M represents 2 half-lives (30 min ⇒ 15 min per half-life). Because half-life is constant regardless of concentration, the reaction is first-order.

Question 8 of 10
What is the order of reaction if the half-life period t1/2 is inversely proportional to the square of initial concentration?

Solution: t1/2 ∝ [A]01-n. Given t1/2 ∝ [A]0-2, 1 - n = -2 ⇒ n = 3.

Question 9 of 10
The pre-exponential factor A in the Arrhenius equation has the same units as:

Solution: In k = A e-Ea/(RT), the exponential term is dimensionless. Thus, A must possess the exact same dimensional units as the rate constant k.

Question 10 of 10
For the decomposition reaction N2O5 → 2NO2 + 1/2 O2, if -d[N2O5]/dt = k1[N2O5], d[NO2]/dt = k2[N2O5], and d[O2]/dt = k3[N2O5], the correct relationship between k1, k2, and k3 is:

Solution: The reaction rate is -d[N2O5]/dt = (1/2) d[NO2]/dt = 2 d[O2]/dt. Therefore, k1 = (1/2) k2 = 2 k3, which multiplies across by 2 to give 2k1 = k2 = 4k3.
