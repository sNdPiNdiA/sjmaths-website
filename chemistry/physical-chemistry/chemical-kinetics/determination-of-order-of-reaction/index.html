Physical Chemistry

Determination of Order of Reaction

The order of a reaction is determined experimentally by observing how the reaction rate varies with the concentration of its reactants. Methods include the initial rate method, integrated rate equation substitution, half-life method, and Ostwald's isolation method.

UP PGT Chemistry Subject Code 02 B.Sc. / M.Sc. Level

Graphical and Integration Methods

  • Zero-order reactions yield a linear plot of [A]t versus time with slope equal to -k.
  • First-order reactions yield a linear plot of ln[A]t versus time with slope equal to -k.
  • Second-order reactions yield a linear plot of 1/[A]t versus time with slope equal to +k.

Half-Life and Fractional Life Methods

  • The half-life of a first-order reaction is independent of the initial concentration ([A]00).
  • For an nth order reaction, logging half-lives at different initial concentrations yields the relation log(t1/2) = constant + (1-n)log([A]0).
  • The overall order n can be extracted directly from the slope of the plot of log(t1/2) versus log([A]0).

Isolation and Initial Rate Methods

  • Ostwald's isolation method simplifies complex multi-reactant rate laws by driving all excess reactants to pseudo-constant concentrations.
  • The initial rate method eliminates the complexity of reverse reactions and product inhibition by measuring rate at t → 0.
  • Logarithmic transformation of initial rates allows simultaneous determination of multiple reactant reaction orders via linear regression.
📐 Key Formulas & Equations
Half-Life Method
t1/2 ∝ [A]01-n
Conditions: Applies to any nth order reaction where other reactant concentrations remain constant
Units: t1/2 in seconds, [A]0 in mol L-1
Integrated Rate Equation Method
k = (1/t) × (1/(n-1)) × [ (1/[A]n-1) - (1/[A]0n-1) ]
Conditions: For n ≠ 1
Units: k in (mol L-1)1-n s-1
Ostwald's Isolation Method
Rate = k' [A]n
Conditions: All reactants except A are taken in large excess
Units: k' is pseudo-rate constant
Initial Rate Method
Rate = k [A]x [B]y
Conditions: Initial rates measured at varying initial concentrations
Units: Rate in mol L-1 s-1, k depends on overall order
⚠️ Important Exceptions & Anomalies
Exception Reaction rate always increases with concentration of all reactants

Observation: Negative order reactions (e.g., ozone decomposition inhibition by oxygen) — Presence of products or intermediates inhibits the forward step, appearing in the denominator of the rate law.

Exception Molecularity always equals the order of the reaction

Observation: Complex reactions (e.g., hydrolysis of methyl acetate, thermal decomposition of acetaldehyde) — Order reflects the rate-determining step stoichiometry, whereas molecularity applies to individual elementary steps.

💡 Shortcuts & Memory Aids
Half-Life Power Rule
n = 1 + [log(t1/t2) / log(a2/a1)]

Application: Directly calculate reaction order n given two half-lives (t1, t2) at two initial concentrations (a1, a2).

Pseudo-Order Shortcut
Order = 1 for reactant in large excess

Application: Instantly recognize pseudo-first-order kinetics in hydrolysis reactions where water is the solvent.

Unit Analysis Trick
Unit of k = (L mol-1)n-1 s-1

Application: Determine the order n rapidly from the given units of the rate constant k.

🎯 Key Exam Points
  • The unit of a zero-order rate constant is mol L-1 s-1.
  • The unit of a first-order rate constant is s-1.
  • The unit of a second-order rate constant is L mol-1 s-1.
  • For a first-order reaction, t99.9% is approximately equal to 10 × t1/2.
  • Ostwald's isolation method is used to determine order for reactions involving multiple reactants.
  • A plot of ln(k) versus 1/T yields the activation energy from its slope via the Arrhenius equation.
🚫 Common Errors & Confusions
  • Confusing molecularity with reaction order; molecularity is always a whole number, whereas order can be fractional or zero.
  • Assuming pseudo-first-order reactions are true first-order elementary reactions.
  • Forgetting that half-life depends on initial concentration for all orders except first order.
  • Using integrated rate equations for reactions that proceed with reversible pathways or complex steps.

⚡ Quick Revision: Determination of Order of Reaction

Core formulas, rapid recall points, and distinguishing criteria at a glance.

Definition of Order

Reaction order is the sum of powers of concentration terms in the experimentally determined rate law expression.

Graphical Analysis

Linearized plots of concentration versus time distinctively separate zero, first, and second-order kinetics.

Half-Life Dependencies

Half-life scaling with initial concentration provides a direct mathematical route to extract the reaction order.

Isolation Technique

Isolating individual reactants by excess reagent conditions simplifies multi-variable rate equations.

📚 Terms & Units Glossary

Reaction Order (n)
Sum of exponents of concentration terms in rate law. Unitless.
Rate Constant (k)
Proportionality constant in rate equation. Units vary with order.
Half-Life (t1/2)
Time required for reactant concentration to drop by half. Measured in seconds.
Molecularity (M)
Number of reacting species colliding simultaneously in an elementary step.
Initial Rate ((dx/dt)t=0)
Rate of reaction at the very beginning of the reaction. mol L-1 s-1.
Pseudo Order (Pseudo-n)
Higher-order reaction made to behave like a lower order by excess reagent.

🎯 Key Takeaways

  • Zero order: t1/2 ∝ [A]0
  • First order: t1/2 = 0.693 / k
  • Second order: t1/2 = 1 / (k[A]0)
  • Arrhenius equation: k = A exp(-Ea / RT)
  • Unit of zero order k: mol L-1 s-1

📌 Quick Recall

  • Order is strictly an experimental quantity determined via rate laws.
  • Graphical methods utilize linear regressions of concentration transformations.
  • Half-life variations isolate the concentration dependency of the rate.
  • Ostwald's isolation method handles multi-reactant systems effectively.

⚖️ Key Differences

Order of Reaction VS Molecularity

Order is experimental and can be fractional/zero; molecularity is theoretical, whole-number, and applies only to elementary steps.

Question 1 of 18

What is the overall order of a reaction if the unit of its rate constant is L2 mol-2 s-1?

Explanation: The general unit for rate constant is (L mol-1)n-1 s-1. For n = 3, unit is (L mol-1)2 s-1 = L2 mol-2 s-1.

Question 2 of 18

In Ostwald's isolation method, how is the order with respect to a specific reactant determined?

Explanation: By taking all other reactants in large excess, their concentrations remain virtually constant, allowing isolation of the target reactant's order.

Question 3 of 18

Which of the following graphical plots gives a straight line for a second-order reaction (A → Products)?

Explanation: The integrated rate equation for second-order is 1/[A] = 1/[A]0 + kt, which is a straight line equation y = c + mx for 1/[A] versus t.

Question 4 of 18

The half-life of a reaction is halved when the initial concentration of the reactant is doubled. What is the order of the reaction?

Explanation: t1/2 ∝ 1/[A]0n-1. If t1/2 halves when [A]0 doubles, 1/2 = (1/2)n-1, giving n-1 = 1, so n = 2.

Question 5 of 18

What is the reaction order if the half-life is independent of the initial concentration?

Explanation: For a first-order reaction, t1/2 = ln(2)/k, which is completely independent of initial concentration.

Question 6 of 18

Which method is best suited to determine the order of a reaction with multiple reactants without changing reaction conditions?

Explanation: Ostwald's isolation method isolates each reactant by keeping others in large excess to find individual orders.

Question 7 of 18

The unit of rate of a chemical reaction is always:

Explanation: Rate of reaction is always change in concentration per unit time, expressed as mol L-1 s-1.

Question 8 of 18

For a zero-order reaction, the rate of reaction is:

Explanation: Rate = k[A]0 = k, meaning it is independent of the reactant concentration.

Question 9 of 18

A plot of log(t1/2) versus log([A]0) for a reaction gives a straight line with a slope of -1. What is the order?

Explanation: Slope = 1 - n. If slope = -1, then 1 - n = -1, which gives n = 2.

Question 10 of 18

Which of the following can be fractional for a chemical reaction?

Explanation: Order is an experimental quantity and can be fractional or zero. Molecularity refers to discrete molecular collisions and must be a positive integer.

Question 11 of 18

In the initial rate method, the slope of log(Rate) versus log[A]0 gives:

Explanation: Log(Rate) = log(k) + n log[A]0, so the slope of the log-log plot gives the order n.

Question 12 of 18

Hydrolysis of ester in the presence of dilute acid is an example of:

Explanation: Water is present in large excess, making its concentration effectively constant, resulting in pseudo-first-order kinetics.

Question 13 of 18

If a reaction has a rate law Rate = k[A]0.5[B]1.5, what is the overall order?

Explanation: Overall order is the sum of exponents: 0.5 + 1.5 = 2.0.

Question 14 of 18

Which differential rate law represents a first-order reaction?

Explanation: First-order differential rate law is -d[A]/dt = k[A].

Question 15 of 18

The time taken for 75% completion of a first-order reaction is equal to:

Explanation: 75% completion corresponds to 2 half-lives (50% in 1 t1/2, and half of the remaining 50% in the second t1/2).

Question 16 of 18

Activation energy of a reaction can be determined by measuring rate constants at:

Explanation: Using the Arrhenius equation form at two temperatures, Ea is calculated from the rate constants k1 and k2.

Question 17 of 18

For a zero-order reaction, the time required for complete consumption of the reactant ([A]0 / k) is:

Explanation: t1/2 = [A]0 / 2k, while complete consumption time t100% = [A]0 / k, which is 2 × t1/2.

Question 18 of 18

Which of the following methods involves plotting reactant concentration against time directly?

Explanation: The integration method (or graphical method) directly plots concentration values against time to match theoretical curves.

Question 1 of 10
What is the order of a reaction whose half-life is inversely proportional to its initial concentration?

Solution: t1/2 ∝ 1/[A]0n-1. For inverse proportionality (n-1 = 1), n = 2.

Question 2 of 10
Calculate the overall order of a reaction with rate constant unit L3 mol-3 s-1.

Solution: Unit is (L mol-1)n-1 s-1. Here n-1 = 3, so n = 4.

Question 3 of 10
What is the slope of the plot of ln[A] versus time for a first-order reaction?

Solution: ln[A] = ln[A]0 - kt, which matches y = c + mx with slope = -k.

Question 4 of 10
Which experimental method relies on measuring the time required for a fixed percentage of reactant to react at different initial concentrations?

Solution: The fractional life method (including half-life method) relates fractional lives to initial concentrations.

Question 5 of 10
In the presence of a catalyst, the activation energy of a reaction is:

Solution: Catalysts provide an alternative pathway with a lower activation energy.

Question 6 of 10
For a reaction with rate law Rate = k[A], what fraction of reactant remains after 3 half-lives?

Solution: After n half-lives, remaining fraction is (1/2)n. For n = 3, (1/2)3 = 1/8.

Question 7 of 10
Which plot is linear for a zero-order reaction?

Solution: [A] = [A]0 - kt, giving a straight line plot of [A] versus t.

Question 8 of 10
Ostwald's isolation method is particularly useful when the reaction involves:

Solution: It simplifies multi-reactant kinetics by isolating one reactant at a time when multiple reactants are present.

Question 9 of 10
If the initial concentration of a reactant in a first-order reaction is doubled, the rate constant k:

Solution: The rate constant k is temperature-dependent and independent of reactant concentrations.

Question 10 of 10
What is the unit of rate constant for a half-order reaction?

Solution: Unit = (L mol-1)n-1 s-1 = (L mol-1)0.5-1 s-1 = (L mol-1)-0.5 s-1 = mol-1/2 L1/2 s-1.
