Physical Chemistry

First-Order Reaction

A first-order reaction is a chemical process whose rate is directly proportional to the first power of the concentration of a single reactant. Its integrated rate law relates reactant concentration exponentially to elapsed time.

UP PGT Chemistry Subject Code 02 B.Sc. / M.Sc. Level

Mathematical Characteristics and Half-Life

  • The unit of rate constant (k) for a first-order reaction is time-1 (e.g., s-1, min-1).
  • The half-life (t1/2) of a first-order reaction is entirely independent of the initial concentration [A]0.
  • A plot of ln[A]t versus time (t) yields a straight line with a slope equal to -k and an intercept equal to ln[A]0.
  • The time required for completion of any given fraction of a first-order reaction is independent of the initial concentration.

Pseudo First-Order Reactions

  • A pseudo first-order reaction is a reaction that is bimolecular or higher order experimentally, but behaves kinetically as a first-order reaction.
  • This occurs when one reactant is present in large excess, rendering its concentration virtually constant throughout the reaction.
  • The acid-catalyzed hydrolysis of an ester (such as methyl acetate) is a classic example of a pseudo first-order reaction.
  • The rate expression simplifies because the concentration of the excess reagent is absorbed into the pseudo rate constant (k').

Fractional Life and Decay Constants

  • The time required for 75% completion (t3/4) of a first-order reaction is exactly twice its half-life (2 × t1/2).
  • The time required for 99.9% completion of a first-order reaction is approximately equal to 10 half-lives.
  • Radioactive decay processes strictly follow first-order kinetics, where the decay constant (λ) is analogous to the rate constant k.
  • The average life (τ) of a first-order reactant is the reciprocal of its rate constant, expressed as τ = 1/k.
📐 Key Formulas & Equations
Half-Life Expression
t1/2 = 0.693 / k
Conditions: Independent of initial reactant concentration
Units: t1/2 in time units, k in time-1
Differential Rate Law
-d[A]/dt = k[A]
Conditions: Elementary first-order process
Units: Rate in mol L-1 s-1, k in s-1
Exponential Concentration Decay
[A]t = [A]0 · e-kt
Conditions: At any given time t
Units: [A] in mol L-1, k in s-1, t in s
Gas-Phase First-Order Kinetics
k = (2.303 / t) · log(Pi / (2Pi - Pt))
Conditions: Reaction of type A(g) → B(g) + C(g) starting with pure A
Units: P in atm or torr, t in time units
Integrated Rate Law (Concentration Form)
k = (2.303 / t) · log([A]0 / [A]t)
Conditions: Single reactant consuming entirely to products
Units: k in s-1 or min-1, t in time units, [A] in mol L-1
⚠️ Important Exceptions & Anomalies
Exception Reaction rate depends heavily on reactant concentration changes over time.

Observation: Pseudo first-order reactions — One reactant is present in vast excess, keeping its concentration virtually constant so the rate depends only on the limiting reactant.

Exception Half-life of zero-order and second-order reactions depends on initial concentration.

Observation: First-order reactions — The mathematical derivation of t1/2 for first-order reactions cancels out the initial concentration term entirely.

Exception Complex multi-step reactions have complex rate laws matching overall stoichiometry.

Observation: Rate-determining step limiting first-order kinetics — If the first step is slow and rate-determining, the overall multi-step sequence can exhibit simple first-order kinetics.

💡 Shortcuts & Memory Aids
75% Completion Rule

Application: Directly calculate time for 75% completion without running full logarithms if half-life is known.

99.9% Completion Rule
t99.9% ≈ 10 × t1/2

Application: Quickly estimate the time required for virtually complete reaction in nuclear chemistry or kinetics problems.

Log Base 10 Conversion
k = (2.303 / t) · log([A]0 / [A]t)

Application: Remember to multiply by 2.303 when converting natural logarithm (ln) to common logarithm (log base 10).

🎯 Key Exam Points
  • The unit of the first-order rate constant is time-1.
  • The half-life of a first-order reaction is independent of initial concentration [A]0.
  • A plot of ln[A] vs time gives a straight line with a negative slope equal to -k.
  • Radioactive decay processes strictly obey first-order kinetics.
  • The time for 99.9% completion of a first-order reaction is equal to 10 half-lives.
  • Inversion of cane sugar and hydrolysis of esters are examples of pseudo first-order reactions.
🚫 Common Errors & Confusions
  • Confusing the units of first-order rate constant (s-1) with zero-order (mol L-1 s-1).
  • Failing to convert natural logarithms (ln) to common logarithms (log10) using the 2.303 factor.
  • Assuming half-life depends on initial concentration for first-order reactions, which only applies to zero and second-order.
  • Forgetting that pseudo first-order reactions depend on excess reactant conditions.

⚡ Quick Revision: First-Order Reaction

Core formulas, rapid recall points, and distinguishing criteria at a glance.

Differential and Integrated Rate Laws

First-order kinetics features a rate proportional to [A] to the first power. The integrated form is ln([A]0/[A]) = kt.

Characteristics of Half-Life

The half-life t1/2 = 0.693/k is constant and independent of the starting amount of reactant.

Pseudo First-Order Kinetics

Bimolecular reactions where one reactant is in large excess follow apparent first-order rate laws.

Graphical Analysis

Linear plots of ln[A] versus t confirm first-order behavior with a slope of -k.

📚 Terms & Units Glossary

Rate Constant (k) (k)
Proportionality constant in rate law; unit s-1 for first-order.
Half-Life (t1/2) (t1/2)
Time required for reactant concentration to reduce to half its initial value.
Integrated Rate Law (ln([A]0/[A]) = kt)
Equation relating concentration remaining to elapsed time.
Pseudo First-Order (Rate = k'[A])
Higher order reaction behaving as first order due to excess reactant.
Decay Constant (λ)
Rate constant equivalent in radioactive first-order disintegration.
Activation Energy (Ea)
Minimum energy barrier required for reactants to form products in J mol-1.

🎯 Key Takeaways

  • k = (2.303 / t) · log([A]0 / [A]t)
  • t1/2 = 0.693 / k
  • Units of k for first-order: time-1
  • t3/4 = 2 × t1/2
  • Radioactive disintegration follows first-order kinetics

📌 Quick Recall

  • First-order rate depends linearly on concentration of one reactant.
  • Half-life is constant and independent of initial concentration.
  • Graphical representation of ln[A] vs t gives straight line slope -k.
  • Pseudo first-order reactions simplify kinetics via large excess of one reagent.

⚖️ Key Differences

First-Order Half-Life VS Zero-Order Half-Life

First-order half-life is independent of [A]0, whereas zero-order half-life is directly proportional to [A]0.

Question 1 of 18

What is the unit of the rate constant for a first-order reaction?

Explanation: Rate = k[A]. Thus k = Rate / [A] = (mol L-1 s-1) / (mol L-1) = s-1.

Question 2 of 18

The half-life of a first-order reaction is 10 seconds. What is its rate constant?

Explanation: k = 0.693 / t1/2 = 0.693 / 10 = 0.0693 s-1.

Question 3 of 18

Which of the following plots gives a straight line for a first-order reaction?

Explanation: The integrated rate law in linear form is ln[A]t = -kt + ln[A]0, so ln[A] vs t gives a straight line.

Question 4 of 18

For a first-order reaction, the time required for 99.9% completion is approximately equal to:

Explanation: t99.9% = (2.303 / k) · log(100 / 0.1) = (2.303 / k) · 3 = 6.909 / k ≈ 10 × (0.693 / k) = 10 t1/2.

Question 5 of 18

Acid-catalyzed hydrolysis of methyl acetate is an example of:

Explanation: Water is present in large excess, making the bimolecular reaction pseudo first-order.

Question 6 of 18

If the initial concentration of reactant is doubled in a first-order reaction, its half-life:

Explanation: The half-life of a first-order reaction (t1/2 = 0.693/k) is independent of the initial concentration.

Question 7 of 18

What is the slope of the line when ln[A] is plotted against time t for a first-order reaction?

Explanation: From ln[A]t = -kt + ln[A]0, comparing with y = mx + c gives slope m = -k.

Question 8 of 18

Radioactive disintegration is a classic example of:

Explanation: Radioactive decay rates depend on the number of radioactive nuclei present, following first-order kinetics.

Question 9 of 18

The time required for 75% completion of a first-order reaction is:

Explanation: t75% = (2.303 / k) · log(100 / 25) = (2.303 / k) · 0.602 = 2 × (0.693 / k) = 2 t1/2.

Question 10 of 18

In a first-order reaction, the concentration of reactant decreases from 0.8 M to 0.2 M in 30 minutes. What is the half-life?

Explanation: 0.8 M → 0.4 M (1 half-life) → 0.2 M (2 half-lives). Total time = 30 mins for 2 half-lives, so t1/2 = 15 minutes.

Question 11 of 18

Which differential rate equation represents a first-order reaction with respect to reactant A?

Explanation: By definition, the rate of a first-order reaction is proportional to the first power of reactant concentration.

Question 12 of 18

For a first-order reaction with rate constant k = 1.15 x 10-3 s-1, what is the half-life?

Explanation: t1/2 = 0.693 / (1.15 × 10-3) ≈ 602 seconds ≈ 600 s.

Question 13 of 18

Can a reaction have a fractional order?

Explanation: Reaction orders are determined experimentally and can be fractional, zero, or negative.

Question 14 of 18

If log([A]0/[A]) is plotted against time t, the slope of the line is equal to:

Explanation: Since k = (2.303 / t) · log([A]0/[A]), rearranging gives log([A]0/[A]) = (k / 2.303)t, so slope is k / 2.303.

Question 15 of 18

Which factor affects the rate constant k of a first-order reaction?

Explanation: The rate constant is dependent on temperature (Arrhenius equation) and presence of catalysts, but independent of concentration.

Question 16 of 18

What is the order of reaction if the unit of rate constant is mol-1 L s-1?

Explanation: Units of k for nth order are (mol L-1)1-n s-1. For n = 2, units are L mol-1 s-1.

Question 17 of 18

In the thermal decomposition of gaseous $N_2O_5$ ($2N_2O_5 → 4NO_2 + O_2$), the reaction order is:

Explanation: Decomposition of $N_2O_5$ is a standard gas-phase first-order reaction.

Question 18 of 18

The average life (τ) of a first-order reaction is related to the rate constant k as:

Explanation: Average life is defined as the reciprocal of the rate constant: τ = 1/k.

Question 1 of 10
What is the time required for 99% completion of a first-order reaction having rate constant k?

Solution: t = (2.303 / k) · log(100 / 1) = (2.303 / k) · 2 = 4.606 / k.

Question 2 of 10
Which of the following statements about a first-order reaction is incorrect?

Solution: A first-order reaction theoretically takes infinite time to reach 100% completion.

Question 3 of 10
The rate constant of a first-order reaction is 4.6 x 10-4 s-1. What is the time required for the reactant concentration to drop from 0.5 M to 0.1 M?

Solution: t = (2.303 / (4.6 × 10-4)) · log(0.5 / 0.1) = (2.303 × 0.699) / (4.6 × 10-4) ≈ 3500 seconds.

Question 4 of 10
What is the order of radioactive decay of uranium?

Solution: All radioactive disintegration processes follow first-order kinetics.

Question 5 of 10
For a first-order reaction, the ratio of time taken for 99.9% completion to the half-life is approximately:

Solution: t99.9% ≈ 10 t1/2, so the ratio is 10.

Question 6 of 10
In a first-order reaction, concentration decreases from 1.0 mol L-1 to 0.25 mol L-1 in 40 minutes. The half-life is:

Solution: 1.0 → 0.5 (1 half-life) → 0.25 (2 half-lives). 2 × t1/2 = 40 mins, so t1/2 = 20 minutes.

Question 7 of 10
Which gas-phase reaction expression is used to find the rate constant k of a first-order reaction A(g) → B(g) + C(g)?

Solution: For A(g) → B(g) + C(g), total pressure at time t is Pt = Pi - x + x + x = Pi + x, so x = Pt - Pi and Pi - x = 2Pi - Pt.

Question 8 of 10
If temperature is increased, how does the rate constant k of a first-order reaction change?

Solution: According to the Arrhenius equation, rate constant increases exponentially with an increase in temperature.

Question 10 of 10
Which differential equation correctly defines a first-order reaction involving reactant X?

Solution: The rate of reaction dx/dt is proportional to the concentration remaining (a-x) raised to the first power.
