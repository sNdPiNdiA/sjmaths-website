Physical Chemistry

Molecularity of Reaction

Molecularity is defined as the number of reacting species (atoms, ions, or molecules) that collide simultaneously in an elementary step to bring about a chemical reaction. It is a theoretical integer value derived strictly from reaction mechanisms and cannot be zero, fractional, or greater than three.

UP PGT Chemistry Subject Code 02 B.Sc. / M.Sc. Level

Fundamental Definition and Characteristics

  • Molecularity applies exclusively to elementary steps and has no physical significance for overall complex reactions.
  • It represents the number of reacting particles colliding simultaneously in a single transition state.
  • Values of molecularity are strictly positive integers (1, 2, or 3) because simultaneous collisions of four or more particles possess negligible statistical probability.
  • It is a theoretical concept determined by analyzing proposed reaction mechanisms.

Classification of Elementary Reactions

  • Unimolecular reactions involve the rearrangement or decomposition of a single molecule, exhibiting first-order kinetics under low-pressure limits.
  • Bimolecular reactions require the simultaneous collision of two species, representing the most common class of elementary chemical steps.
  • Trimolecular (termolecular) reactions involve three species colliding simultaneously, making them extremely rare due to strict orientation and energy requirements.
  • Elementary reactions with molecularity greater than three are virtually unknown in chemical kinetics.

Distinction Between Molecularity and Order

  • Molecularity is a theoretical property derived from stoichiometry of an elementary step, whereas order of a reaction is an experimental value determined from the rate law.
  • Molecularity can never be zero, fractional, or negative, whereas reaction order can be zero, fractional, or negative.
  • For an elementary reaction, the molecularity matches the sum of concentration exponents in the rate law.
  • For complex multi-step reactions, overall molecularity is undefined, but overall order remains well-defined.
📐 Key Formulas & Equations
Bimolecular Elementary Reaction Rate
Rate = k[A][B] or k[A]2
Conditions: Elementary step involving two reactant species
Units: Rate in mol L-1 s-1, k in L mol-1 s-1
Trimolecular Elementary Reaction Rate
Rate = k[A][B][C] or k[A]2[B] or k[A]3
Conditions: Elementary step involving three simultaneous reactant species
Units: Rate in mol L-1 s-1, k in L2 mol-2 s-1
Overall Reaction Rate Law
Rate = k[A]x[B]y
Conditions: Complex reactions where x and y are determined experimentally
Units: x and y are order with respect to A and B respectively
Unimolecular Elementary Reaction Rate
Rate = k[A]
Conditions: Elementary step with single reactant molecule
Units: Rate in mol L-1 s-1, k in s-1
⚠️ Important Exceptions & Anomalies
Exception Molecularity cannot exceed three for elementary steps.

Observation: No known elementary reactions have a molecularity of four or higher. — The probability of simultaneous, highly energetic, and correctly oriented collision of four or more independent particles in solution or gas phase is statistically near zero.

Exception Molecularity must always be a whole number.

Observation: Pseudo-order reactions appear to have fractional or altered molecularity in overall equations. — In reactions like the hydrolysis of an ester, water is present in vast excess, making its concentration effectively constant; molecularity applies only to individual elementary steps, not the overall balanced equation.

Exception Molecularity is identical to reaction order for all single-step reactions.

Observation: Certain elementary reactions involving intermediates or catalysts deviate. — If an elementary step includes a species in high constant concentration or rapid pre-equilibrium, the observed kinetic order may differ from the true molecularity of the rate-determining step.

🎯 Key Exam Points
  • Molecularity is strictly applicable to elementary reactions.
  • The maximum possible molecularity for a chemical reaction is three.
  • Molecularity can never be zero, negative, or fractional.
  • Order of a reaction can be zero, but molecularity can never be zero.
  • For complex reactions, overall molecularity has no physical meaning.
  • In a single-step reaction, molecularity equals the sum of stoichiometric coefficients of reactants.
🚫 Common Errors & Confusions
  • Assuming molecularity can be determined from the overall balanced equation of a complex reaction.
  • Confusing molecularity with the order of a reaction in multi-step mechanisms.
  • Stating that fractional molecularity is possible for pseudo-first-order reactions.
  • Believing that trimolecular reactions are common in gas phase kinetics.

⚡ Quick Revision: Molecularity of Reaction

Core formulas, rapid recall points, and distinguishing criteria at a glance.

Definition of Elementary Steps

Elementary steps represent single microscopic events where reactant particles collide and transform directly into products without intermediates.

Theoretical Limitations

Statistical mechanics restricts simultaneous effective collisions to a maximum of three particles, capping molecularity at three.

Comparison Framework

Molecularity is purely theoretical and mechanism-dependent, whereas reaction order is derived entirely from experimental rate laws.

Pseudo-Unimolecular Reactions

Reactions involving large excesses of one reactant shift kinetic behavior, but true molecularity of the elementary step remains unchanged.

📚 Terms & Units Glossary

Molecularity (M)
Number of reacting particles colliding in an elementary step.
Elementary Step (Single-step reaction)
A reaction that occurs in a single event without intermediates.
Complex Reaction (Multi-step reaction)
A reaction occurring via a sequence of two or more elementary steps.
Reaction Order (n)
Sum of exponents of concentrations in the rate law equation.
Unimolecular (M = 1)
Elementary reaction involving a single reactant molecule.
Trimolecular (M = 3)
Elementary reaction involving three colliding reactant species.

🎯 Key Takeaways

  • Molecularity is always an integer: 1, 2, or 3.
  • Molecularity is a theoretical concept; order is experimental.
  • Overall molecularity for complex reactions is undefined.
  • Elementary steps with molecularity > 3 do not exist.
  • Order and molecularity may or may not be equal for elementary steps.

📌 Quick Recall

  • Molecularity measures collision multiplicity in elementary steps.
  • It is restricted to integer values between 1 and 3.
  • It differs fundamentally from reaction order in determination and scope.
  • Complex reactions are analyzed as a sum of elementary steps.

⚖️ Key Differences

Molecularity VS Reaction Order

Molecularity is theoretical and integral (1-3); order is experimental and can be fractional or zero.

A reaction has a rate law: Rate = k[A]1.5. What can be said about its elementary molecularity?

Question 8 of 20

Why are trimolecular elementary reactions extremely rare?

Explanation: The statistical probability of three particles colliding simultaneously with sufficient energy and correct orientation is extremely low.

Question 9 of 20

Question 17 of 20

Which statement regarding molecularity is incorrect?

Explanation: Molecularity can never be fractional; this statement is incorrect.
