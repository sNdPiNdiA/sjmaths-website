Physical Chemistry

Third-Order Reaction

A third-order reaction is a chemical process whose rate is proportional to the third power of concentration terms of the reactants. Such reactions are relatively rare in chemical kinetics due to the low probability of a triple collision.

UP PGT Chemistry Subject Code 02 B.Sc. / M.Sc. Level

Kinetic Characteristics and Rate Laws

  • The rate of reaction depends on the cube of the reactant concentration for a single-reactant third-order process.
  • The overall molecularity of elementary third-order reactions is strictly three, involving termolecular collisions.
  • The unit of the rate constant (k) for a third-order reaction is L2 mol-2 s-1 or dm6 mol-2 s-1.
  • Plots of 1/[A]2 versus time (t) yield a straight line with a positive slope equal to 2k.

Integrated Equations and Half-Life Behavior

  • The half-life (t1/2) of a third-order reaction is inversely proportional to the square of the initial concentration ([A]0-2).
  • Doubling the initial concentration of a third-order reactant decreases its half-life by a factor of four.
  • The integrated rate expression contains terms with inverse squares of concentrations, unlike first or second-order equations.
  • Reactions proceeding via a termolescular mechanism are exceedingly rare in gas-phase kinetics due to entropy constraints.

Classic Examples and Gas-Phase Kinetics

  • The oxidation of nitric oxide by oxygen, 2NO + O2 → 2NO2, serves as the classic example of a third-order gas-phase reaction.
  • The reaction of ferric chloride with stannous chloride in solution also displays third-order kinetics under specific conditions.
  • Apparent third-order kinetics often arise from a rapid pre-equilibrium step followed by a rate-determining bimolecular step.
  • Temperature dependence of the rate constant follows the standard Arrhenius equation, regardless of reaction order.

⚠️ Important Exceptions & Anomalies
Exception Higher order reactions should be common based on stoichiometry

Observation: Reactions of order higher than three are virtually unknown — The simultaneous collision of four or more independent particles with correct orientation and sufficient energy has a statistically near-zero probability.

Exception Half-life is independent of initial concentration for first-order reactions

Observation: Half-life depends strongly on initial concentration for third-order reactions — The integrated rate law incorporates inverse concentration squared terms, directly coupling t1/2 to [A]0-2.

Exception Stoichiometric coefficients dictate the overall reaction order

Observation: Many reactions with stoichiometric sum of three exhibit second or first-order kinetics — Reaction order is an experimental quantity determined by the rate law, whereas stoichiometry only reflects the overall balanced chemical equation.

  • The half-life of a third-order reaction is inversely proportional to the square of the initial concentration.
  • A plot of 1/[A]2 versus time gives a linear graph with slope equal to 2k.

🚫 Common Errors & Confusions
  • Confusing the unit of third-order rate constant with second-order rate constant.
  • Assuming the stoichiometric coefficients of a balanced equation automatically determine reaction order.
  • Applying the first-order half-life formula (0.693/k) to third-order rate calculations.
  • Forgetting the factor of 2 in the integrated rate law: 1/[A]2 - 1/[A]02 = 2kt.

⚡ Quick Revision: Third-Order Reaction

Core formulas, rapid recall points, and distinguishing criteria at a glance.

Differential Kinetics

The rate of a third-order reaction depends on the third power of the reactant concentration, expressed mathematically as -d[A]/dt = k[A]3.

Integrated Rate Law

Integration of the differential rate equation yields 1/[A]2 - 1/[A]02 = 2kt, where k is the third-order rate constant.

Half-Life Dependency

Unlike first-order kinetics, the half-life of a third-order reaction depends on initial concentration according to t1/2 = 3/(2k[A]02).

Termolecular Mechanism

True third-order reactions require the simultaneous collision of three reactant molecules, making them exceptionally rare in chemical systems.

📚 Terms & Units Glossary

Third-Order Rate Constant (k)
Proportionality constant with units L2 mol-2 s-1.
Termolecular Reaction (Elementary step)
A reaction involving three reacting species colliding simultaneously.
Integrated Rate Equation (1/[A]2 - 1/[A]02 = 2kt)
Relation between concentration and time for equal initial concentrations.
Half-Life (t1/2)
Time required for reactant concentration to reach half its initial value.
Differential Rate Law (-d[A]/dt)
Instantaneous rate expressed as a function of reactant concentration.
Overall Order (n = 3)
Sum of exponents of concentration terms in the rate law.

🎯 Key Takeaways

  • Unit of k: L2 mol-2 s-1
  • Integrated equation: 1/[A]2 - 1/[A]02 = 2kt
  • Half-life formula: t1/2 = 3 / (2k[A]02)
  • Graph: 1/[A]2 vs t gives a straight line with slope = 2k
  • Classic example: 2NO + O2 → 2NO2

📌 Quick Recall

  • Third-order reactions have rates dependent on [A]3.
  • Half-life varies inversely with the square of initial concentration.
  • True termolecular elementary steps are exceedingly rare.
  • Graphical analysis requires plotting 1/[A]2 against time.

⚖️ Key Differences

Molecularity VS Order of Reaction

Molecularity is a theoretical count of colliding species in an elementary step, whereas order is an experimentally determined parameter.

Question 1 of 18

What is the unit of the rate constant for a third-order reaction?

Explanation: The unit of rate constant is given by Ln-1 mol1-n s-1. For n = 3, this evaluates to L2 mol-2 s-1.

Question 2 of 18

Which of the following is a classic example of a third-order gas-phase reaction?

Explanation: The oxidation of nitric oxide (2NO + O2 → 2NO2) is a well-established third-order termolecular reaction.

Question 3 of 18

For a third-order reaction with equal initial concentrations [A]0, what is the expression for the half-life (t1/2)?

Explanation: Substituting [A] = [A]0/2 into the integrated rate law yields t1/2 = 3 / (2k[A]02).

Question 4 of 18

If the initial concentration of a reactant in a third-order reaction is doubled, how does the initial rate change?

Explanation: Rate = k[A]3. Doubling [A] results in (2)3 = 8 times increase in the initial rate.

Question 5 of 18

What plot gives a straight line for a third-order reaction (with equal initial concentrations)?

Explanation: The integrated rate law 1/[A]2 - 1/[A]02 = 2kt shows that plotting 1/[A]2 versus t gives a linear relationship.

Question 6 of 18

What is the slope of the linear plot of 1/[A]2 versus time for a third-order reaction?

Explanation: Rearranging the integrated rate law to 1/[A]2 = 2kt + 1/[A]02 indicates the slope is 2k.

Question 7 of 18

Why are reactions of order higher than three extremely rare?

Explanation: The probability of more than three molecules colliding simultaneously with proper orientation and energy is virtually negligible.

Question 8 of 18

How does the half-life of a third-order reaction change when the initial concentration is halved?

Explanation: Since t1/2 ∝ 1/[A]02, halving [A]0 increases t1/2 by a factor of 1/(1/2)2 = 4.

Question 9 of 18

What is the differential rate equation for a third-order reaction involving a single reactant A?

Explanation: By definition, the rate of a third-order reaction is proportional to the third power of the reactant concentration: -d[A]/dt = k[A]3.

Question 10 of 18

Which factor does NOT affect the rate constant (k) of a third-order reaction?

Explanation: The rate constant k is independent of reactant concentrations; it depends on temperature, activation energy, and catalysts.

Question 11 of 18

In the integrated rate law for a third-order reaction with equal initial concentration 'a', what term appears in the denominator?

Explanation: The integrated rate law is k = (1/2t)[(1/[A]2) - (1/[A]02)], leading to 2a2 terms in half-life and rate expressions.

Question 12 of 18

If a third-order reaction has a rate constant of 2.5 L2 mol-2 s-1, what are its dimensions?

Explanation: The unit L2 mol-2 s-1 corresponds to (mol L-1)-2 s-1, which is concentration-2 time-1.

Question 13 of 18

Can a third-order reaction have a fractional order?

Explanation: Overall reaction orders can be fractional in complex (multi-step) reactions, though elementary third-order reactions are integer-valued.

Question 14 of 18

What is the molecularity of a third-order elementary reaction?

Explanation: Molecularity is the number of reacting particles colliding in an elementary step. A third-order elementary reaction is termolecular (molecularity = 3).

Question 15 of 18

If the rate of a reaction triples when concentration is tripled, what is the order?

Explanation: Rate ∝ [A]n. If rate triples when concentration triples, 31 = 3, so n = 1 (first order).

Question 16 of 18

Which of the following describes the effect of a catalyst on a third-order reaction?

Explanation: A catalyst provides an alternative pathway with a lower activation energy, thereby increasing the rate constant k according to the Arrhenius equation.

Question 17 of 18

For a third-order reaction, if [A] drops from 1.0 M to 0.5 M in time t1, and from 0.5 M to 0.25 M in time t2, what is the relation between t1 and t2?

Explanation: Since t1/2 ∝ 1/[A]02, halving the concentration from 0.5 M increases the half-life by 22 = 4 times. Thus, t2 = 4t1.

Question 18 of 18

Which mathematical operation is primarily used to derive the integrated rate law for a third-order reaction?

Explanation: Integrating the separable differential equation ∫ -d[A]/[A]3 = ∫ k dt yields the third-order integrated rate law.

Question 1 of 10
Determine the overall order of a reaction if its rate law is given by Rate = k[A]1.5[B]1.5.

Solution: Overall order is the sum of concentration exponents: 1.5 + 1.5 = 3.0.

Question 2 of 10
For a third-order reaction, if the concentration of reactant A decreases to 1/3 of its initial value, how does the rate change?

Solution: Rate ∝ [A]3. (1/3)3 = 1/27, so the rate decreases by a factor of 27.

Question 3 of 10
Which of the following units corresponds to a third-order rate constant in terms of atm and time?

Solution: For gas-phase reactions expressed in pressure units, the rate constant unit for order n is (atm)1-n s-1. For n = 3, it is atm-2 s-1.

Question 4 of 10
Calculate the half-life of a third-order reaction where k = 2.0 × 10-3 L2 mol-2 s-1 and [A]0 = 0.5 mol L-1.

Solution: t1/2 = 3 / (2k[A]02) = 3 / (2 × 2.0 × 10-3 × (0.5)2) = 3 / (4 × 10-3 × 0.25) = 3 / 10-3 = 3000 s.

Question 5 of 10
What is the dimension of the rate of a third-order reaction?

Solution: The rate of any reaction always has the dimensions of concentration per unit time, i.e., mol L-1 s-1.

Question 6 of 10
In a third-order reaction with equal initial concentration 'a', the time required for 75% completion of the reaction is:

Solution: At 75% completion, [A] = a/4. Substituting into k = (1/2t)[(1/[A]2) - (1/[A]02)] gives t = (15)/(2ka2).

Question 7 of 10
Which of the following statements is incorrect regarding third-order reactions?

Solution: Third-order reactions are rare in both gas and liquid phases due to the low probability of termolecular collisions.

Question 8 of 10
If a plot of 1/[A]2 versus time yields a straight line with a slope of 0.04 L2 mol-2 s-1, what is the rate constant k?

Solution: The slope of the 1/[A]2 vs t plot is equal to 2k. Therefore, 2k = 0.04 → k = 0.02 L2 mol-2 s-1.

Question 9 of 10
What is the integration constant when integrating the differential rate law for a third-order reaction with equal initial concentration?

Solution: Applying boundary conditions at t = 0, [A] = [A]0 yields the integration constant 1/[A]02.

Question 10 of 10
How does an increase in temperature impact the rate constant of a third-order reaction?

Solution: According to the Arrhenius equation (k = Ae-Ea/RT), raising the temperature increases the rate constant exponentially regardless of reaction order.
