Physical Chemistry

Zero-Order Reaction

A zero-order reaction is a chemical process in which the reaction rate is independent of the concentration of the reactants. The rate of the reaction remains constant throughout the course of the reaction at a given temperature.

UP PGT Chemistry Subject Code 02 B.Sc. / M.Sc. Level

Mathematical Derivation and Integrated Rate Equation

  • The rate of a zero-order reaction is expressed as Rate = k[A]0 = k, where k is the zero-order rate constant.
  • Integration of the differential form yields [A]t - [A]0 = -kt, behaving linearly like y = mx + c.
  • A plot of [A]t versus time t gives a straight line with a slope equal to -k and a y-intercept equal to [A]0.

Characteristics of Half-Life and Completion

  • The half-life of a zero-order reaction t1/2 depends directly on the initial concentration [A]0.
  • Unlike first-order reactions, zero-order reactions proceed to completion in a finite time given by t = [A]0 / k.
  • Successive half-life intervals (e.g., from 50% to 75%) decrease progressively because the remaining initial concentration decreases.

Physical Examples and Experimental Conditions

  • Thermal decomposition of gaseous ammonia on a hot platinum surface at high pressures is a classic example of a zero-order reaction.
  • Enzyme-catalyzed reactions under high substrate concentrations operate via zero-order kinetics because the active sites of the enzyme are saturated.
  • Photochemical reactions like the combination of hydrogen and chlorine gases (H2 + Cl2 → 2HCl) follow zero-order kinetics under constant light intensity.

⚠️ Important Exceptions & Anomalies
Exception Reaction rates generally decrease as reactant concentration decreases over time.

Observation: Zero-order reaction rate remains strictly constant — The rate-determining step does not involve the participation of the reactant molecules in terms of concentration dependency.

Exception Half-life of chemical reactions is independent of initial concentration (e.g., first-order).

Observation: Zero-order half-life is directly proportional to initial concentration — Derived from t1/2 = [A]0 / 2k, making half-life dependent on how much starting material is present.

Exception Catalytic reactions depend on the concentration of both reactants and catalysts.

Observation: Enzymatic reactions become zero-order at very high substrate concentrations — Enzyme active sites become completely saturated, making the reaction velocity independent of further increases in substrate concentration.

💡 Shortcuts & Memory Aids
Unit Identification Trick
Unit of k = mol1-n Ln-1 s-1

Application: For zero-order (n = 0), unit becomes mol L-1 s-1. Always match the unit of k to identify the reaction order quickly in MCQs.

Graph Slope Shortcut
Slope = -k for [A] vs t

Application: If a graph of concentration versus time is a straight line sloping downward, immediately identify the kinetics as zero-order.

Completion Time Calculation
t100% = 2 × t1/2

Application: Since t1/2 = [A]0/2k and t100% = [A]0/k, total completion time is always exactly twice the half-life.

🎯 Key Exam Points
  • The unit of the rate constant for a zero-order reaction is mol L-1 time-1.
  • A plot of concentration [A] versus time t yields a straight line with a negative slope (-k).
  • The half-life of a zero-order reaction is directly proportional to the initial concentration of the reactant.
  • Decomposition of NH3 on a platinum surface at high pressure is a zero-order reaction.
  • Enzyme-catalyzed reactions exhibit zero-order kinetics at high substrate saturation.
  • Zero-order reactions always go to completion in a finite interval of time equal to [A]0 / k.
🚫 Common Errors & Confusions
  • Assuming zero-order reactions have concentration terms in their rate laws; remember Rate = k[A]0 = k.
  • Confusing the half-life expression of zero-order (depends on [A]0) with first-order (independent of [A]0).
  • Forgetting that photochemical reactions involving intense light sources often follow zero-order kinetics.
  • Writing incorrect units for the zero-order rate constant (using s-1 instead of mol L-1 s-1).

⚡ Quick Revision: Zero-Order Reaction

Core formulas, rapid recall points, and distinguishing criteria at a glance.

Differential and Integrated Rate Laws

The rate of a zero-order reaction is constant and independent of reactant concentration, expressed mathematically as [A]t = -kt + [A]0.

Graphical Analysis

Linear plots of reactant concentration versus time verify zero-order kinetics, where the slope directly yields the negative value of the rate constant k.

Half-Life and Completion Time

Zero-order half-life varies directly with initial concentration, and reactions reach 100% completion in finite time intervals.

Practical Examples

Heterogeneous catalytic decompositions and enzyme kinetics under high substrate saturation serve as standard physical examples.

⚖️ Key Differences

Zero-Order Half-Life VS First-Order Half-Life

Zero-order half-life depends on [A]0, whereas first-order half-life is completely independent of [A]0.

Question 1 of 10
What is the slope of the integrated zero-order rate equation when plotted in standard linear form?

Solution: Comparing [A]t = -kt + [A]0 with y = mx + c gives slope m = -k.

Question 2 of 10
Calculate the half-life of a zero-order reaction where [A]0 = 0.40 mol L-1 and k = 2.0 × 10-3 mol L-1 s-1.

Solution: t1/2 = [A]0 / (2k) = 0.40 / (2 × 2.0 × 10-3) = 0.40 / 0.004 = 100 seconds.

Question 3 of 10
Which condition typically favors zero-order kinetics in heterogeneous catalytic reactions?

Solution: At high pressure, catalytic surfaces become saturated with reactant molecules, making the rate independent of pressure.

Question 4 of 10
If a zero-order reaction takes 20 minutes to complete 50%, how much more time is required for 100% completion?

Solution: t1/2 = 20 min. Total completion time t100% = 2 × t1/2 = 40 min. Remaining time = 40 - 20 = 20 minutes.

Question 5 of 10
What is the dimension of the rate constant k for a zero-order reaction in terms of concentration (C) and time (t)?

Solution: k has units of mol L-1 s-1, which corresponds to concentration per unit time (C t-1).

Question 6 of 10
Which of the following plots is NOT linear for a zero-order reaction?

Solution: ln[A] versus t is linear for first-order reactions, not zero-order.

Question 7 of 10
In the photochemical reaction H2 + Cl2 → 2HCl, the reaction order with respect to reactants under constant light is:

Solution: Under constant illumination intensity, rate depends only on photon flux, making it zero-order.

Question 8 of 10
For a zero-order reaction, if [A]0 = 0.6 mol L-1 and k = 0.03 mol L-1 min-1, find the concentration after 10 minutes.

Solution: [A]t = [A]0 - kt = 0.6 - (0.03 × 10) = 0.6 - 0.3 = 0.3 mol L-1.

Question 9 of 10
What is the relation between half-life (t1/2) and completion time (t100%) for a zero-order reaction?

Solution: t100% = [A]0 / k while t1/2 = [A]0 / 2k, hence t100% = 2 × t1/2.

Question 10 of 10
Why do enzyme kinetics follow zero-order behavior at high substrate concentrations?

Solution: Enzyme saturation means maximum turnover rate is achieved, making velocity independent of further substrate concentration increases.
