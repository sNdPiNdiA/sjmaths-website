Kinetic Molecular Theory of Reaction Rates

The kinetic molecular theory of reaction rates relates macroscopic reaction rates to microscopic molecular collisions, velocities, and energy distributions. It posits that chemical reactions occur when reactant molecules collide with sufficient energy and correct orientation.

UP PGT Chemistry Subject Code 02 B.Sc. / M.Sc. Level

Collision Frequency and Molecular Speeds

  • The collision frequency (Z) represents the total number of molecular collisions occurring per unit volume per unit time in a reacting system.
  • Average molecular velocity is proportional to √(8RT / πM), establishing that lighter molecules possess higher collision velocities at a given temperature.
  • Only a small fraction of total collisions, known as effective collisions, result in product formation due to energy and orientation constraints.

Threshold Energy and Orientation Factor

  • Threshold energy is the minimum total energy reactants must possess for a collision to yield products, encompassing both kinetic and potential energy components.
  • The steric factor (ρ) accounts for the spatial orientation requirements during molecular encounters, where incorrect geometry prevents bond rearrangement.
  • Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution curves prove that the fraction of molecules with energy exceeding activation energy (Ea) increases exponentially with temperature.

Potential Energy Surfaces and Reaction Coordinates

  • A reaction coordinate diagram maps potential energy changes along the lowest energy pathway from reactants through the transition state to products.
  • The transition state (activated complex) exists at a potential energy maximum along the reaction coordinate with an extremely fleeting lifetime.
  • Catalysts function by providing an alternative reaction mechanism characterized by a lower activation energy barrier, thereby accelerating both forward and reverse rates.
📐 Key Formulas & Equations
Collision Theory Rate Expression
Rate = ZAB · ρ · exp(-Ea / RT)
Conditions: Bimolecular gas-phase elementary reactions
Units: Rate in mol L-1 s-1, ZAB in collisions L-1 s-1, ρ is steric factor (dimensionless)
Temperature Coefficient
θ = k(T+10) / kT ≈ 2 to 3
Conditions: Applicable for most room temperature homogeneous reactions
Units: θ is dimensionless
Frequency Factor (Collision Number)
ZAB = π d2 · c · nA · nB
Conditions: Hard-sphere collision model for gas molecules
Units: d in meters, c in m s-1, n in molecules m-3
Activation Energy Temperature Derivative
d(ln k)/dT = Ea / (RT2)
Conditions: Van 't Hoff-Arrhenius differential form
Units: T in Kelvin, Ea in J mol-1
Arrhenius Equation
k = A · exp(-Ea / RT)
Conditions: Valid for elementary and complex reactions over moderate temperature ranges
Units: k in s-1 or mol-1 L s-1, Ea in J mol-1, R in J K-1 mol-1, T in Kelvin
⚠️ Important Exceptions & Anomalies
Exception Reaction rates generally double for every 10°C rise in temperature.

Observation: Enzyme-catalyzed reactions and certain explosive chain reactions show non-exponential dependencies or optimum temperature cutoffs. — Thermal denaturation of protein structures at elevated temperatures destroys catalytic activity.

Exception Activation energy is strictly positive for all elementary chemical transformations.

Observation: Certain radical-radical recombinations and ion-molecule reactions exhibit zero or negative activation energy. — These barrierless processes involve attractive electrostatic forces or radical coupling where increasing temperature decreases the collision capture cross-section.

Exception Collision theory predicts rate constants accurately for complex multi-atomic molecules.

Observation: Calculated pre-exponential factors vastly overestimate experimental values for complex molecules. — Internal degrees of freedom and strict spatial orientation requirements lower the actual steric factor significantly below unity.

💡 Shortcuts & Memory Aids
Arrhenius Two-Point Log Shortcut
log(k2 / k1) = (Ea / 2.303R) · [(T2 - T1) / (T1T2)]

Application: Use this direct logarithmic form to rapidly solve for Ea or rate constants at different temperatures without calculating individual exponents.

Temperature Coefficient Quick Estimation
kT2 = kT1 · θ(ΔT / 10)

Application: Quickly estimate rate constant scaling over large temperature jumps using θ = 2 as a standard default when specific values are omitted.

Activation Energy Sign Convention Mnemonic
SLOPE = -Ea / R

Application: In an Arrhenius plot of ln k versus 1/T, remember that a negative slope denotes a positive activation energy.

🎯 Key Exam Points
  • The unit of the pre-exponential factor A matches the units of the specific rate constant k for the corresponding reaction order.
  • The Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution curve broadens and shifts to higher velocities as temperature increases.
  • A negative activation energy implies that the reaction rate decreases with an increase in temperature.
  • According to collision theory, bimolecular gas-phase collision frequency is proportional to the square of concentration.
  • The fraction of effective collisions at room temperature is extremely small due to high activation energy barriers.
  • The activation energy of a reaction is experimentally determined from the slope of the linear Arrhenius plot.
🚫 Common Errors & Confusions
  • Confusing activation energy (Ea) with threshold energy; threshold energy includes the average kinetic energy of reactant molecules prior to collision.
  • Assuming steric factor ρ is always equal to 1 for all bimolecular reactions, ignoring molecular geometry.
  • Forgetting to convert Celsius to Kelvin when substituting temperature values into the Arrhenius equation.
  • Misinterpreting units of activation energy as J instead of J mol-1 when working with the gas constant R.

Core formulas, rapid recall points, and distinguishing criteria at a glance.

Collision Dynamics

Reaction rates depend directly on collision frequency, collision energy, and spatial orientation between reactant particles.

Arrhenius Parameters

The activation energy and pre-exponential factor quantify temperature sensitivity and frequency of successful molecular collisions.

Energy Distribution

Chemical transformation proceeds via an unstable activated complex located at the peak of the potential energy barrier.

📚 Terms & Units Glossary

Collision Frequency (Z)
Total number of collisions per unit volume per unit time. Unit: L-1 s-1.
Activation Energy (Ea)
Minimum excess energy required by reactants to form products. Unit: J mol-1.
Steric Factor (ρ or P)
Ratio of experimental rate constant to calculated collision frequency rate. Dimensionless.
Frequency Factor (A)
Pre-exponential constant accounting for collision frequency and orientation. Matches units of k.
Reaction Coordinate (RC)
Geometrical pathway representing the progress of a reaction from reactants to products.
Activated Complex (X‡)
High-energy transient species at the potential energy maximum.

🎯 Key Takeaways

  • Arrhenius equation: k = A · exp(-Ea / RT)
  • Logarithmic form: log(k2/k1) = (Ea / 2.303R) · [(T2 - T1) / (T1T2)]
  • Temperature coefficient θ ≈ 2 to 3 for a 10°C rise
  • Arrhenius plot slope = -Ea / 2.303R
  • Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution peak shifts right at higher temperatures

📌 Quick Recall

  • Reaction rates scale exponentially with temperature via activation energy.
  • Collision theory integrates frequency, energy, and orientation factors.
  • Catalysis lowers activation energy without altering overall thermodynamics.
  • Transition state theory utilizes thermodynamic parameters for activated complexes.

⚖️ Key Differences

Threshold Energy VS Activation Energy

Threshold energy is total absolute energy required for reaction; activation energy is the excess energy over average reactant energy.

Question 1 of 20

What is the physical significance of the pre-exponential factor A in the Arrhenius equation?

Explanation: The pre-exponential factor A incorporates collision frequency and spatial orientation requirements for bimolecular collisions.

Question 2 of 20

The temperature coefficient for most chemical reactions lies within which range?

Explanation: For most homogeneous reactions, the rate roughly doubles or triples for every 10°C rise, giving a coefficient between 2 and 3.

Question 3 of 20

What is the slope of the straight line obtained by plotting ln k versus 1/T?

Explanation: Taking natural logarithm of k = A · exp(-Ea/RT) gives ln k = ln A - (Ea/R)(1/T), yielding a slope of -Ea/R.

Question 4 of 20

Which factor accounts for the spatial orientation of colliding reactant molecules?

Explanation: The steric factor (ρ) corrects collision theory rates by accounting for directional requirements during bond formation.

Question 5 of 20

How does a catalyst increase the rate of a reaction according to collision theory?

Explanation: Catalysts provide an alternative pathway with a lower activation energy, increasing the fraction of effective collisions.

Question 6 of 20

What is the unit of activation energy in SI units?

Explanation: Activation energy represents energy per mole of reactants, expressed in J mol-1 or kJ mol-1.

Question 7 of 20

According to the Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution, what happens to the fraction of molecules with energy greater than Ea when temperature increases?

Explanation: Higher temperature shifts the distribution curve, exponentially increasing the fraction of molecules exceeding the activation energy.

Question 8 of 20

Which type of reaction can exhibit a negative activation energy?

Explanation: Radical-radical combinations are barrierless processes that proceed faster at lower temperatures, showing negative activation energy.

Question 9 of 20

In the Arrhenius equation, what does exp(-Ea / RT) represent?

Explanation: The Boltzmann factor exp(-Ea/RT) represents the fraction of molecules possessing kinetic energy equal to or exceeding activation energy.

Question 10 of 20

What is the transition state in a chemical reaction coordinate diagram?

Explanation: The transition state corresponds to the potential energy maximum along the reaction coordinate.

Question 11 of 20

If the activation energy of a reaction is zero, how does the rate constant vary with temperature?

Explanation: When Ea = 0, exp(0) = 1, making the rate constant k equal to the pre-exponential factor A and independent of temperature.

Question 12 of 20

Collision theory is most successfully applied to which phase of matter?

Explanation: Gases have well-defined molecular velocities and independent kinetic properties, making hard-sphere collision models highly accurate.

Question 13 of 20

What does a high positive value of activation energy indicate about a reaction?

Explanation: High activation energy makes the rate constant very sensitive to temperature changes, resulting in a large temperature coefficient.

Question 14 of 20

Which equation relates the differential temperature dependence of the rate constant to activation energy?

Explanation: The differential form d(ln k)/dT = Ea/(RT2) is the Van 't Hoff-Arrhenius relation.

Question 15 of 20

What happens to the collision frequency when the concentration of reacting gases is doubled?

Explanation: For a bimolecular collision ZAB ∝ nAnB, doubling both concentrations quadruples the collision frequency.

Question 16 of 20

Why do complex molecules often have steric factors much less than 1?

Explanation: Complex molecules require precise spatial orientation of active sites during impact, drastically reducing the probability of successful collisions.

Question 17 of 20

What is the dimension of the collision frequency constant for a bimolecular gas-phase reaction?

Explanation: Bimolecular rate constants and collision frequency factors operate with second-order units of L mol-1 s-1 (or cm3 molecule-1 s-1).

Question 18 of 20

How does an increase in temperature affect the half-life of a first-order reaction with positive activation energy?

Explanation: Higher temperature increases the rate constant k, which in turn decreases the half-life (t1/2 = ln 2 / k).

Question 19 of 20

Which parameter remains unaffected by the addition of a catalyst?

Explanation: Catalysts alter the pathway and lower Ea, but they do not change the initial and final thermodynamic states (ΔH°).

Question 20 of 20

In collision theory, what limits the rate of extremely fast reactions in solution?

Explanation: Diffusion-controlled reactions in solution are limited by how fast reactant molecules can diffuse through the solvent shell to collide.

Question 1 of 10
Calculate the activation energy of a reaction whose rate constant doubles when temperature increases from 300 K to 310 K. (R = 8.314 J K-1 mol-1)

Solution: Using log(2) = (Ea / 2.303 × 8.314) × (10 / (300 × 310)), Ea calculates to approximately 53.6 kJ mol-1.

Question 2 of 10
Which of the following statements about collision theory is incorrect?

Solution: Only a small fraction of collisions that exceed threshold energy and have proper orientation result in reaction.

Question 3 of 10
If the steric factor ρ is 0.4 and the collision frequency is 1011 L-1 s-1 with Ea = 20 kJ mol-1 at 300 K, what component does ρ modify?

Solution: The steric factor scales total collision frequency to account for geometric constraints.

Question 4 of 10
The differential form of the Arrhenius equation is d(ln k)/dT = ?

Solution: Differentiating ln k = ln A - Ea/RT with respect to T yields d(ln k)/dT = Ea / RT2.

Question 5 of 10
What is the primary role of activation energy in chemical kinetics?

Solution: Activation energy forms the potential energy barrier that reactant molecules must surmount to transform into products.

Question 6 of 10
For a reaction with Ea = 0, what is the value of the temperature coefficient θ?

Solution: If Ea = 0, the rate constant does not change with temperature, making kT+10 / kT equal to 1.

Question 7 of 10
Which plot is used to determine both activation energy and pre-exponential factor?

Solution: The Arrhenius plot of ln k vs 1/T yields slope (-Ea/R) and intercept (ln A).

Question 8 of 10
In Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution curves, the most probable velocity corresponds to:

Solution: The peak of the Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution curve represents the most probable molecular speed.

Question 9 of 10
How does a decrease in activation energy affect the rate constant k at a constant temperature?

Solution: Since k ∝ exp(-Ea/RT), a lower activation energy increases the exponent value, thus increasing k.

Question 10 of 10
What does a negative sign in the Arrhenius activation energy expression signify?

Solution: The negative sign in d(ln k)/dT = Ea/(RT2) coupled with a positive Ea confirms that rate constants increase with temperature.
