Physical Chemistry

Catalysis

Catalysis is the process by which a substance alters the rate of a chemical reaction without itself being consumed in the overall stoichiometry. It functions by providing an alternative reaction pathway with a lower activation energy barrier.

UP PGT Chemistry Subject Code 02 B.Sc. / M.Sc. Level

Homogeneous and Heterogeneous Catalysis

  • Homogeneous catalysis occurs when the catalyst exists in the same phase as the reactants, typically liquid phase solutions.
  • Example of homogeneous catalysis includes the lead chamber process for sulfuric acid synthesis using nitric oxide.
  • Heterogeneous catalysis involves a catalyst in a different phase from the reactants, most commonly a solid catalyst interacting with gases or liquids.
  • Heterogeneous catalytic action proceeds via physical adsorption, chemical adsorption (chemisorption), reaction on surface, and desorption of products.

Characteristics and Thermodynamics of Catalysis

  • A catalyst does not alter the Gibbs free energy change (ΔG°) or the equilibrium constant (Keq) of a reaction.
  • It accelerates both the forward and backward reactions equally, allowing the system to reach equilibrium more rapidly.
  • Catalysts are specific in action; a particular catalyst catalyzes a specific reaction, such as ethanol forming ethene with alumina but ethanal with copper.
  • The presence of a catalyst lowers the activation energy (Ea) exponentially increasing the rate constant according to the Arrhenius equation.

Enzyme Catalysis and Promoters/Poisons

  • Enzymes are complex protein macromolecules that act as highly efficient biological catalysts with maximum turnover numbers.
  • Enzyme catalysis exhibits maximum activity at an optimum temperature (typically 298–310 K) and optimum pH (5–7).
  • Catalytic promoters are substances that increase the activity of a catalyst, such as molybdenum (Mo) acting as a promoter for iron in the Haber process.
  • Catalytic poisons decrease or destroy the catalytic activity by chemisorption onto active sites, such as carbon monoxide poisoning platinum in catalytic converters.
📐 Key Formulas & Equations
Activation Energy Difference
ΔEa = Ea,uncat - Ea,cat
Conditions: Applies to exothermic and endothermic reactions alike.
Units: ΔEa in kJ mol-1
Langmuir Adsorption Isotherm
θ = (KP) / (1 + KP)
Conditions: Monolayer adsorption on uniform catalytic active sites at constant temperature.
Units: θ is fraction of surface covered, P is pressure in atm, K is adsorption equilibrium constant
Rate of Bimolecular Surface Reaction (Hinshelwood Mechanism)
Rate = (k KA PA KB PB) / (1 + KA PA + KB PB)2
Conditions: Both reacting molecules are adsorbed on adjacent catalytic sites.
Units: Rate in mol L-1s-1, P in atm
Enzyme Kinetics (Michaelis-Menten Equation)
v = (Vmax [S]) / (Km + [S])
Conditions: Steady-state approximation applied to enzyme-substrate complexes.
Units: v and Vmax in mol L-1s-1, [S] and Km in mol L-1
Arrhenius Equation with Catalyst
k = A exp(-Ea,cat / RT)
Conditions: Valid for elementary and complex steps where temperature and activation energy are constant.
Units: k in s-1 or M-1s-1, Ea in J mol-1, R in J K-1mol-1, T in K
⚠️ Important Exceptions & Anomalies
Exception Catalysts remain chemically unchanged at the end of a reaction.

Observation: Temporary chemical intermediate formation — The catalyst undergoes transient chemical bonding and state changes during the reaction pathway but is regenerated in the final step.

Exception Increasing temperature always increases the rate of all catalyzed reactions.

Observation: Enzyme-catalyzed reactions at elevated temperatures (> 320 K) — High temperatures denature the protein tertiary structure of enzymes, destroying the active site conformation.

Exception Catalysts increase product yield at equilibrium.

Observation: Thermodynamic equilibrium yield remains unchanged — A catalyst affects forward and backward rates equally, leaving equilibrium concentrations and yield governed strictly by thermodynamics (ΔG).

🎯 Key Exam Points
  • A catalyst alters the reaction path and lowers the activation energy without changing ΔH, ΔS, or ΔG.
  • Promoters enhance catalyst efficiency, while poisons block active sites and deactivate catalysts.
  • In the contact process for H2SO4, Platinized asbestos or V2O5 is used as a heterogeneous catalyst.
  • Zeolites are shape-selective catalysts due to their honey-comb microporous aluminosilicate structures.
  • Ziegler-Natta catalyst [TiCl4 + (C2H5)3Al] is used in the polymerization of ethene to high-density polyethylene.
  • Colloidal solutions act as effective catalysts because of their high surface area-to-volume ratio.
🚫 Common Errors & Confusions
  • Assuming a catalyst can initiate a thermodynamically non-feasible reaction (ΔG > 0).
  • Confusing catalytic promoters with catalysts; promoters do not catalyze the reaction independently.
  • Stating that catalysts change the equilibrium constant Keq of a reversible reaction.
  • Confusing physical adsorption (multilayer, reversible, low ΔHadsorb) with chemisorption (monolayer, specific, high ΔHadsorb).

⚡ Quick Revision: Catalysis

Core formulas, rapid recall points, and distinguishing criteria at a glance.

Mechanism of Catalytic Action

Catalysts provide an alternative reaction path characterized by a lower activation energy, leading to an increased rate constant according to the Arrhenius relation.

Surface Adsorption Theories

Heterogeneous catalysis operates via chemisorption where reactant bonds are weakened on active metal surfaces, facilitating new product bond formation.

Enzyme Kinetics and Specificity

Enzymes follow lock-and-key or induced-fit models, obeying Michaelis-Menten kinetics where reaction velocity saturates at high substrate concentrations.

Industrial Applications

Major industrial processes rely on specific catalysts: Haber process (Fe/Mo), Ostwald process (Pt), and Contact process (V2O5).

📚 Terms & Units Glossary

Catalyst (C)
A substance that changes reaction rate without being consumed.
Promoter (P)
A substance that enhances the activity of a catalyst.
Catalytic Poison (Cp)
A substance that destroys or reduces catalyst activity.
Turnover Number (TON)
Number of reactant molecules converted per active site per unit time.
Active Center (Ac)
Specific unsaturated surface sites where reactant molecules bind.
Zeolite (ZSM-5)
Microporous aluminosilicate functioning as a shape-selective catalyst.

🎯 Key Takeaways

  • Catalysts lower activation energy (Ea).
  • Equilibrium constant (Keq) and ΔG remain unchanged by catalysts.
  • Enzymes operate optimally at 310 K and pH 7.
  • Ziegler-Natta catalyst polymerizes alkenes stereospecifically.
  • Langmuir isotherm assumes monolayer adsorption.

📌 Quick Recall

  • Catalysis is classified into homogeneous and heterogeneous types based on phase uniformity.
  • Surface area and active sites dictate the efficiency of solid catalysts.
  • Autocatalytic reactions exhibit sigmoidal kinetic curves due to product acceleration.
  • Enzyme catalysts show extreme catalytic power and strict substrate specificity.

⚖️ Key Differences

Homogeneous Catalysis VS Heterogeneous Catalysis

Homogeneous occurs in a single phase; heterogeneous involves reactants and catalyst in distinct phases.

Which of the following thermodynamic properties is altered by the presence of a catalyst?

Explanation: A catalyst lowers the activation energy of the reaction pathway but does not change thermodynamic state functions like ΔG, ΔH, or Keq.

Question 2 of 19

In the manufacture of sulfuric acid by the Contact process, the catalyst used is:

Explanation: Vanadium pentoxide (V2O5) is the standard heterogeneous catalyst used for oxidizing SO2 to SO3 in the Contact process.

Question 3 of 19

The function of a catalytic promoter is to:

Explanation: Promoters (such as Mo for Fe in Haber process) enhance the chemical efficiency and adsorptive capacity of the catalyst.

Question 4 of 19

Ziegler-Natta catalyst is composed of:

Explanation: Ziegler-Natta catalysts typically consist of titanium tetrachloride and triethylaluminium used for stereospecific alkene polymerization.

Question 5 of 19

Shape-selective catalysis is a characteristic property of:

Explanation: Zeolites exhibit shape-selective catalysis because their pore sizes restrict reactant or product molecules based on molecular dimensions.

Question 6 of 19

At high pressures, the rate of a gas phase surface-catalyzed reaction typically follows:

Explanation: At high pressures, catalyst surface sites become completely saturated (θ ≈ 1), making the reaction rate independent of pressure (zero order).

Question 7 of 19

The autocatalytic reaction among the following is:

Explanation: In the titration/oxidation of oxalic acid by KMnO4, the Mn2+ ions produced act as an autocatalyst, accelerating the reaction.

Question 8 of 19

Enzymes are chemically classified as:

Explanation: Enzymes are high molecular mass macromolecular globular proteins possessing specific tertiary structures.

Question 9 of 19

Which of the following is an example of homogeneous catalysis?

Explanation: In the lead chamber process, gaseous NO acts as a catalyst in gaseous/liquid sulfur dioxide oxidation, maintaining a single phase.

Question 10 of 19

The efficiency of an enzyme is maximum at:

Explanation: Enzymes exhibit maximum catalytic activity at an optimum temperature, typically around human body temperature (310 K).

Question 11 of 19

Chemisorption differs from physisorption because chemisorption is:

Explanation: Chemisorption is highly specific, involves chemical bond formation, and is typically irreversible with high activation energy.

Question 12 of 19

According to the intermediate compound formation theory, the catalyst:

Explanation: Intermediate compound theory proposes that the catalyst reacts with one reactant to form a temporary intermediate which subsequently reacts with the other reactant.

Question 13 of 19

In Michaelis-Menten kinetics, Km represents:

Explanation: The Michaelis constant Km is equal to the substrate concentration at which the reaction velocity is half of its maximum value (Vmax/2).

Question 14 of 19

Which metal catalyst is commonly used in the hydrogenation of vegetable oils?

Explanation: Finely divided nickel (Ni) is universally used in industrial fat hardening via catalytic hydrogenation.

Question 15 of 19

Poisoning of a catalyst decreases its efficiency because the poison:

Explanation: Catalytic poisons selectively chemisorb onto the active sites of the solid catalyst, rendering them unavailable for reactant molecules.

Question 16 of 19

Negative catalysis refers to a process where the catalyst:

Explanation: A negative catalyst decreases the reaction rate by increasing the activation energy barrier or intercepting reactive intermediates.

Question 17 of 19

The turnover number of a catalyst is defined as:

Explanation: Turnover number (TON) quantifies catalytic efficiency as the number of moles (or molecules) of reactant transformed per active site per unit time.

Question 18 of 19

Which of the following is true for physical adsorption?

Explanation: Physisorption is governed by weak Van der Waals forces, making it non-specific, reversible, and accompanied by low enthalpies of adsorption.

Question 19 of 19

In the decomposition of hydrogen peroxide, phosphoric acid acts as a:

Explanation: Phosphoric acid or glycerol acts as a negative catalyst (inhibitor) that retards the decomposition rate of H2O2.

Question 1 of 10
Which factor is primarily responsible for the high catalytic activity of transition metals?

Solution: Transition metals exhibit variable oxidation states and possess vacant d-orbitals which facilitate chemisorption and intermediate complex formation.

Question 2 of 10
In autocatalytic reactions, the rate of reaction initially:

Solution: Autocatalytic reaction rates start slowly due to low product concentration, then accelerate rapidly as product (catalyst) accumulates.

Question 3 of 10
The Langmuir adsorption isotherm reduces to Freundlich isotherm form at:

Solution: At intermediate pressures, the Langmuir equation simplifies to the Freundlich empirical adsorption isotherm equation (θ = k P1/n).

Question 4 of 10
Which enzyme converts starch into maltose?

Solution: Amylase (ptyalin) catalyzes the hydrolysis of starch into maltose disaccharide units.

Question 5 of 10
Activation energy of an uncatalyzed reaction is 75 kJ/mol. In the presence of a catalyst, it is lowered to 20 kJ/mol. The decrease in activation energy is:

Solution: ΔEa = 75 - 20 = 55 kJ/mol.

Question 6 of 10
Which statement is incorrect regarding catalysts?

Solution: Catalysts cannot make non-spontaneous reactions spontaneous; they only alter the kinetic rate of thermodynamically feasible reactions.

Question 7 of 10
Colloidal catalysts are more efficient than bulk catalysts because:

Solution: Dispersion into colloidal dimensions drastically increases the exposed surface area, exposing more active catalytic sites.

Question 8 of 10
Arsenic acts as a catalytic poison for platinum catalyst in:

Solution: In the contact process, traces of arsenic impurities in SO2 gas poison the platinum catalyst by chemisorbing permanently onto active sites.

Question 9 of 10
The Michaelis constant (Km) signifies:

Solution: Km is inversely proportional to the affinity between the enzyme and its substrate; a lower Km denotes higher binding affinity.

Question 10 of 10
Which of the following represents a heterogeneous catalytic system?

Solution: Hydrogenation of ethene uses solid nickel catalyst with gaseous reactants, constituting a heterogeneous catalytic system.
