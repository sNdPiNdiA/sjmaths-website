Physical Chemistry

Crystal Defects

Crystal defects are departures from the periodic arrangement of atoms or ions in crystalline solids, fundamentally altering their electrical, optical, and mechanical properties. These imperfections are classified into point, line, surface, and volume defects based on their spatial dimensions.

UP PGT Chemistry Subject Code 02 B.Sc. / M.Sc. Level

Stoichiometric Point Defects

  • Schottky defects arise when equal numbers of cations and anions are missing from their lattice sites, maintaining electrical neutrality and decreasing overall crystal density.
  • Frenkel defects occur when an ion (usually the smaller cation) leaves its regular lattice site and occupies an interstitial position, leaving a vacancy behind without altering density.
  • Crystals exhibiting Schottky defects typically have high coordination numbers and similar ionic radii (e.g., NaCl, KCl, CsCl).
  • Crystals exhibiting Frenkel defects feature a large difference in ionic radii and low coordination numbers (e.g., AgCl, AgBr, ZnS).

Non-Stoichiometric Point Defects

  • Metal excess defects due to anion vacancies occur when negative ions are missing from lattice sites, and the trapped electrons in these vacancies form F-centers responsible for color.
  • Metal excess defects due to extra cations involve interstitial cations accompanied by interstitial electrons balancing the charge (e.g., ZnO turns yellow on heating).
  • Metal deficiency defects arise when a cation is missing from its lattice site, compensated by the higher oxidation state of an adjacent metal ion (e.g., Fe0.95O).
  • Non-stoichiometric defects alter the electrical conductivity of solids, often converting insulators or wide-bandgap semiconductors into n-type or p-type semiconductors.

Impurity Defects and Color Centers

  • Impurity defects are introduced by adding foreign atoms, such as doping NaCl with a trace of SrCl2, which generates cation vacancies equal to the excess charge.
  • The concentration of cation vacancies created by aliovalent impurities is directly proportional to the mole fraction of the dopant added.
  • F-centers (from the German Farbenzentrum) absorb visible light due to the electronic transitions of unpaired electrons trapped in anion vacancies.
  • Dislocations, grain boundaries, and stacking faults represent line and surface defects that govern mechanical plasticity and slip systems in metals.
📐 Key Formulas & Equations
Schottky Defect Equilibrium Concentration
n = N exp(-ΔHs / 2RT)
Conditions: Equal number of cation and anion vacancies formed in stoichiometric ionic solids
Units: n = number of Schottky pairs per unit volume, N = total lattice sites, R = gas constant, T = temperature in Kelvin
Density Calculation from Unit Cell Parameters
ρ = (Z × M) / (NA × a3)
Conditions: Cubic crystal system with edge length 'a' and molar mass 'M'
Units: ρ in g cm-3, M in g mol-1, NA in mol-1, a in cm
Arrhenius Relation for Defect Diffusion
D = D0 exp(-Ea / RT)
Conditions: Thermally activated atomic diffusion via vacancy or interstitial mechanisms
Units: D = diffusion coefficient (cm2 s-1), Ea = activation energy (J mol-1)
Frenkel Defect Equilibrium Concentration
n = (N Ni)1/2 exp(-ΔGf / 2RT)
Conditions: Dilute concentration of interstitial and vacancy defects in an ionic crystal
Units: n = number of defects per unit volume, N = normal lattice sites, Ni = interstitial sites
⚠️ Important Exceptions & Anomalies
Exception Ionic solids maintain strict chemical stoichiometry and electrical neutrality.

Observation: Non-stoichiometric compounds like Wüstite (FexO where x ranges from 0.84 to 0.95) deviate significantly from ideal stoichiometry. — Variable oxidation states of transition metal ions (Fe2+ and Fe3+) allow charge balance despite missing metal ions.

Exception Schottky defects are exclusively observed in compounds with high coordination numbers.

Observation: Silver bromide (AgBr) exhibits both Schottky defects and Frenkel defects simultaneously. — Silver ion has an intermediate size and polarizability that permits both interstitial accommodation and vacancy formation.

Exception Introduction of foreign impurities always decreases the density of ionic solids.

Observation: Interstitial solid solutions or interstitial impurities increase the density of the host lattice. — Added atoms occupy preexisting voids without expelling host atoms from the lattice.

🎯 Key Exam Points
  • Schottky defect decreases crystal density because ions leave the crystal lattice entirely.
  • Frenkel defect does not change density as ions merely shift to interstitial sites.
  • AgBr is unique in showing both Schottky and Frenkel defects.
  • F-centers are responsible for imparting color to alkali metal halides upon heating in alkali metal vapor.
  • Doping of AgCl with CdCl2 introduces cation vacancies equal to the concentration of Cd2+ ions.
  • Non-stoichiometric metal deficiency in FeO results in p-type semiconduction due to electron holes.
🚫 Common Errors & Confusions
  • Assuming Frenkel defects decrease density; remember that ions remain inside the crystal framework.
  • Confusing metal excess due to anion vacancies with metal excess due to extra interstitial cations.
  • Forgetting that electrical neutrality must be preserved globally across the crystal lattice in all defect types.
  • Confusing substitutional solid solutions with interstitial solid solutions when calculating density changes.

⚡ Quick Revision: Crystal Defects

Core formulas, rapid recall points, and distinguishing criteria at a glance.

Thermodynamics of Point Defects

Defect formation is an endothermic process driven by an increase in configurational entropy (ΔS > 0), leading to a negative Gibbs free energy change (ΔG < 0) at elevated temperatures.

Stoichiometric Defects

Schottky and Frenkel defects maintain the stoichiometric ratio of cations to anions while creating vacant lattice sites or interstitial species without altering chemical formulas.

Non-Stoichiometric Defects

Metal excess and metal deficiency defects alter the chemical composition ratio, creating electronic defects such as F-centers or transition metal oxidation state variations.

Impurity and Extrinsic Defects

Introduction of aliovalent foreign ions into a host lattice generates controlled point defect concentrations, which dictate semiconductor behavior and ionic conductivity.

📚 Terms & Units Glossary

Point Defect (Zero-dimensional imperfection)
Localized disruption around a single lattice point or atom.
Schottky Defect (Vacant ion pairs)
Equal number of missing cation and anion sites.
Frenkel Defect (Displaced interstitial ion)
Cation occupying an interstitial void with an accompanying vacancy.
F-Center (Farbenzentrum)
Anion vacancy trapping an unpaired electron absorbing visible light.
Aliovalent Impurity (Different valency dopant)
Impurity atom with a different oxidation state than host ions.
Dislocation (Line defect)
Linear crystallographic imperfection governing plastic deformation.

🎯 Key Takeaways

  • Schottky defects decrease density; Frenkel defects keep density constant.
  • AgBr exhibits both Schottky and Frenkel defects.
  • F-centers are created in metal excess alkali halides.
  • Doping NaCl with SrCl2 creates one cation vacancy per Sr2+ ion.
  • Non-stoichiometric FeO is represented as Fe1-xO due to Fe3+ formation.

📌 Quick Recall

  • Crystal defects disrupt long-range periodicity and govern physical properties.
  • Thermodynamic equilibrium determines defect concentrations at specific temperatures.
  • Stoichiometric defects preserve formula ratios; non-stoichiometric defects alter them.
  • Extrinsic doping via impurities tailors electrical and optical characteristics.

⚖️ Key Differences

Schottky Defect VS Frenkel Defect

Schottky involves loss of both cations and anions from the crystal; Frenkel involves displacement of an ion to an interstitial site within the crystal.

Question 1 of 19

Which of the following defects decreases the density of a crystal?

Explanation: Schottky defects involve missing cations and anions from the lattice, reducing the mass per unit volume and thus decreasing density.

Question 2 of 19

Silver bromide (AgBr) is known to exhibit which type of crystal defects?

Explanation: Due to the intermediate size of Ag+ ions, AgBr exhibits both Schottky defects (at higher temperatures) and Frenkel defects.

Question 3 of 19

What is the consequence of introducing SrCl2 impurity into NaCl crystal?

Explanation: To maintain electrical neutrality when one divalent Sr2+ replaces two Na+ ions, one cation vacancy is created.

Question 4 of 19

Which color is observed when NaCl crystals are heated in an atmosphere of sodium vapor?

Explanation: Heating NaCl in Na vapor creates F-centers that absorb in the blue-green region, imparting a yellow or orange color to the crystal.

Question 5 of 19

In stoichiometric crystals, Schottky defects are most likely in compounds with:

Explanation: High coordination numbers and similar ionic radii favor Schottky defect formation as ions can easily leave their regular sites.

Question 6 of 19

Which of the following non-stoichiometric compounds exhibits metal deficiency defect?

Explanation: FeO (Wüstite) typically has metal deficiency where iron is missing and some Fe2+ are oxidized to Fe3+ to maintain neutrality.

Question 7 of 19

What type of defect is represented by a missing atom from its normal lattice site in a non-ionic crystal?

Explanation: A vacancy defect occurs when a lattice site is unoccupied by an atom or ion.

Question 8 of 19

The German term 'Farbenzentrum' refers to:

Explanation: F-center stands for Farbe (color) + Zentrum (center), designating trapped electron color centers.

Question 9 of 19

Which of the following solids will show Frenkel defect due to large difference in cation and anion radii?

Explanation: AgCl has a substantial size difference between Ag+ and Cl-, allowing the small silver ion to occupy interstitial sites.

Question 10 of 19

The electrical conductivity of semiconductors can be increased by introducing defects through a process called:

Explanation: Doping introduces controlled impurity defects that alter charge carrier concentrations and increase conductivity.

Question 11 of 19

What happens to the electrical neutrality of an ionic crystal when a Schottky defect is formed?

Explanation: Equal numbers of missing cations and anions ensure that electrical neutrality is strictly maintained.

Question 12 of 19

Which defect does not alter the stoichiometry of the solid?

Explanation: Schottky and Frenkel defects are stoichiometric point defects because the cation-to-anion ratio remains unchanged.

Question 13 of 19

Planar interfaces separating two grains of different orientations in a polycrystalline material are called:

Explanation: Grain boundaries are surface or interfacial defects separating crystal grains of different crystallographic orientations.

Question 14 of 19

Which metal oxide turns yellow on heating due to metal excess defect and loss of oxygen?

Explanation: Zinc oxide loses oxygen reversibly on heating, leaving Zn2+ ions and electrons in interstitial spaces, turning yellow.

Question 15 of 19

The presence of excess lithium in LiCl crystals imparts which color?

Explanation: Metal excess in LiCl due to lithium vapor treatment produces F-centers that give a pink or magenta color.

Question 16 of 19

Which of the following is classified as a line defect in crystals?

Explanation: Edge and screw dislocations are classic one-dimensional line defects.

Question 17 of 19

What is the effect on density when a Frenkel defect is formed in a crystal?

Explanation: Since ions remain within the crystal boundaries, moving from lattice sites to interstitial voids, density remains constant.

Question 18 of 19

In an ionic crystal of formula AX, if equal numbers of A+ and X- are missing, the defect is:

Explanation: A missing cation-anion pair defines a Schottky defect.

Question 19 of 19

Which equation correctly represents the temperature dependence of defect concentration?

Explanation: Defect concentration follows an Arrhenius relationship with an exponential dependence on activation energy and temperature.

Which crystal defect is characterized by the displacement of an ion from its lattice site to an interstitial site?

Solution: Frenkel defect involves an ion moving into an interstitial space while leaving behind a vacant lattice site.

What is the primary reason for the formation of Schottky defects in ionic solids?

Solution: Comparable ionic sizes and high coordination numbers favor Schottky defect formation.

Question 3 of 10
Which of the following compounds exhibits a metal deficiency defect?

Solution: FeO exhibits non-stoichiometry with metal deficiency, written as Fe1-xO.

Question 4 of 10
F-centers in alkali halides are:

Solution: F-centers are trapping sites where unpaired electrons occupy anion vacancies.

Question 5 of 10
How does the density of a crystal change when a Frenkel defect occurs?

Solution: Since atoms do not leave the crystal structure in a Frenkel defect, density remains constant.

Question 6 of 10
Which alkali halide crystal turns violet when heated in potassium vapor?

Solution: Heating KCl in potassium vapor produces F-centers that absorb green light and appear violet.

Question 7 of 10
In a crystal of NaCl doped with 10-3 mol % of SrCl2, what is the concentration of cation vacancies?

Solution: Each Sr2+ dopant ion generates exactly one cation vacancy, matching the dopant mole percentage.

Question 8 of 10
Which defect is classified as a zero-dimensional imperfection in solid state chemistry?

Solution: Point defects are localized at specific atomic sites and considered zero-dimensional.

Question 9 of 10
Which of the following substances contains both Frenkel and Schottky defects?

Solution: Silver bromide is the classic example exhibiting both major stoichiometric defect types.

Question 10 of 10
What is the sign of the entropy change (ΔS) during the formation of point defects in a crystal lattice?
