Physical Chemistry

Slow Reactions

Slow reactions are chemical processes characterized by extremely low rates, often requiring significant time to reach detectable product concentrations. These reactions typically possess high activation energies (Ea) or involve complex molecular rearrangements that limit the frequency of successful collisions.

UP PGT Chemistry Subject Code 02 B.Sc. / M.Sc. Level

Kinetic Characteristics of Slow Reactions

  • Slow reactions exhibit high activation energy barriers, preventing most collisions from resulting in product formation.
  • The rate constant (k) for these reactions is typically very small, often approaching zero at room temperature.
  • Experimental monitoring requires long time intervals or sensitive analytical techniques to measure concentration changes.
  • These reactions are often temperature sensitive, where small increases in T lead to exponential increases in rate.

Thermodynamic vs Kinetic Control

  • A reaction may be thermodynamically spontaneous (ΔG < 0) but kinetically slow due to a large energy barrier.
  • The transition state energy level is significantly higher than the reactant energy level.
  • Catalysts function by providing an alternative mechanism with a lower Ea, effectively accelerating the reaction.
  • Slow reactions often involve covalent bond breaking or complex structural rearrangements.

Examples and Observations

  • The rusting of iron (4Fe + 3O2 → 2Fe2O3) is a classic example of a slow reaction.
  • Radioactive decay follows first-order kinetics and is inherently slow depending on the isotope half-life.
  • Hydrolysis of esters at room temperature without acid/base catalysis is remarkably slow.
  • Diamond to graphite conversion is a geologically slow process at standard pressure.
📐 Key Formulas & Equations
Rate Law
Rate = k[A]x[B]y
Conditions: Empirical relation for reaction kinetics.
Units: Rate (mol·L-1·s-1), [Concentration] (mol/L)
Activation Energy Relation
ln(k2/k1) = (Ea/R)(1/T1 - 1/T2)
Conditions: Used to determine Ea from two temperature points.
Units: Ea (J/mol), T (K)
Half-life of First Order
t1/2 = 0.693 / k
Conditions: First-order kinetics.
Units: t1/2 (s), k (s-1)
Arrhenius Equation
k = Ae-Ea/RT
Conditions: Applicable for elementary reactions with temperature dependence.
Units: k (s-1 or M-1s-1), Ea (J/mol), R (8.314 J/K·mol), T (K)
⚠️ Important Exceptions & Anomalies
Exception High Ea implies slow reaction

Observation: Enzyme-catalyzed reactions — Enzymes stabilize the transition state, drastically lowering the effective activation energy.

Exception Slow reactions are temperature dependent

Observation: Athermal reactions — Some radical recombination reactions have near-zero activation energy and are diffusion-controlled.

Exception Bond breaking slows reactions

Observation: Ionic reactions in solution — Ionic reactions are typically instantaneous due to electrostatic attraction, bypassing high bond-breaking barriers.

🎯 Key Exam Points
  • Slow reactions are characterized by high activation energy (Ea).
  • The rate of slow reactions is significantly increased by catalysts.
  • Rusting of iron is a slow, spontaneous redox process.
  • Radioactive decay is a first-order kinetic process.
  • Temperature increase exponentially increases the rate constant k.
  • Slow reactions often involve complex bond-breaking steps.
🚫 Common Errors & Confusions
  • Confusing thermodynamic spontaneity with kinetic speed; a reaction can be spontaneous but slow.
  • Assuming all slow reactions are first-order; order must be determined experimentally.
  • Ignoring the effect of catalysts in lowering the activation energy barrier.
  • Misinterpreting the Arrhenius plot slope as Ea without multiplying by R.

⚡ Quick Revision: Slow Reactions

Core formulas, rapid recall points, and distinguishing criteria at a glance.

Activation Energy Barrier

The energy threshold that reactants must overcome to form products. High barriers result in slow reaction rates.

Arrhenius Dependence

The exponential relationship between temperature and the rate constant k, defining the sensitivity of slow reactions to heat.

Catalytic Acceleration

Catalysts lower the activation energy, providing a pathway that bypasses the high-energy transition state of slow reactions.

Kinetic vs Thermodynamic Stability

Thermodynamics dictates the feasibility (ΔG), while kinetics dictates the time required to reach equilibrium.

📚 Terms & Units Glossary

Rate Constant (k)
Proportionality constant in the rate law.
Activation Energy (Ea)
Minimum energy for reaction.
Transition State (TS)
High-energy intermediate configuration.
Catalyst (Cat)
Substance increasing rate without being consumed.
First-order (n=1)
Rate proportional to one reactant concentration.
Half-life (t1/2)
Time for half reactant to be consumed.

🎯 Key Takeaways

  • k = Ae-Ea/RT
  • ln(k) = ln(A) - Ea/RT
  • Rate = -d[R]/dt
  • Ea is always positive.
  • Catalysts do not change ΔG.

📌 Quick Recall

  • Slow reactions have high Ea.
  • Catalysts lower Ea.
  • Temperature increases k.
  • Kinetics ≠ Thermodynamics.

⚖️ Key Differences

Rate VS Rate Constant

Rate depends on concentration; Rate constant is independent of concentration.
