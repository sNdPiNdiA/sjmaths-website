Physical Chemistry

Physical and Chemical Changes

Physical changes involve alterations in the state or physical properties of a substance without modifying its underlying chemical identity or molecular composition. Chemical changes result in the breaking and forming of chemical bonds, leading to the creation of entirely new substances with distinct chemical formulas and properties.

UP PGT Chemistry Subject Code 02 B.Sc. / M.Sc. Level

Thermodynamics of Physical Phase Transitions

  • Physical changes involve changes in intermolecular forces without altering intramolecular covalent or ionic bonds.
  • During a phase transition at constant pressure, the temperature remains constant, and the heat added is equal to the latent heat of transition.
  • The thermodynamic criterion for phase coexistence is ΔG = 0, meaning the chemical potentials of both phases are equal (μA = μB).
  • Physical changes are typically reversible by altering physical parameters such as temperature and pressure.

Energetics and Kinetics of Chemical Changes

  • Chemical changes involve bond reorganization, characterized by a significant change in standard enthalpy (ΔH° ≠ 0).
  • Chemical reactions must overcome an activation energy barrier (Ea), which is generally much higher than the energy barriers of physical changes.
  • Indicators of a chemical change include gas evolution, precipitate formation, permanent color change, and temperature change.
  • Chemical changes are typically irreversible under ordinary physical conditions due to high thermodynamic stability of the products.

Analytical and Spectroscopic Differentiation

  • X-ray Diffraction (XRD) is used to distinguish physical polymorphic transitions (e.g., monoclinic to rhombic sulfur) from chemical transformations.
  • Infrared (IR) Spectroscopy confirms chemical changes by detecting the disappearance of reactant functional groups and the appearance of new vibrational bands.
  • Mass Conservation holds strictly for both changes, but chemical changes alter the specific molar ratios of the chemical species present.
📐 Key Formulas & Equations
Enthalpy of Phase Transition
q = n · ΔHtrans
Conditions: Constant pressure during physical phase changes (e.g., fusion, vaporization).
Units: Heat (q) in Joules (J), Amount of substance (n) in moles (mol), ΔH in kJ/mol
Gibbs Free Energy of Phase Equilibrium
ΔG = ΔH - TΔS = 0
Conditions: At the exact transition temperature (Ttrs) under constant pressure.
Units: ΔG, ΔH in kJ/mol; T in Kelvin (K); ΔS in J/(mol·K)
Van 't Hoff Equation for Chemical Equilibrium
ln(K2 / K1) = -(ΔH° / R) · (1/T2 - 1/T1)
Conditions: Applies to chemical changes at equilibrium over a narrow temperature range.
Units: Equilibrium constant (K) is dimensionless; R = 8.314 J/(mol·K); T in Kelvin (K)
Extent of Reaction
ξ = (ni - ni,0) / νi
Conditions: Applies to chemical changes to quantify the progress of a reaction.
Units: Extent of reaction (ξ) in moles (mol); stoichiometric coefficient (νi) is dimensionless
Law of Conservation of Mass
Σ mreactants = Σ mproducts
Conditions: Applies to all closed chemical and physical systems where no nuclear reactions occur.
Units: Mass (m) in grams (g) or kilograms (kg)
⚠️ Important Exceptions & Anomalies
Exception Dissolution of solutes in water is generally classified as a physical change.

Observation: Dissolution of anhydrous CuSO4 or sodium metal in water. — Anhydrous CuSO4 undergoes a chemical coordination reaction to form [Cu(H2O)6]2+, and sodium metal reacts violently to form NaOH and H2 gas.

Exception Heating a substance causes physical phase changes like melting or sublimation.

Observation: Heating ammonium chloride (NH4Cl). — NH4Cl undergoes thermal dissociation into NH3(g) and HCl(g), which is a chemical change, rather than simple physical sublimation.

Exception Allotropic transitions are physical changes because the elemental identity remains the same.

Observation: Conversion of white phosphorus to red phosphorus. — This transition involves the breaking of covalent P-P bonds in P4 tetrahedra to form a polymeric network, which constitutes a chemical change.

💡 Shortcuts & Memory Aids
The Reversibility Test
If State A ↔ State B occurs solely by ΔT or ΔP, it is Physical.

Application: Use this to quickly identify phase changes. If a chemical reagent or catalyst is required to restore the original state, classify it as a chemical change.

Energy Magnitude Rule of Thumb
ΔH < 50 kJ/mol → Physical; ΔH > 100 kJ/mol → Chemical

Application: Helps solve numerical-conceptual questions where reaction enthalpies are given. Weak van der Waals interactions correspond to physical changes, while covalent/ionic bond changes correspond to chemical changes.

The Empirical Formula Check
Empirical Formulainitial = Empirical Formulafinal → Physical

Application: If the empirical formula of the primary substance does not change (e.g., H2O in ice, liquid, or steam), the process is physical.

🎯 Key Exam Points
  • The sublimation of iodine (I2(s) → I2(g)) is a physical change involving weak dispersion forces.
  • Rusting of iron is an electrochemical chemical change represented by the formation of hydrated iron(III) oxide: Fe2O3 · xH2O.
  • The burning of a candle involves both physical change (melting of wax) and chemical change (combustion of hydrocarbons).
  • The curdling of milk is an irreversible chemical change caused by the lactic acid-induced denaturation of casein proteins.
  • The slaking of lime (CaO + H2O → Ca(OH)2) is a highly exothermic chemical change with ΔH = -63.7 kJ/mol.
  • Crystallization of sodium chloride from its aqueous solution is a physical change, whereas the electrolysis of brine is a chemical change.
🚫 Common Errors & Confusions
  • Confusing the dissolution of sugar (physical) with the dissolution of sodium metal in water (chemical).
  • Assuming all color changes indicate chemical reactions; heating zinc oxide causes a reversible physical color change from white to yellow due to non-stoichiometric crystal defects.
  • Misclassifying the transition of liquid Helium-I to Helium-II at 2.17 K; it is a physical quantum phase transition, not a chemical change.
  • Confusing physical mixing with chemical synthesis; mixing iron filings and sulfur powder at room temperature is physical, but heating the mixture forms FeS, which is chemical.

⚡ Quick Revision: Physical and Chemical Changes

Core formulas, rapid recall points, and distinguishing criteria at a glance.

Thermodynamic Criteria of Changes

Physical changes involve changes in intermolecular states where ΔGtrans = 0 at equilibrium. Chemical changes involve intramolecular bond reorganization driven by a negative Gibbs free energy change (ΔG < 0).

Conservation Laws in Systems

Both physical and chemical changes strictly obey the Law of Conservation of Mass, but only chemical changes alter the molecular identity and stoichiometry of the system.

Phase Rule and Physical Equilibria

Physical equilibria are governed by Gibbs' Phase Rule (F = C - P + 2), where the number of components (C) remains constant throughout the physical transitions.

Activation Energy Barriers

Chemical changes require overcoming significant activation energy barriers (Ea), whereas physical changes like phase transitions depend primarily on thermal energy overcoming weak intermolecular forces.

📚 Terms & Units Glossary

Physical Change (Physical Transition)
A change in the form or state of matter without any change in its chemical composition.
Chemical Change (Chemical Reaction)
A process in which one or more substances are converted into one or more new substances with different properties.
Latent Heat (L)
The heat required to convert a solid into a liquid or vapor, or a liquid into a vapor, without change of temperature.
Activation Energy (Ea)
The minimum amount of energy required to initiate a chemical reaction.
Allotropy (Allotropism)
The property of some chemical elements to exist in two or more different forms, which can undergo physical or chemical transitions.
Sublimation (Phase Transition)
The transition of a substance directly from the solid to the gas state without passing through the liquid state.

🎯 Key Takeaways

  • ΔHfusion is always less than ΔHvaporization for any given substance.
  • Rusting of iron requires both oxygen (O2) and moisture (H2O).
  • Heating ZnO causes a reversible physical color change (white to yellow).
  • Photosynthesis is an endothermic chemical change: 6CO2 + 6H2O → C6H12O6 + 6O2.
  • Explosion of gunpowder is a rapid chemical change producing gaseous products.

📌 Quick Recall

  • Physical changes alter state, shape, or size without changing the molecular formula.
  • Chemical changes produce new substances with distinct chemical identities.
  • Energy changes are significantly larger in chemical reactions compared to physical transitions.
  • Spectroscopic analysis is the definitive method to distinguish between physical and chemical transformations.

⚖️ Key Differences

Dissolution of anhydrous CuSO4 VS Dissolution of CuSO4·5H2O

Dissolving anhydrous CuSO4 is a chemical change (hydration/coordination), whereas dissolving the pentahydrate is a physical dissolution process.

Which of the following is a physical change?

Explanation: Melting of ice is a physical change because it only changes the state of water from solid to liquid without altering its chemical formula (H2O).

Question 2 of 18

Heating of zinc oxide from white to yellow is a:

Explanation: Heating ZnO causes a reversible loss of oxygen from the lattice, creating non-stoichiometric defects that change the color to yellow. On cooling, it absorbs oxygen and turns white again, making it a physical change.

Question 3 of 18

Which of the following processes represents a chemical change?

Explanation: Electrolysis of water decomposes water into hydrogen and oxygen gases, which is a chemical change involving the breaking and forming of covalent bonds.

Question 4 of 18

When anhydrous copper sulfate is dissolved in water, the color changes to blue. This is a:

Explanation: Anhydrous copper sulfate reacts chemically with water to form the coordination complex [Cu(H2O)6]2+, which gives the blue color.

Question 5 of 18

The curdling of milk is an example of:

Explanation: Curdling of milk is an irreversible chemical change where lactic acid denatures and precipitates the casein proteins, forming a new substance.

Question 6 of 18

Which thermodynamic condition holds true for a physical phase transition at its transition temperature?

Explanation: At the transition temperature and constant pressure, the two phases are in equilibrium, meaning the change in Gibbs free energy (ΔG) is zero.

Question 7 of 18

The burning of a candle is:

Explanation: The melting of wax is a physical change, while the combustion of the wax hydrocarbons to produce CO2 and H2O is a chemical change.

Question 8 of 18

Which of the following is a chemical change?

Explanation: Rusting of iron involves the oxidation of iron in the presence of oxygen and moisture to form hydrated iron(III) oxide, which is a chemical change.

Question 9 of 18

The process of slaking of lime is:

Explanation: Slaking of lime (CaO + H2O → Ca(OH)2) is a highly exothermic chemical reaction that releases a large amount of heat.

Question 10 of 18

Heating of ammonium chloride is a:

Explanation: Heating NH4Cl causes it to dissociate chemically into NH3 and HCl gases, which recombine upon cooling. This is a chemical dissociation process.

Which of the following is NOT a chemical change?

Explanation: The chemical reaction Ca(OH)2 + CO2 → CaCO3(s) + H2O produces insoluble calcium carbonate, which turns the solution milky.

The conversion of monoclinic sulfur to rhombic sulfur is a:

Explanation: The transition between allotropes of sulfur (monoclinic to rhombic) is a physical phase transition involving a rearrangement of the crystal lattice without changing the S8 molecular identity.

Explanation: Chemical changes involve the breaking of strong intramolecular bonds, which requires overcoming a significant activation energy barrier.

Question 15 of 18

Question 17 of 18

Explanation: Rusting is an electrochemical process that strictly requires both oxygen (oxidizing agent) and water (electrolyte medium).

Question 18 of 18

Explanation: Sodium reacts violently with water to produce sodium hydroxide and hydrogen gas, releasing a large amount of heat (highly exothermic chemical change).
