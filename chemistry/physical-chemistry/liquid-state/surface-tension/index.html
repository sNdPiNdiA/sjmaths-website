Physical Chemistry

Surface Tension

Surface tension is a thermodynamic property of liquids arising from unbalanced intermolecular forces acting on molecules at the surface interface. It acts parallel to the surface of the liquid and tends to minimize the total surface area.

UP PGT Chemistry Subject Code 02 B.Sc. / M.Sc. Level

Molecular Origin and Thermodynamic Basis

  • Molecules in the bulk liquid experience symmetrical intermolecular forces, resulting in a net zero force vector.
  • Molecules at the surface layer experience unbalanced inward attractive forces due to a lack of neighboring molecules above them.
  • The excess free energy per unit surface area is numerically equal to the surface tension (γ).
  • Systems spontaneously minimize surface area to achieve minimum potential energy and maximum thermodynamic stability.

Capillarity and Contact Angle Relations

  • The contact angle (θ) is the angle formed by the liquid-solid-gas interface measured inside the liquid phase.
  • Liquids with θ < 90° (e.g., water on clean glass) exhibit wetting behavior, meniscus depression, and capillary rise.
  • Liquids with θ > 90° (e.g., mercury on glass) exhibit non-wetting behavior, convex meniscus, and capillary depression.
  • The upward force supporting a capillary column equals the vertical component of the surface tension perimeter force: 2πrγ cosθ.

Effects of Temperature and Solutes

  • Surface tension decreases linearly with an increase in temperature due to increased thermal motion and reduced intermolecular attraction.
  • At the critical temperature ($T_c$), the meniscus between liquid and vapor disappears, and surface tension drops identically to zero.
  • Addition of surface-active agents (surfactants) like soaps or detergents dramatically lowers the surface tension of water.
  • Inorganic electrolytes typically increase the surface tension of water slightly due to hydration effects drawing ions away from the interface.

Work Done in Increasing Surface Area
W = γ × ΔA
Conditions: Isothermal expansion of surface area by ΔA (use 2γΔA for soap films)
Units: W in Joules (J), ΔA in m-2
Capillary Rise Formula
h = 2γ \cosθr ρ g
Conditions: Cylindrical capillary tube of radius r dipped in a liquid of density ρ
Units: h in meters (m), θ = contact angle, g = acceleration due to gravity
Temperature Dependence (Eötvös Rule)
γ (Mv)2/3 = k (Tc - T)
Conditions: Non-associated liquids at temperature T below critical temperature Tc
Units: M = molar mass, v = specific volume, k = Eötvös constant

γ = Fl
Conditions: Force F acting perpendicularly per unit length l along the surface
Units: γ in N m-1 or J m-2
⚠️ Important Exceptions & Anomalies
Exception Surface tension generally decreases with increasing temperature and vanishes only at critical temperature.

Observation: Liquid helium near the lambda point — Quantum mechanical superfluidity effects alter standard thermal expansion and cohesive energy density models.

Exception Addition of solutes (impurities) to a liquid lowers its surface tension.

Observation: Inorganic electrolytes (e.g., NaCl, KCl) — Strong ion-dipole hydration causes ions to be negatively adsorbed at the surface, marginally increasing surface tension.

Exception Liquids rise in capillary tubes when the contact angle is acute.

Observation: Paraffin-coated or greasy glass tubes with water — Hydrophobic coating forces the contact angle to exceed 90°, causing capillary depression instead of rise.

🎯 Key Exam Points
  • The SI unit of surface tension is N m-1 or kg s-2.
  • Spherical shape of rain drops is due to minimum surface area requirement dictated by surface tension.
  • Water rises in a capillary tube because adhesive forces between water and glass exceed cohesive forces of water.
  • Surface tension becomes zero at the critical temperature.
  • Addition of soap lowers the surface tension of water, allowing it to penetrate fabric pores easily.
  • Surface tension is a scalar quantity, though force acts along a specified line segment.
🚫 Common Errors & Confusions
  • Confusing the excess pressure formula for a liquid droplet (2γ/r) with a soap bubble (4γ/r).
  • Assuming inorganic salts decrease surface tension like organic alcohols or surfactants do.
  • Forgetting to multiply by 2 when calculating surface energy changes for films with two free surfaces.
  • Treating the contact angle as measured outside the liquid instead of inside the liquid phase.

⚡ Quick Revision: Surface Tension

Core formulas, rapid recall points, and distinguishing criteria at a glance.

Intermolecular Forces at Interfaces

Surface tension originates from the net inward pull experienced by surface molecules, creating an unbalanced force field.

Thermodynamic Work and Energy

Surface energy is defined as the work required to create unit surface area isothermally: W = γΔA.

Capillarity and Meniscus Curvature

Height of capillary ascent or descent depends directly on surface tension, contact angle, and tube radius.

Temperature Dependence

Surface tension decreases linearly with temperature and approaches zero at the critical temperature.

📚 Terms & Units Glossary

Surface Tension (γ = F/l)
Force per unit length acting perpendicular to an imaginary line on the surface. SI unit: N m-1.
Surface Energy (E = γ × A)
Work done in creating unit area of surface. SI unit: J m-2.
Contact Angle (θ)
Angle between liquid tangent and solid surface inside the liquid droplet.
Capillary Action (h = 2γcosθ/rρg)
Spontaneous rise or depression of liquids in narrow tubes.
Surfactant (Amphiphilic agents)
Substances that drastically reduce the surface tension of water.
Excess Pressure (ΔP)
Pressure difference between inside and outside of a curved liquid surface.

🎯 Key Takeaways

  • Drop Excess Pressure: ΔP = 2γ/r
  • Bubble Excess Pressure: ΔP = 4γ/r
  • Capillary Rise: h = 2γcosθ/(rρg)
  • Effect of Temperature: γ drops as T increases
  • Work of expansion: W = γΔA

📌 Quick Recall

  • Surface tension minimizes surface area.
  • Wetting requires contact angle < 90°.
  • Electrolytes slightly increase surface tension.
  • Organic surfactants decrease surface tension.

⚖️ Key Differences

Cohesive Forces VS Adhesive Forces

Cohesion is between like molecules; adhesion is between unlike molecules (liquid and solid).

Question 1 of 19

What is the SI unit of surface tension?

Explanation: Surface tension is defined as force per unit length, hence its SI unit is N m-1 or kg s-2.

Question 2 of 19

The excess pressure inside a spherical soap bubble of radius r is given by:

Explanation: A soap bubble has two surfaces (inner and outer), hence the excess pressure is 4γ/r.

Question 3 of 19

Surface tension of a liquid generally:

Explanation: Thermal agitation increases with temperature, weakening intermolecular cohesive forces and lowering surface tension.

Question 4 of 19

What happens to the surface tension of water when an inorganic electrolyte like NaCl is added?

Explanation: Strong hydration of ions draws them away from the surface layer, resulting in a slight increase in water surface tension.

Question 5 of 19

The contact angle for a liquid that completely wets the solid surface is:

Explanation: Complete wetting corresponds to a contact angle of 0° where the liquid spreads completely.

Question 6 of 19

If a capillary tube is dipped in water and water rises to height h, what happens to h if the radius of the tube is halved?

Explanation: Since h is inversely proportional to radius r (h = 2γcosθ/rρg), halving r doubles the height h.

Question 7 of 19

The work done in blowing a soap bubble of radius r with surface tension γ is:

Explanation: A soap bubble has two surfaces, so total area change is 2 × (4πr2) = 8πr2. Work W = 8πr2γ.

Question 8 of 19

What is the shape of a liquid drop free from external forces?

Explanation: For a given volume, a sphere has the minimum surface area, making it the most stable shape under surface tension.

Question 9 of 19

Which of the following substances lowers the surface tension of water most effectively?

Explanation: Soaps are surfactants containing hydrophobic tails and hydrophilic heads that concentrate at the surface, drastically lowering surface tension.

Question 10 of 19

At the critical temperature, the surface tension of a liquid:

Explanation: At the critical temperature, the distinction between liquid and vapor phases disappears, dropping surface tension to zero.

Question 11 of 19

The dimension of surface tension is:

Explanation: Surface tension = Force / Length = [MLT-2] / [L] = [MT-2].

Question 12 of 19

Mercury forms a convex meniscus in a glass tube because its contact angle is:

Explanation: Mercury does not wet glass; its cohesive forces exceed adhesive forces, resulting in an obtuse contact angle (~138°) and capillary depression.

Question 13 of 19

If n small water droplets of radius r merge to form a single large drop of radius R, energy is:

Explanation: Surface area decreases when drops merge, releasing excess surface energy as heat.

Question 14 of 19

Capillary depression is observed when:

Explanation: An obtuse contact angle (θ > 90°) results in a downward capillary force causing depression (e.g., mercury in glass).

Question 15 of 19

Surface energy is numerically equal to:

Explanation: Surface energy per unit area equals surface tension (γ = W / ΔA).

Question 16 of 19

Which instrument is commonly used to measure surface tension in laboratory settings?

Explanation: A stalagmometer determines surface tension by counting drop numbers from a calibrated bulb.

Question 17 of 19

Why do hot soups taste better than cold soups?

Explanation: Higher temperature lowers surface tension, enabling the soup to spread rapidly across a larger area of the tongue.

Question 18 of 19

The excess pressure inside a spherical liquid droplet of radius r is:

Explanation: A liquid drop has only one free surface, giving an excess pressure of 2γ/r.

Question 19 of 19

Ramasesh observes that mosquito larvae float on water. This is due to:

Explanation: The surface tension film of water supports small insects without breaking, which is why adding kerosene breaks the film and sinks larvae.

Question 1 of 10
Calculate the excess pressure inside a water droplet of radius 2 mm at 20°C. (Surface tension of water = 0.0727 N m-1)

Solution: ΔP = 2γ/r = (2 × 0.0727) / (2 × 10-3) = 0.0727 / 10-3 = 72.7 Pa.

Question 2 of 10
Which of the following statements is incorrect regarding surface tension?

Solution: Detergents are surfactants that decrease surface tension, not increase it.

Question 3 of 10
If two soap bubbles of radii r1 and r2 coalesce isothermally in vacuum to form a bubble of radius r, then:

Solution: Surface area of soap bubble is proportional to r2. Since surface area is conserved isothermally in vacuum: 8πr2 = 8πr12 + 8πr22 ⇒ r2 = r12 + r22.

Question 4 of 10
A capillary tube of radius r is dipped in water. The mass of water raised in the tube is m. If the radius is doubled, the mass of water raised will be:

Solution: Mass m = πr2 h ρ. Since h ∝ 1/r, m ∝ r2(1/r) ∝ r. Doubling r doubles the mass m.

Question 5 of 10
The contact angle for pure water on a clean glass plate is approximately:

Solution: Pure water on clean glass has a very small acute contact angle, typically around 8°.

Question 6 of 10
What is the excess pressure inside a spherical soap bubble of diameter 4 cm if its surface tension is 0.04 N m-1?

Solution: Radius r = 2 cm = 0.02 m. ΔP = 4γ/r = (4 × 0.04) / 0.02 = 0.16 / 0.02 = 8 Pa. Wait, 0.16 / 0.02 = 8 Pa. Correct option is C.

Question 7 of 10
The phenomenon of capillary action is a direct consequence of:

Solution: Capillary action depends on surface tension, contact angle (wettability), and gravity.

Question 8 of 10
Which factor does NOT affect the height of liquid in a capillary tube?

Solution: Atmospheric pressure acts equally on both the free liquid surface and inside the capillary, cancelling out in capillary rise equations.

Question 9 of 10
Drop number comparison using a stalagmometer for liquid A and liquid B depends on:

Solution: Stalagmometer relation: γ1/γ2 = (n2ρ1)/(n1ρ2).

Question 10 of 10
Why do small mercury droplets appear spherical while large puddles of mercury flatten out?

Solution: Gravity causes large liquid masses to flatten under their own weight, whereas surface tension dominates for small volumes to maintain a spherical form.
