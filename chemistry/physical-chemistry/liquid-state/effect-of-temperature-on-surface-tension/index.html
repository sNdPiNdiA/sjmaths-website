Effect of temperature on surface tension

Surface tension is defined as the force acting per unit length perpendicular to an imaginary line drawn on the liquid surface. As temperature increases, the kinetic energy of molecules increases, which decreases the intermolecular forces and consequently reduces the surface tension.

UP PGT Chemistry Subject Code 02 B.Sc. / M.Sc. Level

Thermodynamic Dependence and Molecular Origin

  • Surface tension decreases linearly with an increase in temperature under normal conditions.
  • At the critical temperature (Tc), the meniscus between liquid and vapor disappears, and surface tension drops to zero.
  • Increased thermal agitation disrupts the inward net attractive forces acting on surface molecules, lowering the free energy.

Temperature Coefficient of Surface Tension

  • The rate of change of surface tension with respect to temperature is represented by the temperature coefficient dγ/dT.
  • For almost all pure liquids, the value of dγ/dT is negative.
  • The molar surface energy decreases as temperature rises, reaching zero at the critical point.

Effect of Solutes and Impurities

  • Capillary-active solutes like soaps and detergents drastically decrease the surface tension of water.
  • Capillary-inactive inorganic electrolytes like NaCl slightly increase the surface tension of water with temperature.
  • The temperature dependence of aqueous solutions depends heavily on the hydration shell structure around solute ions.
📐 Key Formulas & Equations
Ramsay-Shields Equation
γ(Mv)2/3 = k(Tc - T - 6)
Conditions: Modified Eötvös equation accounting for association of molecules in polar liquids
Units: γ in N/m, M in kg/mol, v in m3/kg, T in Kelvin
Katayama Equation
γ = k(ρL - ρV)4
Conditions: Relates surface tension to density difference between liquid and vapor phases
Units: γ in N/m, ρL and ρV in kg/m3
van der Waals Surface Tension Relation
γ = γ0 (1 - T/Tc)n
Conditions: Empirical relation where n is typically approximately 11/9
Units: γ0 is surface tension at 0 K, Tc is critical temperature
Eötvös Rule
γ(Mv)2/3 = k(Tc - T)
Conditions: Valid for non-associated unassociated liquids at temperatures well below critical temperature
Units: γ in N/m, M in kg/mol, v in m3/kg, T in Kelvin, k is Eötvös constant
⚠️ Important Exceptions & Anomalies
Exception Surface tension strictly decreases continuously with increasing temperature for all phases.

Observation: Liquid Helium-4 near the Lambda point (λ-transition) — Quantum superfluidity effects alter density fluctuations and surface excitations near 2.17 K.

Exception Addition of any solute decreases the surface tension of a solvent.

Observation: Inorganic electrolytes in water (e.g., NaCl, NaOH) — Strong ion-dipole interactions pull ions away from the interface into the bulk, increasing surface tension slightly.

T = Tc → γ = 0

Sign of Temperature Coefficient
dγ/dT < 0

Application: Remember that temperature and surface tension move in opposite directions; heating destroys surface tension.

Application: Useful for estimating molecular weights or critical temperatures of normal unassociated liquids.

🎯 Key Exam Points
  • Surface tension becomes zero at the critical temperature.
  • The temperature coefficient dγ/dT for most liquids is negative.
  • Eötvös rule relates surface density and temperature with an empirical constant k.
  • Addition of soap lowers the surface tension of water significantly.
  • Surface tension is a scalar quantity with SI units of N/m or J/m2.
  • Liquid metals like mercury exhibit high surface tension due to strong metallic bonding.
🚫 Common Errors & Confusions
  • Assuming surface tension increases with temperature because kinetic energy increases.
  • Forgetting that surface tension units can be expressed as J/m2.
  • Confusing the temperature coefficient of viscosity with that of surface tension.
  • Assuming organic solutes increase surface tension like inorganic salts do.

⚡ Quick Revision: Effect of temperature on surface tension

Core formulas, rapid recall points, and distinguishing criteria at a glance.

Definition of Surface Tension

Surface tension represents the work required to create a unit area of surface, stemming from unbalanced intermolecular forces acting on surface molecules.

Thermal Dependence

Thermal energy directly opposes cohesive forces, causing surface tension to diminish smoothly toward zero at the critical temperature.

Associated Liquids Correction

Associated liquids like water and alcohols deviate from the simple Eötvös rule, necessitating Ramsay-Shields modifications.

Influence of Surfactants

Surfactants accumulate at the liquid-air interface, lowering surface free energy and altering temperature gradients across the surface.

📚 Terms & Units Glossary

Surface Tension (γ or σ)
Force per unit length or energy per unit area. SI unit: N/m.
Critical Temperature (Tc)
Temperature above which liquid and gas phases are indistinguishable.
Eötvös Constant (k)
Universal constant for unassociated liquids in molar surface energy relation.
Temperature Coefficient (dγ/dT)
Rate of change of surface tension with temperature.
Cohesive Forces (Fc)
Intermolecular attractive forces between like molecules.
Capillarity (h)
Rise or depression of liquid in a narrow tube governed by surface tension.

🎯 Key Takeaways

  • γ → 0 as T → Tc
  • dγ/dT is negative for pure liquids
  • Eötvös equation: γ(Mv)2/3 = k(Tc - T)
  • Surfactants decrease surface tension
  • Inorganic electrolytes slightly increase surface tension of water

📌 Quick Recall

  • Surface tension originates from net inward pull on surface molecules.
  • Temperature increases molecular kinetic energy, reducing net inward pull.
  • Surface tension vanishes at critical temperature.
  • Deviations occur in associated liquids corrected by Ramsay-Shields.

⚖️ Key Differences

Surface Tension VS Viscosity

Surface tension is force per unit length at an interface, whereas viscosity is resistance to fluid flow.

What happens to the surface tension of a liquid as its temperature is increased?

Explanation: Higher temperature increases thermal motion, weakening intermolecular cohesive forces and reducing surface tension.

Question 2 of 19

At what temperature does the surface tension of a liquid become zero?

Explanation: At the critical temperature, the liquid and vapor phases merge, and the boundary surface disappears, making surface tension zero.

Question 3 of 19

The temperature coefficient of surface tension (dγ/dT) for most pure liquids is:

Explanation: Since surface tension decreases with rising temperature, the derivative dγ/dT is negative.

Question 4 of 19

Which of the following equations is the Eötvös rule for surface tension?

Explanation: The Eötvös rule states that molar surface energy γ(Mv)2/3 is proportional to (Tc - T).

Question 5 of 19

Why does Ramsay-Shields equation modify the Eötvös equation?

Explanation: Associated liquids contain hydrogen bonding clusters, requiring a subtraction term (-6) in the temperature factor.

Question 6 of 19

Explanation: Inorganic electrolytes are capillary-inactive and slightly increase the surface tension of water.

Question 7 of 19

What is the SI unit of surface tension?

Explanation: Surface tension is force per unit length, giving N/m (or J/m2).

Question 8 of 19

Soap solution is used to wash clothes effectively because it:

Explanation: Soaps are surfactants that lower water's surface tension, allowing better wetting and penetration into fabrics.

Question 9 of 19

In the Eötvös equation, what does 'v' represent?

Explanation: In γ(Mv)2/3, M is molar mass and v is specific volume, making Mv the molar volume.

Question 10 of 19

Which liquid exhibits unusually high surface tension due to strong intermolecular forces?

Explanation: Water has extensive hydrogen bonding, giving it an exceptionally high surface tension among common liquids.

Question 11 of 19

If temperature is doubled in Kelvin scale, surface tension:

Explanation: Surface tension decreases approximately linearly with temperature, not inversely or proportionally to T.

Question 12 of 19

What is the physical significance of the Eötvös constant 'k'?

Question 14 of 19

Why do hot soups taste better than cold soups?

Explanation: Lower surface tension at higher temperatures helps the liquid spread more efficiently across the tongue.

Question 15 of 19

Which factor opposes surface tension in liquids?

Explanation: Thermal energy increases molecular motion and randomizes positions, acting against the cohesive forces responsible for surface tension.

Question 16 of 19

What is the nature of the curve when surface tension is plotted against temperature (T < Tc)?

Explanation: For most temperature ranges below critical temperature, surface tension decreases approximately linearly with temperature.

Question 17 of 19

Which type of substance exhibits positive temperature coefficient of surface tension?

Explanation: Virtually all liquids show a negative temperature coefficient of surface tension.

Question 18 of 19

Explanation: Katayama modified Eötvös equation using (ρL - ρV) to hold valid near the critical temperature.

Question 19 of 19

The work done in increasing the surface area of a liquid by ΔA at constant temperature is:

Explanation: Surface energy work is defined as product of surface tension and change in surface area (W = γ ΔA).
