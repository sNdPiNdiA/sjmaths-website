Physical Chemistry

Viscosity

Viscosity is the measure of a fluid's internal resistance to flow arising from intermolecular forces and momentum transfer. It decreases with increasing temperature in liquids but increases in gases.

UP PGT Chemistry Subject Code 02 B.Sc. / M.Sc. Level

Origin and Nature of Viscosity in Liquids and Gases

  • In liquids, viscosity arises primarily from strong intermolecular forces (such as hydrogen bonding and dipole-dipole interactions) and molecular entanglement.
  • In gases, viscosity originates from the transfer of momentum between adjacent molecular layers due to random thermal molecular motion.
  • The SI unit of dynamic viscosity is pascal-second (Pa s), and the CGS unit is poise (P), where 1 P = 0.1 Pa s.
  • Fluidity (φ) is defined as the reciprocal of dynamic viscosity and measures how easily a liquid flows.

Experimental Measurement Using Ostwald Viscometer

  • An Ostwald viscometer determines the relative viscosity of a liquid by comparing its efflux time with that of a reference liquid (typically water).
  • The working relation is η1 / η2 = (t1 ρ1) / (t2 ρ2), where t is time and ρ is density.
  • Precise temperature control is mandatory because viscosity values change significantly with even minor fluctuations in temperature.
  • The viscometer must be kept perfectly vertical during timing to ensure constant hydrostatic head pressure.

Factors Affecting Viscosity

  • Temperature causes liquid viscosity to decrease exponentially due to thermal energy overcoming intermolecular attractive forces.
  • Conversely, gas viscosity increases with temperature because higher molecular velocities accelerate momentum transfer between layers.
  • Molecular weight and structure increase liquid viscosity; long-chain or branched hydrocarbons exhibit higher resistance to flow due to mechanical interlocking.
  • Presence of solutes such as electrolytes generally increases the viscosity of water due to ion-dipole hydration sheaths.
📐 Key Formulas & Equations
Poiseuille's Equation for Liquid Flow
V = πP r4 t / (8η l)
Conditions: Streamline flow through a capillary tube
Units: V in m3, P in Pa, r in m, t in s, η in Pa s, l in m
Relative Viscosity
ηrel = η / η0 = (t ρ) / (t0 ρ0)
Conditions: Measured using an Ostwald viscometer
Units: Dimensionless ratio
Temperature Dependence (Arrhenius Equation)
η = A exp(Ea / RT)
Conditions: Liquid phase viscosity over moderate temperature ranges
Units: η in Pa s, Ea in J mol-1, R in J K-1 mol-1, T in K
Fluidity
φ = 1 / η
Conditions: Inverse measure of viscosity
Units: φ in Pa-1 s-1 or poise-1
Newton's Law of Viscosity
τ = η (dv/dx)
Conditions: Laminar flow of Newtonian fluids
Units: τ in N m-2, η in Pa s or poise, dv/dx in s-1
⚠️ Important Exceptions & Anomalies
Exception Viscosity of liquids decreases uniformly with an increase in temperature.

Observation: Water between 0°C and 4°C exhibits anomalous behavior. — Structural rearrangement and breakdown of bulky ice-like cluster arrangements cause a slight deviation in the initial thermal trend.

Exception Viscosity increases with molecular mass in homologous series.

Observation: Highly branched or spherical molecules of equivalent molar mass. — Spherical molecules have smaller surface areas and lesser intermolecular entanglement compared to extended linear chains.

Exception Gases increase in viscosity with temperature according to kinetic theory.

Observation: Very high temperatures or extreme pressures where dense gas behavior dominates. — Intermolecular potentials begin to play a significant role, deviating from pure kinetic momentum transfer models.

💡 Shortcuts & Memory Aids
Ostwald Ratio Shortcut
ηunknown = ηwater × (tunk ρunk) / (tw ρw)

Application: Directly substitute known water viscosity, time ratios, and density ratios to compute unknown viscosity without unit conversions.

Temperature Trend Mnemonic
Liquid Down, Gas Up

Application: Remember 'LD-GU': Liquid viscosity goes Down with temperature, Gas viscosity goes Up with temperature.

Unit Conversion Trick
1 Poise = 0.1 Pa·s = 1 g cm-1 s-1

Application: Instantly convert CGS poise values to SI Pascal-seconds by multiplying by 0.1.

🎯 Key Exam Points
  • The SI unit of dynamic viscosity is Pa s or kg m-1 s-1.
  • Viscosity of liquids decreases with temperature following the Arrhenius relation.
  • Gas viscosity increases with temperature due to increased molecular collision frequency and momentum transfer.
  • Ostwald viscometer is used to measure relative viscosity of pure liquids and dilute solutions.
  • Fluidity is defined as the reciprocal of dynamic viscosity (φ = 1/η).
  • Hydrogen-bonded liquids like glycerol and water show remarkably high viscosity values relative to non-polar hydrocarbons of similar mass.
🚫 Common Errors & Confusions
  • Confusing kinematic viscosity with dynamic viscosity; kinematic viscosity is dynamic viscosity divided by density (ν = η/ρ).
  • Assuming gas viscosity decreases with temperature like liquids; gases actually become more viscous when heated.
  • Forgetting to account for liquid density when using an Ostwald viscometer for relative viscosity calculations.
  • Treating fluid flow as turbulent when applying Poiseuille's equation, which strictly requires laminar or streamline flow.

⚡ Quick Revision: Viscosity

Core formulas, rapid recall points, and distinguishing criteria at a glance.

Molecular Origin of Viscosity

Viscosity is internal friction opposing fluid layer sliding, governed by intermolecular forces in liquids and momentum transfer in gases.

Quantitative Measurement

Ostwald viscometers utilize efflux time measurements of calibrated liquid volumes under gravity to calculate relative viscosity coefficients.

Thermal Dependence

Liquid viscosity decreases with temperature due to thermal energy overcoming cohesive forces, whereas gas viscosity increases with temperature.

Hydrodynamic Laws

Poiseuille's equation relates volume flow rate through capillary tubes directly to pressure gradient and fourth power of radius.

📚 Terms & Units Glossary

Dynamic Viscosity (η)
Tangential stress divided by velocity gradient. SI unit: Pa s.
Kinematic Viscosity (ν = η / ρ)
Ratio of dynamic viscosity to density. SI unit: m2 s-1.
Fluidity (φ = 1 / η)
Reciprocal of dynamic viscosity.
Poise (P)
CGS unit of dynamic viscosity, equal to 0.1 Pa s.
Efflux Time (t)
Time taken for a liquid level to fall between two marks in a viscometer.
Activation Energy of Flow (Ea)
Energy barrier required for molecules to move past one another in a liquid.

🎯 Key Takeaways

  • Newton's Law: τ = η (dv/dx)
  • Ostwald Viscometer formula: η1/η2 = (t1ρ1)/(t2ρ2)
  • Liquid viscosity decreases with temperature; gas viscosity increases with temperature.
  • 1 Poise = 1 g cm-1 s-1 = 0.1 Pa s
  • Fluidity is the inverse of viscosity.

📌 Quick Recall

  • Viscosity measures resistance to flow.
  • Liquid viscosity stems from intermolecular cohesion.
  • Gas viscosity stems from molecular momentum exchange.
  • Poiseuille equation applies to streamline capillary flow.

⚖️ Key Differences

Dynamic Viscosity (η) VS Kinematic Viscosity (ν)

Dynamic viscosity measures internal resistance force, whereas kinematic viscosity measures the diffusive momentum rate (η/ρ).

Question 1 of 10
Calculate the dynamic viscosity of a liquid if its kinematic viscosity is 2.5 × 10-6 m2 s-1 and its density is 800 kg m-3.

Solution: η = ν × ρ = 2.5 × 10-6 × 800 = 2.0 × 10-3 Pa s.

Question 2 of 10
If the time taken for water and an organic liquid of density 0.8 g cm-3 to flow through an Ostwald viscometer are 100 s and 80 s respectively, and water viscosity is 1.0 cP, what is the relative viscosity of the liquid?

Solution: ηrel = (tunk ρunk) / (tw ρw) = (80 × 0.8) / (100 × 1.0) = 64 / 100 = 0.64.

Question 3 of 10
How many times does the rate of flow through a capillary increase if the radius is tripled, keeping all other parameters constant?

Solution: Flow rate is proportional to r4. Thus, 34 = 81 times increase.

Question 4 of 10
Which liquid exhibits anomalous viscosity behavior near 4°C?

Solution: Water exhibits structural density and viscosity anomalies near 4°C due to hydrogen-bonded cluster dynamics.

Question 5 of 10
What is the dimension of fluidity (φ)?

Solution: Fluidity is 1/η. Since η has dimensions [M L-1 T-1], fluidity has dimensions [M-1 L T1].

Question 6 of 10
Which statement is incorrect regarding viscosity?

Solution: Liquid viscosity decreases with temperature, not increases.

Question 7 of 10
The unit Stokes is equivalent to:

Solution: Stokes is the CGS unit for kinematic viscosity, defined as cm2 s-1.

Question 8 of 10
In the relation τ = η (dv/dx), what does dv/dx represent?

Solution: dv/dx represents the velocity gradient (rate of change of velocity with distance perpendicular to flow).

Question 9 of 10
What is the effect of adding a non-electrolyte solute that increases intermolecular interactions in a solvent?

Solution: Stronger solute-solvent interactions impede smooth laminar flow, thereby increasing solution viscosity.

Question 10 of 10
Which of the following compounds has the lowest viscosity due to absence of strong hydrogen bonding?

Solution: Dimethyl ether cannot form intermolecular hydrogen bonds like water, alcohols, or polyols, resulting in much lower viscosity.
