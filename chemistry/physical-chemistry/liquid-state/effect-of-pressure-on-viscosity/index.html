Effect of pressure on viscosity

Viscosity of most liquids increases exponentially with applied external pressure due to decreased intermolecular free volume. Conversely, the viscosity of water at temperatures below 32°C decreases with increasing pressure owing to structural breakdown of its open ice-like clusters.

UP PGT Chemistry Subject Code 02 B.Sc. / M.Sc. Level

Thermodynamic Basis of Pressure Dependent Viscosity

  • Fluid flow requires creation of a local void or hole into which an adjacent molecule can jump.
  • Applying external pressure reduces free volume, making molecular translocation increasingly difficult and raising viscosity.
  • The activation volume (ΔV‡) represents the volume change associated with forming the activated transition state for flow.

Behavior of Normal Liquids Versus Water

  • In non-associated organic liquids, viscosity strictly increases with pressure because molecular packing tightens.
  • Liquid water exhibits an anomalous viscosity decrease under initial pressure up to ~2000 atm at low temperatures.
  • Pressure disrupts the open tetrahedral hydrogen-bonded structure of water, shifting it to a denser, more mobile packing arrangement.

Gases and Extreme Pressure Regimes

  • The viscosity of gases at low to moderate pressures is virtually independent of pressure according to kinetic theory.
  • At ultra-high pressures, gas molecules are squeezed close together, causing gas viscosity to increase significantly like a dense fluid.
  • Temperature changes alter viscosity inversely in liquids (decreases with T) but directly in gases (increases with T).
📐 Key Formulas & Equations
Activation Volume Formulation
(&partial; ln η / &partial; P)T = ΔV‡ / RT
Conditions: Transition-state theory of liquid viscosity
Units: ΔV‡ in m3 mol-1, R in J mol-1 K-1, T in K
Kinematic Viscosity Definition
ν = η / ρ
Conditions: Applicable for all fluid dynamics calculations under variable density ρ
Units: ν in m2 s-1, η in Pa·s, ρ in kg m-3
Temperature-Pressure Combined Viscosity Relation
η = A exp((Ea + PV‡) / RT)
Conditions: When both thermal energy and pressure work terms affect flow activation energy
Units: Ea in J mol-1, V‡ in m3 mol-1
Barus Equation for Viscosity-Pressure Dependence
ηP = η0 exp(α P)
Conditions: Moderate pressure ranges for standard non-associated liquids
Units: η in Pa·s, P in Pa, α in Pa-1
⚠️ Important Exceptions & Anomalies
Exception Viscosity of all liquids increases with an increase in hydrostatic pressure.

Observation: Liquid water below 32°C and liquid bismuth, gallium, and antimony. — Application of pressure collapses their open, ice-like or open-lattice solid structures into denser liquid configurations, facilitating molecular sliding.

Exception Viscosity of gases is independent of pressure.

Observation: Gases at very high pressures (> 10 atm). — Mean free path becomes comparable to molecular diameters, and intermolecular collision frequency scales with density.

🎯 Key Exam Points
  • The viscosity of typical liquids increases exponentially with applied hydrostatic pressure.
  • Water shows an anomalous decrease in viscosity with pressure up to 2000 atm at temperatures below 32°C.
  • Barus equation relates high-pressure viscosity ηP to zero-pressure viscosity η0 via pressure coefficient α.
  • Gas viscosity is independent of pressure at low pressures due to the cancellation of density and mean free path terms.
  • The flow of liquids under pressure is treated as a reaction rate process involving an activation volume.
  • An increase in temperature decreases liquid viscosity, while an increase in pressure increases it.
🚫 Common Errors & Confusions
  • Assuming water follows the normal positive pressure-viscosity trend across all temperature ranges.
  • Confusing gas viscosity pressure-independence with liquid viscosity pressure-dependence.
  • Forgetting that kinematic viscosity involves fluid density which also changes slightly under extreme pressures.
  • Treating the Barus pressure coefficient α as a dimensionless constant instead of having units of Pa-1.

Core formulas, rapid recall points, and distinguishing criteria at a glance.

Free Volume Theory

Viscosity is governed by vacant space between molecules. Pressure reduces free volume, impeding flow.

Anomalous Water Behavior

Pressure breaks ice-like open clusters in cold water, temporarily lowering viscosity.

Barus Equation

Exponential growth model relating dynamic viscosity to applied pressure via coefficient α.

Kinetic Theory of Gases

Explains why gas viscosity is independent of pressure until high density regimes are reached.

📚 Terms & Units Glossary

Dynamic Viscosity (η)
Tangential frictional force per unit area divided by velocity gradient. SI unit: Pa·s.
Kinematic Viscosity (ν = η/ρ)
Ratio of dynamic viscosity to density. SI unit: m2 s-1.
Barus Coefficient (α)
Pressure coefficient of viscosity. SI unit: Pa-1.
Activation Volume (ΔV‡)
Volume change to form flow transition state. SI unit: m3 mol-1.
Free Volume (Vf)
Unoccupied volume in a liquid lattice available for molecular motion.
Fluidity (φ = 1/η)
Reciprocal of viscosity. SI unit: Pa-1 s-1.

🎯 Key Takeaways

  • Normal liquid viscosity increases with pressure: (&partial;η/&partial;P) > 0
  • Water below 32°C shows anomalous viscosity decrease with initial pressure
  • Barus formula: ηP = η0 exp(αP)
  • Gas viscosity is independent of pressure at low pressures
  • Activation volume governs pressure dependence via transition-state theory

📌 Quick Recall

  • Pressure reduces free volume in liquids, increasing resistance to flow.
  • Water's hydrogen-bonded open structure causes viscosity anomalies under pressure.
  • Gas viscosity depends on molecular collisions and temperature, not pressure at normal levels.
  • Barus and activation volume models quantify high-pressure fluid behavior.

⚖️ Key Differences

Liquid Viscosity-Pressure Trend VS Gas Viscosity-Pressure Trend

Liquids show strong exponential increase with pressure; gases show zero dependence at low pressures.
