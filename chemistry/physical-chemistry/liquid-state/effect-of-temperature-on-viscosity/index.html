Effect of temperature on viscosity

Viscosity of liquids decreases exponentially with an increase in temperature due to the weakening of intermolecular attractive forces. Conversely, the viscosity of gases increases with temperature due to an increase in molecular collision frequency.

UP PGT Chemistry Subject Code 02 B.Sc. / M.Sc. Level

Temperature Dependence in Condensed Phases

  • The viscosity of liquids decreases systematically as temperature increases because thermal energy overcomes intermolecular forces.
  • Molecules acquire sufficient kinetic energy to jump into adjacent vacant holes or voids, reducing resistance to flow.
  • The relationship is non-linear and is well-approximated by the Andrade exponential relation.
  • A plot of ln η versus 1/T yields a straight line whose slope directly determines the activation energy of viscous flow.

Thermodynamic and Kinetic Parameters of Flow

  • Viscous flow in liquids is treated as a rate process analogous to chemical reactions requiring an activation barrier.
  • The free energy of activation (ΔG‡) comprises both enthalpy (ΔH‡) and entropy (ΔS‡) components.
  • Associated liquids like water and glycerol possess higher activation energies due to extensive and directional hydrogen bonding networks.
  • Temperature elevation disrupts these directional networks rapidly, causing a sharper drop in viscosity compared to non-associated liquids.

Gaseous Viscosity Contrast

  • Unlike liquids, the viscosity of gases increases with a rise in temperature.
  • According to kinetic theory, gas viscosity is proportional to the mean free path and molecular velocity, both of which increase with temperature.
  • Gas viscosity is practically independent of pressure at moderate ranges, a counterintuitive result resolved by kinetic theory.
  • Momentum transfer via molecular translation dominates gaseous friction rather than intermolecular cohesion.
📐 Key Formulas & Equations
Logarithmic Andrade Equation
ln η = ln A + BT
Conditions: Linear form of Andrade equation for graphical determination of activation energy
Units: T in Kelvin, η in Pa·s
Frenkel-Eyring Equation
η = h N_AV_m eΔ G\ddaggerRT
Conditions: Based on the theory of absolute reaction rates applied to liquid flow
Units: h is Planck constant, N_A is Avogadro number, V_m is molar volume
Viscosity Activation Energy
E_a = R d(\ln η)d(1/T)
Conditions: Derived from Arrhenius-type temperature dependence of liquid flow
Units: E_a in J mol-1, R in J K-1 mol-1
Chapman-Enskog Equation for Gases
η = 5 √π m k_B T16 π σ^2 \Omega(2,2)*
Conditions: Dilute gases at low pressures based on kinetic theory
Units: m is mass, k_B is Boltzmann constant, T in Kelvin, σ is collision diameter
Andrade Equation
η = A eB / T
Conditions: Applicable for liquids over moderate temperature ranges
Units: η in Pa·s or cP, T in Kelvin, A and B are constants
⚠️ Important Exceptions & Anomalies
Exception Liquid viscosity generally decreases with increasing temperature

Observation: Liquid helium below the lambda point (superfluid He-II) — Exhibits zero viscosity due to quantum mechanical Bose-Einstein condensation phenomena.

Exception Liquid viscosity decreases continuously with heating

Observation: Certain molten polymers and sulfur above 160°C — Polymer chain entanglement or thermal polymerization increases molecular friction temporarily before thermal expansion dominates.

Exception Gas viscosity increases with temperature

Observation: Extremely dense gases under high pressures — Intermolecular forces begin to dominate behavior, shifting the gas toward liquid-like temperature dependence.

💡 Shortcuts & Memory Aids
Andrade Slope Rule
Slope = B = E_a / R

Application: Use the slope of the ln η vs 1/T plot directly to compute the viscous activation energy without converting units prematurely.

Liquid vs Gas Temperature Trend Mnemonic
Liquid: Heat → Thin (lower η). Gas: Heat → Thick (higher η).

Application: Instantly recall opposite trends for condensed vs gaseous phases during rapid conceptual MCQs.

Hydrogen Bonding Indicator
High B value in Andrade equation → Strong H-bonding

Application: Identify liquids with extensive associative networks by comparing the steepness of their temperature-viscosity curves.

🎯 Key Exam Points
  • The viscosity of most liquids drops by about 2% for every 1°C rise in temperature.
  • Andrade equation expresses liquid viscosity as η = A eB/T.
  • Gas viscosity increases with temperature because molecular thermal velocity increases.
  • Water has an anomalously high activation energy for viscous flow due to hydrogen bonding.
  • The unit of coefficient of viscosity in SI is Pa·s or kg m-1 s-1.
  • CGS unit of viscosity is Poise (P) or centipoise (cP), where 1 cP = 10-3 Pa·s.
🚫 Common Errors & Confusions
  • Confusing the temperature trend of liquids with gases; remember gases become more viscous when heated.
  • Assuming viscosity is strictly linear with temperature in liquids; the dependence is exponential (Andrade).
  • Forgetting to convert Celsius to Kelvin when substituting into the Andrade exponential term.
  • Neglecting the effect of molecular association and hydrogen bonding on the constant B.

⚡ Quick Revision: Effect of temperature on viscosity

Core formulas, rapid recall points, and distinguishing criteria at a glance.

Nature of Liquid Viscosity

Liquid viscosity arises from intermolecular drag forces that oppose relative sliding motion between fluid layers.

Thermal Energy and Activation

Thermal energy supplies the activation energy needed for molecules to slip past potential energy barriers created by neighbors.

Andrade Exponential Law

The Andrade equation quantitatively connects fluid viscosity to absolute temperature via an exponential activation factor.

Kinetic Basis for Gases

Gaseous viscosity increases with temperature due to enhanced momentum transfer driven by faster molecular motion.

📚 Terms & Units Glossary

Viscosity (η)
Measure of a fluid's internal resistance to flow. SI unit: Pa·s.
Fluidity (φ = 1/η)
Reciprocal of viscosity, representing ease of flow.
Andrade Constant (B)
Constant proportional to the activation energy of viscous flow.
Activation Energy (E_a)
Energy barrier required for molecular displacement in liquids.
Poise (P)
CGS unit of dynamic viscosity equal to 0.1 Pa·s.
Kinematic Viscosity (ν = η / ρ)
Ratio of dynamic viscosity to fluid density. Unit: m2/s.

🎯 Key Takeaways

  • Liquids: Temperature up → Viscosity down.
  • Gases: Temperature up → Viscosity up.
  • Andrade Equation: η = A eB/T.
  • Log form: ln η = ln A + B/T.
  • Activation energy E_a = R × slope.

📌 Quick Recall

  • Viscosity measures internal fluid friction.
  • Liquid flow is thermally activated.
  • Gaseous momentum transfer increases with temperature.
  • Hydrogen bonding greatly enhances liquid viscosity and temperature sensitivity.

⚖️ Key Differences

Dynamic Viscosity (η) VS Kinematic Viscosity (ν)

Dynamic viscosity measures internal resistance force, whereas kinematic viscosity accounts for the fluid's density (ν = η/ρ).

How does the viscosity of a typical liquid vary with an increase in temperature?

Explanation: Thermal energy overcomes intermolecular attractions, causing liquid viscosity to decrease exponentially according to the Andrade equation.

Question 2 of 19

What is the primary reason for the increase in viscosity of gases with temperature?

Explanation: According to kinetic theory, gas viscosity depends on molecular velocity and momentum transfer, which increase with temperature.

Question 3 of 19

The Andrade equation for liquid viscosity is given by:

Explanation: The standard Andrade relation is η = A eB/T, reflecting the exponential dependence on temperature.

Question 4 of 19

In the linear plot of ln η versus 1/T for a liquid, what does the slope represent?

Explanation: From ln η = ln A + B(1/T), the slope of the line equals the constant B, which relates to the activation energy.

Which of the following liquids is expected to have the highest activation energy for viscous flow?

Explanation: Water forms an extensive hydrogen-bonded network, resulting in a higher energy barrier for molecular displacement.

Question 6 of 19

What is the SI unit of coefficient of viscosity (η)?

Explanation: Pascal-second (Pa s) or kg m-1 s-1 is the standard SI unit for dynamic viscosity.

Question 7 of 19

How does pressure affect the viscosity of a dilute gas at constant temperature?

Explanation: Kinetic theory predicts that the viscosity of a dilute gas is independent of pressure over moderate ranges.

Question 8 of 19

Kinematic viscosity is defined as:

Explanation: Kinematic viscosity (ν) is the ratio of dynamic viscosity (η) to density (ρ).

Question 9 of 19

Fluidity (φ) is mathematically expressed as:

Explanation: Fluidity is the reciprocal of viscosity (φ = 1/η).

Question 10 of 19

When a gas is heated, its viscosity increases proportional to approximately:

Explanation: Kinetic theory shows gas viscosity is proportional to the square root of absolute temperature (T1/2).

Question 11 of 19

The constant B in the Andrade equation is related to activation energy (E_a) by:

Explanation: B corresponds to E_a / R, where R is the universal gas constant.

Question 12 of 19

Which factor primarily opposes flow in liquids?

Explanation: Intermolecular forces create potential wells that molecules must escape to flow.

Question 13 of 19

What happens to the fluidity of a liquid when temperature is increased?

Explanation: Since viscosity decreases with temperature, its reciprocal (fluidity) increases.

Question 14 of 19

Liquids with strong intermolecular hydrogen bonds generally exhibit:

Explanation: Strong directional bonds increase both absolute viscosity and its sensitivity to temperature changes.

Question 15 of 19

The CGS unit of viscosity, Poise, is equivalent to:

Explanation: 1 Poise = 1 dyne s cm-2 = 0.1 Pa s.

Question 16 of 19

In terms of collision theory, gas viscosity depends on:

Explanation: Chapman-Enskog equation relates gas viscosity to molecular mass, collision diameter, and mean free path.

Question 17 of 19

Why do lubricants become less effective at very high operating temperatures?

Explanation: Excessive temperature lowers viscosity to a point where the fluid film fails to prevent metal-to-metal contact.

Question 18 of 19

The temperature coefficient of viscosity for liquids is:

Explanation: As temperature increases, liquid viscosity decreases, giving a negative temperature coefficient.

Question 19 of 19

Superfluid Helium-II exhibits which unusual viscosity property below 2.17 K?

Explanation: He-II transitions to a superfluid state with zero viscosity, flowing without friction.
