Physical Chemistry

Ideal Solutions

Ideal solutions are liquid mixtures in which the intermolecular forces between unlike molecules are equal to the forces between like molecules. These systems obey Raoult's Law across all concentrations and temperatures.

UP PGT Chemistry Subject Code 02 B.Sc. / M.Sc. Level

Thermodynamic Criteria for Ideality

  • An ideal solution exhibits zero enthalpy of mixing (ΔHmix = 0) because solute-solute, solvent-solvent, and solute-solvent interactions are identical.
  • The volume of mixing is identically zero (ΔVmix = 0), ensuring the total volume equals the sum of the individual component volumes.
  • The entropy of mixing (ΔSmix) is always positive due to increased randomness upon the dispersion of molecules.
  • The free energy of mixing (ΔGmix) is negative, confirming that the formation of an ideal solution is a spontaneous process.

Raoult's Law and Vapor-Liquid Equilibrium

  • Raoult's Law states that the partial vapor pressure of any volatile component in a solution is directly proportional to its mole fraction.
  • The proportionality constant is the vapor pressure of the pure component (p°) at the same temperature.
  • A linear relationship is observed when plotting partial vapor pressure against the mole fraction of a component.
  • Both volatile solute and volatile solvent follow Raoult's Law over the entire range of composition from x = 0 to x = 1.

Molecular Characteristics and Examples

  • Ideal solutions are formed by mixing components that are structurally and chemically similar, possessing comparable polarities and molecular sizes.
  • Classic examples include n-hexane and n-heptane, benzene and toluene, and bromoethane and chloroethane.
  • The magnitude of A-B interactions equals the average of A-A and B-B intermolecular forces.
  • Strictly speaking, infinite dilution approaches ideal behavior for real solutions, but true ideal solutions across all concentrations are rare.

⚠️ Important Exceptions & Anomalies
Exception Solutions formed from similar liquids should always be ideal.

Observation: Benzene and toluene form nearly ideal solutions, but mixtures of polar molecules often show large deviations. — Differences in dipole-dipole interactions disrupt the uniformity of molecular environments.

Exception Total volume of a mixture equals the sum of individual volumes.

Observation: Real solutions exhibit volume expansion or contraction upon mixing (ΔVmix ≠ 0). — Unequal intermolecular attractive forces lead to closer packing or expanded free space between unlike molecules.

Exception Enthalpy change upon mixing is zero for solutions of isomers.

Observation: Even structural isomers like o-xylene and p-xylene may deviate slightly from ideality. — Differences in molecular geometry and crystal packing energies affect intermolecular potentials.

🎯 Key Exam Points
  • For an ideal solution, the enthalpy of mixing (ΔHmix) is equal to zero.
  • The volume change upon mixing (ΔVmix) for an ideal solution is zero.
  • Benzene and toluene form a binary mixture that closely approximates ideal solution behavior.
  • Raoult's law is mathematically expressed as pA = xA p°A.
  • The entropy of mixing for ideal solutions is always positive (ΔSmix > 0).
  • In an ideal solution, solute-solute and solvent-solvent interactions are virtually identical to solute-solvent interactions.
🚫 Common Errors & Confusions
  • Assuming all liquid mixtures of chemically similar compounds are strictly ideal; slight deviations often exist.
  • Confusing Raoult's law with Henry's law; remember Raoult's law applies to the solvent when x approaches 1, and to both components in ideal solutions.
  • Neglecting temperature dependence of vapor pressure when applying Raoult's law calculations.
  • Assuming ΔGmix = 0 for ideal solutions; Gibbs free energy of mixing is always negative for spontaneous mixing.

⚡ Quick Revision: Ideal Solutions

Core formulas, rapid recall points, and distinguishing criteria at a glance.

Definition and Thermodynamic Conditions

Ideal solutions satisfy Raoult's law across the entire concentration range with ΔHmix = 0 and ΔVmix = 0.

Raoult's Law Formulation

The partial vapor pressure of each component is proportional to its mole fraction in the liquid phase multiplied by its pure vapor pressure.

Molecular Level Interactions

Uniform intermolecular forces between identical and non-identical molecules govern the thermodynamic properties of ideal systems.

Vapor Phase Composition

Dalton's law of partial pressures combines with Raoult's law to determine the exact mole fraction of components in the vapor equilibrium.

📚 Terms & Units Glossary

Ideal Solution (pA = xAp°A)
A solution that obeys Raoult's law at all concentrations.
Raoult's Law (pi = xip°i)
Relates partial vapor pressure to mole fraction.
Enthalpy of Mixing (ΔHmix)
Heat absorbed or evolved when components mix, zero for ideal solutions.
Volume of Mixing (ΔVmix)
Change in total volume upon mixing, zero for ideal solutions.
Mole Fraction (xi = ni / ∑n)
Ratio of moles of a component to total moles.
Vapor Pressure (Ptotal)
Pressure exerted by vapor in equilibrium with liquid.

🎯 Key Takeaways

  • ΔHmix = 0 for ideal solutions
  • ΔVmix = 0 for ideal solutions
  • Raoult's law equation: pA = xA p°A
  • ΔSmix > 0 for all ideal solutions
  • Intermolecular forces: A-A = B-B = A-B

📌 Quick Recall

  • Ideal solutions follow Raoult's law unconditionally.
  • Enthalpy and volume changes upon mixing are strictly zero.
  • Components must be structurally and chemically similar.
  • Entropy of mixing is always positive, driving spontaneity.

⚖️ Key Differences

Ideal Solution VS Ideal Gas

Ideal gases have zero intermolecular forces; ideal solutions have equal and non-zero intermolecular forces between all species.

Question 6 of 19

For an ideal binary solution, if the mole fraction of component A is 0.4, what is the mole fraction of component B?

Explanation: The sum of mole fractions in a binary mixture equals 1; therefore, xB = 1 - 0.4 = 0.6.

Which of the following interactions is equal in an ideal binary solution of A and B?

Explanation: In an ideal solution, solute-solute (A-A), solvent-solvent (B-B), and solute-solvent (A-B) interactions are all equal in magnitude.

Question 8 of 19

What is the Gibbs free energy of mixing (ΔGmix) for the formation of an ideal solution?

Explanation: The formation of a solution is a spontaneous process, meaning ΔGmix must be negative.

Question 1 of 10
For an ideal solution of two volatile liquids A and B, if xA = 0.3 and p°A = 100 torr, p°B = 200 torr, what is the total vapor pressure?

Solution: xB = 1 - 0.3 = 0.7. Ptotal = (0.3 × 100) + (0.7 × 200) = 30 + 140 = 170 torr.

Question 2 of 10
Which of the following thermodynamic quantities is negative for the formation of an ideal solution?

Solution: The Gibbs free energy of mixing (ΔGmix) must be negative for any spontaneous mixing process.

Question 3 of 10
Which characteristic is NOT exhibited by ideal solutions?

Solution: Ideal solutions do not form azeotropes; azeotrope formation is a consequence of large deviations from Raoult's law.

Question 4 of 10
The vapor pressure of a pure liquid A is 40 mmHg. What is its partial vapor pressure in an ideal solution where its mole fraction is 0.5?

Solution: pA = xA p°A = 0.5 × 40 = 20 mmHg.

Question 5 of 10
Which of the following pairs is expected to deviate the most from ideal behavior?

Solution: Water and HCl react strongly and dissociate, leading to extreme non-ideal behavior.

Question 6 of 10
In an ideal binary solution, if the mole fraction of component A is 1, the total vapor pressure equals:

Solution: When xA = 1, xB = 0, so Ptotal = p°A.

Question 7 of 10
Which condition regarding intermolecular forces ensures ideal solution behavior?

Solution: For ideality, unlike molecular interactions must equal like molecular interactions.

Question 8 of 10
What is the mole fraction of a solvent in an ideal binary solution when the mole fraction of the solute is 0.05?

Solution: xsolvent = 1 - xsolute = 1 - 0.05 = 0.95.

Question 9 of 10
Why is ΔHmix equal to zero for an ideal solution?

Solution: The energy required to break A-A and B-B interactions is exactly balanced by the energy released in forming A-B interactions.

Question 10 of 10
If a liquid mixture obeys Raoult's law over a limited concentration range, it is considered ideal:

Solution: Real solutions often approach ideal behavior (obeying Raoult's law) within dilute concentration ranges.
