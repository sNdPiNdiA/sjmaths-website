Lowering of Vapour Pressure

Lowering of vapour pressure is a colligative property observed when a non-volatile solute is dissolved in a volatile liquid solvent. The magnitude of relative lowering of vapour pressure depends solely on the mole fraction of the solute particles present in the ideal dilute solution.

UP PGT Chemistry Subject Code 02 B.Sc. / M.Sc. Level

Thermodynamic Origin and Raoult's Law

  • The vapour pressure of a liquid is the pressure exerted by its vapour in equilibrium with its liquid phase at a given temperature.
  • When a non-volatile solute is added, the surface area occupied by solvent molecules decreases, reducing the rate of vaporization.
  • According to Raoult's Law, the partial vapour pressure of any volatile component in a solution is directly proportional to its mole fraction.
  • The mathematical expression is given by p1 = p1°x1, where p1° is the vapour pressure of the pure solvent.

Colligative Nature and Mathematical Formulation

  • The reduction in vapour pressure (Δp = p1° - p1) is independent of the chemical nature of the solute.
  • The relative lowering of vapour pressure is strictly proportional to the mole fraction of the solute (x2).
  • For dilute solutions, the number of moles of solute (n2) is negligible compared to the solvent (n1), simplifying calculations.
  • Molecular mass determination of unknown solutes is achieved via the relation M2 = (w2 · M1 · p1°) / (w1 · Δp).

Ostwald-Walker Experimental Method

  • The Ostwald-Walker method is used experimentally to determine the relative lowering of vapour pressure of a solution.
  • Dry air is bubbled successively through a train of bulbs containing the solution and then the pure solvent.
  • The loss in mass of the solution bulb is proportional to the lowering of vapour pressure, while the solvent bulb absorbs the remaining solvent.
  • The total mass of solvent lost is proportional to the vapour pressure of the pure solvent.
📐 Key Formulas & Equations
Absolute Lowering of Vapour Pressure
Δp = p1° - p1 = p1° · x2
Conditions: Non-volatile solute dissolved in a volatile solvent
Units: Δp in atm, mmHg, or kPa
Relative Lowering of Vapour Pressure
(p1° - p1) / p1° = x2 = n2 / (n1 + n2)
Conditions: Applicable to binary dilute solutions
Units: Dimensionless ratio
Limiting Form for Dilute Solutions
(p1° - p1) / p1° ≈ n2 / n1 = (w2 · M1) / (M2 · w1)
Conditions: Very dilute solutions where n2 << n1
Units: w in grams, M in g mol-1
Raoult's Law for Non-volatile Solutes
p1 = x1 · p1°
Conditions: Ideal dilute solutions containing non-volatile solutes
Units: p1 and p1° in atm, mmHg, or kPa; x1 is dimensionless
⚠️ Important Exceptions & Anomalies
Exception Relative lowering of vapour pressure is independent of solute nature for ideal solutions.

Observation: Electrolytes deviate significantly from expected values. — Solutes that undergo dissociation or association alter the total number of particles in solution, requiring the Van 't Hoff factor (i).

Exception Raoult's law applies strictly to ideal solutions.

Observation: Real solutions exhibit positive or negative deviations. — Differences in solute-solvent intermolecular adhesive forces compared to cohesive solvent-solvent forces alter escaping tendencies.

Exception Vapour pressure strictly decreases monotonically with solute addition.

Observation: Volatile solutes increase total system vapour pressure. — If the added solute is volatile, it contributes its own partial vapour pressure according to Dalton's law, invalidating simple non-volatile equations.

💡 Shortcuts & Memory Aids
Dilute Solution Approximation Shortcut
(p1° - p1) / p1° = (w2 / M2) × (M1 / w1)

Application: Use this expanded form directly when given masses and molar masses to bypass mole fraction calculations.

Van 't Hoff Correction Mnemonic
i × x2

Application: Always multiply the solute mole fraction by the dissociation factor 'i' if the solute is an ionic salt like NaCl or CaCl2.

Solvent Mass Unit Check
M1 in g mol-1, w1 in g

Application: Ensure consistency between grams and kilograms when calculating molar mass to avoid powers-of-ten errors.

🎯 Key Exam Points
  • Lowering of vapour pressure is a colligative property depending on the number of solute species.
  • The relative lowering of vapour pressure is equal to the mole fraction of the solute in an ideal solution.
  • Raoult's law is a special case of Henry's law where the Henry's law constant equals the vapour pressure of the pure solvent.
  • Electrolytes show abnormal colligative properties due to particle dissociation quantified by the Van 't Hoff factor.
  • Ostwald-Walker dynamic method measures loss in mass to determine relative vapour pressure lowering.
  • Units of vapour pressure must be uniform across all terms in Raoult's law calculations.
🚫 Common Errors & Confusions
  • Confusing absolute lowering (Δp) with relative lowering (Δp / p1°).
  • Applying non-volatile equations to volatile liquid mixtures without considering Dalton's Law.
  • Forgetting to apply the Van 't Hoff factor 'i' for ionic solutes like K4[Fe(CN)6].
  • Using weight percentage directly as mole fraction without converting through molar masses.

⚡ Quick Revision: Lowering of Vapour Pressure

Core formulas, rapid recall points, and distinguishing criteria at a glance.

Vapour Pressure Equilibrium

Vapour pressure is established when the rate of condensation equals the rate of vaporization in a closed container at constant temperature.

Raoult's Formulation

The vapour pressure lowering is directly proportional to the relative concentration of solute particles, forming the basis of colligative property measurements.

Limiting Dilution Laws

In extremely dilute solutions, mathematical approximations simplify relative lowering equations to direct ratios of number of moles.

Electrolyte Corrections

Dissociation and association phenomena require scaling solute mole fractions by the experimentally determined Van 't Hoff factor.

📚 Terms & Units Glossary

Vapour Pressure (p)
Pressure exerted by vapour in equilibrium with liquid at constant temperature. Unit: atm or mmHg.
Pure Solvent Vapour Pressure (p1°)
Vapour pressure of pure volatile liquid component. Unit: atm.
Mole Fraction (x)
Ratio of moles of a component to total moles in solution. Dimensionless.
Colligative Property (Colligative Property)
Property dependent on number of solute particles rather than their chemical identity.
Ostwald-Walker Method (Ostwald-Walker)
Gravimetric experimental technique for measuring relative lowering of vapour pressure.
Van 't Hoff Factor (i)
Ratio of actual particles in solution to formula units added. Dimensionless.

🎯 Key Takeaways

  • Relative lowering of vapour pressure = x2
  • p1 = p1°x1
  • Δp = p1° - p1 = p1°x2
  • For dilute solutions: (p1° - p1) / p1° = (n2 / n1)
  • M2 = (w2 · M1 · p1°) / (w1 · Δp)

📌 Quick Recall

  • Addition of non-volatile solutes depresses solvent vapour pressure.
  • Relative lowering is independent of temperature for ideal dilute solutions.
  • Experimental verification is efficiently performed via the Ostwald-Walker gas train method.
  • Abnormal molar masses stem from solute dissociation or association.

⚖️ Key Differences

Absolute Lowering VS Relative Lowering

Absolute lowering is p1° - p1, whereas relative lowering is (p1° - p1) / p1°.

Which of the following is a colligative property?

According to Raoult's law, the relative lowering of vapour pressure for a solution of a non-volatile solute is equal to:

Question 3 of 19

What is the vapour pressure of water at 100°C under standard atmospheric conditions?

Explanation: By definition, the normal boiling point of water occurs when its vapour pressure equals standard atmospheric pressure, which is 760 mmHg (1 atm).

Question 4 of 19

If 1 mole of a non-volatile solute is dissolved in 9 moles of water, the mole fraction of the solvent is:

Explanation: Mole fraction of solvent x1 = n1 / (n1 + n2) = 9 / (9 + 1) = 0.9.

Question 5 of 19

Explanation: The Ostwald-Walker dynamic method is specifically designed to determine the relative lowering of vapour pressure experimentally.

Question 6 of 19

For a very dilute solution, the relative lowering of vapour pressure is approximately equal to:

Explanation: When n2 << n1, n1 + n2 ≈ n1, making the ratio equal to n2 / n1.

Question 7 of 19

Which solute will produce the maximum lowering of vapour pressure at the same molal concentration in water?

Explanation: AlCl3 dissociates into 4 ions (i = 4), producing the highest number of particles and thus the maximum colligative effect.

Question 8 of 19

Raoult's law is valid for:

Explanation: Raoult's law strictly applies to ideal dilute solutions containing non-volatile solutes.

Question 9 of 19

In the expression (p1° - p1) / p1° = w2M1 / (M2w1), w2 represents:

Explanation: In standard notation, subscript 1 denotes the solvent and subscript 2 denotes the solute; hence w2 is the weight of the solute.

Question 10 of 19

What happens to the vapour pressure of a liquid when a non-volatile solid is dissolved in it?

Explanation: The addition of a non-volatile solute lowers the escaping tendency of solvent molecules, decreasing the vapour pressure.

Question 11 of 19

The dimension of relative lowering of vapour pressure is:

Explanation: Since it is a ratio of pressure differences to pure pressure, units cancel out, making it a dimensionless quantity.

Question 12 of 19

If the vapour pressure of pure water is 23.8 mmHg and that of an aqueous solution is 23.4 mmHg, the absolute lowering is:

Explanation: Absolute lowering Δp = p1° - p1 = 23.8 - 23.4 = 0.4 mmHg.

Question 13 of 19

Which of the following conditions favors ideal behavior in solutions regarding vapour pressure?

Explanation: Ideal behavior and strict adherence to Raoult's law occur at high dilution with non-volatile solutes.

Question 14 of 19

The Van 't Hoff factor 'i' for benzoic acid in benzene experiencing dimerization is:

Explanation: For dimerization, two molecules combine to form one particle, so i = 1/2 = 0.5.

Question 15 of 19

Which instrument or apparatus is classically associated with dynamic vapour pressure lowering measurements?

Explanation: The Ostwald-Walker dynamic method uses a train of bulbs for air stream saturation to measure vapour pressure lowering.

Question 16 of 19

If the mole fraction of a non-volatile solute in a binary solution is 0.05, what is the relative lowering of vapour pressure?

Explanation: Relative lowering equals the mole fraction of the solute, which is given as 0.05.

Question 17 of 19

Why does a non-volatile solute lower vapour pressure?

Explanation: Solute molecules at the surface block solvent molecules from escaping into the vapour phase.

Question 18 of 19

Which law provides the theoretical foundation for calculating molecular masses from relative lowering of vapour pressure?

Explanation: Raoult's law directly connects relative vapour pressure lowering to the mole fraction and molar masses.

Question 19 of 19

In the Ostwald-Walker apparatus, what is the function of the final guard tubes containing anhydrous CaCl2?

Explanation: Guard tubes absorb all remaining solvent vapour escaping the train to ensure accurate mass loss and gain measurements.

The vapour pressure of water at 20°C is 17.5 mmHg. If 18 g of glucose is added to 178.2 g of water, the vapour pressure of the resulting solution will be:
Answer: Option A (17.35 mmHg) Moles of glucose = 18/180 = 0.1 mol. Moles of water = 178.2/18 = 9.9 mol. (17.5 - p) / 17.5 = 0.1 / (0.1 + 9.9) = 0.01. Thus 17.5 - p = 0.175, so p = 17.325 ≈ 17.35 mmHg.

Question 2 of 10
Which of the following aqueous solutions exhibits the lowest vapour pressure at a given temperature?

Solution: Al2(SO4)3 produces 5 ions per formula unit, yielding the highest particle concentration and greatest vapour pressure lowering.

Question 3 of 10
The mathematical expression for Raoult's law for a solution containing a non-volatile solute is:

Solution: Raoult's law states that the partial vapour pressure of the solvent equals the pure vapour pressure multiplied by its mole fraction.

Question 4 of 10
In the Ostwald-Walker experiment, the loss in weight of the solution bulb is proportional to:

Solution: Loss in mass of the solution bulb is proportional to the lowering of vapour pressure (Δp).

Question 5 of 10
If 6 g of a urea (NH2CONH2, molar mass = 60 g mol-1) is dissolved in 90 g of water, what is the relative lowering of vapour pressure?

Solution: Moles of urea = 6/60 = 0.1 mol. Moles of water = 90/18 = 5 mol. Relative lowering = 0.1 / (0.1 + 5) = 0.1 / 5.1 ≈ 0.0196 ≈ 0.02.

Question 6 of 10
What is the primary reason why vapour pressure decreases upon adding a non-volatile solute?

Solution: Solute particles occupy surface area, reducing the escaping tendency and vaporization rate of solvent molecules.

Question 7 of 10
For an ideal solution obeying Raoult's law across the entire composition range, the plot of vapour pressure vs mole fraction of solvent is:

Solution: p1 = p1°x1 represents a linear equation (y = mx) passing through the origin.

Question 8 of 10
Which of the following assumptions is made in dilute solution approximations for vapour pressure lowering?

Solution: In very dilute solutions, solute moles are negligible compared to solvent moles, so n1 + n2 ≈ n1.

Question 9 of 10
If the relative lowering of vapour pressure of a solution is 0.018 and the pure solvent vapour pressure is 760 mmHg, what is the absolute lowering?

Solution: Δp = 0.018 × 760 = 13.68 mmHg.

Question 10 of 10
Colligative properties depend upon:

Solution: Colligative properties depend exclusively on the number of solute particles relative to solvent particles, regardless of their nature.
