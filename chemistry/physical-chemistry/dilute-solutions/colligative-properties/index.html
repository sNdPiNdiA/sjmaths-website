Physical Chemistry

Colligative properties

Colligative properties are physical properties of dilute solutions that depend solely on the number of dissolved solute particles rather than their chemical nature. These properties include relative lowering of vapor pressure, elevation of boiling point, depression of freezing point, and osmotic pressure.

UP PGT Chemistry Subject Code 02 B.Sc. / M.Sc. Level

Thermodynamic Basis of Colligative Properties

  • The addition of a non-volatile solute decreases the chemical potential of the solvent, lowering its vapor pressure according to Raoult's Law.
  • Boiling point elevation occurs because the lower vapor pressure requires a higher temperature for the solution vapor pressure to reach atmospheric pressure.
  • Freezing point depression arises because solute particles disrupt the crystal lattice formation of the solid solvent, requiring lower thermal energy removal.
  • Osmotic pressure represents the hydrostatic pressure required to halt the spontaneous net flow of solvent molecules through an ideal semipermeable membrane.

Mathematical Formulation and Molality Dependence

  • Both boiling point elevation (ΔTb) and freezing point depression (ΔTf) are directly proportional to the molality (m) of the solution.
  • The ebullioscopic constant (Kb) and cryoscopic constant (Kf) are characteristic properties of the solvent alone and are independent of the solute identity.
  • Osmotic pressure (π) uses molar concentration (C) instead of molality, making it experimentally preferred for high molar mass macromolecules like proteins and polymers.
  • For dilute aqueous solutions, molality and molarity are approximately equal due to solution density approaching that of pure water.

Abnormal Molecular Masses and Van 't Hoff Correction

  • Electrolytes exhibit experimental colligative property values greater than predicted due to dissociation into multiple ions.
  • Solutes that undergo association (such as benzoic acid in benzene forming dimers) show lower colligative effects and higher apparent molecular masses.
  • The Van 't Hoff factor (i) corrects colligative property equations for incomplete dissociation or association using the degree of dissociation (α).
  • For complete dissociation into n ions, i = 1 + (α - 1)n, where α equals 1 for strong electrolytes.
📐 Key Formulas & Equations
Elevation of Boiling Point
ΔTb = i × Kb × m
Conditions: Dilute solutions, constant pressure
Units: ΔTb in K or °C, Kb in K kg mol-1, m in mol kg-1
Depression of Freezing Point
ΔTf = i × Kf × m
Conditions: Dilute solutions where only pure solvent crystallizes
Units: ΔTf in K or °C, Kf in K kg mol-1, m in mol kg-1
Osmotic Pressure (Van 't Hoff Equation)
π = i × C × R × T = i × (n2 / V) × R × T
Conditions: Ideal dilute solutions across a semipermeable membrane
Units: π in atm or Pa, C in mol L-1, R in L atm K-1 mol-1, T in K
Van 't Hoff Factor
i = Normal Molar Mass / Abnormal Molar Mass = Total moles of particles after dissociation/association / Initial moles before dissociation/association
Conditions: Solutions containing electrolytes undergoing association or dissociation
Units: Dimensionless factor
Relative Lowering of Vapor Pressure (Raoult's Law)
(P1° - P1) / P1° = x2 = n2 / (n1 + n2)
Conditions: Dilute solutions of non-volatile, non-electrolyte solutes
Units: Dimensionless ratio
⚠️ Important Exceptions & Anomalies
Exception Colligative properties depend only on the number of solute particles and not their chemical nature.

Observation: Apparent deviations occur in concentrated solutions due to interionic or intermolecular interactions. — Non-ideal behavior causes effective particle concentration (activity) to differ from stoichiometric molality.

Exception Electrolytes like NaCl have a Van 't Hoff factor (i) equal to 2.

Observation: At high concentrations, 'i' is less than 2 due to ionic pairing and electrostatic attraction. — Oppositely charged ions form temporary ion pairs, reducing the effective number of independent free particles.

Exception Solutes like ethanoic acid in water dissociate into ions.

Observation: In non-polar solvents like benzene, carboxylic acids undergo hydrogen bonding to form dimers. — Association of two molecules into one structural unit halves the effective particle concentration, making i = 0.5.

💡 Shortcuts & Memory Aids
Van 't Hoff Factor Quick Count
i = 1 + n(α) for dissociation where n is number of ions

Application: For strong electrolytes (α = 1), simply count the total number of ions produced per formula unit (e.g., Al2(SO4)3 → 2Al3+ + 3SO42- ∴ i = 5).

Osmotic Pressure Shortcut
πV = i n R T

Application: Use this integrated form for numericals involving protein molar mass determination to avoid calculating intermediate concentrations.

Freezing/Boiling Ratio
ΔTb / ΔTf = Kb / Kf

Application: Useful for finding unknown boiling point elevations when freezing point depression is given for the same solvent.

🎯 Key Exam Points
  • Osmotic pressure method is best suited for determining the molecular masses of polymers and proteins because measurements are conducted at room temperature and small concentrations show large measurable heights.
  • The cryoscopic constant (Kf) for water is 1.86 K kg mol-1, meaning a 1 m ideal non-electrolyte solution freezes at -1.86 °C.
  • The ebullioscopic constant (Kb) for water is 0.52 K kg mol-1.
  • Raoult's law is a special case of Henry's law where the Henry's law constant (KH) equals the vapor pressure of the pure solvent (P°).
  • Addition of common salt to ice-water mixtures lowers the freezing point, forming a freezing mixture used to preserve biological samples.
  • Reverse osmosis is applied in seawater desalination by exerting external pressure greater than the osmotic pressure on the solution side.
🚫 Common Errors & Confusions
  • Confusing molality (m) with molarity (M) in boiling and freezing point equations; always use molality as it is temperature-independent.
  • Forgetting to multiply by the Van 't Hoff factor (i) when dealing with ionic solutes like electrolytes.
  • Assuming the Van 't Hoff factor for weak acids or bases is equal to the number of ions produced without considering the degree of dissociation (α).
  • Applying Raoult's law directly to solutions containing volatile solutes without accounting for the partial pressure contributed by the solute.

⚡ Quick Revision: Colligative properties

Core formulas, rapid recall points, and distinguishing criteria at a glance.

Colligative Property Nature

Colligative properties depend strictly on the ratio of solute to solvent particles, remaining independent of chemical identity.

Vapor Pressure Lowering

The relative lowering of vapor pressure equals the mole fraction of the non-volatile solute in the liquid mixture.

Thermal Shifts

Boiling point elevation and freezing point depression are linear functions of solution molality scaled by solvent constants.

Osmosis and Osmotic Pressure

Osmotic pressure is a colligative property obeying the gas-like equation πV = nRT for ideal dilute solutions.

📚 Terms & Units Glossary

Colligative Property (Property dependent on particle count)
Physical property scaling with the number of solute species present in solution.
Molality (m = moles of solute / kg of solvent)
Concentration expressed as moles of solute per kilogram of solvent (mol kg-1).
Van 't Hoff Factor (i)
Ratio of experimental colligative property to calculated normal property.
Cryoscopic Constant (Kf)
Freezing point depression of a 1 molal solution (K kg mol-1).
Ebullioscopic Constant (Kb)
Boiling point elevation of a 1 molal solution (K kg mol-1).
Semipermeable Membrane (SPM)
Barrier permitting selective passage of solvent molecules while blocking solute.

🎯 Key Takeaways

  • ΔTb = i · Kb · m
  • ΔTf = i · Kf · m
  • π = i · C · R · T
  • (P1° - P1) / P1° = x2
  • i = 1 + n(α)

📌 Quick Recall

  • Colligative properties scale with particle count.
  • Molality is temperature-independent; molarity varies with temperature.
  • Electrolytes require the Van 't Hoff factor correction.
  • Osmotic pressure is ideal for high molecular mass polymer characterization.

⚖️ Key Differences

Boiling Point Elevation (Kb) VS Freezing Point Depression (Kf)

Kb depends on solvent heat of vaporization whereas Kf depends on solvent heat of fusion.

The Van 't Hoff factor (i) for a dilute solution of BaCl2 assuming complete dissociation is:

Explanation: BaCl2 dissociates into one Ba2+ ion and two Cl- ions, giving a total of 3 particles (i = 3).

Which aqueous solution will have the highest freezing point?

Explanation: Glucose is a non-electrolyte with the lowest particle count (i = 1), resulting in the lowest freezing point depression and thus the highest freezing point.

Question 4 of 18

Molality of a solution is defined as:

Explanation: Molality is strictly defined as the number of moles of solute dissolved in one kilogram of solvent.

Question 5 of 18

Which colligative property is most suitable for determining the molecular mass of polymers?

Explanation: Osmotic pressure generates measurable values even at very low concentrations, making it ideal for macromolecules.

Question 6 of 18

If benzoic acid undergoes dimerization in benzene, its Van 't Hoff factor is approximately:

Explanation: Dimerization means 2 molecules associate into 1 particle, so i = 1/2 = 0.5.

Question 7 of 18

The units of the ebullioscopic constant (Kb) are:

Explanation: Kb = ΔTb / m, where ΔTb is in Kelvin and m is in mol kg-1, yielding K kg mol-1.

Question 8 of 18

According to Raoult's Law, the relative lowering of vapor pressure of a dilute solution containing a non-volatile solute is equal to:

Explanation: (P1° - P1) / P1° = x2, which is the mole fraction of the solute.

Question 9 of 18

What happens to the boiling point of water when table salt is added?

Explanation: Adding a non-volatile solute elevates the boiling point of the solvent.

Question 10 of 18

For a 0.1 m aqueous solution of NaCl, assuming complete dissociation, the boiling point elevation (ΔTb) given Kb = 0.52 is:

Explanation: ΔTb = i · Kb · m = 2 × 0.52 × 0.1 = 0.104 K.

Question 11 of 18

Which property is independent of temperature?

Explanation: Molality involves mass of solvent, which does not change with temperature, unlike volume-based concentrations.

Question 12 of 18

Reverse osmosis is used for:

Explanation: Applying pressure greater than osmotic pressure forces pure water out of seawater across a semipermeable membrane in desalination.

Question 13 of 18

The cryoscopic constant depends on:

Explanation: Kf is a characteristic constant determined solely by the thermodynamic properties of the pure solvent.

Question 14 of 18

If a substance undergoes dissociation in solution, the experimental molar mass is:

Explanation: Due to increased particle count, colligative properties increase, leading to an artificially lower experimental molar mass.

Question 15 of 18

Which of the following has the highest osmotic pressure at 300 K?

Explanation: CaCl2 produces 3 ions (i = 3), yielding the highest particle concentration and highest osmotic pressure (π = iCRT).

Question 16 of 18

The unit of osmotic pressure in SI is:

Explanation: The SI unit for pressure, including osmotic pressure, is the Pascal (Pa).

Question 17 of 18

Raoult's law is applicable to:

Question 18 of 18

Question 1 of 10
Calculate the boiling point of a solution containing 18 g of glucose (C6H12O6) in 100 g of water. (Kb for water = 0.52 K kg mol-1, Boiling point of pure water = 100 °C)

Solution: Moles of glucose = 18 / 180 = 0.1 mol. Molality = 0.1 / 0.1 kg = 1 m. ΔTb = 1 × 0.52 = 0.52 °C. Boiling point = 100.52 °C.

Question 2 of 10
Which aqueous solution has the lowest vapor pressure at 25 °C?

Solution: Lowering of vapor pressure depends directly on particle concentration. AlCl3 yields 4 particles, resulting in the lowest vapor pressure.

Question 3 of 10
The degree of dissociation (α) of a weak electrolyte AB with Van 't Hoff factor 1.8 and n = 2 is:

Solution: i = 1 + α(n - 1) ⇒ 1.8 = 1 + α(2 - 1) ⇒ α = 0.80.

Question 4 of 10
If 0.1 moles of NaCl is dissolved in 1 kg of water, the freezing point of the solution will be: (Kf = 1.86)

Solution: ΔTf = i · Kf · m = 2 × 1.86 × 0.1 = 0.372 °C, so freezing point is -0.372 °C.

Question 5 of 10
Colligative properties are applicable to:

Solution: Colligative properties strictly apply to dilute solutions where thermodynamic ideality is approached.

Question 6 of 10
Which solute pair will produce identical osmotic pressures at the same temperature?

Solution: Urea and glucose are both non-electrolytes with i = 1 and equal molarities, producing identical osmotic pressures.

Question 7 of 10
The molar mass of a solute determined by measuring freezing point depression will be abnormally high if the solute:

Solution: Association decreases particle count, lowering colligative effect and yielding an abnormally high calculated molar mass.

Question 8 of 10
What is the osmotic pressure of a 0.02 M solution of sucrose at 300 K? (R = 0.0821 L atm K-1 mol-1)

Solution: π = iCRT = 1 × 0.02 × 0.0821 × 300 = 0.4926 atm.

Question 9 of 10
Which of the following constants depends on the heat of vaporization of the solvent?

Solution: The ebullioscopic constant Kb is derived thermodynamically using the latent heat of vaporization.

Question 10 of 10
Addition of a non-volatile solute to a solvent causes:

Solution: Vapor pressure is lowered, requiring a higher temperature to reach boiling, causing boiling point elevation.
