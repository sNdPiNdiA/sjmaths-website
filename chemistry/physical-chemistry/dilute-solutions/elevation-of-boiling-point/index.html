Elevation of Boiling Point

Elevation of boiling point is a colligative property where the boiling point of a pure solvent increases upon the addition of a non-volatile solute. The magnitude of elevation is directly proportional to the molal concentration of the solute particles in dilute solutions.

UP PGT Chemistry Subject Code 02 B.Sc. / M.Sc. Level

Thermodynamic Derivation and Vapor Pressure Lowering

  • The addition of a non-volatile solute lowers the vapor pressure of the solvent, requiring a higher temperature for the vapor pressure to equal atmospheric pressure.
  • At the boiling point, the vapor pressure of the liquid equals the external atmospheric pressure (101.3 kPa).
  • The chemical potential of the solvent in the solution is lower than that of the pure liquid solvent, expanding the liquid stability range towards higher temperatures.
  • The proportionality constant Kb depends exclusively on the thermodynamic properties of the solvent, such as latent heat of vaporization.

Colligative Nature and Concentration Dependency

  • Elevation of boiling point is a colligative property, depending solely on the total number of dissolved solute particles rather than their chemical identity.
  • Molality (m) is preferred over molarity (M) because molality is temperature-independent, whereas volume changes with temperature.
  • In dilute solutions, ΔTb scales linearly with the molal concentration of the dispersed phase.
  • For binary ionic solutions, each formula unit generates multiple kinetic particles, multiplying the effective molality by the number of ions.

Abnormal Molecular Masses and Van 't Hoff Factor

  • Solutes undergoing dissociation (e.g., NaCl, K2SO4) exhibit a Van 't Hoff factor (i) greater than 1, increasing ΔTb.
  • Solutes undergoing association (e.g., benzoic acid in benzene) show an 'i' factor less than 1, reducing the expected boiling point elevation.
  • The experimental molar mass calculated using standard formulas will be abnormally low for dissociating solutes and abnormally high for associating solutes.
  • Degree of dissociation (α) is calculated using the relation i = 1 + (n - 1)α where n is the number of ions per molecule.
📐 Key Formulas & Equations
Molal Elevation Constant (Ebullioscopic Constant)
Kb = R × M1 × Tb21000 × ΔHvap
Conditions: Applicable for any pure liquid solvent
Units: R in J mol-1 K-1, M1 in g mol-1, Tb in K, ΔHvap in J mol-1
Molar Mass Determination via Boiling Point Elevation
M2 = Kb × W2 × 1000ΔTb × W1
Conditions: W2 grams of solute in W1 grams of solvent
Units: M2 in g mol-1, W1, W2 in grams, ΔTb in K
Van 't Hoff Factor Incorporation
ΔTb = i × Kb × m
Conditions: For solutes that undergo dissociation or association in solution
Units: i is dimensionless, ΔTb in K, Kb in K kg mol-1, m in mol kg-1
Ebullioscopic Law
ΔTb = Kb × m
Conditions: Dilute solutions containing non-volatile, non-electrolyte solutes
Units: ΔTb in K or °C, Kb in K kg mol-1, m in mol kg-1
⚠️ Important Exceptions & Anomalies
Exception Colligative properties depend only on the number of solute particles in ideal dilute solutions.

Observation: Concentrated solutions deviate significantly from linearity. — Interionic or intermolecular forces become prominent, altering the effective activity of the solvent and solute particles.

Exception Electrolytes dissociate completely in aqueous solution, giving 'i' equal to the number of ions.

Observation: Weak electrolytes and strong electrolytes in concentrated regimes exhibit incomplete dissociation. — Ion-pairing and electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions reduce the effective number of independent particles.

Exception Association of molecules like carboxylic acids halves the number of effective particles.

Observation: Polar solvents disrupt the hydrogen-bonded dimers. — Solvent-solute interactions overcome solute-solute hydrogen bonding, preventing dimerization in polar media like water.

💡 Shortcuts & Memory Aids
Molality Shortcut for Dilute Aqueous Solutions
m ≈ M (for dilute aqueous solutions where density ≈ 1 g/mL)

Application: Quickly substitute molarity for molality in quick estimations when solution density is close to pure water.

Van 't Hoff Factor Quick Count
i = Total number of ions produced per formula unit (for strong electrolytes)

Application: For Al2(SO4)3, quickly count 2(Al3+) + 3(SO42-) = 5 particles.

Boiling Point of Solution Calculation
Tb = Tb° + ΔTb

Application: Remember to add the calculated elevation directly to the normal boiling point of the pure solvent (e.g., 100°C for water).

🎯 Key Exam Points
  • The unit of the ebullioscopic constant (Kb) is K kg mol-1 or °C kg mol-1.
  • Boiling point elevation is directly proportional to the molality of the solution.
  • Kb depends exclusively on the nature of the solvent, not on the solute.
  • Van 't Hoff factor (i) accounts for association or dissociation of solute particles.
  • For glucose or urea, the Van 't Hoff factor (i) is equal to 1.
  • Molality is preferred over molarity because molality is independent of temperature changes.
🚫 Common Errors & Confusions
  • Using molarity instead of molality in numerical formulas; always use molality (mol kg-1).
  • Forgetting to include the Van 't Hoff factor (i) for ionic salts like NaCl or CaCl2.
  • Confusing the boiling point of the solution (Tb) with the boiling point elevation (ΔTb).
  • Assuming Kb is constant for all solvents; Kb is a characteristic property unique to each solvent.

⚡ Quick Revision: Elevation of Boiling Point

Core formulas, rapid recall points, and distinguishing criteria at a glance.

Vapor Pressure and Boiling Point Relation

Adding a non-volatile solute lowers solvent vapor pressure, requiring a higher temperature to reach atmospheric pressure.

Mathematical Formulation

The elevation ΔTb is expressed as Kb × m, establishing a direct link to molal concentration.

Colligative Principles

Properties depend strictly on particle count, enabling accurate determination of unknown molecular masses.

Electrolyte Corrections

The Van 't Hoff factor modifies basic equations to account for ionic dissociation and molecular association.

📚 Terms & Units Glossary

Boiling Point Elevation (ΔTb)
Difference between solution and solvent boiling points in Kelvin.
Ebullioscopic Constant (Kb)
Elevation in boiling point for a 1 molal solution with SI unit K kg mol-1.
Molality (m)
Moles of solute per kilogram of solvent (mol kg-1).
Van 't Hoff Factor (i)
Ratio of experimental colligative property to calculated normal property.
Non-volatile Solute (Solute Vapor Pressure ≈ 0)
A substance with negligible vapor pressure at test temperatures.
Colligative Property (Latin 'colligare')
Property depending on particle number, not chemical nature.

Molality (m) VS Molarity (M)

Question 1 of 18

Which of the following is the correct unit for the ebullioscopic constant (Kb)?

Explanation: Kb is defined as ΔTb / m, where ΔTb is in Kelvin and m is in mol kg-1, yielding K kg mol-1.

Question 2 of 18

What is the Van 't Hoff factor (i) for a dilute aqueous solution of K4[Fe(CN)6] assuming complete dissociation?

Explanation: K4[Fe(CN)6] dissociates into 4 K+ ions and 1 [Fe(CN)6]4- ion, totaling 5 particles.

Question 3 of 18

Which property among the following is a colligative property?

Explanation: The absolute boiling point is not colligative, but the elevation of boiling point (ΔTb) is a colligative property.

Question 4 of 18

If 1 mol of NaCl is dissolved in 1 kg of water, the boiling point elevation is approximately:

Explanation: NaCl dissociates into Na+ and Cl- (i = 2). Thus, ΔTb = 2 × Kb × 1 = 2 Kb.

Question 5 of 18

Why is molality preferred over molarity for calculating colligative properties?

Explanation: Molarity involves volume, which changes with temperature due to thermal expansion or contraction. Molality uses mass, which is temperature-independent.

Question 6 of 18

The boiling point of a solution containing a non-volatile solute is:

Explanation: Lowering vapor pressure requires raising the temperature to reach atmospheric pressure, increasing the boiling point.

Question 7 of 18

What is the value of the Van 't Hoff factor for benzoic acid when it dimerizes completely in benzene?

Explanation: Upon complete dimerization, 2 moles of solute form 1 mole of dimer, so i = 1/2 = 0.5.

Question 8 of 18

The ebullioscopic constant depends upon:

Explanation: Kb is a constant characteristic of the pure solvent, derived from its heat of vaporization and boiling point.

Question 9 of 18

Which of the following aqueous solutions has the highest boiling point?

Explanation: AlCl3 produces 4 ions (i = 4), yielding the highest particle concentration and greatest boiling point elevation.

Question 10 of 18

The relationship between boiling point elevation and molality is:

Explanation: Ebullioscopic law states ΔTb = Kb × m, showing direct linearity with molality.

Question 11 of 18

When a non-volatile solute is added to a solvent, the vapor pressure of the solution at a given temperature is:

Explanation: Solute particles occupy surface sites, reducing escaping tendency and lowering vapor pressure.

Question 12 of 18

Abnormal molecular mass is observed when solutes undergo:

Explanation: Any process that alters the particle count from the expected formula value causes abnormal molar mass.

Question 13 of 18

Calculate the molal elevation constant if latent heat of vaporization is 335 J g-1 for a solvent of molar mass 18 g mol-1 boiling at 373 K. (R = 8.314)

Explanation: Using Kb = (R × M × T2) / (1000 × ΔHvap), substituting values yields ~0.52 K kg mol-1 for water.

Question 14 of 18

Which of the following 0.1 m solutions will show the minimum elevation in boiling point?

Explanation: Glucose is a non-electrolyte (i = 1), whereas the others are electrolytes with i > 1, giving the minimum elevation.

Question 15 of 18

If the degree of dissociation of a weak electrolyte AB is α, the Van 't Hoff factor 'i' is given by:

Explanation: For AB → A+ + B- (n = 2), i = 1 + (n - 1)α = 1 + α.

Question 16 of 18

Which equation correctly represents the determination of molar mass using boiling point elevation?

Explanation: M2 = (Kb × W2 × 1000) / (ΔTb × W1) is the exact algebraic arrangement.

Question 17 of 18

At the boiling point of a liquid, its vapor pressure is:

Explanation: By definition, boiling occurs when vapor pressure equals the prevailing external atmospheric pressure.

Question 18 of 18

What happens to the boiling point elevation when the mass of the non-volatile solute dissolved in a fixed mass of solvent is doubled?

Explanation: Molality doubles when solute mass is doubled at constant solvent mass, directly doubling ΔTb.

Question 1 of 10
Calculate the boiling point of a solution prepared by dissolving 30 g of urea (Molar mass = 60 g mol-1) in 500 g of water. (Kb for water = 0.52 K kg mol-1)

Solution: Molality = (30 / 60) × (1000 / 500) = 1 mol kg-1. ΔTb = 0.52 × 1 = 0.52 K. Boiling point = 100 + 0.52 = 100.52°C.

Question 2 of 10
Which colligative property is most suitable for determining the high molecular weight of polymers and proteins?

Solution: Osmotic pressure is measured at room temperature and uses concentrations in molarity, making it ideal for delicate biomolecules and large polymers.

Question 3 of 10
If Kb of water is 0.52 K kg mol-1, what is the elevation in boiling point for a 2 molal aqueous solution of sucrose?

Solution: ΔTb = Kb × m = 0.52 × 2 = 1.04 K.

Question 4 of 10
A 0.5 molal solution of a non-volatile, non-electrolyte in benzene has a boiling point elevation of 2.56 K. What is the Kb of benzene?

Solution: Kb = ΔTb / m = 2.56 / 0.5 = 5.12 K kg mol-1.

Question 5 of 10
The boiling point of a solvent increases by 0.52 K when 0.1 moles of a solute are dissolved in 100 g of solvent. The Kb of the solvent is:

Solution: Molality = 0.1 mol / 0.1 kg = 1 mol kg-1. Kb = ΔTb / m = 0.52 / 1 = 0.52? Wait: 0.1 mol in 100g = 1 mol/kg. So Kb = 0.52. Let's recalculate: 0.1 mol / 0.1 kg = 1 molal. ΔTb = 0.52. Thus Kb = 0.52 / 1 = 0.52. Option correction: 0.52.

Question 6 of 10
Which of the following conditions is mandatory for the validity of the ebullioscopic equation ΔTb = Kb × m?

Solution: The linear relationship holds strictly in dilute solutions with non-volatile solutes.

Question 7 of 10
If the Van 't Hoff factor of a 0.1 M Ba(NO3)2 solution is 2.74, what is its degree of dissociation (α)?

Solution: Ba(NO3)2 gives n = 3 ions. i = 1 + (n - 1)α ⇒ 2.74 = 1 + 2α ⇒ 1.74 = 2α ⇒ α = 0.87.

Question 8 of 10
An aqueous solution freezes at -0.186°C. What is the elevation in boiling point for this solution if Kf = 1.86 and Kb = 0.52 K kg mol-1?

Solution: ΔTf = 0.186 ⇒ m = 0.186 / 1.86 = 0.1 mol kg-1. ΔTb = 0.52 × 0.1 = 0.052°C.

Question 9 of 10
Addition of common salt to water causes:

Solution: NaCl is a non-volatile solute that elevates the boiling point of water.

Question 10 of 10
The molecular mass of a solute determined by measuring elevation in boiling point will be abnormally high if the solute:

Solution: Association decreases particle count, lowering ΔTb and yielding an abnormally high calculated molecular mass.
