Physical Chemistry

Raoult's Law

Raoult's law states that the partial vapor pressure of each volatile component in an ideal solution is directly proportional to its mole fraction in the liquid phase. The law serves as the fundamental thermodynamic basis for describing vapor-liquid equilibria in dilute binary solutions.

UP PGT Chemistry Subject Code 02 B.Sc. / M.Sc. Level

Thermodynamic Foundation and Ideal Solutions

  • An ideal solution strictly obeys Raoult's law across the entire range of concentrations from x = 0 to x = 1.
  • The enthalpy of mixing (ΔHmix) and volume of mixing (ΔVmix) are exactly zero for ideal solutions.
  • Intermolecular adhesive forces between solute (A) and solvent (B) molecules equal cohesive forces (A-A and B-B).

Colligative Properties and Non-Volatile Solutes

  • For non-volatile solutes, the vapor pressure contribution of the solute is zero, causing a depression in total solution vapor pressure.
  • The relative lowering of vapor pressure depends solely on the mole fraction of the solute and is independent of the solute's chemical nature.
  • Colligative properties such as elevation in boiling point (ΔTb) and depression in freezing point (ΔTf) derive directly from Raoult's law.

Deviations from Raoult's Law

  • Positive deviations occur when A-B intermolecular interactions are weaker than A-A and B-N interactions, resulting in higher vapor pressure than predicted (ΔHmix > 0).
  • Negative deviations occur when A-B interactions are stronger, yielding lower vapor pressure than ideal (ΔHmix < 0).
  • Systems exhibiting extreme positive or negative deviations form azeotropes at specific compositions where liquid and vapor compositions become identical.
📐 Key Formulas & Equations
Total Vapor Pressure of a Binary Solution
Ptotal = PA°xA + PB°xB
Conditions: Ideal solutions of two volatile liquids A and B
Units: Ptotal, PA°, PB° in atm or kPa
Relative Lowering of Vapor Pressure
(PA° - P) / PA° = xB = nB / (nA + nB)
Conditions: Solutions containing non-volatile, non-electrolyte solutes
Units: nA, nB in moles; pressures in consistent units
Vapor Phase Mole Fraction (Dalton's Law Coupling)
yi = Pi / Ptotal
Conditions: Vapor phase behaving as an ideal gas mixture
Units: yi is mole fraction in gas phase; pressures in atm
Raoult's Law for a Volatile Component
Pi = xi × Pi°
Conditions: Ideal binary solutions containing volatile solute and solvent
Units: Pi, Pi° in atm or torr; xi is dimensionless
⚠️ Important Exceptions & Anomalies
Exception Ideal solutions follow Raoult's law at all concentrations.

Observation: Real solutions only approach ideal behavior at extreme infinite dilution (xsolvent → 1). — Intermolecular distances are large enough in dilute solutions that solute-solute interactions become negligible.

Exception Partial vapor pressure is directly proportional to total mole fraction.

Observation: Electrolytes like NaCl show significantly larger vapor pressure lowering than calculated. — Solute dissociation increases the actual number of dissolved particles in solution, requiring Van 't Hoff factor (i) incorporation.

Exception Non-volatile solutes do not contribute to vapor pressure.

Observation: Substances with measurable volatility at system temperatures (e.g., water in concentrated glycerol) violate this assumption. — Finite vapor pressure of the second component requires modified two-component volatile equilibrium equations.

💡 Shortcuts & Memory Aids
Vapor Phase Composition Shortcut
1 / Ptotal = yA/PA° + yB/PB°

Application: Use this reciprocal relation to quickly find total pressure when vapor phase mole fractions (y) are given instead of liquid mole fractions.

Van 't Hoff Factor Integration
Pi = i × xi × Pi°

Application: Multiply the mole fraction term by the dissociation factor (i) when dealing with ionic solutes like salts or acids.

Azeotrope Identification Trick
Maximum boiling = Negative deviation; Minimum boiling = Positive deviation

Application: Instantly answer matching questions in exams by pairing minimum boiling azeotropes with positive vapor pressure deviations.

🎯 Key Exam Points
  • Raoult's law is a special case of Henry's law where the Henry's law constant equals the vapor pressure of the pure component (KH = P°).
  • Solutions that obey Raoult's law across the entire concentration range are termed ideal solutions.
  • The relative lowering of vapor pressure is a colligative property equal to the mole fraction of the solute.
  • Acetone and chloroform form a non-ideal solution showing large negative deviation due to hydrogen bonding.
  • Azeotropes are constant boiling mixtures that distill without change in composition.
  • The sum of mole fractions in both liquid phase (xA + xB) and vapor phase (yA + yB) is always equal to 1.
🚫 Common Errors & Confusions
  • Confusing liquid phase mole fraction (x) with vapor phase mole fraction (y); they are rarely equal unless pressures of pure components are identical.
  • Applying Raoult's law directly to electrolyte solutions without multiplying by the Van 't Hoff factor (i).
  • Assuming ideal solutions have non-zero enthalpy changes; ideal mixing involves zero heat change.
  • Incorrectly treating Henry's law as valid for high solute concentrations instead of dilute solutions.

⚡ Quick Revision: Raoult's Law

Core formulas, rapid recall points, and distinguishing criteria at a glance.

Raoult's Law Equation

PA = xAPA° establishes that partial pressure equals pure vapor pressure times mole fraction.

Ideal vs Non-Ideal Solutions

Ideal solutions obey Raoult's law linearly; non-ideal solutions deviate positively or negatively based on intermolecular interactions.

Colligative Properties Connection

Lowering of vapor pressure derived from Raoult's law forms the mathematical basis for all other colligative property formulas.

Dalton's Law Integration

Combining Raoult's law and Dalton's law of partial pressures allows calculation of vapor phase composition (yi).

📚 Terms & Units Glossary

Raoult's Law (Pi = xiPi°)

Ideal Solution (ΔHmix = 0)
Solution obeying Raoult's law at all concentrations.
Mole Fraction (xi = ni / ∑n)
Ratio of moles of a component to total moles.
Azeotrope (Constant Boiling Mixture)
Liquid mixture distilling with constant composition.
Van 't Hoff Factor (i)
Ratio of normal to abnormal colligative property value.
Henry's Law (P = KHx)
Governs gas solubility in liquids at low concentrations.

🎯 Key Takeaways

  • Ptotal = PA°xA + PB°xB
  • Relative lowering = (P° - P)/P° = xsolute
  • yA = PA / Ptotal
  • For positive deviation: ΔH > 0, ΔV > 0
  • For negative deviation: ΔH < 0, ΔV < 0

📌 Quick Recall

  • Raoult's law relates vapor pressure to liquid composition.
  • Non-volatile solutes lower the vapor pressure proportionally.
  • Deviations stem from intermolecular force discrepancies.
  • Vapor phase is richer in the more volatile component.

⚖️ Key Differences

Raoult's Law VS Henry's Law

Raoult's law applies when the component is the solvent (x → 1), whereas Henry's law applies when the component is a dilute solute (x → 0).

Question 1 of 18

Which of the following conditions must be satisfied for a solution to be ideal?

Explanation: An ideal solution must follow Raoult's law and have zero enthalpy and volume changes upon mixing.

Question 2 of 18

What is the relative lowering of vapor pressure for a dilute solution equal to?

Explanation: According to Raoult's law, the relative lowering of vapor pressure is equal to the mole fraction of the non-volatile solute.

Question 3 of 18

Which liquid pair exhibits a large positive deviation from Raoult's law?

Explanation: Ethanol and water break hydrogen bonding upon mixing, resulting in a higher vapor pressure and positive deviation.

Question 4 of 18

Acetone and chloroform form a negative deviation from Raoult's law because:

Explanation: Chloroform hydrogen bonds with the oxygen of acetone, strengthening interactions and lowering vapor pressure.

Question 5 of 18

What is the vapor phase mole fraction (yA) related to?

Explanation: By Dalton's law of partial pressures, yA = PA / Ptotal.

Question 6 of 18

When does Raoult's law become a special case of Henry's law?

Explanation: When KH = P°, Henry's law equation transforms directly into Raoult's law.

Question 7 of 18

Which of the following solutions will show the highest vapor pressure at 25°C?

Explanation: Any solute lowers the vapor pressure of pure water; hence pure water has the highest vapor pressure.

Question 8 of 18

For a binary ideal liquid solution, if PA° > PB°, then the vapor phase will be:

Explanation: The component with the higher pure vapor pressure (lower boiling point) concentrates in the vapor phase.

Question 9 of 18

What type of deviation is exhibited by a mixture of n-hexane and n-heptane?

Explanation: Structurally similar hydrocarbons form nearly ideal solutions that obey Raoult's law.

Question 10 of 18

The total vapor pressure of an ideal binary liquid solution is given by:

Explanation: Total vapor pressure is the sum of partial vapor pressures according to Dalton's and Raoult's laws.

Question 11 of 18

Which constant boiling mixture has a boiling point lower than either of its pure components?

Explanation: Minimum boiling azeotropes show large positive deviations, meaning higher vapor pressure and lower boiling point.

Question 12 of 18

What is the effect on the boiling point of a solvent when a non-volatile solute is added?

Explanation: Lowering vapor pressure means higher temperature is required to reach atmospheric pressure, elevating the boiling point.

Question 13 of 18

Which of the following laws is applicable to dilute solutions of gases in liquids?

Explanation: Henry's law governs the solubility of gases in liquids at low partial pressures.

Question 14 of 18

If ΔHmix > 0 for a liquid mixture, the mixture:

Explanation: Endothermic mixing (ΔH > 0) corresponds to weaker interactions and positive deviations from Raoult's law.

Question 15 of 18

Calculate the mole fraction of water in a solution containing 36 g of water and 46 g of ethanol.

Explanation: Moles of water = 36/18 = 2. Moles of ethanol = 46/46 = 1. Mole fraction of water = 2 / (2 + 1) = 2/3 = 0.67.

Question 16 of 18

Which property is NOT a colligative property?

Explanation: Optical activity depends on molecular structure and chirality, not the number of particles.

Question 17 of 18

In a binary solution of volatile liquids A and B, if xA = 0.4, what is xB?

Explanation: The sum of mole fractions in a binary mixture is always equal to 1; hence xB = 1 - 0.4 = 0.6.

Question 18 of 18

Maximum boiling azeotropes are formed by solutions that show:

Explanation: Strong attractive forces cause negative deviations, reducing vapor pressure and elevating the boiling point to a maximum.

Model Question
If the vapor pressures of pure liquids A and B are 400 mm Hg and 600 mm Hg respectively, and total pressure is 500 mm Hg, what is the mole fraction of A in the liquid phase assuming ideal behavior?
Answer: Option A (0.5) Ptotal = PA°xA + PB°(1 - xA). 500 = 400xA + 600(1 - xA) → 500 = 600 - 200xA → 200xA = 100 → xA = 0.5.

Question 1 of 10
The vapor pressure of pure benzene at a certain temperature is 640 mm Hg. A non-volatile solid weighing 2.175 g is added to 39 g of benzene (molar mass = 78 g/mol). The vapor pressure of the solution is 600 mm Hg. What is the molar mass of the solid substance?

Solution: (P° - P)/P° = wBMA / (MBwA). (640 - 600)/640 = (2.175 × 78) / (MB × 39). 40/640 = (2.175 × 2) / MB → 1/16 = 4.35 / MB → MB = 16 × 4.35 = 69.6 g/mol.

Question 2 of 10
Which condition indicates a negative deviation from Raoult's law?

Solution: Negative deviation means observed vapor pressure is lower than calculated ideal pressure due to stronger interactions.

Question 3 of 10
What is the mole fraction of a solute in an aqueous solution whose vapor pressure lowering is 2% of the pure water vapor pressure?

Solution: Relative lowering ΔP / P° = 0.02, which equals the mole fraction of the solute.

Question 4 of 10
Which of the following liquid pairs represents an ideal solution?

Solution: Chlorobenzene and bromobenzene are chemically similar isomers that form ideal solutions obeying Raoult's law.

Question 5 of 10
In a mixture of two volatile liquids A and B, if the mole fraction of A in the vapor phase is yA and in liquid phase is xA, under what condition is yA > xA?

Solution: The component with the higher vapor pressure (PA° > PB°) is more volatile and concentrates in the vapor phase (yA > xA).

Question 6 of 10
Calculate the total vapor pressure of a solution containing 1 mole of liquid A (PA° = 150 torr) and 2 moles of liquid B (PB° = 300 torr).

Solution: Total moles = 3. xA = 1/3, xB = 2/3. Ptotal = (150 × 1/3) + (300 × 2/3) = 50 + 200 = 250 torr.

Question 7 of 10
Why do mixtures showing positive deviations from Raoult's law form minimum boiling azeotropes?

Solution: Positive deviations cause vapor pressure to reach a maximum, corresponding to the lowest boiling point (minimum boiling azeotrope).

Question 8 of 10
Which thermodynamic parameter is zero for the formation of an ideal binary solution?

Solution: Enthalpy of mixing (ΔHmix) and volume of mixing (ΔVmix) are strictly zero for ideal solutions.

Question 9 of 10
The boiling point of a solvent is elevated by 0.52 K when 1 mole of a non-volatile solute is dissolved in 1 kg of solvent. What is the ebullioscopic constant (Kb)?

Solution: ΔTb = Kb × m. With m = 1 mol/kg, Kb = ΔTb = 0.52 K kg/mol.

Question 10 of 10
Which of the following statements about Henry's law and Raoult's law is correct?

Solution: Henry's law matches Raoult's law when the proportionality constant KH equals the vapor pressure of the pure volatile component.
