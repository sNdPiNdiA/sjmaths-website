Physical Chemistry

Molecular-weight determination by colligative properties

Colligative properties depend solely on the number of solute particles rather than their chemical nature in dilute solutions. Measuring these properties allows the calculation of the molecular weight of unknown solutes.

UP PGT Chemistry Subject Code 02 B.Sc. / M.Sc. Level

Thermodynamic Basis of Colligative Properties

  • Colligative properties arise from the lowering of the chemical potential of the solvent upon addition of a non-volatile solute.
  • Raoult's Law dictates that the partial vapour pressure of a volatile solvent is directly proportional to its mole fraction in solution.
  • The decrease in vapour pressure drives both the boiling point elevation and the freezing point depression.
  • Osmotic pressure represents the hydrostatic pressure required to halt solvent flow across an ideal semipermeable membrane.

Mathematical Derivation for Molecular Weight

  • Rearranging the boiling point equation yields M2 = (Kb × w2 × 1000) / (ΔTb × w1) for solute molar mass.
  • Osmotic pressure provides the most accurate molecular weights for macromolecules and polymers due to large measurable values at low concentrations.
  • Cryoscopic constant Kf depends exclusively on the thermodynamic properties of the solvent via latent heat of fusion.
  • Ebullioscopic constant Kb is calculated using the universal gas constant, solvent molar mass, and enthalpy of vaporization.

Abnormal Molecular Weights and Van 't Hoff Factor

  • Electrolytes yield abnormal molar masses because dissociation increases the total particle count, reducing the apparent molecular weight.
  • Association of solute molecules in non-polar solvents halves or thirds the particle count, resulting in an abnormally high molar mass.
  • The Van 't Hoff factor i modifies all colligative equations: ΔTb = i · Kb · m.
  • For complete dissociation of a binary electrolyte like NaCl, the theoretical i value is 2.
📐 Key Formulas & Equations
Elevation of Boiling Point (Ebullioscopy)
ΔTb = Kb × m = (Kb × w2 × 1000) / (M2 × w1)
Conditions: Dilute solutions of non-volatile solutes
Units: ΔTb in K; Kb in K kg mol-1; m in mol kg-1
Depression of Freezing Point (Cryoscopy)
ΔTf = Kf × m = (Kf × w2 × 1000) / (M2 × w1)
Conditions: Dilute solutions where solvent crystallizes pure
Units: ΔTf in K; Kf in K kg mol-1
Osmotic Pressure
π = c · R · T = (n2 / V) · R · T = (w2 / M2) · (R · T / V)
Conditions: Dilute solutions across a semipermeable membrane
Units: π in atm; V in Liters; T in Kelvin; R = 0.0821 L atm K-1 mol-1
Van 't Hoff Factor Correction
i = (Observed Colligative Property) / (Calculated Colligative Property) = 1 + α(n - 1)
Conditions: Electrolytes undergoing dissociation or association
Units: Dimensionless factor α = degree of dissociation/association
Relative Lowering of Vapour Pressure
(P0 - P) / P0 = (w2 × M1) / (M2 × w1)
Conditions: Non-volatile, non-electrolyte solute in an ideal dilute solution
Units: P0, P in atm or mmHg; w, M in g and g mol-1
⚠️ Important Exceptions & Anomalies
Exception Colligative properties depend only on solute concentration

Observation: Applies strictly to ideal and extremely dilute solutions — Interionic and intermolecular interactions become non-negligible at higher concentrations, causing deviations.

Exception Electrolytes dissociate completely in aqueous solution

Observation: Weak electrolytes and strong electrolytes in concentrated solutions — Ion-pairing and incomplete dissociation lead to a lower effective Van 't Hoff factor than stoichiometry predicts.

Exception Solutes do not associate in solution

Observation: Carboxylic acids (e.g., acetic acid in benzene) — Hydrogen bonding drives extensive dimerization, reducing the number of active kinetic particles by half.

💡 Shortcuts & Memory Aids
Osmotic Pressure Dominance
π > ΔTb > ΔTf > ΔP

Application: Use osmotic pressure for high molecular weight polymers because it can be measured accurately at room temperature in dilute solutions.

Quick Van 't Hoff Calculation
i = 1 + (x + y - 1)α

Application: For a salt AxBy dissociating with degree α, instantly compute the correction factor without writing full ice-tables.

Solvent Constant Mnemonic
Kf = [R × (Tf)2 × M1] / [1000 × ΔHfusion]

Application: Use this relation when latent heat of fusion is provided instead of direct cryoscopic constants.

🎯 Key Exam Points
  • Osmotic pressure method is best suited for determining the molecular weights of polymers, proteins, and macromolecules.
  • The unit of molal depression constant (Kf) is K kg mol-1 or °C kg mol-1.
  • Addition of common salt to ice lowers the freezing point down to -21.2 °C (eutectic temperature).
  • Acetic acid dissolved in benzene shows a molecular weight double its actual formula weight due to dimerization.
  • Van 't Hoff factor 'i' is less than 1 for associating solutes and greater than 1 for dissociating solutes.
  • For non-electrolytes, the value of the Van 't Hoff factor 'i' is identically equal to 1.
🚫 Common Errors & Confusions
  • Confusing molarity (M) with molality (m) in boiling and freezing point equations; always use molality as it is temperature-independent.
  • Failing to include the Van 't Hoff factor 'i' when calculating molecular weights of ionic salts.
  • Assuming Kb and Kf depend on the solute nature; they are strictly characteristic properties of the solvent.
  • Neglecting unit conversions, especially converting grams to kilograms when applying molality formulas.

⚡ Quick Revision: Molecular-weight determination by colligative properties

Core formulas, rapid recall points, and distinguishing criteria at a glance.

Colligative Properties Foundation

Colligative properties scale with the number of moles of solute particles rather than their identity. They provide straightforward routes to experimental molar mass determination.

Boiling and Freezing Point Shifts

The shift in phase transition temperatures is proportional to solution molality. The proportionality constants Kb and Kf are specific to each solvent.

Osmotic Pressure Equations

Vant Hoff equation π = cRT relates osmotic pressure directly to molar concentration, making it ideal for large biomolecules with tiny molalities.

Abnormal Molar Masses

Electrolytic dissociation and molecular association alter particle counts, requiring the introduction of the Van 't Hoff factor to correct molar mass calculations.

📚 Terms & Units Glossary

Colligative Property (Property based on particle count)
Physical property of solutions dependent on number, not type, of solute particles.
Molality (m = n2 / w1(kg))
Number of moles of solute dissolved in one kilogram of solvent.
Ebullioscopic Constant (Kb)
Boiling point elevation produced by a one molal solution.
Cryoscopic Constant (Kf)
Freezing point depression produced by a one molal solution.
Semipermeable Membrane (SPM)
Membrane permitting selective passage of solvent molecules while blocking solutes.
Van 't Hoff Factor (i)
Ratio of experimental colligative property to calculated normal property.

🎯 Key Takeaways

  • Osmotic pressure equation: π = cRT
  • Boiling point elevation: ΔTb = i · Kb · m
  • Freezing point depression: ΔTf = i · Kf · m
  • Van 't Hoff factor for association (n molecules into 1): i = 1 - (α/n) + α
  • Relative lowering of vapour pressure: (P0 - P) / P0 = x2

📌 Quick Recall

  • Colligative properties depend on particle concentration.
  • Molar mass is inversely proportional to colligative property magnitude.
  • Association decreases particle count and raises apparent molecular weight.
  • Dissociation increases particle count and lowers apparent molecular weight.

⚖️ Key Differences

Kb (Ebullioscopic Constant) VS b (Boiling Point)

Kb is a constant characteristic of the solvent relating molality to temperature elevation; boiling point is the temperature where vapour pressure equals atmospheric pressure.

Question 1 of 19

Which of the following is a colligative property?

Explanation: Osmotic pressure is a colligative property, whereas vapour pressure and boiling point themselves are not, though their lowerings/elevations are.

Question 2 of 19

The unit of the cryoscopic constant Kf is:

Explanation: Kf = ΔTf / m, so its unit is Kelvin divided by mol kg-1, giving K kg mol-1.

Question 3 of 19

Which aqueous solution has the highest boiling point at 1 m concentration?

Explanation: AlCl3 produces 4 ions (i = 4), giving the highest particle concentration and thus the greatest boiling point elevation.

Question 4 of 19

If benzoic acid undergoes complete dimerization in benzene, its Van 't Hoff factor 'i' is:

Explanation: For dimerization (2 molecules → 1 aggregate), i = 1 - α + (α/2) = 1 - 0.5 = 0.5 for complete association.

Question 5 of 19

The molecular weight of polymers is best determined by measuring:

Explanation: Osmotic pressure generates measurable values even at very low concentrations, making it ideal for high molecular weight macromolecules.

Question 6 of 19

What is the Van 't Hoff factor for a K4[Fe(CN)6] solution assuming 100% dissociation?

Explanation: K4[Fe(CN)6] dissociates into 4 K+ ions and 1 [Fe(CN)6]4- ion, totaling 5 particles.

Question 7 of 19

Raoult's law states that for a dilute solution, relative lowering of vapour pressure is equal to:

Explanation: By Raoult's law, (P0 - P) / P0 = x2 (mole fraction of solute).

Question 8 of 19

Which property is most sensitive for determining molar mass of dilute solutions?

Explanation: Osmotic pressure measurements can be performed precisely at ambient temperatures with large signal magnitudes.

Question 9 of 19

Addition of a non-volatile solute to a pure solvent causes:

Explanation: Freezing point depression occurs when a non-volatile solute is dissolved in a solvent.

Question 10 of 19

If a salt dissociates into 3 ions with 80% dissociation degree, its Van 't Hoff factor is:

Explanation: i = 1 + α(n - 1) = 1 + 0.8(3 - 1) = 1 + 1.6 = 2.6.

Question 11 of 19

Cryoscopic depression depends on:

Explanation: ΔTf = Kf · m, where Kf depends on the solvent and m depends on solute-to-solvent mass ratio.

Question 12 of 19

The molar mass determined by freezing point depression for benzoic acid in benzene is:

Explanation: Benzoic acid dimerizes in benzene, halving particle count and doubling the apparent molecular weight.

Question 13 of 19

Which expression represents molality?

Explanation: Molality is defined as moles of solute per kilogram of solvent.

Question 14 of 19

The boiling point of a solution is defined as the temperature where:

Explanation: Boiling occurs when the vapour pressure of the liquid equals the surrounding external pressure.

Question 15 of 19

For an aqueous solution of glucose, the Van 't Hoff factor is:

Explanation: Glucose is a non-electrolyte and does not dissociate, so i = 1.

Question 16 of 19

Which of the following is an intensive property among colligative properties?

Explanation: Colligative properties depend on concentration (molality or molarity), which are intensive properties.

Question 17 of 19

If Kf for water is 1.86 K kg mol-1, the depression in freezing point for 0.1 m glucose is:

Explanation: ΔTf = 1.86 × 0.1 = 0.186 K.

Question 18 of 19

Reverse osmosis occurs when:

Explanation: Applying a hydrostatic pressure exceeding the osmotic pressure forces solvent flow from solution to pure solvent (reverse osmosis).

Question 19 of 19

Molecular weight determined via colligative properties for electrolytes is always:

Explanation: Because dissociation increases particle count, the calculated molar mass (M2 ∝ 1/particles) comes out lower than formula weight.

Question 1 of 10
Calculate the molal elevation constant of a solvent if its boiling point is 350 K and latent heat of vaporization is 300 J g-1 (Molar mass = 100 g mol-1).

Solution: Kb = [R × (Tb)2 × M] / [1000 × ΔHvap]. Substituting values: [8.314 × (350)2 × 100] / [1000 × 300 × 100] ≈ 2.02.

Question 2 of 10
A 0.5 percent aqueous solution of a protein is isotonic with a 3 percent solution of glucose. What is the molecular weight of the protein?

Solution: Cprotein / Mprotein = Cglucose / Mglucose ⇒ (0.5 / M) = (3.0 / 180) ⇒ M = (0.5 × 180) / 3 = 3000 g mol-1.

Question 3 of 10
The freezing point of a solution containing 0.2 g of acetic acid in 20.0 g of benzene is lowered by 0.45 K. (Kf for benzene = 5.12 K kg mol-1). What is the percentage association of acetic acid?

Solution: Molar mass calculated = (5.12 × 0.2 × 1000) / (0.45 × 20) = 113.78 g mol-1. Normal mass = 60. i = 60 / 113.78 = 0.527. For dimerization, i = 1 - α/2 ⇒ α ≈ 0.95 or 95%.

Question 4 of 10
Which aqueous solution has a freezing point closest to that of 0.1 M glucose?

Solution: Urea is a non-electrolyte and at 0.1 M provides the exact same particle concentration as 0.1 M glucose.

Question 5 of 10
If the osmotic pressure of a solution is 2.46 atm at 300 K, its molar concentration is:

Solution: π = cRT ⇒ 2.46 = c × 0.0821 × 300 ⇒ c = 2.46 / 24.63 = 0.1 M.

Question 6 of 10
The apparent degree of dissociation of a weak electrolyte AB (M = 100) whose 2% solution has a freezing point of -0.372 °C (Kf = 1.86) is:

Solution: m = (2 × 1000) / (100 × 98) ≈ 0.204. Measured ΔTf = 0.372. i = 0.372 / (1.86 × 0.204) ≈ 1.0 (complete dissociation adjusted for molality approximation gives i=2, thus α=100%).

Question 7 of 10
Which property is independent of temperature?

Solution: Molality uses mass of solvent, which does not vary with temperature changes.

Question 8 of 10
Lowering of vapour pressure is directly proportional to:

Solution: By Raoult's Law, relative lowering equals solute mole fraction, and absolute lowering is proportional to it.

Question 9 of 10
What happens to the boiling point when common salt is added to water?

Solution: Adding a non-volatile solute elevates the boiling point of the liquid.

Question 10 of 10
If 1 mole of NaCl is added to 1 kg of water, the boiling point elevation (ΔTb) assuming complete ionization (Kb = 0.52) is:

Solution: ΔTb = i · Kb · m = 2 × 0.52 × 1 = 1.04 K.
