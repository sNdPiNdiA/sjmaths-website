Depression of Freezing Point

Depression of freezing point is a colligative property defined as the decrease in the freezing point of a solvent upon the addition of a non-volatile solute. The magnitude of this decrease is directly proportional to the molal concentration of the solute particles in the solution.

UP PGT Chemistry Subject Code 02 B.Sc. / M.Sc. Level

Thermodynamic Basis of Freezing Point Lowering

  • The freezing point of a liquid is the temperature at which its liquid and solid phases have identical vapor pressures.
  • Addition of a non-volatile solute lowers the vapor pressure of the solvent, intersecting the solid solvent vapor pressure curve at a lower temperature.
  • The chemical potential of the solvent in the solution is lower than that of the pure liquid solvent at the same temperature.
  • Solid solvent separates out from the solution when its chemical potential equals that of the solvent in the solution, occurring at a reduced temperature (ΔTf).

Cryoscopic Constant and Solvent Properties

  • The cryoscopic constant (Kf) or molal depression constant is a characteristic property dependent solely on the nature of the solvent, not the solute.
  • Solvents with high enthalpies of fusion (ΔHfusion) exhibit smaller Kf values.
  • The value of Kf for water is 1.86 K kg mol-1.
  • Cryoscopic measurements provide an accurate method for determining the molar mass of unknown organic solutes.

Abnormal Molecular Masses and Electrolytes

  • Electrolytes dissociate into multiple ions in solution, increasing particle concentration and causing an abnormally large depression in freezing point.
  • The Van 't Hoff factor (i) corrects for association (i < 1) or dissociation (i > 1) of solute particles.
  • For complete dissociation of a binary electrolyte like NaCl, the theoretical Van 't Hoff factor is 2.
  • Association of molecules (e.g., benzoic acid in benzene) halves the effective particle concentration, leading to a smaller ΔTf than expected.
📐 Key Formulas & Equations
Molality Calculation
m = (WB × 1000) / (MB × WA)
Conditions: Where WB is solute mass (g), MB is solute molar mass (g mol-1), WA is solvent mass (g)
Units: WA, WB in grams; MB in g mol-1; m in mol kg-1
Cryoscopic Constant Relation
Kf = (R × MA × Tf2) / (1000 × ΔHfusion)
Conditions: Applies to pure solvents where MA is molar mass of solvent and Tf is normal freezing point
Units: R in J mol-1 K-1, ΔHfusion in J mol-1, Tf in K
Van 't Hoff Factor Inclusion
ΔTf = i × Kf × m
Conditions: Solutions containing electrolytes that undergo association or dissociation
Units: i is dimensionless; other units standard
Freezing Point Depression Equation
ΔTf = Kf × m
Conditions: Dilute solutions containing non-volatile, non-electrolyte solutes
Units: ΔTf in Kelvin (K) or °C, Kf in K kg mol-1, m in mol kg-1
⚠️ Important Exceptions & Anomalies
Exception Colligative properties depend only on the number of solute particles.

Observation: Electrolytes show deviations from expected colligative values. — Interionic attraction in concentrated solutions reduces the effective number of independent particles, requiring the use of activity coefficients.

Exception Solutes increase ΔTf linearly with molality.

Observation: Highly concentrated solutions show non-linear divergence from the theoretical line. — Ideality breaks down at higher concentrations due to solute-solvent interactions and volume occupied by solute particles.

Exception Association of carboxylic acids in non-polar solvents yields i = 0.5.

Observation: Degree of association varies with initial concentration and temperature. — Dimerization equilibria shift depending on the concentration of monomeric units in the non-polar medium.

💡 Shortcuts & Memory Aids
Molar Mass Shortcut
MB = (1000 × Kf × WB) / (ΔTf × WA)

Application: Directly substitute given masses and temperature depression to calculate unknown molecular weights in seconds.

Van 't Hoff Ion Count Quick Rule
i = 1 + α(n - 1)

Application: Use degree of dissociation (α) and total ions per molecule (n) to instantly determine the modified ΔTf.

Freezing Point of Solution
Tf = Tf° - ΔTf

Application: Remember that freezing point depression means you must SUBTRACT ΔTf from the pure solvent freezing point (0 °C for water), not add.

🎯 Key Exam Points
  • The unit of molal depression constant (Kf) is K kg mol-1 or °C kg mol-1.
  • For water, the cryoscopic constant Kf is 1.86 K kg mol-1.
  • Freezing point depression is a colligative property because it depends on the number of solute particles, not their chemical nature.
  • The Van 't Hoff factor 'i' is greater than 1 for strong electrolytes like KCl, HCl, and MgSO4.
  • Association of solute molecules leads to a Van 't Hoff factor less than 1 (e.g., benzoic acid in benzene).
  • Addition of non-volatile solute lowers the freezing point of the solvent.
🚫 Common Errors & Confusions
  • Forgetting to subtract ΔTf from 0 °C when finding the actual freezing point of an aqueous solution.
  • Confusing molality (m) with molarity (M); freezing point depression equations strictly require molality.
  • Neglecting the Van 't Hoff factor 'i' when calculating ΔTf for ionic salts like CaCl2.
  • Assuming Kf is constant across different solvents; Kf is a unique property of each specific solvent.

⚡ Quick Revision: Depression of Freezing Point

Core formulas, rapid recall points, and distinguishing criteria at a glance.

Colligative Nature

Freezing point depression is proportional to the number of solute moles dissolved in a fixed mass of solvent, independent of molecular identity.

Thermodynamic Derivation

Equating chemical potentials of liquid and solid phases yields the depression equation linked to the latent heat of fusion.

Cryoscopic Constant

Kf is determined by solvent thermal and thermodynamic properties via the expression involving enthalpy of fusion.

Electrolyte Corrections

The Van 't Hoff factor accounts for ion dissociation or molecular association, modifying calculated colligative properties.

📚 Terms & Units Glossary

Freezing Point (Tf)
Temperature where liquid and solid vapor pressures are equal.
Freezing Point Depression (ΔTf)
Difference in freezing point between pure solvent and solution.
Cryoscopic Constant (Kf)
Depression in freezing point for a 1 molal solution.
Molality (m)
Moles of solute per kilogram of solvent (mol kg-1).
Van 't Hoff Factor (i)
Ratio of experimental colligative property to calculated normal value.
Colligative Property (Property dependent on particle count)
Properties scaling with solute particle concentration.

🎯 Key Takeaways

  • ΔTf = Kf × m
  • Kf for water = 1.86 K kg mol-1
  • i = (Normal Molar Mass) / (Observed Molar Mass)
  • Freezing point of solution is always lower than pure solvent
  • Electrolytes increase the expected ΔTf proportional to ion count

📌 Quick Recall

  • Colligative properties require molality, not molarity.
  • Vapor pressure lowering causes both boiling point elevation and freezing point depression.
  • Association decreases particle count while dissociation increases particle count.
  • Kf depends exclusively on solvent characteristics.

⚖️ Key Differences

Molality (m) VS Molarity (M)

Molality relates moles of solute to kg of solvent and is temperature independent; molarity relates moles to solution volume and varies with temperature.

Question 1 of 10
Calculate the freezing point of an aqueous solution containing 6 g of urea (Molar mass = 60 g mol-1) in 500 g of water (Kf = 1.86 K kg mol-1).

Solution: m = (6 / 60) × (1000 / 500) = 0.2 mol kg-1. ΔTf = 1.86 × 0.2 = 0.372 K. Tf = -0.372 °C.

Question 2 of 10
Which colligative property is best suited for determining high molecular masses of polymers?

Solution: Osmotic pressure is measured at room temperature and uses concentration terms advantageous for large macromolecules.

Question 3 of 10
If the degree of dissociation of a weak electrolyte AB is 0.4, its Van 't Hoff factor is:

Solution: i = 1 + α(n - 1) = 1 + 0.4(2 - 1) = 1.4.

Question 4 of 10
A 0.5 molal solution of a substance in benzene shows a freezing point depression of 2.56 K. If Kf for benzene is 5.12 K kg mol-1, the substance is:

Solution: Expected ΔTf = 5.12 × 0.5 = 2.56 K. Since observed equals calculated, i = 1, meaning normal solute in benzene (wait, if calculated is 2.56 and observed is 2.56, i = 1, so it exists as monomer).

Question 5 of 10
The freezing point of a dilute solution is directly proportional to:

Solution: ΔTf ∝ m (molality).

Question 6 of 10
Which of the following salts has the highest Van 't Hoff factor in dilute aqueous solution?

Solution: K3[Fe(CN)6] gives 4 ions, yielding the highest 'i' value among the choices.

Question 7 of 10
Cryoscopic constant depends on:

Solution: Kf is a characteristic constant determined solely by the solvent.

Question 8 of 10
An aqueous solution freezes at -0.186 °C. What is the molality of the solution? (Kf = 1.86 K kg mol-1)

Solution: m = ΔTf / Kf = 0.186 / 1.86 = 0.1 mol kg-1.

Question 9 of 10
If a solution is frozen, the first crystals to separate from solution are always:

Solution: Solid solvent crystallizes out first upon reaching the freezing point of the solution.

Question 10 of 10

Solution: BaCl2 dissociates into 1 Ba2+ and 2 Cl- ions, totaling 3 particles.
