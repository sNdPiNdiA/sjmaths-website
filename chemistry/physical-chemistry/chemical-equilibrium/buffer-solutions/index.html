Physical Chemistry

Buffer Solutions

A buffer solution is an aqueous system that resists changes in pH upon the addition of small amounts of strong acid or strong base. It typically consists of a weak acid and its conjugate base, or a weak base and its conjugate acid.

UP PGT Chemistry Subject Code 02 B.Sc. / M.Sc. Level

Acidic and Basic Buffer Systems

  • An acidic buffer is prepared by mixing a weak acid (e.g., CH3COOH) and its salt with a strong base (e.g., CH3COONa) maintaining a pH < 7.
  • A basic buffer is prepared by mixing a weak base (e.g., NH4OH) and its salt with a strong acid (e.g., NH4Cl) maintaining a pH > 7.
  • The reserve acidity of an acidic buffer is provided by the unreacted weak acid and the salt's conjugate base.
  • Buffer action relies on the common ion effect to suppress the dissociation of the weak acid or weak base.

Mechanism of Buffer Action

  • Upon adding H+ ions to an acidic buffer, they combine with the excess conjugate base anions (A-) to form undissociated weak acid (HA), keeping pH nearly constant.
  • Upon adding OH- ions to an acidic buffer, they are neutralized by the unreacted weak acid (HA) to produce water and conjugate base (A-).
  • Dilution of a buffer solution does not alter its pH, because the ratio of salt to acid concentrations remains unchanged.
  • Addition of large amounts of strong acid or base exceeds the buffer capacity, causing a drastic shift in pH.

Quantitative Characteristics and Limitations

  • Maximum buffer capacity occurs when the concentration of the weak acid equals the concentration of its conjugate salt ([Acid] = [Salt]), making pH = pKa.
  • The useful pH range of a buffer system is generally given by the interval pKa ± 1.
  • Buffer capacity (β) increases with higher absolute concentrations of both the buffer components.
  • Temperature variations alter the dissociation constant (Ka) and thereby slightly affect the buffer pH.
📐 Key Formulas & Equations
Henderson-Hasselbalch Equation for Basic Buffers
pOH = pKb + log([Salt] / [Base])
Conditions: Applied to solutions containing a weak base and its salt with a strong acid.
Units: Concentrations in mol dm-3; dimensionless log ratio.
Buffer Capacity Equation
β = dn / d(pH)
Conditions: Measures the resistance of a buffer to pH change upon addition of strong acid or base.
Units: dn in moles per liter, pH is dimensionless.
Ion Product of Water
pKw = pKa + pKb = 14.00 (at 25°C)
Conditions: Applies to conjugate acid-base pairs in aqueous solutions at 298 K.
Units: Dimensionless negative logarithmic scales.
Henderson-Hasselbalch Equation for Acidic Buffers
pH = pKa + log([Salt] / [Acid])
Conditions: Applied to solutions containing a weak acid and its salt with a strong base.
Units: Concentrations in mol dm-3; dimensionless log ratio.
⚠️ Important Exceptions & Anomalies
Exception Buffer capacity is independent of absolute concentration in dilute solutions

Observation: Extremely dilute buffers fail to maintain pH stability — When buffer component concentrations approach the autoionization concentration of water (10-7 M), water's own dissociation dictates the pH.

Exception Salts of strong acids and strong bases form neutral solutions

Observation: High ionic strength solutions exhibit slight pH shifts — Activity coefficients deviate significantly from unity at high ionic strengths, altering effective hydrogen ion activities.

💡 Shortcuts & Memory Aids
Log Ratio Shortcut
pH = pKa + log(Msalt / Macid)

Application: Use volume-molarity equivalence directly without converting to absolute moles when mixing equal volumes.

Maximum Efficiency Rule
[Salt] = [Acid] ⇒ pH = pKa

Application: Instantly identify that pH equals pKa whenever salt and weak acid molarities are identical in the problem.

Basic Buffer Conversion Trick
pH = 14 - pKb - log([Salt] / [Base])

Application: Convert basic buffer calculations directly to pH scale to avoid intermediate pOH subtraction errors.

🎯 Key Exam Points
  • The pH of a buffer solution remains unchanged upon dilution.
  • Maximum buffer capacity occurs when [Salt] = [Acid].
  • Effective pH range of a buffer is pKa ± 1.
  • Blood maintains a constant pH of ~7.4 via carbonic acid-bicarbonate buffer system.
  • Addition of strong acid to CH3COOH/CH3COONa consumes CH3COO-.
  • The buffer capacity depends directly on the concentrations of the buffer components.
🚫 Common Errors & Confusions
  • Assuming buffer pH changes upon addition of pure water; dilution does not alter pH.
  • Forgetting to use concentrations instead of volumes when applying the Henderson-Hasselbalch equation.
  • Confusing pKa and pKb values when dealing with conjugate acid-base pairs.
  • Neglecting the change in total volume when mixing solutions of weak acid and its salt.

⚡ Quick Revision: Buffer Solutions

Core formulas, rapid recall points, and distinguishing criteria at a glance.

Definition and Composition

Buffer solutions resist pH alterations by neutralizing added H+ or OH- ions through reserve acidic and basic components.

Henderson-Hasselbalch Relations

Relates buffer pH or pOH directly to the dissociation constants and the logarithmic concentration ratio of salt to weak acid/base.

Buffer Capacity

Quantitative measure of resistance to pH change, maximized when molar concentrations of conjugate acid-base pairs are equal.

Biological Significance

Essential biological fluids like blood utilize specialized buffer systems to sustain strict physiological pH homeostasis.

📚 Terms & Units Glossary

Buffer Capacity (β)
Moles of strong acid or base required to change pH of 1 liter of buffer by 1 unit.
Acid Dissociation Constant (Ka)
Equilibrium constant for the dissociation of a weak acid in water.
Base Dissociation Constant (Kb)
Equilibrium constant for the dissociation of a weak base in water.
Common Ion Effect (Suppression Effect)
Reduction in dissociation of a weak electrolyte by adding a common ion.
Henderson-Hasselbalch Equation (pH Formula)
Mathematical relation between pH, pKa, and salt-to-acid ratio.
Conjugate Acid-Base Pair (Bronsted Pair)
Species differing by a single proton (H+).

🎯 Key Takeaways

  • pH = pKa + log([Salt]/[Acid])
  • pOH = pKb + log([Salt]/[Base])
  • Effective buffer range = pKa ± 1
  • Maximum buffer action when [Salt] = [Acid]
  • pKw = pKa + pKb = 14 at 25°C

📌 Quick Recall

  • Buffers resist pH changes upon small additions of acid or base.
  • Acidic buffers consist of weak acid and its salt with a strong base.
  • Basic buffers consist of weak base and its salt with a strong acid.
  • Dilution has no effect on the pH of an ideal buffer solution.

⚖️ Key Differences

Acidic Buffer pH VS Basic Buffer pH

Acidic buffers maintain pH < 7 using weak acid/salt, whereas basic buffers maintain pH > 7 using weak base/salt.

Question 1 of 19

Which of the following mixtures forms an acidic buffer solution?

Explanation: A mixture of a weak acid (CH3COOH) and its salt with a strong base (CH3COONa) constitutes an acidic buffer.

Question 2 of 19

What is the pH of a buffer solution when the concentration of salt equals the concentration of weak acid?

Explanation: When [Salt] = [Acid], log(1) = 0, so pH = pKa according to the Henderson-Hasselbalch equation.

Question 3 of 19

Which system is responsible for maintaining the pH of human blood?

Explanation: The carbonic acid-bicarbonate buffer system (H2CO3 / HCO3-) regulates blood pH at approximately 7.4.

Question 4 of 19

What happens to the pH of an acidic buffer solution when a small amount of distilled water is added?

Explanation: Dilution does not alter the ratio of salt to acid concentrations, leaving the pH unchanged.

Question 5 of 19

What is the effective pH range for a buffer system with a weak acid having pKa = 4.75?

Explanation: The effective buffer range is given by pKa ± 1, which equals 4.75 ± 1 (i.e., 3.75 to 5.75).

Question 6 of 19

Which of the following is a basic buffer?

Explanation: NH4OH (weak base) and NH4Cl (salt of weak base and strong acid) form a basic buffer.

Question 7 of 19

Buffer capacity is maximum when:

Explanation: Maximum buffer capacity and resistance to pH changes occur when the molar concentrations of the salt and the weak acid are equal.

Question 8 of 19

Addition of a strong acid to an acidic buffer (CH3COOH + CH3COONa) primarily reacts with:

Explanation: Added H+ ions from the strong acid react with the conjugate base CH3COO- to form undissociated CH3COOH.

Question 9 of 19

What is the pOH of a basic buffer when [Salt] = [Base]?

Explanation: When salt and base concentrations are equal, log(1) = 0, leading to pOH = pKb.

Question 10 of 19

Which salt undergoes hydrolysis to give an acidic solution?

Explanation: NH4Cl is a salt of a weak base (NH4OH) and a strong acid (HCl), and its cation hydrolyzes to yield an acidic solution.

Question 11 of 19

The Henderson-Hasselbalch equation is derived from the expression for:

Explanation: It is derived directly by taking the negative logarithm of the acid dissociation constant expression Ka.

Question 12 of 19

If pKa of acetic acid is 4.74, what is the pKb of its conjugate base (acetate ion) at 25°C?

Explanation: pKa + pKb = 14.00, so pKb = 14.00 - 4.74 = 9.26.

Question 13 of 19

Which combination will NOT act as a buffer solution?

Explanation: HCl is a strong acid and NaCl is a neutral salt; strong acid-strong salt mixtures do not possess buffering properties.

Question 14 of 19

What is the buffer capacity (β) formula?

Explanation: Buffer capacity is defined as the ratio of increments of strong base/acid (dn) to the resulting change in pH (d(pH)).

Question 15 of 19

Which factor increases buffer capacity?

Explanation: Higher concentrations of both the weak acid/base and its corresponding salt provide greater reserve neutralizing capacity.

Question 16 of 19

What happens when a small amount of strong base is added to an acidic buffer?

Explanation: The weak acid component neutralizes the added OH- ions, preventing a drastic change in pH.

Question 17 of 19

Which of the following is a conjugate acid-base pair?

Explanation: NH4+ and NH3 differ by a single proton (H+), constituting a conjugate acid-base pair.

Question 18 of 19

In a basic buffer, addition of a strong base is neutralized by:

Explanation: The conjugate acid of the weak base (e.g., NH4+) neutralizes added OH- ions to form the weak base and water.

Question 19 of 19

The pH of a 1 L solution containing 0.1 M CH3COOH and 0.1 M CH3COONa (pKa = 4.74) is:

Explanation: Since [Salt] = [Acid] = 0.1 M, pH = pKa + log(1) = 4.74 + 0 = 4.74.

Question 1 of 10
What is the pH of a buffer solution prepared by mixing 0.2 mol of CH3COOH and 0.2 mol of CH3COONa in 1 L of water? (pKa = 4.74)

Solution: Since [Salt] = [Acid] = 0.2 M, pH = pKa + log(1) = 4.74.

Question 2 of 10
Which of the following buffer pairs is used to maintain alkaline pH in biochemical assays?

Solution: NH4OH / NH4Cl is a basic buffer system maintaining pH values greater than 7.

Question 3 of 10
If the pKa of a weak acid is 5.0, what is the optimum pH for a buffer made from this acid?

Solution: Optimum buffer capacity occurs when pH equals pKa, which is 5.0.

Question 4 of 10
Which statement is correct regarding buffer solutions?

Solution: Buffers resist pH changes when small amounts of strong acid or strong base are added.

Question 5 of 10
Calculate the pH of a basic buffer containing 0.1 M NH4OH and 0.01 M NH4Cl. (pKb = 4.74)

Solution: pOH = 4.74 + log(0.01 / 0.1) = 4.74 - 1 = 3.74. Therefore, pH = 14 - 3.74 = 10.26.

Question 6 of 10
What is the effect of adding a strong base to a basic buffer (NH4OH + NH4Cl)?

Solution: Added OH- ions react with NH4+ ions from the salt to form undissociated NH4OH and water.

Question 7 of 10
Which of the following has the highest buffer capacity?

Solution: Equal and higher concentrations (0.1 M each) provide the highest buffer capacity and reserve neutralizing power.

Question 8 of 10
What is the conjugate acid of NH3?

Solution: Adding a proton (H+) to NH3 yields its conjugate acid, NH4+.

Question 9 of 10
If a buffer has a pH of 5.0 and pKa = 5.0, what is the ratio of [Salt] to [Acid]?

Solution: pH = pKa + log([Salt]/[Acid]) ⇒ 5.0 = 5.0 + log([Salt]/[Acid]) ⇒ log(ratio) = 0 ⇒ ratio = 1 : 1.

Question 10 of 10
Which of the following indicators is suitable for titrating a weak acid against a strong base?

Solution: The equivalence point of a weak acid-strong base titration lies in the alkaline range (pH 8-10), making phenolphthalein the appropriate indicator.
