Physical Chemistry

Degree of Dissociation

The degree of dissociation is defined as the fraction of one mole of a reactant that undergoes dissociation into product species at equilibrium. It is expressed numerically as α and ranges between 0 and 1, or 0% to 100%.

UP PGT Chemistry Subject Code 02 B.Sc. / M.Sc. Level

Quantitative Definition and Thermodynamic Equilibrium

  • The degree of dissociation (α) quantifies the extent of a reversible chemical decomposition reaction at a given temperature and pressure.
  • For a general reaction A ⇌ B + C, if initial moles are 1 and dissociation is α, equilibrium moles are (1-α), α, and α.
  • The total number of moles at equilibrium becomes equal to 1 + (ν - 1)α, where ν is the stoichiometric sum of dissociation products.
  • Equilibrium constants (Kc or Kp) are expressed explicitly in terms of α and initial concentration or total pressure.

Ostwald's Dilution Law and Weak Electrolytes

  • Ostwald's dilution law establishes a quantitative correlation between the dissociation constant of a weak electrolyte and its concentration.
  • For weak electrolytes where α << 1, the expression simplifies to α = √(Ka / c) or α = √(Ka × V).
  • The degree of dissociation increases with dilution (V increases or c decreases) due to the shift in equilibrium toward the ion side per Le Chatelier's principle.
  • At infinite dilution (c → 0), the degree of dissociation theoretically approaches unity (α → 1).

Colligative Properties and Van't Hoff Factor

  • Electrolytes exhibit abnormal colligative properties because dissociation increases the total number of particles in solution.
  • The Van't Hoff factor (i) relates directly to the degree of dissociation via the equation i = 1 + (ν - 1)α.
  • For association processes (e.g., dimerization of acetic acid in benzene), the formula modifies to i = 1 + (1/ν - 1)α.
  • Experimental determination of colligative properties like freezing point depression allows direct calculation of α.

⚠️ Important Exceptions & Anomalies
Exception Degree of dissociation increases monotonically with dilution for all electrolytes.

Observation: Strong electrolytes like NaCl or HCl — Strong electrolytes are completely dissociated (α ≈ 1) at all accessible concentrations; interionic attractions at higher concentrations affect activity coefficients rather than true dissociation fractions.

Exception Van't Hoff factor i is greater than 1 for dissociating solutes.

Observation: Carboxylic acids in non-polar solvents (e.g., acetic acid in benzene) — Solutes undergo intermolecular hydrogen bonding causing association (dimerization), making i < 1 and giving a negative or unconventional apparent dissociation calculation.

💡 Shortcuts & Memory Aids
ICE Table Shortcut for Binary Dissociation
K = (α^2 × P) / (1 - α^2)

Application: Use this quadratic-free approximation when K is very small, or solve directly for α when K and P are given.

Molar Mass Shortcut
α = (Theoretical Molar Mass - Experimental Molar Mass) / (Experimental Molar Mass × (ν - 1))

Application: Quickly compute α from vapor density measurements where D = Theoretical Molar Mass / 2 and d = Experimental Molar Mass / 2.

Dilution Law Approximation
α ∝ 1 / √c

Application: When concentration is decreased by a factor of 100, the degree of dissociation of a weak electrolyte increases by a factor of 10.

🎯 Key Exam Points
  • The value of α for a completely non-dissociating substance is zero.
  • For PCl5 ⇌ PCl3 + Cl2, the total moles at equilibrium is 1 + α.
  • Vapor density (d) is inversely proportional to the Van't Hoff factor and average molecular mass.
  • Ostwald's dilution law fails completely for strong electrolytes because they dissociate completely even at high concentrations.
  • The unit of degree of dissociation is none, as it is a ratio of moles.
  • An increase in temperature increases α for endothermic dissociation reactions in accordance with Van't Hoff isochore.
🚫 Common Errors & Confusions
  • Confusing degree of dissociation (α) with dissociation constant (Ka); α is a dimensionless fraction while Ka has concentration dimensions.
  • Applying Ostwald's dilution law formula α = √(K/c) to strong electrolytes where dissociation is complete.
  • Failing to account for the correct stoichiometric coefficient ν in the Van't Hoff equation i = 1 + (ν - 1)α.
  • Treating association reactions with standard dissociation formulas without modifying the stoichiometry term.

⚡ Quick Revision: Degree of Dissociation

Core formulas, rapid recall points, and distinguishing criteria at a glance.

Definition and Expression

Degree of dissociation (α) measures the fraction of reactant molecules dissociated into products at chemical equilibrium.

Ostwald's Law

Relates ionization constant and concentration for weak electrolytes via K = cα2/(1-α).

Colligative Correlation

Connects abnormal molar mass and Van't Hoff factor i to find experimental degree of dissociation.

Environmental Factors

Dilution increases α for weak electrolytes, whereas temperature dependence follows the sign of reaction enthalpy.

📚 Terms & Units Glossary

Degree of Dissociation (α)
Fraction of reactant dissociated at equilibrium; dimensionless.
Van't Hoff Factor (i)
Ratio of normal to abnormal colligative property; dimensionless.
Ostwald's Dilution Law (K = cα2/(1-α))
Relation governing weak electrolyte dissociation.
Equilibrium Constant (Kc / Kp)
Ratio of product to reactant activities at equilibrium.
Normal Molar Mass (Mnormal)
Calculated formula mass of substance in g mol-1.
Apparent Molar Mass (Mobs)
Experimental molar mass derived from colligative data in g mol-1.

🎯 Key Takeaways

  • α ranges from 0 to 1.
  • i = 1 + (ν - 1)α for dissociation.
  • For weak electrolyte, α = √(K/c).
  • Total moles at equilibrium = 1 + (ν - 1)α.
  • Vapor density relation: α = (D - d) / [d(ν - 1)].

📌 Quick Recall

  • Degree of dissociation quantifies equilibrium extent.
  • Weak electrolytes follow Ostwald dilution law.
  • Colligative properties verify theoretical dissociation values.
  • Dilution promotes dissociation for weak acids and bases.

⚖️ Key Differences

Degree of Dissociation (α) VS Dissociation Constant (K)

α is a variable fraction dependent on concentration, while K is a thermodynamic constant dependent only on temperature.

Question 1 of 18

What is the value of degree of dissociation (α) for a substance that does not dissociate at all?

Explanation: When no dissociation occurs, the number of dissociated moles is zero, making α = 0.

Question 2 of 18

According to Ostwald's dilution law, the degree of dissociation of a weak electrolyte is:

Explanation: For weak electrolytes, α = √(K/c), showing that α is inversely proportional to the square root of concentration.

Question 3 of 18

For the dissociation of binary electrolyte AB ⇌ A+ + B-, the total number of moles at equilibrium is:

Explanation: Initial moles = 1, equilibrium moles = 1 - α + α + α = 1 + α (since ν = 2).

Question 4 of 18

What is the Van't Hoff factor (i) for a completely dissociated ternary electrolyte (e.g., CaCl2)?

Explanation: CaCl2 dissociates into 3 ions (Ca2+ and 2Cl-). For complete dissociation (α = 1), i = ν = 3.

Question 5 of 18

How does dilution affect the degree of dissociation of a weak acid?

Explanation: Dilution shifts the equilibrium forward according to Le Chatelier's principle, increasing the degree of dissociation.

Question 6 of 18

The relationship between vapor density (D) and experimental molar mass (M) is:

Explanation: Molar mass is twice the vapor density for any gas or vapor.

Question 7 of 18

For a reaction PCl5 ⇌ PCl3 + Cl2, if α is the degree of dissociation, the mole fraction of PCl3 at equilibrium is:

Explanation: Total moles = 1 + α. Moles of PCl3 = α. Mole fraction = α / (1 + α).

Question 8 of 18

What is the upper limit of the degree of dissociation (α) for any reaction?

Explanation: Degree of dissociation represents a fraction of the total moles, with a maximum value of 1 (100%).

Question 9 of 18

Which of the following expressions correctly relates the Van't Hoff factor (i) and degree of dissociation (α) for a general electrolyte dissociating into ν ions?

Explanation: The standard relation is derived from equilibrium moles divided by initial moles: i = [1 + (ν - 1)α] / 1.

Question 10 of 18

Ostwald's dilution law fails in the case of strong electrolytes because:

Explanation: Strong electrolytes possess an effective α close to 1 across operational concentrations, rendering the mass action equilibrium ratio inapplicable.

Question 11 of 18

If the degree of dissociation of a weak monobasic acid is 0.04 in 0.01 M solution, its dissociation constant Ka is:

Explanation: Ka = cα2 / (1 - α) ≈ cα2 = 0.01 × (0.04)2 = 10-2 × 1.6 × 10-3 = 1.6 × 10-5.

Question 12 of 18

What is the apparent molar mass of a substance undergoing complete dissociation when its normal molar mass is M?

Explanation: Apparent molar mass is normal molar mass divided by the Van't Hoff factor (which equals ν at complete dissociation).

Question 13 of 18

In the dissociation of N2O4 ⇌ 2NO2, let initial moles be 1 and degree of dissociation be α. The total moles at equilibrium are:

Explanation: N2O4 yields 2 moles of NO2, so ν = 2. Total moles = 1 + (ν-1)α = 1 + α.

Question 14 of 18

Which factor affects the numerical value of the degree of dissociation for a weak electrolyte?

Explanation: Equilibrium constants and consequently degrees of dissociation change with temperature based on reaction enthalpy.

Question 15 of 18

If vapor density of equilibrium mixture of N2O4 and NO2 is D and initial vapor density of N2O4 is d, then α is given by:

Explanation: Using M = 2D and m = 2d, α = (D - d) / [d(ν-1)]. Since ν = 2, α = (D - d)/d.

Question 16 of 18

For a weak acid HA, if the concentration is halved, the degree of dissociation will:

Explanation: Since α ∝ 1/√c, halving concentration increases α by a factor of 1/√(1/2) = √2.

Question 17 of 18

Which of the following represents the correct unit for the degree of dissociation?

Explanation: Degree of dissociation is a ratio of moles to moles, making it dimensionless.

Question 18 of 18

When acetic acid is dissolved in benzene, the Van't Hoff factor i is found to be approximately 0.5. This indicates:

Explanation: An i value less than 1 indicates association, specifically dimerization into (CH3COOH)2 in non-polar solvents.

Question 1 of 10
Calculate the degree of dissociation of a 0.02 M weak acid solution having an ionization constant Ka = 2 × 10-5.

Solution: α = √(Ka / c) = √(2 × 10-5 / 0.02) = √(10-3) = √0.001 ≈ 0.0316.

Question 2 of 10
What is the Van't Hoff factor for BaCl2 at 100% dissociation?

Solution: BaCl2 dissociates into one Ba2+ and two Cl- ions, totaling 3 ions (ν = 3).

Question 3 of 10
In the dissociation of PCl5 into PCl3 and Cl2, if α is 0.5, the fractional amount of PCl5 dissociated is:

Solution: A degree of dissociation of 0.5 directly corresponds to 50% dissociation of the initial reactant.

Question 4 of 10
Which of the following equations correctly relates apparent molar mass (M) and normal molar mass (M0) for an electrolyte dissociating into ν ions with degree of dissociation α?

Solution: Since i = M0 / M and i = 1 + (ν - 1)α, M0 / M = 1 + (ν - 1)α.

Question 5 of 10
If the concentration of a weak acid is decreased from 0.1 M to 0.001 M, its degree of dissociation will increase by a factor of:

Solution: α ∝ 1/√c. The ratio of concentrations is 0.1 / 0.001 = 100. The square root of 100 is 10.

Question 6 of 10
For a binary weak electrolyte, if α << 1, Ostwald's dilution law expression K = cα2 / (1 - α) simplifies to:

Solution: When α << 1, (1 - α) ≈ 1, so K = cα2, which rearranges to α = √(K / c).

Question 7 of 10
What is the total number of moles of gas present at equilibrium when 1 mole of N2O4 is dissociated to an extent α?

Solution: N2O4(g) ⇌ 2NO2(g). Total moles = 1 - α + 2α = 1 + α.

Question 8 of 10
The freezing point depression of a 0.1 m aq. solution of a weak acid HA is measured. If i = 1.05, what is the degree of dissociation?

Solution: For a monobasic acid, ν = 2. i = 1 + (ν - 1)α ⇒ 1.05 = 1 + (2 - 1)α ⇒ α = 0.05.

Question 9 of 10
Which of the following compounds has the highest degree of dissociation in aqueous solution at equal concentration?

Solution: HCl is a strong mineral acid and exhibits complete dissociation (α ≈ 1), unlike the other weak acids.

Question 10 of 10
How does an increase in pressure affect the degree of dissociation of PCl5 in the equilibrium PCl5 ⇌ PCl3 + Cl2?

Solution: The forward reaction involves an increase in total moles of gas (ν = 2 vs 1). Increased pressure shifts equilibrium backward per Le Chatelier's principle, decreasing α.
