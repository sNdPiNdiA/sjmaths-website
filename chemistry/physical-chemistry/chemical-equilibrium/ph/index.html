Physical Chemistry

pH and Ionic Equilibrium

The pH scale quantifies hydrogen ion activity in aqueous solutions as the negative decadic logarithm of hydronium ion concentration. Ionic equilibrium governs the dissociation of weak acids and bases, establishing dynamic equilibrium constants such as Ka, Kb, and Kw.

UP PGT Chemistry Subject Code 02 B.Sc. / M.Sc. Level

Acid-Base Concepts and Self-Ionization of Water

  • The self-ionization of water is an endothermic process, causing Kw to increase as temperature rises.
  • Neutrality at elevated temperatures does not correspond to pH 7.0; at 37°C, neutral pH is approximately 6.81 because Kw > 10-14.
  • According to the Arrhenius definition, acids yield H+ ions while bases yield OH- ions in aqueous solution.
  • The Brønsted-Lowry theory defines acids as proton donors and bases as proton acceptors, creating conjugate acid-base pairs.

Buffer Solutions and Hydrolysis of Salts

  • A buffer solution resists changes in pH upon the addition of small amounts of strong acid or strong base.
  • Salts of weak acids and strong bases undergo anionic hydrolysis, resulting in an alkaline aqueous solution where pH > 7.
  • The buffer capacity is maximal when the concentration of the weak acid equals the concentration of its conjugate base ([Acid] = [Salt]).
  • Salts derived from weak acids and weak bases yield an acidic, neutral, or basic solution depending on whether Ka > Kb, Ka = Kb, or Ka < Kb.

Polyprotic Acids and Solubility Product

  • For polyprotic acids like H3PO4, successive dissociation constants decrease significantly (Ka1 >> Ka2 >> Ka3).
  • The pH of a polyprotic acid solution is predominantly determined by the first dissociation step due to the magnitude of Ka1.
  • Precipitation of a sparingly soluble salt occurs when the ionic product (IP) exceeds the solubility product constant (Ksp).
  • The presence of a common ion suppresses the dissociation of weak electrolytes and reduces the solubility of sparingly soluble salts.

⚠️ Important Exceptions & Anomalies
Exception pH of water is always 7 at any temperature.

Observation: pH of pure neutral water varies with temperature. — Self-ionization of water is endothermic (ΔH > 0); increasing temperature shifts equilibrium right, increasing [H+] and lowering neutral pH.

Exception Strong acids always give pH = -log[Acid].

Observation: Extremely dilute strong acid solutions (e.g., 10-8 M HCl) do not yield acidic pH = 8. — Contribution of H+ ions from the auto-ionization of water becomes significant and must be accounted for.

Exception Salts of strong acids and strong bases form neutral solutions.

Observation: Certain salts show slight hydrolysis at high concentrations due to ionic strength effects. — Activity coefficients deviate from unity in concentrated ionic solutions, altering effective hydronium ion concentrations.

💡 Shortcuts & Memory Aids
Logarithm Approximation Shortcut
If [H+] = a × 10-b, then pH ≈ b - log(a)

Application: Quickly estimate pH without a calculator (e.g., if [H+] = 2 × 10-4, pH = 4 - 0.3 = 3.7).

Buffer pH Shortcut for Equal Moles
pH = pKa

Application: When salt and acid concentrations are equal, log(1) = 0, so pH directly equals pKa.

AB Type Salt Solubility Shortcut
S = √(Ksp)

Application: For binary sparingly soluble salts like AgCl or BaSO4, solubility is simply the square root of Ksp.

🎯 Key Exam Points
  • The ionic product of water (Kw) at 298 K is exactly 1.0 × 10-14 mol2 L-2.
  • A buffer zone is generally operational within the pH range of pKa ± 1.
  • Hydrolysis constant (Kh) for a salt of weak acid and strong base is given by Kw / Ka.
  • The degree of hydrolysis (&h) for a salt of weak acid and weak base is independent of concentration.
  • Ostwald's dilution law applies to weak electrolytes where degree of dissociation α << 1.
  • The pH of a 0.01 M NaOH solution is 12 at 298 K.
🚫 Common Errors & Confusions
  • Forgetting that pH scale is logarithmic; diluting an acid by a factor of 10 increases pH by only 1 unit.
  • Assuming neutral water always has a pH of 7 regardless of temperature.
  • Using total molarity instead of active species concentration when calculating pH of concentrated solutions.
  • Confusing Ka with pKa values in buffer calculations, leading to inverted ratios.

⚡ Quick Revision: pH and Ionic Equilibrium

Core formulas, rapid recall points, and distinguishing criteria at a glance.

Logarithmic pH Scale

Defines acidity via hydronium ion concentration using negative base-10 logarithms.

Buffer Action

Maintains stable pH via conjugate acid-base pairs consuming added H+ or OH-.

Salt Hydrolysis

Interaction of salt ions with water molecules, producing acidic or basic aqueous environments.

Solubility Product Principle

Equilibrium constant governing the saturation limits of sparingly soluble ionic precipitates.

📚 Terms & Units Glossary

pH (pH = -log[H+])
Measure of acidity or basicity of an aqueous solution.
Kw (Kw = [H+][OH-])
Ionic product of water, equal to 10-14 at 298 K.
Buffer Capacity (β = dn / d(pH))
Ability of a buffer solution to resist pH change.
Ksp (Solubility Product)
Equilibrium constant for dissolution of a solid ionic compound.
Degree of Dissociation (α = ndiss / ntotal)
Fraction of total electrolyte molecules dissociated into ions.
Hydrolysis Constant (Kh)
Equilibrium constant for reaction of salt ions with water.

🎯 Key Takeaways

  • pH + pOH = 14 at 298 K
  • Ka × Kb = Kw for conjugate acid-base pairs
  • pH of strong acid = -log[H+]
  • pH of strong base = 14 + log[OH-]
  • Henderson equation: pH = pKa + log([Salt]/[Acid])

📌 Quick Recall

  • pH scale runs from 0 to 14 in dilute aqueous solutions at standard conditions.
  • Buffers consist of weak acids/bases and their respective salts.
  • Temperature changes alter the value of Kw and neutral pH.
  • Common ion effect reduces dissociation and solubility.

⚖️ Key Differences

Equivalence Point VS End Point

Equivalence point is the stoichiometric point of reaction, whereas end point is where the indicator changes color.

Question 1 of 18

What is the pH of a 10-8 M HCl solution at 298 K?

Explanation: Due to the contribution of H+ from water autoionization, the total [H+] = 10-8 + 10-7 ≈ 1.1 × 10-7 M, yielding a pH of approximately 6.98.

Question 2 of 18

Which of the following mixtures forms an acidic buffer?

Explanation: An acidic buffer consists of a weak acid (CH3COOH) and its salt with a strong base (CH3COONa).

Question 3 of 18

What is the pOH of a solution with [OH-] = 1.0 × 10-3 M?

Explanation: pOH = -log[OH-] = -log(1.0 × 10-3) = 3.

Question 4 of 18

How does an increase in temperature affect the pH of pure water?

Explanation: Self-ionization of water is endothermic. Higher temperature increases Kw, raising [H+] and thus lowering the pH.

Question 5 of 18

What is the conjugate base of H2PO4-?

Explanation: A conjugate base is formed by removing a proton (H+). Removing H+ from H2PO4- yields HPO42-.

Question 6 of 18

What is the pH of a 0.001 M NaOH solution?

Explanation: [OH-] = 10-3 M, so pOH = 3. Therefore, pH = 14 - 3 = 11.

Question 7 of 18

Which salt solution will be alkaline in water?

Explanation: CH3COONa is a salt of a weak acid and strong base; acetate ion undergoes hydrolysis to produce OH-.

Question 8 of 18

Ostwald's dilution law is applicable to:

Explanation: Ostwald's dilution law relates degree of dissociation to the dissociation constant specifically for weak electrolytes.

Question 9 of 18

What is the pH of a solution where [H+] = 2 × 10-5 M? (log 2 = 0.30)

Explanation: pH = -log(2 × 10-5) = 5 - log 2 = 5 - 0.30 = 4.7.

Question 10 of 18

If Ka of a weak acid is 1.8 × 10-5, what is its pKa?

Explanation: pKa = -log(1.8 × 10-5) = 5 - log(1.8) = 5 - 0.26 = 4.74.

Question 11 of 18

Which indicator is suitable for titrating a strong acid against a weak base?

Explanation: A strong acid vs weak base titration has an acidic equivalence point, making methyl orange (pH range 3.1-4.4) ideal.

Question 12 of 18

The solubility product of AB2 is expressed as:

Explanation: AB2 dissociates into A2+ and 2B-. Thus, Ksp = [A2+][B-]2.

Question 13 of 18

What happens to the solubility of AgCl when NaCl is added to the solution?

Explanation: Addition of NaCl introduces common Cl- ions, shifting the solubility equilibrium of AgCl to the left (common ion effect).

Question 14 of 18

Buffer capacity is maximum when the ratio of salt to acid is:

Explanation: When [Salt] = [Acid], log(1) = 0, and the buffer has equal resistance to both added acid and base.

Question 15 of 18

What is the pH of 0.05 M H2SO4 assuming complete dissociation?

Explanation: [H+] = 2 × 0.05 = 0.1 M. pH = -log(0.1) = 1.0.

Question 16 of 18

Which species acts both as a Brønsted acid and a Brønsted base?

Explanation: HCO3- can donate a proton to form CO32- or accept a proton to form H2CO3.

Question 17 of 18

What is the unit of the ionic product of water (Kw)?

Explanation: Kw = [H+][OH-], so its units are (mol L-1) × (mol L-1) = mol2 L-2.

Question 18 of 18

Which of the following is a Lewis acid?

Explanation: BF3 has an incomplete octet on boron, enabling it to accept an electron pair (Lewis acid).

Question 1 of 10
What is the pH of a mixture containing 50 mL of 0.1 M HCl and 50 mL of 0.2 M NaOH?

Solution: Moles of H+ = 5 mmol; moles of OH- = 10 mmol. Excess OH- = 5 mmol in 100 mL total volume = 0.05 M. pOH = -log(0.05) = 1.3. pH = 14 - 1.3 = 12.7.

Question 2 of 10
Which of the following salts undergoes cationic hydrolysis?

Solution: NH4Cl is derived from a strong acid (HCl) and a weak base (NH4OH); the ammonium cation undergoes hydrolysis.

Question 3 of 10
The solubility of Ag2CrO4 is s mol L^-1. What is its solubility product?

Solution: Ag2CrO4 → 2Ag+ + CrO42-. Ksp = (2s)2(s) = 4s3.

Question 4 of 10
What is the pH of a 0.01 M solution of acetic acid (Ka = 1.6 × 10-5)?

Solution: [H+] = √(Ka × C) = √(1.6 × 10-5 × 10-2) = √(1.6 × 10-7) ≈ 4 × 10-4. pH = 4 - log(4) = 4 - 0.6 = 3.4.

Question 5 of 10
Which indicator is suitable for strong acid-strong base titrations?

Solution: The pH jump at the equivalence point of a strong acid-strong base titration is very sharp (pH 4 to 10), so both methyl orange and phenolphthalein work.

Question 6 of 10
If the pKa of a weak acid is 4.8, what is the pH of a buffer with equal concentrations of acid and salt?

Solution: When [Salt] = [Acid], pH = pKa = 4.8.

Question 7 of 10
What is the hydronium ion concentration of a solution with pH = 5.3?

Solution: pH = 5.3 → [H+] = 10-5.3 = 10-6 + 0.7 = antilog(0.7) × 10-6 ≈ 5.0 × 10-6 M.

Question 8 of 10
What is the degree of hydrolysis of 0.1 M CH3COONa given Ka = 1.0 × 10-5 and Kw = 1.0 × 10-14?

Solution: h = √(Kw / (Ka × C)) = √(10-14 / (10-5 × 0.1)) = √(10-8) = 10-4.

Question 9 of 10
Which property is characteristic of a buffer solution?

Solution: Diluting a buffer solution changes the ratio of salt to acid/base by the same factor, so the pH remains unchanged.

Question 10 of 10
What is the pOH of 0.02 M Ba(OH)2 assuming complete dissociation?

Solution: [OH-] = 2 × 0.02 = 0.04 M. pOH = -log(0.04) = 2 - log(4) = 2 - 0.6 = 1.4.
