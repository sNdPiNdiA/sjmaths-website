Physical Chemistry

Abnormal Molecular Weight

Abnormal molecular weights occur when solutes undergo association or dissociation in solution, resulting in colligative property values that differ from expected normal values. The van 't Hoff factor corrects for these deviations by quantifying the actual number of particles in solution relative to the number of formula units dissolved.

UP PGT Chemistry Subject Code 02 B.Sc. / M.Sc. Level

Colligative Properties and Particle Count Deviations

  • Colligative properties depend strictly on the total number of solute particles rather than their chemical identity.
  • Electrolytes exhibit higher colligative properties than non-electrolytes at the same molar concentration due to dissociation into multiple ions.
  • The van 't Hoff factor i directly relates observed colligative property values to theoretical values calculated assuming no dissociation or association.

Solute Dissociation and Molecular Weight Depression

  • When strong electrolytes like NaCl or K3[Fe(CN)6] dissociate, the number of particles increases in solution.
  • An increased particle count causes an artificially high depression in freezing point and elevation in boiling point, yielding an abnormally low molecular weight.
  • The theoretical maximum limit of i equals the total number of ions n produced per formula unit at infinite dilution.

Solute Association and Molecular Weight Elevation

  • Carboxylic acids such as acetic acid undergo dimerization in non-polar solvents like benzene through intermolecular hydrogen bonding.
  • Association reduces the total number of kinetic entities, leading to lower colligative property values than expected.
  • Reduced colligative property magnitude results in an experimentally determined molecular weight that is abnormally high (e.g., double the formula mass for a complete dimer).
📐 Key Formulas & Equations
Van 't Hoff Factor via Molecular Weight
i = Mnormal / Mabnormal
Conditions: Applied when measured molar mass deviates from the true formula mass
Units: Dimensionless
Degree of Dissociation (α)
α = (i - 1) / (n - 1)
Conditions: For a solute molecule yielding n ions upon complete dissociation
Units: Dimensionless (0 ≤ α ≤ 1)
Degree of Association (α)
α = (1 - i) / (1 - (1 / n))
Conditions: For n molecules associating to form an aggregate of size n
Units: Dimensionless (0 ≤ α ≤ 1)
Modified Osmotic Pressure Equation
π = i · C · R · T
Conditions: Non-ideal solutions undergoing association or dissociation
Units: π in atm, C in mol L-1, R in L atm K-1 mol-1, T in K
Van 't Hoff Factor (i) via Colligative Properties
i = πobserved / πcalculated = ΔTf,observed / ΔTf,calculated
Conditions: Valid for dilute ideal solutions
Units: Dimensionless
⚠️ Important Exceptions & Anomalies
Exception Strong electrolytes fully dissociate at all concentrations, giving integer van 't Hoff factors.

Observation: Incomplete dissociation at higher concentrations due to interionic attraction. — Electrostatic forces of attraction between oppositely charged ions form ion pairs, effectively reducing the active particle count.

Exception Acetic acid exhibits a van 't Hoff factor of 1 in aqueous solutions.

Observation: In benzene, acetic acid exhibits an 'i' value approaching 0.5. — Non-polar solvents favor extensive hydrogen-bonded dimer formation, whereas polar water molecules solvate individual acetate and hydronium ions.

Exception Colligative properties scale linearly with molality for all solutes.

Observation: Deviations occur rapidly in solutions exceeding 0.1 m for ionic solutes. — Debye-Hückel limiting law effects and non-ideal thermodynamic activities override simple concentration proportionality.

💡 Shortcuts & Memory Aids
Quick Ion Count Shortcut
i ≈ n (assuming 100% dissociation)

Application: For strong electrolytes like Al2(SO4)3, set n = 2 + 3 = 5 to immediately calculate colligative shifts.

Association vs Dissociation Mnemonic
Dissociation → i > 1, Mobs < Mtheo | Association → i < 1, Mobs > Mtheo

Application: Instantly eliminate incorrect multiple-choice options by checking if molar mass moves opposite to particle count.

Dimerization Shortcut
For 100% dimerization, i = 0.5

Application: Saves calculation time when evaluating solutes forming simple dimers in non-polar media.

🎯 Key Exam Points
  • The van 't Hoff factor is mathematically defined as normal molecular mass divided by abnormal molecular mass.
  • For binary electrolytes completely dissociated in water, the theoretical van 't Hoff factor equals 2.
  • Benzoic acid dissolved in benzene shows an abnormal molecular weight of approximately 244 g/mol due to dimerization.
  • Degree of dissociation (α) is calculated using the formula α = (i - 1) / (n - 1).
  • Non-electrolytes like glucose and urea always maintain a van 't Hoff factor of exactly 1.
  • Colligative properties are inversely proportional to the experimentally determined molecular weight of the solute.
🚫 Common Errors & Confusions
  • Assuming degree of dissociation α equals the van 't Hoff factor i without accounting for ion stoichiometry n.
  • Forgetting that association lowers colligative property values while increasing the apparent molecular weight.
  • Applying ideal dilute solution equations directly to concentrated ionic solutions without activity coefficient corrections.
  • Confusing the number of ions produced per molecule (n) with the stoichiometric coefficient in a balanced chemical equation.

⚡ Quick Revision: Abnormal Molecular Weight

Core formulas, rapid recall points, and distinguishing criteria at a glance.

Van 't Hoff Factor Foundation

The van 't Hoff factor (i) corrects colligative property equations for solute association or dissociation.

Dissociation Mechanics

Electrolytes break into multiple ions, increasing particle count, lowering observed molecular weight, and making i > 1.

Association Mechanics

Solutes like carboxylic acids form dimers or polymers, decreasing particle count, raising observed molecular weight, and making i < 1.

Quantitative Calculations

Degrees of dissociation and association are directly extracted from measured colligative property deviations via i.

📚 Terms & Units Glossary

Van 't Hoff Factor (i)
Ratio of observed colligative property to calculated normal colligative property.
Degree of Dissociation (α (Dissociation))
Fraction of total moles of electrolyte that dissociate into ions.
Degree of Association (α (Association))
Fraction of total moles of solute that associate to form larger aggregates.
Normal Molecular Weight (Mnormal)
Calculated molar mass from the chemical formula (g mol-1).
Abnormal Molecular Weight (Mabnormal)
Experimentally determined molar mass from colligative properties (g mol-1).
Colligative Property (π, ΔTf, ΔTb, ΔP)
Properties depending solely on the number of solute particles.

🎯 Key Takeaways

  • i = Mnormal / Mabnormal
  • For dissociation: i = 1 + α(n - 1)
  • For association: i = 1 + α((1/n) - 1)
  • Non-electrolytes: i = 1, Mobs = Mtheo
  • Dimerization in benzene: i ≈ 0.5 for complete association

📌 Quick Recall

  • Association decreases particle count and increases molecular weight.
  • Dissociation increases particle count and decreases molecular weight.
  • The van 't Hoff factor unifies all colligative property corrections.
  • Degrees of dissociation and association range between 0 and 1.

⚖️ Key Differences

i > 1 VS i < 1

i > 1 indicates dissociation (more particles), whereas i < 1 indicates association (fewer particles).

Question 1 of 10
Calculate the apparent molecular mass of NaCl if its observed freezing point depression in water is twice that calculated for a non-electrolyte of the same molality.

Solution: Since i = 2, Mabnormal = Mnormal / 2, meaning the apparent molecular mass is half the actual mass.

Question 2 of 10
A 0.1 molal solution of acetic acid in benzene freezes at a temperature 0.2 °C lower than pure benzene. If Kf for benzene is 5.12 K kg mol-1, what is the state of acetic acid?

Solution: Calculated ΔTf = 0.1 × 5.12 = 0.512 K. Observed is 0.2 K, showing i < 1, confirming association/dimerization.

Question 3 of 10
Which electrolyte has the highest van 't Hoff factor at infinite dilution?

Solution: K4[Fe(CN)6] produces 5 ions upon complete dissociation, yielding the highest 'i' value among the choices.

Question 4 of 10
If the degree of dissociation of BaCl2 is 80%, what is the expected van 't Hoff factor?

Solution: For BaCl2, n = 3. i = 1 + α(n - 1) = 1 + 0.8(3 - 1) = 1 + 1.6 = 2.6.

Question 5 of 10
Why does benzoic acid show a molecular weight of 244 g/mol in benzene instead of the theoretical 122 g/mol?

Solution: Intermolecular hydrogen bonding in non-polar benzene causes two molecules of benzoic acid to associate into a dimer of mass 244 g/mol.

Question 6 of 10
Which of the following pairs will have the same van 't Hoff factor in dilute aqueous solution assuming 100% dissociation?

Solution: Both NaCl and KCl dissociate into 2 ions each, giving an ideal van 't Hoff factor of 2.

Question 7 of 10
In the equation ΔTf = i Kf m, the term 'm' represents:

Solution: Molality (m) is used in freezing point and boiling point elevation formulas because it is independent of temperature.

Question 8 of 10
If a solute has a van 't Hoff factor less than 1, which of the following is true regarding its colligative properties?

Solution: Association reduces particle count, causing colligative properties to be lower than expected for normal solutes.

Question 9 of 10
Determine the percentage dissociation of a dibasic acid (n = 3) whose 0.01 m aqueous solution has a van 't Hoff factor of 2.6.

Solution: α = (i - 1) / (n - 1) = (2.6 - 1) / (3 - 1) = 1.6 / 2 = 0.80 or 80%.

Question 10 of 10
Which factor directly governs the extent of abnormal molecular weight behavior in electrolyte solutions?

Solution: The extent of dissociation or association directly determines the particle concentration and resulting abnormal molecular weight.
