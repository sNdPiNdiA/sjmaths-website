Physical Geography — Biosphere

Ecology: Comprehensive Geographic Analysis, Physical & Human Mechanisms, and Spatial Distribution

High-yield conceptual breakdown and physical-human geographical foundations of Ecology tailored for UP TGT/PGT Geography and UGC-NET mastery.

UP PGT Geography UP TGT Social Science B.Sc. / M.Sc. Level

Ecology, as a core branch of Physical Geography within the Biosphere, examines the complex reciprocal relationships between organisms and their biotic/abiotic environments. Coined by Ernst Haeckel in 1869 ('Oekologie'), the discipline merges principles of geomorphology, climatology, and biogeography to explain spatial patterns of life. In the context of competitive examinations like UP PGT Geography and UGC-NET, ecology forms the foundation for understanding biome distribution, energy flow pyramids, succession dynamics, and anthropogenic alterations to ecosystems. Spatial variations in ecological systems are heavily dictated by macro-climatic gradients, edaphic factors, and topographic barriers, making spatial synthesis critical for exam success.

1. Fundamental Concepts and Ecosystem Structure

Ecosystem energetics, trophic dynamics, and the laws of thermodynamics governing biotic-abiotic interactions.

  • Ecosystem term coined by A.G. Tansley in 1935, representing a self-regulating functional unit of biosphere.
  • Biotic components include producers (autotrophs), consumers (heterotrophs), and decomposers (saprotrophs).
  • Abiotic components encompass climatic factors (solar radiation, temperature, precipitation) and edaphic factors (soil structure, pH, nutrients).
  • Lindeman's 10% Law: Only about 10% of energy is transferred from one trophic level to the next; 90% is lost as metabolic heat.
  • Ecological pyramids (Eltonian pyramids) of number, biomass, and energy can be upright or inverted depending on the biome.
Comparative Analysis: Unlike energy pyramids which are strictly upright due to thermodynamic laws, pyramids of numbers and biomass can be inverted (e.g., in aquatic ecosystems or single-tree ecosystems).
  • Energy flow is unidirectional (Sun to Producers to Consumers to Decomposers).
  • Nutrient cycling is cyclic/biogeochemical (Carbon, Nitrogen, Phosphorus cycles).

2. Biogeochemical Cycles and Nutrient Dynamics

The circulation of chemical elements between living organisms and their geological environment.

  • Gaseous cycles include Nitrogen, Carbon, and Oxygen, characterized by atmospheric reservoirs.
  • Sedimentary cycles include Phosphorus, Calcium, and Sulphur, where the Earth's crust is the primary reservoir.
  • Nitrogen Fixation converts inert atmospheric N2 into ammonia via Rhizobium bacteria, lightning, and industrial Haber-Bosch process.
  • Carbon cycle operates via photosynthesis, respiration, oceanic absorption, and lithospheric fossilization.
  • Eutrophication occurs due to excessive nutrient loading (Nitrates and Phosphates), causing algal blooms and oxygen depletion.
Comparative Analysis: Gaseous cycles have a swift turnover rate and global buffer capacity compared to sedimentary cycles, which are slower and prone to localized depletion.
  • Nutrients are conserved and recycled endlessly within the biosphere.
  • Anthropogenic interventions severely disrupt natural carbon and nitrogen stoichiometric ratios.

3. Ecological Succession and Biome Distribution

Directional change in ecosystem structure over time culminating in a stable climax community.

  • Primary succession begins on lifeless substrates (e.g., bare rock, cooled lava) starting with pioneer species like lichens.
  • Secondary succession occurs in areas where natural vegetation has been removed but soil remains (e.g., abandoned agricultural fields, post-wildfire forests).
  • Clementsian Monoclimax theory emphasizes climate as the sole determining factor of the climax community.
  • Polyclimax theory (Tansley) argues that multiple factors including soil, topography, and fire determine local climax states.
  • Major global biomes (Tundra, Taiga, Temperate Deciduous, Tropical Rainforest, Desert, Savanna) are dictated by temperature and precipitation gradients (Whittaker's Biome Classification).
Comparative Analysis: Primary succession takes millennia due to soil formation processes, whereas secondary succession is rapid because an organic soil base already exists.
  • Succession increases species diversity and biomass up to the climax stage.
  • Whittaker's diagram using moisture and temperature is a high-frequency question area in UGC-NET.

4. Applied Ecology and Environmental Geography

Human-environment interactions, biodiversity conservation, and ecological footprints.

  • Biodiversity hotspots concept introduced by Norman Myers (1988); requires >1,500 endemic vascular plants and <30% primary vegetation remaining.
  • In-situ conservation includes National Parks, Wildlife Sanctuaries, and Biosphere Reserves (e.g., Nilgiri, Nanda Devi).
  • Ex-situ conservation includes botanical gardens, gene banks, seed vaults, and zoological parks.
  • Ecological Niche (Grinnellian vs. Eltonian) defines an organism's functional role and space within a community.
  • Edge effect and Ecotone: Transitional zones between two biomes exhibit high species richness and density.
Comparative Analysis: In-situ conservation preserves species within their natural habitats allowing evolutionary processes, whereas ex-situ safeguards endangered stock artificially.
  • India hosts 4 out of 36 global biodiversity hotspots (Himalaya, Indo-Burma, Western Ghats, Sundaland).
  • Ecotones feature high biodiversity known as the edge effect.

Spatial Patterns & Regional Distribution

🌐 Global Geographic Belts & Spatial Variation

Global ecological zones follow latitudinal and altitudinal thermal belts. Tropical Rainforests dominate equatorial lowlands (Amazon, Congo, Southeast Asia) characterized by high insolation and year-round precipitation. Boreal forests (Taiga) form a circumpolar coniferous belt across North America and Eurasia. Tundra zones occupy high-latitude Arctic margins featuring permafrost and low-stature dwarf vegetation. Deserts align with subtropical high-pressure descending air masses (Horse Latitudes) around 30° N/S latitudes, alongside rain-shadow continental interiors.

🇮🇳 Indian Subcontinent Context & Regional Corridors

India exhibits remarkable ecological diversity divided into 10 biogeographic zones by Wildlife Institute of India (WII). These range from the Trans-Himalayan cold desert and Himalayan highlands to the Gangetic Plains, Western Ghats, semi-arid Deccan peninsula, and coastal mangrove ecosystems like the Sundarbans. The Western Ghats and North-East Himalayan regions stand out as global biodiversity hotspots experiencing high endemism and intense anthropogenic pressure.

📐 Key Geographic Formulas, Scales & Classifications
Lindeman's Efficiency (Trophic Transfer Efficiency)
En = (I_n / I_n-1) * 100
Parameters: En = Ecological efficiency at trophic level n; I_n = Energy ingestion at trophic level n; I_n-1 = Energy ingestion at previous trophic level n-1. Typically averages 10%.
Exam Focus: Frequently tested in UGC-NET numerical and conceptual matching questions regarding energy loss across food chains.
Simpson's Diversity Index (D)
D = 1 - sum((n / N)^2)
Parameters: n = total number of organisms of a particular species; N = total number of organisms of all species. Varies between 0 (low diversity) and 1 (high diversity).
Exam Focus: Appears in PGT/NET questions related to quantitative biogeography and community ecology metrics.

Mnemonics, Memory Shortcuts & Exam Traps

💡 India's 4 Biodiversity Hotspots
HIWS (Himalaya, Indo-Burma, Western Ghats, Sundaland)

H = Himalaya, I = Indo-Burma, W = Western Ghats, S = Sundaland. Remembers all four biodiversity hotspots located partially or wholly within India.

⚠️ Exam Trap: Examiners often add 'Eastern Ghats' as a distractor trap; remember Eastern Ghats is NOT a biodiversity hotspot.
💡 Ecological Succession Stages
P-S-S-C (Pioneers Start Succession Climax)

P = Pioneer community, S = Seral stages (seres), S = Sub-climax, C = Climax community.

⚠️ Exam Trap: Do not confuse primary succession pioneers (lichens/mosses on bare rock) with secondary succession pioneers (hardy annual weeds on pre-existing soil).

📚 High-Yield Geographic Terminology & Definitions

Ecology UP PGT/UGC-NET Fundamental

The scientific study of the interactions between organisms and their physical and biotic environment, a term coined by Ernst Haeckel in 1869.

Ecosystem UP TGT Core

A self-sustaining functional unit comprising all living organisms (biotic community) interacting with each other and their non-living (abiotic) environment.

Ecological Niche UGC-NET Conceptual

The functional role and position of a species in its ecosystem, including all its interactions with the biotic and abiotic factors.

Biotic Succession UP PGT Geography

The progressive directional change in the composition of an ecological community over time, culminating in a stable climax community.

Trophic Level UP TGT Basic

The specific step or position in the food chain or food web occupied by an organism based on its primary source of nutrition.

Biomagnification UGC-NET Environment

The process whereby the concentration of toxic substances (like heavy metals or persistent organic pollutants) increases at each successive trophic level.

Ecotone UP PGT Frequently Asked

A transitional zone or boundary area between two or more diverse ecological communities, often exhibiting high biodiversity and edge effect.

Biizing / Biomass UGC-NET Quantitative

The total dry weight of living organic matter present in an ecosystem at a specific time, usually expressed per unit area.

Climax Community UP TGT Standard

The final, stable, self-perpetuating stage of ecological succession that is in equilibrium with the prevailing regional climate.

Edaphic Factor UP PGT Biogeography

Abiotic environmental factors relating to the physical and chemical characteristics of soil that influence plant and animal distribution.

Ecological Footprint UGC-NET Modern Trends

A measure of human demand on the Earth's ecosystems, comparing human consumption of natural resources with Earth's ecological capacity to regenerate.

Autecology UP PGT Terminology

The study of the ecological relationships between an individual species or population and its environment.

Synecology UP PGT Terminology

The study of groups of organisms (communities or ecosystems) in relation to their environment.

Homeostasis UGC-NET Core

The self-regulating property of an ecosystem to maintain internal stability and resistance against external disturbances.

🧭 Prescribed Scholars, Laws & Spatial Models

Lindeman's Ten Percent Law (1942)

Only about 10 percent of the energy is transferred from one trophic level to the next higher level, while the remaining 90 percent is lost as heat during respiration and metabolic processes.

Gause's Competitive Exclusion Principle (1934)

Two species competing for the exact same limited resources cannot coexist indefinitely if other ecological factors remain constant; one will outcompete the other.

Liebig's Law of the Minimum (1840)

Growth is dictated not by total resources available, but by the scarcest resource, known as the limiting factor.

Shelford's Law of Tolerance (1913)

The presence and success of an organism depend upon the set of conditions where each environmental factor has both minimum and maximum limits, known as tolerance limits.

Clements' Monocimax Theory of Succession (1916)

Ecological succession is a deterministic, highly predictable process driven entirely by climate, culminating in a single climatic climax community for any given region.

Instant evaluation with detailed spatial & physical explanations for each question.

Question 1 of 19
Who among the following coined the term 'Ecology' (Ökologie)?

Rationale: Ernst Haeckel coined the term 'Ökologie' in 1869 to describe the economy of living organisms and their relationships with organic and inorganic environments.

Question 2 of 19
The term 'Ecosystem' was first proposed by which ecologist?

Rationale: Sir Arthur George Tansley introduced the term 'ecosystem' in 1935 to denote the foundational interacting system of biotic and abiotic components.

Question 3 of 19
Which of the following ecological pyramids is always upright in any natural ecosystem?

Rationale: The pyramid of energy is always upright because energy flows unidirectionally and undergoes continuous dissipation according to the laws of thermodynamics, with energy decreasing at successive trophic levels.

Question 4 of 19
An inverted pyramid of biomass is typically observed in which of the following ecosystems?

Rationale: In an open aquatic ecosystem, the biomass of phytoplankton (primary producers) is often lower than that of zooplankton (primary consumers) at any given moment, resulting in an inverted pyramid of biomass.

Question 5 of 19
What is an 'Ecotone'?

Rationale: An ecotone is a transition area between two adjacent, different ecological communities, such as the boundary between a forest and a grassland.

Question 6 of 19
The 'Edge Effect' in ecology refers to:

Rationale: Edge effect refers to the tendency for a greater number of species and higher population density to be found in an ecotone than in either of the bordering ecosystems.

Question 7 of 19
According to Lindeman's Ten Percent Law, what percentage of energy is lost as heat between successive trophic levels?

Rationale: Only about 10% of energy is passed to the next trophic level, which implies that 90% of the energy is lost during metabolic processes and respiration.

Question 8 of 19
Gause's Principle of Competitive Exclusion states that:

Rationale: Gause's principle dictates that complete competitors cannot coexist; one species will outcompete and eliminate the other from that niche.

Question 9 of 19
The sequential development of biotic communities in a previously barren and sterile area (like bare rock) is called:

Rationale: Primary succession starts in lifeless areas such as regions lacking soil or where soil is incapable of sustaining life (e.g., lava flows, newly formed sand dunes, or bare rocks).

Question 10 of 19
Which of the following is an example of secondary ecological succession?

Rationale: Secondary succession occurs on pre-existing soil where an existing community has been removed by disturbance, such as a forest fire, flood, or abandoned farm.

Question 11 of 19
Liebig's Law of the Minimum emphasizes that:

Rationale: Liebig stated that survival and growth depend on the nutrient or factor available in minimum quantity relative to demand.

Question 12 of 19
Which biogeochemical cycle is sedimentary and does not have a significant atmospheric phase?

Rationale: Phosphorus is a sedimentary cycle because its reservoir pool is primarily in Earth's crust and rocks, lacking a gaseous atmospheric phase.

Question 13 of 19
The gradual accumulation of chemical pollutants in living organisms up the food chain is known as:

Rationale: Biomagnification (or biological magnification) refers to the amplification of toxin concentration at each higher trophic level.

Question 14 of 19
The term 'Biocoenosis' was coined by Karl Möbius to describe:

Rationale: Karl Möbius used 'biocoenosis' (1877) to describe interacting living communities inhabiting a uniform habitat.

Question 15 of 19
Which type of species acts as a crucial indicator of environmental health and pollution levels, such as lichens?

Rationale: Indicator species, such as lichens sensitive to sulfur dioxide, reflect the specific environmental conditions or pollution status of an ecosystem.

Question 16 of 19
A species whose presence and impact on an ecosystem is disproportionately large relative to its abundance is called a:

Rationale: Keystone species exert a strong control on community structure; their removal causes drastic structural collapse in the ecosystem.

Question 17 of 19
What does 'Autecology' focus on?

Rationale: Autecology is the study of individual species in relation to their environment, unlike synecology which studies entire communities.

Question 18 of 19
The process of nutrient enrichment in aquatic ecosystems leading to algal blooms and oxygen depletion is called:

Rationale: Eutrophication is nutrient enrichment (typically by nitrates and phosphates) causing excessive plant and algal growth, eventually leading to oxygen depletion (hypoxia).

Question 19 of 19
Which pioneer species typically initiates primary succession on bare rock surfaces?

Rationale: Lichens secrete acids that weather bare rock surfaces, initiating soil formation and paving the way for mosses and higher plants in primary succession.

Who postulated the concept of 'Climax Community' as being solely determined by regional climate (Monocimax theory)?

Official Benchmark Explanation: F.E. Clements (1916) propounded the monocimax theory, asserting that climate is the master determinant and all successional sequences in a region converge toward a single climatic climax.

Match List-I (Law/Concept) with List-II (Proponent): (a) Competitive Exclusion Principle - 1. E. Suess (b) Ten Percent Law - 2. G.F. Gause (c) Biosphere Concept - 3. R.L. Lindeman (d) Ecosystem Term - 4. A.G. Tansley Select the correct code:

Official Benchmark Explanation: G.F. Gause proposed Competitive Exclusion (1934); R.L. Lindeman gave the 10% energy law (1942); Eduard Suess coined/popularized Biosphere; A.G. Tansley coined Ecosystem (1935).

UP TGT Geography (Previous Year Pattern)
Which of the following is a gaseous biogeochemical cycle?

Official Benchmark Explanation: The nitrogen cycle (along with carbon and oxygen cycles) is classified as a gaseous biogeochemical cycle because its main reservoir is the atmospheric gas pool.

UP PGT Geography (Previous Year Pattern)
The transition zone between two distinct biotic communities with high species diversity is known as:

Official Benchmark Explanation: An ecotone represents the edge zone or ecological tension line between adjacent communities, characterized by high biodiversity and the 'edge effect'.

UGC-NET Geography (Previous Year Pattern)
In an aquatic ecosystem, the pyramid of numbers is typically:

Official Benchmark Explanation: In aquatic ecosystems, a single large producer (like phytoplankton) supports numerous herbivores (zooplankton), which in turn support carnivores, making the pyramid of numbers inverted.

UP PGT Geography (Previous Year Pattern)
The term 'Biosphere' was first coined by which Austrian geologist?

Official Benchmark Explanation: Eduard Suess coined the term 'biosphere' in 1875 to describe the place on Earth's surface where life dwells, later expanded into a comprehensive geochemical system by V.I. Vernadsky.

Question 1 of 10
Which of the following describes 'Polyimax' or 'Polyclimax' theory of ecological succession?

Solution: Proposed by A.G. Tansley and developed by R. Whittaker, the polyclimax theory suggests that a region can support multiple climax communities controlled by edaphic, topographic, or animal factors.

Question 2 of 10
What is the primary difference between autotrophic and heterotrophic succession?

Solution: Autotrophic succession starts with inorganic environment and autotrophs (high P/R ratio), whereas heterotrophic succession starts in organic-rich environments with heterotrophs predominating (P/R < 1).

Question 3 of 10
Shelford's Law of Tolerance expands upon Liebig's Law by introducing:

Solution: Victor Shelford (1913) stated that organisms have ecological tolerance ranges bounded by both minimum and maximum thresholds, beyond which individuals cannot survive.

Question 4 of 10
Which type of pyramid can never be inverted under any natural ecological circumstance?

Solution: The pyramid of energy is always upright because according to the Second Law of Thermodynamics, energy dissipation is absolute during transfer between trophic levels.

Question 5 of 10
In the context of ecological pyramids, a spindle-shaped pyramid of numbers is typically observed in:

Solution: In a forest ecosystem, a single large tree (producer) supports many herbivorous birds and insects, which in turn support fewer parasites and hyperparasites, creating a spindle-shaped (narrow at base and top, wide in middle) pyramid of numbers.

Question 6 of 10
What does the term 'Ecological Amplitude' signify?

Solution: Ecological amplitude refers to the range of environmental conditions (such as temperature, moisture, salinity) in which an organism can successfully survive and reproduce.

Question 7 of 10
Which term describes species that migrate into a new area and disrupt native ecosystems due to lack of natural predators?

Solution: Invasive alien species are non-native organisms that spread widely outside their natural distribution range, often causing severe ecological and economic damage.

Question 8 of 10
The concept of the 'Biochemical Cycles' highlights that chemical elements:

Solution: Biogeochemical cycles demonstrate how nutrients flow through closed-loop pathways involving biological, geological, and chemical phases.

Question 9 of 10
In trophic dynamics, what does 'Net Primary Productivity (NPP)' represent?

Solution: NPP is Gross Primary Productivity (GPP) minus the energy lost through plant respiration (R), representing the actual biomass available for consumption by heterotrophs.

Question 10 of 10
Which ecosystem has the highest average net primary productivity per unit area globally?

Solution: Tropical rain forests have optimal temperature, abundant rainfall, and year-round sunlight, yielding the highest net primary productivity among terrestrial ecosystems.
