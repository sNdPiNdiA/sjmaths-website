Physical Geography — Lithosphere

Mountains: Comprehensive Geographic Analysis, Physical & Human Mechanisms, and Regional Distribution

High-yield conceptual breakdown and physical/human geographical foundations of Mountains tailored for competitive exam mastery.

UP PGT Geography UP TGT Social Science B.Sc. / M.Sc. Level

Mountains represent the highest-order positive relief features of the Earth's lithosphere, characterized by steep slopes, high relative relief, and profound altitudinal zonation. From the perspective of physical geography and geomorphology, their genesis is intricately linked to endogenetic forces—primarily plate tectonics, vulcanicity, and block faulting—which interact with exogenetic agents of denudation to shape global topography. Fold mountains, formed predominantly through orogenic belts along convergent plate boundaries, constitute the most extensive and structurally complex mountain systems on Earth, playing a vital role in global atmospheric circulation, precipitation patterns, and fluvial hydrology.

1. Morphological Classification and Types of Mountains

Classification of mountains based on their mode of origin, tectonic setting, and internal geological structure.

  • Fold Mountains (Orogenic): Formed by the compression of sedimentary strata in geosynclines along convergent boundaries (e.g., Himalayas, Rockies, Andes, Alps).
  • Block Mountains (Fault-block): Generated by tensional forces leading to faulting, creating horsts (upthrown blocks) and grabens (downthrown blocks) like the Vosges and Black Forest.
  • Volcanic Mountains (Accumulative): Built by the continuous accumulation of volcanic material, lava, and pyroclasts over vents (e.g., Mauna Kea, Mt. Kilimanjaro, Mt. Fuji).
  • Residual or Relict Mountains (Erosional): Remnants of ancient mountain systems lowered by prolonged sub-aerial denudation, such as the Aravallis, Appalachians, and Ural Mountains.
Comparative Analysis: Contrasts sharply with plateaus and plains by exhibiting high relative relief and steep altitudinal gradients, whereas plateaus maintain extensive flat-topped summits elevated above surrounding lowlands.
  • Fold mountains are characterized by anticlines and synclines.
  • Block mountains are bounded by steep normal or reverse faults.
  • Residual mountains serve as indicators of advanced geomorphic cycle stages.

2. Origin and Tectonic Theories of Fold Mountains

Geological mechanisms explaining the lateral compression, crustal shortening, and uplift of extensive fold mountain chains.

  • Geosynclinal Orogeny Theory (Kober): Posited that mobile, shallow water depressions called geosynclines accumulated vast sediments, later compressed into mountain ranges (orogens) by lateral movement of rigid forelands (Kratogens).
  • Thermal Contraction Theory (Jeffreys): Suggested Earth's interior cooling led to crustal wrinkling, though largely superseded by modern plate tectonics.
  • Plate Tectonic Theory (Morgan, Le Pichon): Explains fold mountain genesis via continent-continent convergence (e.g., Indian and Eurasian plates) and ocean-continent subduction (e.g., Nazca and South American plates).
  • Benioff Zones and Accretionary Prisms: Subduction zones feature dipping seismic zones and scraped-off marine sediments forming complex accretionary wedges.
Comparative Analysis: Kober's static geosynclinal model relies on vertical subsidence and lateral foreland pressure, whereas the dynamic Plate Tectonic model relies on lithospheric slab pull, ridge push, and convective mantle currents.
  • Himalayas are a classic Cenozoic continent-continent collision orogen.
  • Andes represent Cordilleran-type ocean-continent subduction orogeny.

3. Global Distribution and Major Orogenic Belts

Spatial arrangement of global mountain chains along major tectonic plate boundaries and linear orogenic belts.

  • Circum-Pacific Belt ('Ring of Fire'): Continuous chain of young fold mountains and volcanic arcs surrounding the Pacific Ocean, including the Rockies, Andes, and Japanese ranges.
  • Alpine-Himalayan Belt: Major belt formed by the closure of the Tethys Sea, stretching from the Atlas Mountains through the Alps, Zagros, Himalayas, to Southeast Asia.
  • Hercynian and Caledonian Belts: Older Paleozoic orogenic belts marking ancient plate collisions, including the Scandinavian Caledonides and European Hercynian massifs.
  • Latitudinal vs Meridional Trends: Alpine-Himalayan belt exhibits a predominantly latitudinal (east-west) trend, whereas the American Cordilleras display strict meridional (north-south) alignment.
Comparative Analysis: Young fold mountains feature rugged peaks, high seismic activity, and active uplift, while ancient relict mountains feature rounded tops, subdued relief, and tectonic stability.
  • Tethys Sea sediments are the primary parent material of the Alpine-Himalayan mountain chains.
  • Young fold mountains are composed primarily of sedimentary rocks of marine origin.

Spatial Patterns & Regional Distribution

🌐 Global Geographic Belts & Spatial Variation

Global mountain distribution is strictly controlled by global tectonics. The two primary linear mega-belts account for the vast majority of high-altitude relief: the Circum-Pacific seismic and volcanic belt and the Alpine-Himalayan collision belt. Additional interior continental blocks feature older, eroded mountain roots like the Urals, Great Dividing Range, and the Drakensberg.

🇮🇳 Indian Subcontinent Context & Regional Corridors

India's topography features the mighty Himalayan mountain system along its northern and northeastern borders, formed by the northward drift of the Indian plate. Divided parallelly into the Trans-Himalayas, Greater Himalayas (Himadri), Lesser Himalayas (Himachal), and Outer Himalayas (Shiwaliks). Peninsular India contains relict block and fold mountains including the Aravallis, Vindhyas, Satpuras, Western Ghats (fault scarps), and Eastern Ghats.

Hypsographic Curve Analysis
A = f(h) where A is surface area and h is elevation relative to sea level
Parameters: A represents cumulative surface area; h represents elevation or depth relative to mean sea level.
Exam Focus: Frequently asked in UGC-NET regarding the percentage of Earth's surface occupied by high mountain elevations (above 1000m).
Adiabatic Lapse Rate for Mountain Meteorology
DALR approx 9.8°C/km; SALR approx 5°C/km to 6°C/km
Parameters: DALR = Dry Adiabatic Lapse Rate; SALR = Saturated Adiabatic Lapse Rate; expressed in °C drop per kilometer of vertical ascent.
Exam Focus: Essential for calculating orographic precipitation, rain shadow effects, and snowline elevations on mountain slopes in UP PGT/NET questions.

Mnemonics, Memory Shortcuts & Exam Traps

💡 Major Fold Mountain Eras (Caledonian, Hercynian, Alpine)
C-H-A (Can Horses Accelerate?)

C = Caledonian (Silurian-Devonian), H = Hercynian/Variscan (Carboniferous-Permian), A = Alpine (Tertiary/Cenozoic).

⚠️ Exam Trap: Examiners often interchange the geological periods of Hercynian and Caledonian orogenies; remember Caledonian is older.
💡 Himalayan Longitudinal Divisions from North to South
T-G-L-O (Trains Generally Leave On-time)

T = Trans-Himalayas, G = Greater Himalayas, L = Lesser Himalayas, O = Outer Himalayas (Shiwaliks).

⚠️ Exam Trap: Do not confuse longitudinal divisions (T-G-L-O) with transverse river-valley regional divisions (Punjab, Kumaon, Nepal, Assam Himalayas).

📚 High-Yield Geographic Terminology & Definitions

Fold Mountain UP PGT Core

Mountains formed by the compression of crustal layers, resulting in large-scale folding into anticlines and synclines.

Horst UGC-NET Factual

An upland block of the Earth's crust that has been lifted up between two parallel faults by tectonic forces.

Graben UP TGT Standard

A depressed block of land bordered by parallel fault lines, forming a rift valley due to tensional forces.

Anticline Morphology

An arch-shaped fold in rock strata where the oldest strata are located at the core.

Syncline Morphology

A trough-like downward fold in rock strata where the youngest strata are located at the core.

Nappe Advanced Tectonics

A large sheet-like body of rock that has been moved large distances over underlying strata by faulting or folding.

Orogeny UGC-NET Core

The process of natural mountain building involving intense folding, faulting, metamorphism, and igneous activity.

Epeirogeny Geotectonics

Broad-scale continental uplift or subsidence that does not cause severe folding or faulting.

Root of Mountain Isostasy

The deep sialic crustal projection into the denser sima mantle beneath major mountain ranges, as explained by Airy's theory of isostasy.

Laccolith Volcanism

A dome-shaped intrusive igneous body that arches up the overlying strata, common in dome mountains.

Klippe Advanced Geomorphology

An isolated remnant of a nappe that has been separated from the main body by erosion.

Fenster Advanced Geomorphology

A window or opening in a nappe where underlying younger or different rocks are exposed through erosion.

Cuesta Landform Analysis

A ridge with a gentle slope on one side (dip slope) and a steep cliff or scarp slope on the other, formed on tilted strata.

🧭 Prescribed Scholars, Laws & Spatial Models

Airy's Hypothesis of Isostasy (1855)

States that mountains have deep 'roots' of lower density material extending into the denser mantle, maintaining buoyancy (Uniform density, varying depth).

Pratt's Hypothesis of Isostasy (1859)

Postulates that varying topographic heights are compensated by lateral variations in rock density above a common compensation depth (Uniform depth, varying density).

Haug's Geosynclinal Theory (1900)

Emphasized that mountain building (orogeny) takes place in long, mobile, shallow marine troughs called geosynclines where thick sediments accumulate before lateral compression.

Plate Tectonic Theory (Morgan, Le Pichon, McKenzie) (1967-1968)

Explains fold mountains as the direct consequence of convergent plate boundaries (continent-continent collision like Himalayas, or ocean-continent subduction like the Andes).

Question 1 of 19
Which of the following mountain ranges is classified as a young fold mountain formed during the Tertiary period?

Rationale: The Himalayas were formed during the Tertiary period due to the collision of the Indian and Eurasian plates. Aravallis and Appalachians are ancient fold mountains.

Question 2 of 19
According to Airy's theory of Isostasy, how do high mountain ranges maintain balance?

Rationale: Airy proposed uniform density with varying depths of roots (Uniform density, varying depth concept).

Question 3 of 19
The Vosges and the Black Forest mountains are classic examples of which type of mountain?

Rationale: Vosges and Black Forest are classic horst mountains formed by faulting along the Rhine rift valley.

Question 4 of 19
What is a 'Graben'?

Rationale: A graben is a down-faulted crustal block bordered by normal faults, forming rift valleys.

Question 5 of 19
Which scholar proposed that mountain building occurs in mobile marine troughs known as geosynclines?

Rationale: Emil Haug formulated the Geosynclinal Orogenic theory, later expanded by Kober.

Question 6 of 19
Which of the following is an example of an accumulation of volcanic materials rather than tectonic folding?

Rationale: Mount Kilimanjaro is a volcanic mountain (stratovolcano), whereas the others are major fold mountains.

Question 7 of 19
What term is used for an isolated remnant of a nappe that has been separated from its main body by erosion?

Rationale: A klippe is an erosional remnant of a transported nappe sheet resting on younger rocks.

Question 8 of 19
Pratt's model of isostasy assumes which of the following conditions?

Rationale: Pratt's model assumes blocks of different heights have varying densities above a uniform line of compensation.

Question 9 of 19
The Aravalli range in India represents which stage of mountain evolution?

Rationale: Aravallis are among the oldest fold mountains in the world and have been heavily worn down, now standing as residual mountains.

Question 10 of 19
Which plate tectonic boundary interaction is primarily responsible for the formation of the Andes Mountains?

Rationale: The Andes are formed by the subduction of the Nazca plate beneath the South American continental plate.

Question 11 of 19
In structural geology, an 'anticline' is characterized by:

Rationale: An anticline is an upward arching fold where the oldest strata are exposed in the core.

Question 12 of 19
Which mountain system was formed during the Caledonian Orogeny?

Rationale: The Caledonian orogeny (Silurian-Devonian) formed the mountains of Scotland, Scandinavia, and parts of the northern Appalachians.

Question 13 of 19
What is a 'Fenster' (Window) in structural geology?

Rationale: A fenster is an opening or window through a nappe exposing older or different rocks underneath.

Question 14 of 19
Which of the following orogenies is associated with the formation of the Hercynian (Variscan) mountain systems in Europe?

Rationale: The Hercynian orogeny took place during the late Paleozoic (Carboniferous-Permian) era.

Question 15 of 19
What type of slope is typically associated with a cuesta?

Rationale: Cuestas have an asymmetrical cross-section with a gentle dip slope and a steep scarp slope.

Question 16 of 19
The Sierra Nevada mountains in California are best known as an example of:

Rationale: Sierra Nevada is a classic tilted fault-block mountain range.

Question 17 of 19
Which term describes broad regional crustal uplift without significant folding?

Rationale: Epeirogenic movements are continent-building vertical movements causing broad uplift or subsidence.

Question 18 of 19
Which mountain range acts as a climatic divide between the Mediterranean climate and Central European climate?

Rationale: The Alps form a major climatic barrier separating northern Europe from the Mediterranean basin.

Question 19 of 19
What is the primary force responsible for the creation of block mountains and rift valleys?

Rationale: Tensional forces pull the crust apart, causing normal faulting that produces horsts and grabens.

UP PGT Geography (Previous Year Pattern)
Who among the following propounded the theory that 'mountains are rootless' based on density compensation?

Official Benchmark Explanation: Pratt argued that mountains do not have roots; instead, compensation is achieved through variations in rock density.

UP TGT Geography (Previous Year Pattern)
The Black Forest mountains of Germany are an example of:

Official Benchmark Explanation: The Black Forest and Vosges are classic horst (block) mountains flanking the Rhine rift valley.

UGC-NET Geography (Previous Year Pattern)
Match List-I (Orogenic Epoch) with List-II (Mountain Range): A. Caledonian 1. Urals B. Hercynian 2. Appalachians (Central) C. Alpine 3. Himalayas D. Precambrian 4. Laurentian Correct Code:

Official Benchmark Explanation: Caledonian corresponds to early Paleozoic systems, Hercynian to late Paleozoic (Urals/Appalachians), Alpine to Tertiary (Himalayas), and Precambrian to ancient shields/ranges (Laurentian).

UP PGT Geography (Previous Year Pattern)
Which of the following is NOT a fault-block mountain?

Official Benchmark Explanation: Kilimanjaro is a volcanic mountain, whereas Sierra Nevada, Vosges, and the Salt Range are fault-block mountains.

UGC-NET Geography (Previous Year Pattern)
The concept of 'Geosynclines' as the 'cradles of mountains' was extensively elaborated by:

Official Benchmark Explanation: Leopold Kober used the geosyncline concept to explain orogenesis through his 'Orogenic zones' and 'Kratogen' theory.

UP TGT Geography (Previous Year Pattern)
Which type of fold is characterized by limbs that are parallel to each other?

Official Benchmark Explanation: In an isoclinal fold, the compressive forces are so strong that both limbs dip in the same direction at the same angle, running parallel to each other.

Question 1 of 10
Consider the following statements regarding Airy's and Pratt's views on Isostasy: 1. Airy assumed uniform density of crustal blocks with varying root depths. 2. Pratt assumed uniform depth of compensation with varying rock densities. Which of the statements given above is/are correct?

Solution: Both statements accurately capture the foundational postulates of Airy (uniform density, varying depth) and Pratt (varying density, uniform depth).

Question 2 of 10
In the context of structural geology, what is a 'Recumbent Fold'?

Solution: A recumbent fold has an axial plane that is essentially horizontal, pushing one limb over the other.

Question 3 of 10
Which tectonic mechanism best accounts for the uplift of the Tibetan Plateau alongside the Himalayan mountain chain?

Solution: The Tibetan Plateau's massive elevation is maintained by crustal thickening and continuous underthrusting resulting from the continent-continent collision.

Question 4 of 10
What differentiates a 'Nappe' from an ordinary fold?

Solution: A nappe is a large-scale tectonic sheet displaced horizontally along a low-angle thrust fault due to intense compression.

Question 5 of 10
Which mountain range is formed at a convergent boundary where two oceanic plates meet (Island Arc / Subduction zone mountain)?

Solution: The Japanese mountain ranges are part of island arc systems formed by ocean-ocean convergence and subduction.

Question 6 of 10
The Rhine Valley flanked by the Vosges and Black Forest is an ideal example of:

Solution: The Rhine rift valley is a classic graben bordered by horst mountains (Vosges and Black Forest).

Question 7 of 10
In Kober's geosynclinal theory, what term did he use for the stable, rigid blocks flanking the geosyncline (or 'orogen')?

Solution: Kober referred to rigid stable masses flanking the mobile geosynclines as 'Kratogens', which squeeze the sediments (Chine) into mountain ranges (Randketten).

Question 8 of 10
Which of the following mountain building epochs is the oldest?

Solution: Precambrian orogenic movements are the oldest, predating Paleozoic Caledonian and Hercynian epochs and Cenozoic Alpine orogeny.

Question 9 of 10
What is an 'Overturned Fold'?

Solution: In an overturned fold, intense pressure tilts one limb past the vertical so that strata on both limbs dip in the same direction.

Question 10 of 10
How do residual mountains differ from fold or block mountains?

Solution: Residual mountains (e.g., Aravallis, Nilgiris, Sierras of Spain) are surviving remnants of formerly higher mountain systems lowered by prolonged erosion.
