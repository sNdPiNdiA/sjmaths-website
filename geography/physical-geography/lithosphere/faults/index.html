Physical Geography — Lithosphere

Faults: Comprehensive Geographic Analysis, Physical & Human Mechanisms, and Regional Distribution

High-yield conceptual breakdown and physical/human geographical foundations of Faults tailored for competitive exam mastery.

UP PGT Geography UP TGT Social Science B.Sc. / M.Sc. Level

Faults represent fractures or discontinuity surfaces in the Earth's crust along which significant displacement has occurred as a result of rock deformation. As a critical component of endogenic geomorphology within the lithosphere, faulting is driven by tectonic stresses including tensional, compressional, and shearing forces. These processes produce distinct structural landforms such as horsts, grabens, fault-line scarps, and rift valleys, altering regional drainage patterns and influencing lithospheric stability.

1. Mechanics, Stress Fields, and Fault Geometry

Fault formation is governed by Anderson's Theory of Faulting, relating principal stress axes ($sigma_1, sigma_2, sigma_3$) to specific mechanical failure planes.

  • Hanging wall moves down relative to footwall under tensional stress.
  • Hanging wall moves up relative to footwall under compressional stress.
  • Strike-slip faults feature horizontal displacement along vertical or near-vertical planes.
  • Oblique-slip faults combine components of both dip-slip and strike-slip displacement.
  • Dip angle measures the inclination of the fault plane relative to the horizontal surface.
Comparative Analysis: Unlike folds which represent ductile deformation under slow compression, faults represent brittle failure and sudden rupture under critical yield strength limits.
  • Footwall is always beneath the fault plane; hanging wall rests above it.
  • Tensional stress creates normal faults; compressional stress creates reverse/thrust faults.

2. Structural Landforms and Associated Geomorphology

Differential erosion and tectonic uplift along fault planes produce macro-scale relief features and structural landforms.

  • Rift Valleys (Graben) formed by parallel normal faults where the central block drops down.
  • Horst (Block Mountains) formed when a central block remains stationary or is uplifted between parallel normal faults.
  • Fault Scarps represent the steep topographic step formed directly by differential vertical displacement.
  • Fault-Line Scarps are secondary relief features formed by differential erosion along an ancient, inactive fault line.
  • Shed/Sapped drainage patterns and linear valleys frequently mark active fault traces.
Comparative Analysis: Hercynian and Alpine tectonic phases produce contrasting fault block styles based on basement rock rigidity and preexisting structural grain.
  • East African Rift Valley and Rhine Graben are classic textbook examples of graben systems.
  • Vindhyan and Kaimur scarps display complex fault-line erosion histories.

3. Classification Systems for Competitive Examinations

UP TGT/PGT and UGC-NET examinations frequently test precise morphological and kinematic classifications of faults.

  • Normal Fault: High angle ($45^circ-90^circ$), crustal stretching and thinning.
  • Reverse Fault: Low angle ($<45^circ$), known as a thrust fault when nearly horizontal (Nappe structures).
  • Transform Fault: Conservative plate boundary where lithosphere is neither created nor destroyed (e.g., San Andreas).
  • Step Fault: Series of normal faults dipping in the same direction, creating staircase topography.
  • Radial and Ring Faults: Associated with volcanic calderas and crypto-explosion structures.
Comparative Analysis: Thrust faults involve crustal shortening and stacking of strata, whereas normal faults result in crustal extension and basin creation.
  • Hanging wall upward motion is diagnostic of reverse and thrust faults.
  • Strike-slip faults are categorized into dextral (right-lateral) and sinistral (left-lateral).

Spatial Patterns & Regional Distribution

🌐 Global Geographic Belts & Spatial Variation

Faulting is concentrated along tectonic plate boundaries and intraplate rift zones. Major global fault systems include the San Andreas Fault (Transform), the Great African Rift Valley System (Normal/Rift), the Alpine-Himalayan belt (Thrust/Reverse), and the Basin and Range Province of North America.

🇮🇳 Indian Subcontinent Context & Regional Corridors

In India, major fault systems include the Narmada-Son Lineament (NSL), the Cambay Graben, the Damodar Valley fault basins ( Gondwana coal fields), the Main Boundary Thrust (MBT) and Himalayan Frontal Thrust (HFT), and the Kachchh Mainland Fault associated with the 2001 Bhuj earthquake.

📐 Key Geographic Formulas, Scales & Classifications
Richter Magnitude Scale
M = \log_{10}(A) - \log_{10}(A_0)
Parameters: M = magnitude, A = maximum amplitude of seismic waves on a Wood-Anderson seismograph, A_0 = correction factor for distance from epicenter
Exam Focus: Represents logarithmic energy release; each whole number increase represents a 32-fold increase in energy released.
Mercalli Intensity Scale
I = f(Damage, Human Perception, Structural Effects)
Parameters: I = Roman numerals (I to XII) assessing macroseismic ground effects and structural damage
Exam Focus: Unlike Richter (magnitude/energy), Mercalli measures local intensity and varies spatially based on distance and substrate geology.

Mnemonics, Memory Shortcuts & Exam Traps

💡 Hanging Wall vs Footwall Mnemonic
HANGing down is NORMAL

In a Normal fault, the HANGing wall moves DOWN relative to the footwall due to tension.

⚠️ Exam Trap: Examiners often trick candidates by reversing the relative motion of hanging walls in reverse faults.
💡 Strike-Slip Lateral Motion Mnemonic
De-Right, Si-Left (D&S)

Dextral = Right-lateral motion; Sinistral = Left-lateral motion when looking across the fault plane.

⚠️ Exam Trap: Do not confuse strike-slip offset direction with dip-slip vertical displacement.

📚 High-Yield Geographic Terminology & Definitions

Fault UP PGT Core

A fracture or zone of fractures along which two blocks of lithospheric crust have moved relative to each other.

Hanging Wall UGC-NET Concept

The block of rock that lies immediately above an inclined fault plane.

Footwall UGC-NET Concept

The block of rock that lies immediately beneath an inclined fault plane.

Normal Fault UP TGT Standard

A dip-slip fault in which the hanging wall moves downward relative to the footwall, resulting from tensional forces.

Reverse Fault UP PGT Standard

A dip-slip fault in which the hanging wall moves upward relative to the footwall, resulting from compressional forces.

Thrust Fault UGC-NET High Yield

A low-angle reverse fault (dip generally less than 45 degrees) where older strata are displaced over younger strata.

Strike-Slip Fault UP PGT Standard

A fault in which movement is primarily horizontal, parallel to the strike of the fault plane (e.g., San Andreas Fault).

Horst Landform Mapping

An upland block of land bounded by normal faults on either side, standing higher than the adjacent dropped blocks.

Graben Landform Mapping

A depressed block of land bordered by parallel normal faults, forming a rift valley or trough.

En Echelon Faults Advanced Structural

A series of closely spaced, parallel or subparallel faults that overlap or step aside one another.

Transform Fault Plate Tectonics

A special type of strike-slip fault that forms a plate boundary, accommodating relative horizontal slip between tectonic plates.

Slickensides Micro-structure

Polished and striated rock surfaces produced by friction along a fault plane during movement.

Myrmekite Advanced Petro-structure

A micro-structural intergrowth found in sheared metamorphic and fault zones.

🧭 Prescribed Scholars, Laws & Spatial Models

Anderson's Theory of Faulting (1951)

Postulates that faulting occurs when the shear stress exceeds the shear strength of the rock, classified into three stress regimes (maximum, intermediate, and minimum principal stress axes oriented vertically or horizontally) producing normal, strike-slip, and reverse faults.

Hurtubise and Suppe's Fault-Bend Folding Model (1983)

Explains how strata fold as they move over bends in non-planar fault surfaces, particularly in thrust-fault and fold belts.

Suess and The Rift Valley Theory (Late 19th Century)

Eduard Suess explained the formation of major trench-like depressions (Rift Valleys like the Rhine and East African Rift) through crustal tension and subsidence of grabens between parallel fault systems.

Question 1 of 18
Which of the following faults is characterized by the hanging wall moving downward relative to the footwall?

Rationale: A normal fault is produced by tensional forces where the hanging wall moves down relative to the footwall due to gravity and crustal extension.

Question 2 of 18
An elevated block of land lying between two parallel normal faults is known as:

Rationale: A horst (or block mountain) is an uplifted fault block bounded by normal faults, whereas a graben is the down-dropped block.

Question 3 of 18
The San Andreas Fault in California is a classic example of which type of fault?

Rationale: The San Andreas Fault is a transform fault operating as a dextral (right-lateral) strike-slip fault where the Pacific Plate moves northwest relative to the North American Plate.

Question 4 of 18
A thrust fault is best defined as:

Rationale: Thrust faults are low-angle reverse faults (<45 degrees) that allow older rock strata to be thrust horizontally over younger strata.

Question 5 of 18
Which of the following forces is primarily responsible for the creation of normal faults?

Rationale: Tensional forces stretch the Earth's crust, thinning it and causing blocks of land to drop along normal fault planes.

Question 6 of 18
What is the term for polished and striated rock surfaces found along a fault plane?

Rationale: Slickensides are grooved and polished surfaces caused by frictional rubbing of rock blocks moving past each other along a fault.

Question 7 of 18
The East African Rift System is a prime example of topography formed by:

Rationale: The East African Rift Valley system is a classic continental rift zone dominated by grabens and associated volcanism due to crustal extension.

Question 8 of 18
In a dip-slip fault where the fault plane is inclined, the block resting on top of the fault plane is called the:

Rationale: By definition in structural geology, an observer standing underground on the fault plane would have their feet on the footwall and hang their lantern on the hanging wall.

Question 9 of 18
Which scholar formulated the foundational mechanical stress theory of faulting based on principal stress axes?

Rationale: E.M. Anderson (1951) formulated the mechanical theory of faulting relating fault types to orientations of principal stress axes.

Question 10 of 18
A fault in which the net slip has both dip-slip and strike-slip components is termed:

Rationale: An oblique-slip fault exhibits simultaneous movement along both the dip and the strike of the fault plane.

Question 11 of 18
What is a listric fault?

Rationale: Listric faults have curved fault surfaces that concave upward, flattening out at depth, commonly associated with crustal extension and detachment surfaces.

Question 12 of 18
Fault breccia is best described as:

Rationale: Fault breccia consists of angular, broken rock fragments (clasts) cemented together, formed by mechanical fragmentation within active fault zones.

Question 13 of 18
Klippen and tectonic windows are classic erosional features associated with which type of fault?

Rationale: Erosion of an overriding thrust sheet can create an isolated remnant called a Klippen, while erosion through a hole in the thrust sheet reveals underlying rocks forming a tectonic window (fenster).

Question 14 of 18
A step fault refers to:

Rationale: Step faults consist of multiple parallel normal faults where successive blocks drop down in the same direction, creating a step-like topography.

Question 15 of 18
Which term describes the compass direction of the line formed by the intersection of a fault plane with the horizontal plane?

Rationale: Strike is the compass direction of the horizontal line on an inclined plane, while dip is the angle of inclination measured perpendicular to the strike.

Question 16 of 18
Ring faults are typically associated with:

Rationale: Ring faults form circular or arcuate fracture systems resulting from the collapse of the roof into an emptied magma chamber during caldera formation.

Question 17 of 18
In a dextral strike-slip fault, across the fault, an opposing feature appears to have shifted to the:

Rationale: In a dextral (right-lateral) strike-slip fault, if you stand on one side and look across, the opposite block has moved to the right.

Question 18 of 18
Mylonite is a fine-grained, compact metamorphic rock formed in:

Rationale: Mylonites are ductile shear zone rocks formed at deeper crustal levels where high temperatures and intense plastic deformation grind and recrystallize minerals.

Question 1 of 10
Consider the following statements regarding fault mechanics: 1. Normal faults result from vertical maximum principal stress. 2. Strike-slip faults result from vertical intermediate principal stress. 3. Reverse faults occur where maximum principal stress is horizontal. Which of the statements given above are correct?

Solution: According to Anderson's theory of faulting, normal faults occur when the maximum principal stress is vertical; strike-slip faults occur when the intermediate principal stress is vertical; and reverse faults occur when the maximum principal stress is horizontal.

Question 2 of 10
In structural geology, what does the term 'pitch' or 'rake' signify in a fault plane?

Solution: Pitch or rake is the angle measured on the fault plane between the strike line and the net slip vector, defining the direction of movement.

Question 3 of 10
Which structural feature is formed when a strike-slip fault bends or steps over in a way that creates local crustal extension?

Solution: A releasing bend (or stepover) along a strike-slip fault results in local extension, forming a pull-apart basin (e.g., Dead Sea basin).

Question 4 of 10
Which of the following is NOT a direct geomorphic indicator of active faulting?

Solution: A peneplain is a low-relief landform produced by prolonged subaerial erosion near base level, indicating tectonic quiescence, not active faulting.

Question 5 of 10
What distinguishes a 'horst' from a 'fault-block mountain'?

Solution: While often used interchangeably, a horst specifically denotes a symmetrical uplifted block between normal faults, whereas fault-block mountains can encompass asymmetric tilted blocks (tilt-block mountains).

Question 6 of 10
Which type of fault movement characterizes the Alpine Fault in New Zealand?

Solution: The Alpine Fault is a major oblique-slip fault accommodating both horizontal shear (Pacific-Australian plate motion) and uplift of the Southern Alps.

Question 7 of 10
What is 'Hade' in fault terminology?

Solution: Hade is the complement of dip; it is the angle that the fault plane makes with the vertical (90 degrees minus the dip angle).

Question 8 of 10
Which of the following sedimentary or metamorphic rocks is characteristically formed directly within intense shallow fault crush zones?

Solution: Cataclasite is a cohesive, fine-grained fault rock formed by brittle crushing and fracturing (cataclasis) of rocks in fault zones.

Question 9 of 10
Triangular facets along a mountain front typically indicate:

Solution: Triangular facets are formed when active normal faulting truncates the lower ends of mountain spurs, leaving triangular gable-like remnants facing the fault valley.

Question 10 of 10
In the context of plate tectonics, transform faults differ from fracture zones because transform faults:

Solution: Transform faults are active plate boundaries with seismicity occurring between offset ridge crests, whereas fracture zones are older, inactive aseismic scars extending outward from the ridge.
