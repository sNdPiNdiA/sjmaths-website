Physical Geography — Lithosphere

Earth Movements: Comprehensive Geographic Analysis, Physical & Human Mechanisms, and Regional Distribution

High-yield conceptual breakdown and physical geographical foundations of Earth Movements tailored for competitive exam mastery in UP TGT/PGT Geography and UGC-NET.

UP PGT Geography UP TGT Social Science B.Sc. / M.Sc. Level

Earth movements constitute the fundamental geomorphic processes driven by internal (endogenetic) and external (exogenetic) forces that continually shape and reshape the Earth's crust. Endogenetic movements, originating from thermal convection currents within the Earth's mantle, are broadly categorized into catastrophic/sudden movements (earthquakes, volcanism) and diastrophic/slow movements (epeirogenic and orogenic forces). Epeirogenic movements operate vertically over vast continental areas, resulting in uplift or subsidence without severe crustal deformation, whereas orogenic movements operate horizontally under intense tangential compression and tension, resulting in folding, faulting, and mountain building. Understanding these mechanics is vital for analyzing global relief features, plate tectonics, and regional seismic hazards.

1. Endogenetic Forces & Diastrophic Movements

Internal thermal dynamics drive long-term crustal deformation through vertical epeirogeny and horizontal orogeny.

  • Endogenetic forces derive their energy from internal heat, radioactivity, and primordial thermal energy within the Earth's interior.
  • Diastrophic movements are slow, secular, and widespread tectonic processes affecting large tracts of the lithosphere.
  • Epeirogenic movements act vertically (radius of the earth), causing continent-building through massive uplift (emergent coasts) or subsidence (submergent coasts).
  • Orogenic movements act horizontally (tangential), creating linear fold mountain systems along convergent plate boundaries.
  • Compression forces generate anticlines, synclines, overfolds, recumbent folds, and nappe structures.
  • Tension forces pull the crust apart, creating normal faults, rift valleys (graben), and block mountains (horsts).
Comparative Analysis: Contrasting epeirogenic (vertical, broad, continent-building, gentle dips) with orogenic (horizontal, narrow, mountain-building, intense folding/faulting) forces.
  • Diastrophic forces operate over geological epochs.
  • Epeirogeny means 'continent-making'; orogeny means 'mountain-making'.

2. Sudden Endogenetic Movements: Earthquakes and Volcanism

Catastrophic release of strain energy along fault planes causes seismic wave propagation and crustal rupture.

  • Earthquakes represent sudden shock waves generated by elastic rebound theory along active tectonic faults.
  • Focus (Hypocenter) is the point of origin underground; Epicenter is the point on the surface directly above it.
  • Seismic waves include Primary (P) waves, Secondary (S) waves, and Surface (Rayleigh and Love) waves.
  • Volcanism involves the eruption of magma, gases, and pyroclastic debris onto the Earth's surface through vents or fissures.
  • Volcanic landforms depend on magma viscosity; basic/mafic lava forms shield volcanoes, while acidic/felsic lava forms composite cones and domes.
  • Distribution of both phenomena is concentrated strictly along plate margins: Circum-Pacific Belt, Mid-Atlantic Ridge, and Alpine-Himalayan Belt.
Comparative Analysis: P-waves are longitudinal/compressional and travel through solids and liquids; S-waves are transverse/shear and travel only through solids.
  • Elastic Rebound Theory by H.F. Reid explains earthquake mechanics.
  • Over 70 percent of global earthquakes occur along the Circum-Pacific Ring of Fire.

3. Exogenetic Forces & Denudation Processes

Atmospheric, hydrological, and biological agents act as leveling forces wearing down elevated relief.

  • Exogenetic forces derive energy from solar radiation and gravity, operating as destructive or leveling agents (gradation).
  • Weathering is the in-situ mechanical disintegration and chemical decomposition of rocks without transport.
  • Mass wasting involves gravity-driven downslope movement of weathered regolith (slumping, creep, landslides).
  • Erosion is the active acquisition and removal of rock debris by dynamic agents like running water, glaciers, wind, and waves.
  • Deposition is the laying down of eroded material when agent velocity or energy drops.
  • The combined action of weathering, mass wasting, and erosion produces a graded profile across landscape evolution models.
Comparative Analysis: Weathering is static and preparatory; erosion is dynamic and transport-oriented.
  • Exogenetic processes work relentlessly to achieve a base level of erosion.
  • Climatic geomorphology dictates the dominance of specific exogenetic agents across latitude and moisture zones.

Spatial Patterns & Regional Distribution

🌐 Global Geographic Belts & Spatial Variation

Earth movements are globally mapped along major tectonic plate boundaries. The Circum-Pacific Belt ('Ring of Fire') accounts for nearly 80% of seismic and volcanic activity due to subduction zones. The Alpine-Himalayan belt stretches from the Mediterranean across Iran and the Himalayas, resulting from continent-continent collision between Eurasian and Indo-Australian/African plates. The Mid-Atlantic Ridge represents a divergent constructive plate margin marked by seafloor spreading and fissure-type volcanism.

🇮🇳 Indian Subcontinent Context & Regional Corridors

India's tectonic framework is defined by the ongoing northward collision of the Indian Plate with the Eurasian Plate. The Extra-Peninsular region (Himalayas) is tectonically active, youthful, and prone to severe earthquakes (Zones IV and V) and mass movements. The Peninsular Plateau represents a stable, ancient shield block shaped by ancient epeirogenic faulting (Narmada and Tapti rift valleys) and basaltic fissure eruptions (Deccan Traps). The Indo-Gangetic alluvial trough is a vast foredeep basin formed by crustal downwarping ahead of the rising Himalayan orogen.

📐 Key Geographic Formulas, Scales & Classifications
Richter Magnitude Scale
M = log10(A) - log10(A0) = log10(A/A0)
Parameters: M = magnitude; A = maximum amplitude of seismic wave on a standard Wood-Anderson torsion seismograph; A0 = empirical function of distance from epicenter.
Exam Focus: A logarithmic scale where each whole number increase represents a 10-fold increase in measured wave amplitude and approximately 32 times increase in released energy.
Modified Mercalli Intensity Scale
Qualitative Scale: Roman Numerals I to XII
Parameters: I = Not felt; V = Felt by nearly everyone; X = Masonry structures destroyed, rails bent; XII = Total damage, waves seen on ground surface.
Exam Focus: Measures the observed effects and structural damage of an earthquake at a specific location, unlike the magnitude scale which measures absolute energy at the source.

Mnemonics, Memory Shortcuts & Exam Traps

💡 Types of Diastrophic Movements
E-O (Earth's Origin / Epeirogenic & Orogenic)

E stands for Epeirogenic (vertical/continent-building) and O stands for Orogenic (horizontal/mountain-building).

⚠️ Exam Trap: Examiners frequently swap the definitions of epeirogenic (vertical) and orogenic (horizontal) movements in matching-type questions.
💡 Seismic Wave Arrival Order
PSL (Primary, Secondary, Love/Surface)

P-waves arrive first (fastest), S-waves arrive second, and L/Surface waves arrive last (slowest but most destructive).

⚠️ Exam Trap: Do not confuse wave speed with destructiveness; P-waves are fastest, but Surface waves cause maximum structural damage.

📚 High-Yield Geographic Terminology & Definitions

Diastrophism UP PGT Core

All movements of the earth's crust produced by endogenetic forces that elevate, depress, fold, or fault rock strata.

Epeirogenic Movements UGC-NET Factual

Broad-scale radial or vertical movements of the earth's crust leading to continent-building, uplift, or subsidence without intense deformation.

Orogenic Movements UP TGT Standard

Tangential or horizontal earth movements characterized by intense folding, faulting, and crustal shortening, resulting in mountain building.

Isostasy UGC-NET Concept

The state of gravitational equilibrium between the earth's lithosphere and asthenosphere such that the tectonic plates float at an elevation proportional to their thickness and density.

Plunging Anticline Structural Geomorphology

An anticlinal fold whose axis is inclined at an angle to the horizontal plane, causing the fold axis to dip into the ground.

Grabens Landform Analysis

A depressed block of land bounded by parallel normal faults, formed by tensional forces in the earth's crust.

Horst Fault Terminology

An elevated block of land situated between two parallel normal faults, created during crustal extension.

Nappe Advanced Tectonics

A large sheet-like body of rock that has been moved more or less horizontally above a thrust fault from its original position.

Klippe UGC-NET Advanced

An isolated remnant of a nappe that has been separated from the main thrust sheet by severe erosion.

Fenster Structural Feature

A window-like erosion opening through a nappe or thrust sheet that exposes underlying autochthonous rocks.

Pedestal Rocks Geomorphic Agent

Mushroom-shaped erosional landforms created by differential wind abrasion in arid and semi-arid regions.

Wegener's Continental Drift Historical Tectonics

A hypothesis proposing that continents have moved horizontally across the globe over geological time, initially forming a supercontinent called Pangaea.

Seafloor Spreading Plate Tectonics

The process by which new oceanic crust is formed through volcanic activity at mid-ocean ridges and gradually moves away from the ridge.

🧭 Prescribed Scholars, Laws & Spatial Models

Airy's Hypothesis of Isostasy (1855)

Uniform density of crustal blocks with varying thicknesses; 'roots' of lighter material project down into denser mantle (Mountain roots).

Pratt's Hypothesis of Isostasy (1859)

Varying density of crustal blocks resting over a uniform compensating substratum; inverse relationship between height and density ('Uniform depth, varying density').

Alfred Wegener's Continental Drift Theory (1912)

All continents were once assembled into Pangaea surrounded by Panthalassa; drifted apart due to equatorward and westward tidal and centrifugal forces.

Arthur Holmes' Thermal Convection Current Theory (1928-1929)

Radioactive decay generates thermal convection currents within the mantle, acting as the driving mechanism for continental drift and plate tectonics.

J. Tuzo Wilson & Plate Tectonics Paradigm (1965-1968)

Lithosphere is broken into rigid plates floating on the asthenosphere, interacting at divergent, convergent, and transform boundaries.

Interactive Concept Practice

Instant evaluation with detailed spatial & physical explanations for each question.

Question 1 of 18
Which of the following forces is primarily responsible for Epeirogenic movements?

Rationale: Epeirogenic movements are broad, slow vertical or radial movements originating from the earth's center that lead to uplift or subsidence of continental landmasses without significant deformation.

Question 2 of 18
Who among the following propounded that mountains have 'roots' of lighter crustal material extending deep into the mantle?

Rationale: G.B. Airy proposed that crustal blocks of uniform density have varying depths, meaning higher blocks (like mountains) extend deeper roots into the denser substrate.

Question 3 of 18
Pratt's theory of isostasy is best summarized by which of the following phrases?

Rationale: Pratt argued that blocks of different heights have varying densities, all resting on a common level of compensation (depth of compensation).

Question 4 of 18
A fault in which the hanging wall has moved downward relative to the footwall is known as:

Rationale: Normal faults are produced by tensional forces where gravity pulls the hanging wall down relative to the footwall.

Question 5 of 18
The Rhine Valley (Graben) is a classic example of which of the following geological structures?

Rationale: The Rhine Valley is a classic rift valley bounded by normal faults, formed due to tensional forces pulling the crust apart.

Question 6 of 18
In a recumbent fold, the axial plane is:

Rationale: A recumbent fold has an axial plane that is nearly horizontal, indicating intense lateral compression.

Question 7 of 18
Which term describes an isolated erosional remnant of a nappe?

Rationale: A klippe is an outlier of a nappe that has been completely surrounded by erosion, exposing younger rocks underneath.

Question 8 of 18
Alfred Wegener postulated that Pangaea began to break apart during which geological period?

Rationale: According to Wegener, the supercontinent Pangaea started breaking up during the Mesozoic era, specifically around the Jurassic period.

Question 9 of 18
What was the primary force cited by Wegener for the equatorward movement of continents?

Rationale: Wegener invoked the pole-fleeing force (Fluchtungskraft) caused by the earth's rotation and equatorial bulge, alongside tidal forces.

Question 10 of 18
Who formulated the Thermal Convection Current Theory in 1928?

Rationale: Arthur Holmes proposed that thermal convection currents in the mantle act as the driving engine for continental drift.

Question 11 of 18
Which of the following structures represents an upfold where older rock strata occupy the core?

Rationale: An anticline is an arch-like fold in which rock layers are warped upward, with the oldest strata exposed in the center.

Question 12 of 18
A fault characterized by horizontal displacement along the fault plane is called a:

Rationale: Strike-slip faults (such as the San Andreas Fault) involve predominantly horizontal movement parallel to the strike of the fault plane.

Question 13 of 18
What does a 'Fenster' represent in structural geology?

Rationale: Fenster (German for 'window') refers to an erosional window through a thrust sheet or nappe exposing the rocks beneath.

Question 14 of 18
Endogenetic forces derive their principal energy from:

Rationale: Endogenetic forces are internal forces powered by geothermal energy, residual heat, and radioactive decay within the earth's interior.

Question 15 of 18
Which of the following mountain ranges is classified as a fold mountain of the Alpine tertiary orogenic phase?

Rationale: The Himalayas, Alps, Andes, and Rockies are young fold mountains formed during the Tertiary epoch of the Alpine orogeny.

Question 16 of 18
The term 'Isostasy' was first coined by which scholar?

Rationale: C.E. Dutton coined the term 'Isostasy' in 1889 to describe the mechanical stability of the earth's crust.

Question 17 of 18
When both limbs of a fold dip in the same direction at the same angle, the fold is known as:

Rationale: In an isoclinal fold, the limbs dip in the same direction and at approximately the same angle, running parallel to each other.

Question 18 of 18
Which forces are responsible for creating horsts and grabens?

Rationale: Horsts and grabens are fault-block landforms created by tensional forces that stretch and fracture the crust.

Previous Years Questions & Exam Benchmarks

Real and benchmark questions aligned with UP PGT, TGT, and National Eligibility tests.

UP PGT Geography (Previous Year Pattern)
Who among the following propounded the theory of Isostasy based on the concept of 'uniform depth and varying density'?

Official Benchmark Explanation: J.H. Pratt postulated that varying surface elevations are compensated by lateral variations in crustal rock density above a common depth of compensation.

UP TGT Geography (Previous Year Pattern)
Which of the following is an example of a block mountain (Horst)?

Official Benchmark Explanation: The Vosges Mountains (along with the Black Forest mountains) in Europe are classic examples of horsts formed by faulting.

UGC-NET Geography (Previous Year Pattern)
Match List-I (Term) with List-II (Definition/Feature): A. Fenster - 1. Erosional outlier of a nappe B. Klippe - 2. Window exposing underlying rocks C. Horst - 3. Upthrown block between faults D. Graben - 4. Downthrown block between faults Correct Code:

Official Benchmark Explanation: Fenster is an erosional window (A-2), Klippe is an outlier of a nappe (B-1), Horst is an upthrown fault block (C-3), and Graben is a downthrown fault block (D-4).

UP PGT Geography (Previous Year Pattern)
The 'pole-fleeing force' proposed by Alfred Wegener to explain continental drift is related to:

Official Benchmark Explanation: Wegener argued that rotation of the earth causes a centrifugal force directed toward the equator (pole-fleeing force), helping continents drift equatorward.

UGC-NET Geography (Previous Year Pattern)
Which of the following fold types has axial planes inclined such that one limb is pushed over the other?

Official Benchmark Explanation: In an overturned fold, compression is strong enough that one limb is tilted beyond the vertical, resulting in one limb resting over the other.

Question 1 of 10
Consider the following statements regarding Airy's and Pratt's models of Isostasy: 1. Airy assumed uniform density of crustal blocks with varying roots. 2. Pratt assumed uniform depth of compensation with varying densities. Which of the statements given above is/are correct?

Solution: Both statements are accurate representations of the core postulates of Airy (uniform density, varying roots) and Pratt (varying density, uniform depth of compensation).

Question 2 of 10
A mountain range formed by the collision of two continental tectonic plates is typically characterized by:

Solution: Continent-continent collisions (e.g., India-Asia) generate intense compressional stress, leading to widespread folding, nappe formation, thrust faulting, and crustal thickening.

Question 3 of 10
Which structural feature is formed when compressional forces are extremely intense, causing the upper block of a thrust fault to move tens of kilometers over the lower block?

Solution: A nappe is a large-scale recumbent fold or thrust sheet driven horizontally over underlying strata by intense horizontal compression.

Question 4 of 10
The San Andreas Fault in California is an ideal terrestrial example of which type of fault boundary?

Solution: The San Andreas Fault is a classic right-lateral strike-slip fault representing a transform plate boundary where plates slide horizontally past one another.

Question 5 of 10
What is the primary difference between orogenic and epeirogenic movements?

Solution: Orogenic (mountain-building) forces are tangential and cause intense deformation, whereas epeirogenic forces are radial and cause broad regional uplift or depression without major deformation.

Question 6 of 10
In the context of structural geology, what does an 'anticlinorium' refer to?

Solution: An anticlinorium is a large regional anticline upon which smaller fold structures are superimposed.

Question 7 of 10
Which of the following geophysical evidence strongly supported Harry Hess's concept of Seafloor Spreading?

Solution: Vine-Matthews-Morley hypothesis confirmed seafloor spreading by discovering symmetrical magnetic striping on either side of mid-ocean ridges, recording earth's magnetic field reversals.

Question 8 of 10
If a rock stratum dips at an angle of 90 degrees (vertical), how is the fold classified?

Solution: An upright or vertical fold features an axial plane that is vertical and limbs that dip moderately and symmetrically away from the axis.

Question 9 of 10
Which tectonic setting is primarily responsible for the formation of island arcs like the Japanese archipelago or the Philippines?

Solution: Island arcs are volcanic archipelagos formed above oceanic-oceanic convergent subduction zones where one oceanic plate subducts beneath another.

Question 10 of 10
The concept that 'the crust of the earth is floating in gravitational equilibrium on the denser plastic mantle' is known as:

Solution: Isostasy explains how lighter crustal masses float on denser mantle material in hydrostatic balance.
