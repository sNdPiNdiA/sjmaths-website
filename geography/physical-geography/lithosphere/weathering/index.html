Physical Geography — Lithosphere

Weathering: Comprehensive Geographic Analysis, Physical & Human Mechanisms, and Regional Distribution

High-yield conceptual breakdown and physical/human geographical foundations of Weathering tailored for competitive exam mastery.

UP PGT Geography UP TGT Social Science B.Sc. / M.Sc. Level

Weathering is the in-situ breakdown and decomposition of rocks and minerals at or near the Earth's surface through the direct action of atmospheric and biotic agents, operating without significant transport of the altered materials. As a foundational endogenic-exogenic interface process in physical geography, it prepares the regolith for subsequent mass wasting and fluvial erosion, deeply influencing pedogenesis, global carbon sequestration, and geochemical cycling. Spatial variations in weathering regimes are strictly controlled by climatic parameters, lithological resistance, and time, dictating distinct morphological landscapes ranging from tor-studded granitic uplands to deeply leached tropical lateritic profiles.

1. Foundations and Mechanics of Weathering

In-situ mechanical disintegration and chemical decomposition of rocks controlled by atmospheric elements and lithological properties.

  • Distinction between cataclastic mechanical weathering (disintegration) and chemical weathering (decomposition).
  • Role of differential thermal expansion and contraction in arid and semi-arid environments.
  • Frost wedging and gelifraction driven by the 9 percent volume expansion of freezing water in periglacial zones.
  • Haloclasty or salt crystal growth exerting high crystallization pressures in coastal and hyper-arid settings.
  • Unloading and pressure release leading to sheet jointing, exfoliation, and the formation of domical inselbergs.
Comparative Analysis: Mechanical weathering dominates in cold-dry climates producing angular clasts (felsenmeer), whereas chemical weathering dominates in warm-humid climates producing clay-rich regolith and rounded boulders.
  • Weathering is exclusively an in-situ process requiring no transport medium.
  • Surface area-to-volume ratio increases exponentially as mechanical fragmentation progresses.

2. Chemical and Biotic Weathering Processes

Molecular-level alteration of primary mineral structures into stable secondary minerals through hydration, oxidation, reduction, carbonation, and solution.

  • Solution (dissolution): complete dissociation of soluble minerals like halite and gypsum in water.
  • Carbonation: reaction of carbonic acid with calcium carbonate, driving karst topography and cavern genesis.
  • Hydration: chemical addition of water molecules to mineral lattices, causing swelling and internal stress (e.g., anhydrite to gypsum).
  • Hydrolysis: primary reaction where hydrogen ions replace metallic cations in silicates, forming secondary clay minerals.
  • Oxidation and Reduction: electron transfer reactions altering iron and manganese bearing minerals under fluctuating water tables.
Comparative Analysis: Hydrolysis is irreversible and destroys primary silicate frameworks, whereas solution is fully reversible depending on solute concentration and temperature.
  • Goldich stability series dictates that minerals crystallizing at high temperatures melt and weather fastest at surface conditions.
  • Biotic weathering combines mechanical root pry with biochemical chelation by organic acids.

3. Climatic Controls and Morphogenetic Regions

Peltier's and Büdel's morphogenetic models linking temperature-precipitation thresholds to dominant weathering types.

  • Peltier's glacial-fluvial-aeolian matrix showing maximum chemical weathering in tropical wet regimes.
  • Büdel's concept of 'Double Planation' operating via deep basal weathering in humid tropics.
  • Spheroidal weathering and corestone development via subsurface chemical attack along intersecting joint planes.
  • Inselberg and bornhardt formation through differential sub-aerial stripping of deep saprolite mantles.
  • Impact of anthropogenic acid rain accelerating chemical weathering rates on building stones and monuments.
Comparative Analysis: Peltier's diagram contrasts sharply with modern quantitative weathering indices like the Chemical Index of Alteration (CIA).
  • Deep saprolite mantles in the tropics are often relicts of past humid climatic phases.
  • Climatic geomorphology relies on matching specific weathering regimes to distinct landscape assemblages.

Spatial Patterns & Regional Distribution

🌐 Global Geographic Belts & Spatial Variation

Chemical weathering dominates the humid equatorial and tropical belts (Amazon, Congo, Southeast Asia) where high temperatures and abundant rainfall maximize hydrolysis and leaching, producing thick latosols and bauxitic crusts. Mechanical weathering prevails in high-latitude periglacial tundras and high-altitude mountain chains (Himalayas, Andes, Alps) due to intense frost action, as well as in subtropical deserts (Sahara, Atacama) driven by extreme diurnal thermal shock and salt crystallization.

🇮🇳 Indian Subcontinent Context & Regional Corridors

India exhibits clear zonal weathering patterns: deep lateritic and saprolitic weathering zones characterize the Western Ghats, Chota Nagpur Plateau, and parts of Odisha due to high monsoonal precipitation. Granitic corestone formation and boulder tors dominate the Peninsular gneissic terrain of Karnataka and Telangana. Semi-arid mechanical weathering and salt efflorescence (reh/kallar) characterize the Indo-Gangetic plains and western Rajasthan, while intense periglacial freeze-thaw shattering operates in the Ladakh and Trans-Himalayan cold desert zones.

📐 Key Geographic Formulas, Scales & Classifications
Chemical Index of Alteration (CIA)
CIA = [Al2O3 / (Al2O3 + CaO* + Na2O + K2O)] × 100
Parameters: Molecular proportions of oxides; CaO* represents calcium strictly bound to the silicate fraction (excluding carbonates and apatites). Values near 50 indicate unweathered igneous rocks, while values approaching 100 indicate intense removal of mobile cations (Ca, Na, K) leaving residual aluminum and iron oxides.
Exam Focus: Frequently tested in UGC-NET and PGT Geography for assessing the degree of paleoweathering, chemical maturity of sedimentary basins, and climatic intensity.
Goldich Weathering Stability Series
Olivine → Anorthite → Pyroxene → Amphibole → Biotite → Albite → K-Feldspar → Muscovite → Quartz
Parameters: Ranking of common silicate minerals inverse to Bowen's Reaction Series, demonstrating that minerals crystallizing earliest at high temperatures and pressures are least stable at Earth-surface weathering conditions.
Exam Focus: Core theoretical concept for UP PGT and UGC-NET matching questions regarding mineral resistance order and soil parent material weathering sequences.

Mnemonics, Memory Shortcuts & Exam Traps

💡 Goldich Mineral Stability Order
O-P-A-B-A-K-M-Q (Only Papa Always Buys All Kids Many Quarters)

Stands for Olivine, Pyroxene, Amphibole, Biotite, Albite, K-Feldspar, Muscovite, Quartz in order of increasing resistance to weathering.

⚠️ Exam Trap: Examiners often trick candidates by reversing Bowen's series; remember Goldich is the exact reverse sequence of mineral crystallization.
💡 Types of Mechanical Weathering
U-F-S-T (Uncles Find Salt Tasty)

Stands for Unloading (pressure release), Frost wedging, Salt crystallization (haloclasty), and Thermal expansion.

⚠️ Exam Trap: Do not confuse mechanical processes like hydrolysis (which is chemical) with physical disintegration drivers.

📚 High-Yield Geographic Terminology & Definitions

Weathering UP PGT Core Concept

The on-site mechanical breakdown and chemical alteration of rocks at or near the Earth's surface by atmospheric, biotic, and water agents without major transport.

Exfoliation UGC-NET High Yield

The peeling away of concentric rock shells or slabs from the main rock mass, commonly caused by pressure release or intense thermal expansion.

Chelation UP TGT Advanced

A biological weathering process where organic acids secreted by plants and microorganisms bond with metallic cations, effectively dissolving rock minerals.

Spheroidal Weathering Landform Morphogenesis

A form of chemical weathering where jointed rectangular rock blocks are weathered into rounded, concentric boulder shapes due to faster decay along corners and edges.

Hydration Mineral Alteration

A chemical weathering process where mineral structures absorb water molecules directly into their crystal lattice, causing volumetric expansion and structural weakening.

Hydrolysis UGC-NET Favorite

The chemical reaction between hydrogen or hydroxyl ions of water and mineral ions, representing the primary mode of silicate mineral decomposition.

Oxidation Chemical Weathering

The reaction of rock minerals, particularly iron-bearing silicates, with atmospheric oxygen to form oxides and hydroxides, resulting in characteristic reddish-brown staining.

Carbonation Karst Geomorphology

The mixing of water with carbon dioxide to form weak carbonic acid, which readily dissolves carbonate rocks such as limestone and dolomite.

Peltier's Glacial-Morphogenetic Regions Climatic Geomorphology

A climatic classification diagram using mean annual temperature and rainfall to delineate dominant types of mechanical and chemical weathering across the globe.

Frost Wedging Periglacial Process

A mechanical weathering process driven by the volumetric expansion of water (~9%) when it freezes within rock fractures and pore spaces.

Thermal Exfoliation Arid Zone Process

The granular disintegration or flaking of rocks due to extreme diurnal temperature fluctuations causing differential expansion and contraction of constituent minerals.

Haloclasty Arid Weathering

Salt crystal growth within rock pores and fractures in arid or coastal environments, exerting high crystallization pressure that leads to rock disintegration.

Regolith Pedology Link

The heterogeneous layer of unconsolidated mineral and rock debris overlying solid, unweathered bedrock.

Root Pry Biogeomorphology

A biological weathering mechanism where plant roots grow into narrow rock joints, widening them through mechanical pressure.

🧭 Prescribed Scholars, Laws & Spatial Models

Louis Peltier's Morphogenetic Regions (1950)

Constructed a climatic graph correlating mean annual temperature and rainfall to define distinct zones of maximum mechanical weathering, moderate weathering, and intense chemical weathering.

Goldich's Dissolution/Weathering Stability Series (1938)

Demonstrated that the order of mineral weathering is essentially the reverse of Bowen's Reaction Series; minerals crystallizing last at lower temperatures (e.g., quartz) are most stable, while those forming first (e.g., olivine) weather most rapidly.

Julius Büdel's Climatic Geomorphology (1948)

Introduced the concept of 'Etchplains' and 'Double Weathering Fronts', emphasizing that deep chemical weathering occurs beneath a soil cover in humid tropics before being stripped to form inselbergs.

Van't Hoff's Rule of Chemical Reaction Rates (1884)

States that for every 10°C rise in temperature, the rate of chemical reactions approximately doubles, explaining why chemical weathering accelerates drastically in hot, humid equatorial regions.

Interactive Concept Practice

Instant evaluation with detailed spatial & physical explanations for each question.

Question 1 of 18
Which of the following processes is strictly classified as a mechanical (physical) weathering mechanism?

Rationale: Haloclasty (salt weathering) is a physical weathering process driven by the crystallization pressure of salts in rock pores. Carbonation, hydrolysis, and oxidation are chemical weathering processes.

Question 2 of 18
Goldich's stability series states that which of the following minerals is most resistant to weathering?

Rationale: Quartz crystallizes last in Bowen's Reaction Series under low temperatures and pressures, making it exceptionally stable against chemical and mechanical weathering according to Goldich's series.

Question 3 of 18
Peltier's climatic diagram indicates that the maximum rate of chemical weathering occurs under which climatic conditions?

Rationale: High temperatures accelerate chemical reaction rates (Van't Hoff's rule) and abundant precipitation provides the necessary moisture and leaching agents, maximizing chemical weathering in tropical rainforest regimes.

Question 4 of 18
What is the primary cause of exfoliation domes in granitic terrains?

Rationale: Exfoliation domes (like Stone Mountain in the USA) form when deeply buried intrusive igneous rocks are exposed by erosion, causing pressure release (unloading) and subsequent expansion and fracturing in concentric sheets.

Question 5 of 18
Spheroidal weathering is most commonly associated with which parent rock type possessing intersecting joints?

Rationale: Basalt and granite possess systematic joint networks. Chemical weathering penetrates these joints, attacking block corners from multiple directions and eroding them into rounded, concentric spheres.

Question 6 of 18
During the process of hydrolysis, what happens to silicate minerals like feldspar?

Rationale: Hydrolysis replaces cations in silicate structures with hydrogen ions from water, converting feldspars into secondary clay minerals (like kaolinite) while releasing soluble ions.

Question 7 of 18
Which weathering process is primarily responsible for the formation of karst topography and caverns?

Rationale: Carbonation involves carbonic acid reacting with calcium carbonate in limestone, dissolving the rock to create subterranean caverns, sinkholes, and distinctive karst landscapes.

Question 8 of 18
What percentage volume expansion occurs when liquid water freezes into ice within rock fractures?

Rationale: Water expands by approximately 9% upon freezing, generating tremendous internal pressures (up to 2000 kg/cm²) that easily fracture competent rocks.

Question 9 of 18
Which of the following environmental settings experiences the most intense mechanical weathering due to insolation?

Rationale: Hot deserts experience extreme diurnal temperature ranges, causing differential thermal expansion and contraction of minerals, leading to granular disintegration and thermal exfoliation.

Question 10 of 18
The term 'regolith' refers to:

Rationale: Regolith is the blanket of weathered rock, mineral debris, and soil that rests on unweathered solid bedrock beneath it.

Question 11 of 18
Which of the following is an example of biological weathering?

Rationale: Chelation is a biochemical process where organisms (lichens, mosses, bacteria) release organic compounds that chemically breakdown rock minerals.

Question 12 of 18
Which iron mineral undergoes oxidation to give weathered soils and rocks a characteristic rust-red or yellow color?

Rationale: Iron-bearing minerals like biotite, pyrite, and olivine oxidize when exposed to oxygen and water, forming limonite, hematite, and goethite, which impart reddish-yellow hues.

Question 13 of 18
In the context of climatic geomorphology, what is an 'Etchplain'?

Rationale: Formulated by Julius Büdel, etchplains are created through a two-stage process involving deep basal chemical weathering (etching) followed by the removal of the saprolite mantle.

Question 14 of 18
Hydration differs from hydrolysis because hydration involves:

Rationale: Hydration involves the reversible incorporation of water molecules into mineral crystal structures (e.g., anhydrite turning to gypsum), whereas hydrolysis involves ionic decomposition.

Question 15 of 18
Which rock type is most susceptible to mechanical weathering via thermal expansion and contraction of differing mineral crystals?

Rationale: Coarse-grained rocks like granite possess minerals with different thermal expansion coefficients, causing internal stresses when heated and cooled rapidly.

Question 16 of 18
The depth of weathering is generally greatest in which of the following regions?

Rationale: Humid tropical regions provide continuous warmth and moisture, sustaining deep chemical weathering profiles (saprolite) that can exceed tens of meters in thickness.

Question 17 of 18
Which weathering process causes anhydrite to transform into gypsum?

Rationale: The addition of water to anhydrous calcium sulfate (anhydrite) forms gypsum through hydration, accompanied by significant volumetric expansion.

Question 18 of 18
What is 'granular disintegration'?

Rationale: Granular disintegration occurs when coarse-grained rocks disintegrate crystal by crystal due to differential thermal expansion or chemical decay of binding agents.

Previous Years Questions & Exam Benchmarks

Real and benchmark questions aligned with UP PGT, TGT, and National Eligibility tests.

UP PGT Geography (Previous Year Pattern)
Who among the following geomorphologists formulated the climatic geomorphology model relating weathering types to mean annual temperature and rainfall?

Official Benchmark Explanation: Louis Peltier published his famous morphogenetic regions chart in 1950, which correlates climatic parameters (temperature and precipitation) with dominant geomorphic processes such as maximum mechanical or chemical weathering.

UGC-NET Geography (Previous Year Pattern)
Match List-I (Weathering Process) with List-II (Chemical/Physical Mechanism): List-I: A. Hydrolysis B. Carbonation C. Oxidation D. Haloclasty List-II: 1. Reaction with carbonic acid 2. Addition of oxygen 3. Reaction with hydrogen/hydroxyl ions 4. Crystal growth of salts Select the correct code:

Official Benchmark Explanation: Hydrolysis involves reaction with water ions (A-3); Carbonation involves carbonic acid (B-1); Oxidation involves oxygen addition (C-2); Haloclasty involves salt crystal growth (D-4). Thus, the correct sequence is A-3, B-1, C-2, D-4.

UP TGT Geography (Previous Year Pattern)
Which of the following minerals stands at the apex of maximum resistance in Goldich's Weathering Series?

Official Benchmark Explanation: Goldich's weatherability series shows that minerals forming at lower temperatures and pressures are most stable at the Earth's surface. Quartz is the most resistant mineral among common silicates.

UP PGT Geography (Previous Year Pattern)
Exfoliation domes are primarily produced by which of the following mechanisms?

Official Benchmark Explanation: Unloading (pressure release) allows plutonic rocks like granite, which formed under immense confining pressure at depth, to expand and fracture in curved, concentric sheets when exposed at the surface.

UGC-NET Geography (Previous Year Pattern)
Consider the following statements regarding weathering: 1. Chemical weathering is rapid in hot and dry desert environments. 2. Frost wedging is most active in periglacial and high-altitude regions with frequent freeze-thaw cycles. 3. Goldich's series is the inverse of Bowen's reaction series. Which of the above statements are correct?

Official Benchmark Explanation: Statement 1 is incorrect because chemical weathering requires moisture and is slowest in dry deserts. Statements 2 and 3 are correct: frost wedging thrives in freeze-thaw regimes, and Goldich's series is indeed the inverse of Bowen's reaction series.

UP PGT Geography (Previous Year Pattern)
The transformation of anhydrite into gypsum by the uptake of water molecules is an example of:

Official Benchmark Explanation: Hydration is the process where mineral structures incorporate water molecules directly into their lattice without altering chemical bonding type, as seen in the conversion of anhydrite to gypsum.

Question 1 of 10
Which of the following combinations correctly pairs a weathering process with its primary geomorphic result?

Solution: Hydrolysis chemically alters silicates into secondary clay minerals and saprolite. Exfoliation domes result from unloading, sinkholes from carbonation, and salt crusts from haloclasty/evaporation.

Question 2 of 10
Why is quartz exceptionally durable in sedimentary cycles compared to ferromagnesian silicates like olivine?

Solution: Quartz possesses a robust three-dimensional framework structure with strong silicon-oxygen bonds and lacks distinct planes of weakness (cleavage), making it highly resistant to both mechanical and chemical attack.

Question 3 of 10
In Peltier's geomorphological cycle classification, which region is characterized by 'moderate mechanical weathering and strong chemical weathering'?

Solution: In tropical and savanna climates with moderate-to-high temperatures and seasonal rainfall, chemical weathering dominates over mechanical weathering.

Question 4 of 10
What role do organic acids play in biological weathering?

Solution: Chelation involves organic ligands (acids) produced by roots, lichens, and fungi grabbing and removing metal ions (like Fe and Al) from silicate minerals, breaking down the crystal structure.

Question 5 of 10
Which of the following statements about spheroidal weathering is geomorphologically accurate?

Solution: Spheroidal weathering attacks rectangular blocks bounded by joints. Since corners are exposed to weathering from three sides and edges from two, they weather faster than flat faces, producing rounded concentric shells.

Question 6 of 10
How does vegetation indirectly influence chemical weathering rates in a forested ecosystem?

Solution: Root and microbial respiration in vegetated soils increases CO2 levels in soil air well above atmospheric levels, producing more carbonic acid upon contact with water and accelerating chemical weathering.

Question 7 of 10
Which of the following minerals is most susceptible to chemical weathering via carbonation?

Solution: Calcite, the primary mineral in limestone and marble, is readily dissolved by weak carbonic acid via carbonation.

Question 8 of 10
The breakdown of rocks by alternating wetting and drying cycles, particularly in marine or arid clay-rich environments, is known as:

Solution: Slaking is the mechanical disintegration of rocks or clay-rich sediments caused by stresses generated during rapid wetting and drying cycles.

Question 9 of 10
In the context of the double weathering front model by Julius Büdel, where does the active 'basal weathering front' lie?

Solution: The basal weathering front is the boundary where active chemical weathering attacks the fresh bedrock beneath the overlying saprolite layer.

Question 10 of 10
Why are mechanical weathering processes dominant in high-altitude alpine and polar environments?

Solution: Low temperatures inhibit chemical reaction rates, while fluctuating temperatures around 0°C create frequent freeze-thaw conditions that favor frost wedging and physical disintegration.
