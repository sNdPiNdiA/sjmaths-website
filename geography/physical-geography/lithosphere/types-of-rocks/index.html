Physical Geography — Lithosphere

Types Of Rocks: Comprehensive Geographic Analysis, Physical & Human Mechanisms, and Regional Distribution

High-yield conceptual breakdown and physical/human geographical foundations of Types Of Rocks tailored for competitive exam mastery.

UP PGT Geography UP TGT Social Science B.Sc. / M.Sc. Level

The lithosphere is fundamentally composed of rocks—naturally occurring solid aggregates of minerals or mineraloids. Lithospheric dynamics are governed by the continuous transformation of these lithic units through the Rock Cycle, driven by internal (endogenic) forces like plate tectonics, magmatism, and metamorphism, as well as external (exogenic) forces of weathering, erosion, and sedimentation. Rocks are fundamentally categorized into Igneous, Sedimentary, and Metamorphic classes based on their mode of genesis. Igneous rocks, formed via the cooling and solidification of molten magma or lava, serve as the primary or 'parent' rocks of the Earth's crust. Sedimentary rocks accumulate layer by layer through lithification of weathered detrital fragments, chemical precipitates, or organic remains, forming the principal archives of paleontological and climatic history. Metamorphic rocks emerge from pre-existing rocks subjected to intense heat, pressure, and chemically active fluids without melting. Spatial distribution of these rock types dictates regional geomorphology, soil fertility, hydrological regimes, and economic mineral belts globally and across the Indian subcontinent.

1. Igneous Rocks: Origin, Intrusive vs Extrusive Forms, and Bowens Reaction Series

Crystallization of silicate melts under varying cooling regimes dictates mineralogical composition and structural morphology.

  • Originates from cooling of magma (intrusive) or lava (extrusive).
  • Divided into Plutonic (deep-seated, slow cooling, coarse-grained like granite), Hypabyssal (shallow depth, intermediate like dolerite), and Volcanic (surface, rapid cooling, fine-grained/glassy like basalt).
  • Bowen's Reaction Series explains temperature-dependent mineral crystallization sequence from ultramafic/mafic to felsic minerals.
  • Silica content classifies them into Felsic (>66% SiO2, light-colored, e.g., granite), Intermediate (52-66%, e.g., diorite), Mafic (45-52%, dark-colored, e.g., basalt), and Ultramafic (<45%, e.g., peridotite).
  • Key structures include batholiths, laccoliths, sills, dykes, and pahoehoe/aa lava flows.
Comparative Analysis: Unlike layered sedimentary rocks, igneous rocks are massive, non-fossiliferous, and crystalline. Intrusive plutonic rocks cool slowly under high pressure yielding phaneritic textures, whereas volcanic extrusives cool rapidly yielding aphanitic or glassy textures.
  • Granite and basalt form the fundamental sialic and simatic layers of the Earth's crust.
  • Bowen's continuous and discontinuous branches dictate mineral stability under surface weathering.

2. Sedimentary Rocks: Diagenesis, Lithification, and Classification

Denudation, transport, deposition, and diagenetic compaction/cementation of detrital, chemical, or organic materials.

  • Formed via exogenic agents through weathering, mass wasting, transportation, deposition, and lithification.
  • Lithification involves compaction (reduction of pore space) and cementation (binding via silica, calcite, or iron oxide).
  • Mechanically/Clastic sedimentary rocks: Breccia, conglomerate, sandstone, shale (classified by grain size via Wentworth scale).
  • Chemically precipitated sedimentary rocks: Limestones, dolomites, rock salt, gypsum, and chert.
  • Organically/Biologically derived sedimentary rocks: Coal (peat, lignite, bituminous, anthracite), chalk, and coral limestone.
  • Characteristic structural features include stratification (bedding planes), ripple marks, mud cracks, and fossil inclusions.
Comparative Analysis: Unlike primary igneous rocks, sedimentary rocks cover 75% of the Earth's land surface exposed area but constitute only 5% of the crust's volume. They act as stratigraphic markers and hydrocarbon reservoirs.
  • Shale is the most abundant sedimentary rock, accounting for over 50% of the total sedimentary volume.
  • Fossils and paleoclimatic indicators are exclusively found in stratified sedimentary formations.

3. Metamorphic Rocks: Agents, Types, and Facies Concepts

Solid-state mineralogical and structural readjustment of parent protoliths under elevated temperature, pressure, and fluid activity.

  • Thermal (Contact) Metamorphism: Caused by high heat from magma intrusion; creates non-foliated rocks like marble (from limestone) and quartzite (from sandstone).
  • Dynamic (Cataclastic) Metamorphism: Caused by mechanical shearing along fault zones; produces fault breccia and mylonite.
  • Regional (Dynamothermal) Metamorphism: Caused by simultaneous high temperature and directed tectonic pressure; produces foliated textures like slate, phyllite, schist, and gneiss.
  • Metasomatism: Chemical alteration of rocks by hydrothermal fluids introducing new mineral components.
  • Metamorphic facies concept (Eskola): Groups of rocks formed under specific P-T stability fields (e.g., greenschist, amphibolite, granulite, eclogite).
Comparative Analysis: Igneous rocks form from melts and sedimentary rocks from surface debris, whereas metamorphic rocks retain solid-state characteristics while undergoing complete textural and mineralogical phase changes.
  • Gneissification represents extreme regional metamorphism where mineral segregation forms distinct light and dark bands.
  • Limestone converts to marble, sandstone to quartzite, and granite to gneiss.

4. The Rock Cycle and Lithospheric Dynamics

James Hutton's principle of uniformitarianism embodied in the continuous recycling of Earth's crustal materials through geological time.

  • Magma crystallizes into Igneous rocks via cooling.
  • Igneous, sedimentary, or metamorphic rocks undergo weathering and erosion to form sedimentary deposits.
  • Burial and lithification transform sediments into Sedimentary rocks.
  • Heat and pressure transform sedimentary or igneous rocks into Metamorphic rocks.
  • Extreme subduction and melting return all rock types to magma reservoirs within the asthenosphere.
  • Non-linear pathways exist, such as igneous rocks transforming directly into metamorphic rocks or sedimentary rocks melting back into magma.
Comparative Analysis: The rock cycle bridges endogenic tectonics with exogenic geomorphology, demonstrating that no rock is permanent on geological timescales.
  • The rock cycle is powered by Earth's internal thermal engine and external solar energy.
  • Coined fundamentally by James Hutton, the 'Father of Geology'.

Spatial Patterns & Regional Distribution

🌐 Global Geographic Belts & Spatial Variation

Igneous and metamorphic rocks dominate Precambrian continental shields and cratons (e.g., Canadian Shield, Baltic Shield, Brazilian Shield, Australian Shield), forming the stable continental cores. Volcanic igneous belts strictly align with global plate boundaries—specifically the Circum-Pacific 'Ring of Fire' (convergent boundaries), Mid-Atlantic Ridge (divergent boundaries), and East African Rift Valley. Extensive sedimentary basins occupy downwarped geosynclinal or cratonic basins such as the Gulf Coast Basin, Mesopotamian Basin, and the West Siberian Basin.

🇮🇳 Indian Subcontinent Context & Regional Corridors

Peninsular India is a stable Precambrian shield dominated by Archean gneissic complexes, Dharwarian schistose metasedimentary belts (rich in metallic minerals), and Purana sedimentary basins (Cuddapah and Vindhyan). The Deccan Trap region (Maharashtra, Madhya Pradesh, Gujarat) features over 500,000 sq km of horizontal Cretaceous-Eocene basaltic lava flows. The Extra-Peninsular region (Himalayas) consists of complexly folded and thrust-faulted sedimentary, metamorphic, and intrusive igneous rocks. The Indo-Gangetic alluvial plains represent a massive foredeep filled with quaternary unlithified sedimentary deposits derived from Himalayan erosion.

📐 Key Geographic Formulas, Scales & Classifications
Wentworth Sedimentary Grain Size Scale
phi (\phi) = -\log_2 (d_mm)
Parameters: phi is the Wentworth phi scale unit; d_mm is the grain diameter in millimeters. Boulders (>256mm), Cobbles (64-256mm), Pebbles (2-64mm), Sand (1/16-2mm), Silt (1/256-1/16mm), Clay (<1/256mm).
Exam Focus: Frequently tested in UGC-NET and UP PGT for matching clastic sedimentary class names with precise metric size ranges and phi values.
Geothermal Gradient Calculation
dT/dz \approx 25^\circ\text{C to } 30^\circ\text{C per km of depth}
Parameters: T is temperature in Celsius; z is depth in kilometers. Drives thermal metamorphism and melting parameters in crustal rocks.
Exam Focus: Crucial for questions addressing the depth thresholds required for greenschist to amphibolite metamorphic facies transitions in orogenic belts.

Mnemonics, Memory Shortcuts & Exam Traps

💡 Bowen's Reaction Series Mineral Crystallization Order
O-P-A-B-P-M-Q (Only Poor Archaeologists Buy Pots Made Quietly)

Stands for Olivine, Pyroxene, Amphibole, Biotite, Plagioclase Feldspar, Muscovite, Quartz in the discontinuous and continuous crystallization sequence.

⚠️ Exam Trap: Examiners often trick candidates by stating Quartz crystallizes first; remember quartz is the last mineral to crystallize (lowest temperature, most stable at Earth's surface).
💡 Metamorphic Protolith Transformation (Parent to Daughter)
LS-Q, L-M, S-S, G-G (Lush Swamps Quickly, Leave Many Soft Shells, Give Great Gems)

Sandstone becomes Quartzite; Limestone becomes Marble; Shale becomes Slate/Schist; Granite becomes Gneiss.

⚠️ Exam Trap: Do not confuse thermal contact metamorphism (non-foliated like marble/quartzite) with regional dynamothermal metamorphism (foliated like slate/gneiss/schist).

📚 High-Yield Geographic Terminology & Definitions

Lithogenesis UP PGT Core

The process of rock formation, encompassing the origin, accumulation, and consolidation of mineral particles into solid rock masses.

Diagenesis UGC-NET High Yield

The chemical and physical changes undergone by a sedimentary rock after its initial deposition and during and after its lithification.

Lithification UP TGT Standard

The process by which unconsolidated sediments are transformed into solid sedimentary rock through compaction and cementation.

Palingenesis Advanced Concept

The partial or complete melting of pre-existing rocks under extreme temperature and pressure to form new magmatic material.

Metasomatism UGC-NET Concept

The chemical alteration of a rock by hydrothermal and other fluids, involving the addition or removal of chemical components.

Batholith UP PGT Mapping

A large discordant pluton formed from cooled magma deep in the Earth's crust, typically composed of granitic rocks.

Sill UP TGT Direct

A concordant sheet-like igneous intrusion that has injected itself parallel to the bedding planes of pre-existing rock strata.

Dyke UP TGT Direct

A discordant sheet-like intrusive igneous body that cuts across the structural grain or bedding planes of country rocks.

Laccolith UP PGT Core

A dome-shaped concordant igneous intrusion that arches up the overlying strata, forming a lens-like geometry.

Cataclastic Metamorphism UGC-NET Factual

Metamorphism produced primarily by mechanical deformation and crushing of rocks along fault zones under low temperature.

Augen Gneiss UP PGT Identification

A metamorphic rock exhibiting eye-shaped feldspar porphyroblasts set in a finer-grained foliated matrix.

Ophiolite Suite UGC-NET Advanced

A distinctive assemblage of upper mantle and oceanic crust rocks found on land, indicative of ancient ocean basins.

Skarn UGC-NET Specialist

Calc-silicate rocks formed by metasomatic replacement of carbonate rocks at contacts with intrusive magmas.

Phacolith UP PGT Morphology

A concordant lens-shaped pluton occupying the crest of an anticline or the trough of a syncline.

🧭 Prescribed Scholars, Laws & Spatial Models

Bowen's Reaction Series (1928)

Demonstrates the sequence in which minerals crystallize from a cooling basaltic magma, divided into continuous (plagioclase feldspars) and discontinuous (ferromagnesian silicates) branches.

Law of Superposition (Steno's Law) (1669)

States that in undeformed stratigraphic sequences of sedimentary rocks, older layers lie at the bottom and younger layers are deposited progressively above them.

Principle of Uniformitarianism (James Hutton) (1785)

Asserts that the geological processes shaping the Earth today (like rock formation, erosion, and sedimentation) have operated uniformly throughout geological time ('the present is the key to the past').

Eskola's Metamorphic Facies Concept (1920)

Classifies metamorphic rocks based on mineral assemblages that reach chemical equilibrium under specific ranges of temperature and pressure conditions.

Question 1 of 18
Which of the following rocks is classified as an intrusive acidic igneous rock?

Rationale: Granite is an intrusive (plutonic) igneous rock with high silica content (>66%), making it acidic. Basalt and gabbro are basic/mafic, and peridotite is ultrabasic.

Question 2 of 18
Which sedimentary rock is formed through chemical precipitation rather than mechanical accumulation?

Rationale: Rock salt (halite) is a chemical sedimentary rock formed by the evaporation of saline water bodies. Sandstone, shale, and conglomerate are clastic/mechanically formed rocks.

Question 3 of 18
Which of the following metamorphic pairs represents correct parent rock to metamorphic product transition?

Rationale: Shale undergoes low-grade regional metamorphism to transform into slate. Limestone transforms to marble, and sandstone transforms to quartzite.

Question 4 of 18
A concordant, lens-shaped igneous intrusion that develops in the crest of an anticline is known as:

Rationale: A phacolith is a lens-shaped pluton located along the axes of folds, specifically in anticlinal crests or synclinal troughs.

Question 5 of 18
In Bowen's Reaction Series, which mineral is the first to crystallize from cooling magma at high temperatures?

Rationale: Olivine is the first mineral to crystallize in the discontinuous branch of Bowen's Reaction Series at the highest temperatures.

Question 6 of 18
Which type of rock covers the maximum percentage of the Earth's continental land surface?

Rationale: While igneous and metamorphic rocks form the bulk of the Earth's crust by volume, sedimentary rocks form a thin discontinuous veneer covering approximately 75% of the continental surface.

Question 7 of 18
Which of the following is an example of an organogenic (biogenic) sedimentary rock?

Rationale: Coal is an organic sedimentary rock formed from the accumulation and compaction of plant debris in swampy environments over geological epochs.

Question 8 of 18
The conversion of loose sediments into coherent solid rock through compaction and cementation is termed:

Rationale: Lithification is the specific geological term for compaction and cementation converting loose sediments into sedimentary rock.

Question 9 of 18
Which igneous structure forms a vast saucer-shaped or basin-like concordant intrusive body?

Rationale: A lopolith is a large, concordant, saucer-shaped or central depression-forming intrusive igneous mass, often associated with sagging crust.

Question 10 of 18
What is the primary mineral constituent of Marble?

Rationale: Marble is a non-foliated metamorphic rock resulting from the metamorphism of limestone or dolostone, composed primarily of recrystallized calcite (CaCO3) or dolomite.

Question 11 of 18
Which of the following rocks exhibits vesicular texture formed by trapped gas bubbles during rapid cooling?

Rationale: Pumice is an extrusive igneous rock that forms when frothy lava containing abundant gases solidifies rapidly, resulting in a vesicular, highly buoyant texture.

Question 12 of 18
Regional metamorphism is primarily driven by which of the following agents?

Rationale: Regional metamorphism affects large crustal areas and is driven by intense tectonic forces creating high pressure, differential stress, and elevated temperatures.

Question 13 of 18
Which term describes rocks formed from fragments of pre-existing rocks weathered, transported, and deposited?

Rationale: Clastic (or detrital) sedimentary rocks are composed of mineral fragments and rock debris derived from mechanical weathering.

Question 14 of 18
Which scale is used by geologists to determine the relative hardness of minerals comprising rocks?

Rationale: Mohs hardness scale ranks minerals from Talc (1) to Diamond (10) based on scratch resistance, aiding in rock-forming mineral identification.

Question 15 of 18
What type of texture is characterized by alternating light and dark mineral bands in metamorphic rocks like gneiss?

Rationale: Gneissic banding is a type of metamorphic foliation where dark-colored ferromagnesian minerals and light-colored quartz/feldspar segregate into distinct bands under intense directed pressure.

Question 16 of 18
Which of the following is classified as an intermediate igneous rock?

Rationale: Andesite is an intermediate extrusive igneous rock with silica content ranging between 52% and 63%, sitting between mafic basalt and felsic rhyolite.

Question 17 of 18
Which geological process destroys sedimentary, igneous, and metamorphic rocks by melting them down into magma?

Rationale: Anatexis or melting under extreme sub-crustal temperatures converts any rock type back into primary magma, completing the rock cycle.

Question 18 of 18
Quartzite is formed due to the thermal or regional metamorphism of which parent rock?

Rationale: Quartzite is a dense, hard non-foliated metamorphic rock formed when quartz-rich sandstone is subjected to heat and pressure.

UP PGT Geography (Previous Year Pattern)
Who propounded the concept of 'Bowen's Reaction Series' explaining the crystallization of minerals from magma?

Official Benchmark Explanation: N.L. Bowen (1928) experimentally demonstrated the sequence of mineral crystallization from cooling basaltic magma, a foundational concept in petrology tested frequently in UP PGT.

UGC-NET Geography (Previous Year Pattern)
Match List-I (Rock Type) with List-II (Genesis/Category): (a) Slate - 1. Chemical Sedimentary (b) Gabbro - 2. Metamorphic (c) Dolomite - 3. Intrusive Igneous (d) Obsidian - 4. Extrusive Igneous (Glassy) Correct Code:

Official Benchmark Explanation: Slate is metamorphic (from shale), Gabbro is intrusive basic igneous, Dolomite is a chemical/biochemical sedimentary carbonate rock, and Obsidian is extrusive volcanic glass.

UP TGT Geography (Previous Year Pattern)
Which of the following is NOT a primary metamorphic rock feature?

Official Benchmark Explanation: Bedding planes are characteristic primary depositional features of sedimentary rocks, whereas foliation, lineation, and schistosity are characteristic features developed during metamorphism.

UP PGT Geography (Previous Year Pattern)
Batholiths are large granitic intrusive bodies typically associated with:

Official Benchmark Explanation: Batholiths are massive discordant intrusive plutons exposed extensively in continental shields and the roots or cores of major orogenic fold mountain belts.

UGC-NET Geography (Previous Year Pattern)
Consider the following statements regarding sedimentary rocks: 1. They are stratified and stratified layers are called beds. 2. They cover about 75% of the Earth's continental surface. 3. They contain fossils of plants and animals. Which of the above statements are correct?

Official Benchmark Explanation: All statements are correct. Sedimentary rocks display stratification (bedding), form a major land surface veneer (75%), and preserve organic fossils crucial for paleontology and relative geochronology.

Question 1 of 10
Which of the following mineral groups forms the most abundant component of the Earth's crust by weight?

Solution: Silicates (such as quartz, feldspars, micas, and pyroxenes) make up approximately 92% of the Earth's crustal minerals by volume and constitute the primary building blocks of most rocks.

Question 2 of 10
Contact metamorphism differs from regional metamorphism primarily because it is driven by:

Solution: Contact (thermal) metamorphism occurs locally around intrusive igneous bodies where high temperature and low differential pressure recrystallize the surrounding country rocks (thermal aureole).

Question 3 of 10
Which intrusive igneous landform cuts across the structural bedding planes of country rock at an angle?

Solution: Dykes are discordant intrusive sheets that cut across the pre-existing bedding planes or structures of the host rock, whereas sills are concordant and lie parallel to them.

Question 4 of 10
Which of the following sedimentary rock types is formed through the accumulation of skeletal remains composed of silica (e.g., radiolaria or diatoms)?

Solution: Chert (and flint) can form biogenically from the accumulation and lithification of siliceous microfossil skeletons like diatoms, radiolarians, or sponge spicules.

Question 5 of 10
What is the correct sequence of decreasing silica content among the following igneous rocks?

Solution: Granite is felsic (>66% silica), Andesite is intermediate (52-63%), Basalt is mafic (45-52%), and Peridotite is ultramafic (<45% silica).

Question 6 of 10
Which metamorphic rock displays non-foliated, interlocking mosaic textures of quartz crystals rendering it exceptionally weather-resistant?

Solution: Quartzite is a non-foliated metamorphic rock composed of interlocking quartz crystals, making it extremely durable and resistant to chemical weathering and erosion, often forming prominent ridges.

Question 7 of 10
The process by which distinct mineralogical bands form in gneissic rocks under extreme directed pressure is called:

Solution: Metamorphic differentiation is the chemical segregation of minerals into light and dark bands under high-grade metamorphic conditions.

Question 8 of 10
Which term designates the totality of physical, chemical, and biological changes experienced by sediments from deposition to lithification?

Solution: Diagenesis encompasses all low-temperature, low-pressure transformations that occur in sediments after deposition, including compaction, cementation, and authigenesis.

Question 9 of 10
Which of the following pairs of extrusive and intrusive igneous rocks share identical mineralogical and chemical compositions?

Solution: Rhyolite is the extrusive (volcanic) equivalent of granite (plutonic/intrusive), sharing the same felsic mineral composition but differing in crystal texture due to cooling rate.

Question 10 of 10
In the context of the rock cycle, which transformation represents the path from a metamorphic rock to a sedimentary rock?

Solution: Exposed metamorphic rocks undergo subjection to surface exogenic processes: uplift, weathering, erosion, transportation, deposition, and subsequent lithification to become sedimentary rocks.
