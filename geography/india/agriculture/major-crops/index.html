India — Agriculture

Major Crops: Comprehensive Geographic Analysis, Physical & Human Mechanisms, and Regional Distribution

High-yield conceptual breakdown and physical/human geographical foundations of Major Crops tailored for competitive exam mastery.

UP PGT Geography UP TGT Social Science B.Sc. / M.Sc. Level

The geography of Indian agriculture reflects a complex interplay of physiographic variations, agro-climatic zones, and historical institutional frameworks. Crop distribution is fundamentally governed by deterministic physical parameters including thermal regimes, precipitation thresholds, and edaphic conditions, which are subsequently modified by human interventions such as irrigation networks, high-yielding variety (HYV) seeds, and minimum support prices (MSP). For aspirants of UP TGT/PGT Geography and UGC-NET, mastering this domain requires shifting beyond mere rote memorization of top-producing states to understanding the underlying locational theories (such as Von Thünen's spatial land-use model), moisture indices, and crop-combination regions proposed by J.C. Weaver and Rafiullah.

1. Agro-Climatic Determinants and Cereal Crop Distributions (Rice and Wheat)

Cereal production is dictated by thermal-moisture regimes, soil classification, and critical growing-degree-days (GDD).

  • Rice requires high temperature (>24°C) and heavy rainfall (>100 cm) or assured irrigation; alluvial clayey soils are ideal due to high water-retention capacity.
  • Primary rice-producing states include West Bengal, Uttar Pradesh, Punjab, and Andhra Pradesh, with coastal deltas forming surplus baskets.
  • Wheat is a rabi crop requiring a cool growing season (10°C to 15°C) and bright sunshine at ripening, with moderate rainfall (50-75 cm).
  • Major wheat belts are concentrated in North-West India, specifically Punjab, Haryana, Western Uttar Pradesh, and Madhya Pradesh.
Comparative Analysis: Contrasts Kharif (monsoon-dependent, water-intensive like rice) vs Rabi (winter, temperate-adapted like wheat) thermal-moisture requirements.
  • Punjab and Haryana exhibit high land productivity despite low natural precipitation due to intensive groundwater exploitation and canal irrigation.
  • Eastern India possesses high potential for rice but suffers from lower yield gaps compared to the north-west.

2. Commercial and Cash Crops: Cotton, Sugarcane, and Jute Geographies

Industrial crop localization is governed by the raw material weight-loss principle and specific edaphic-thermal thresholds.

  • Cotton requires 210 frost-free days, bright sunshine, and moderate rainfall (50-100 cm); Regur (Black Cotton soil) derived from Deccan traps is optimal.
  • Major cotton zones span Maharashtra, Gujarat, Telangana, and parts of Punjab/Haryana (Black and Alluvial soils).
  • Sugarcane is a long-duration tropical/sub-tropical crop requiring high temperature (21-27°C) and heavy rainfall (75-100 cm); it is a heavy consumer of water.
  • Uttar Pradesh and Maharashtra dominate sugarcane production, with a historical shift toward the peninsular zone due to higher sugar recovery rates.
  • Jute requires hot, humid climates (24-35°C), high rainfall (>150 cm), and is concentrated almost exclusively in the Ganga-Brahmaputra delta (West Bengal, Assam).
Comparative Analysis: Compares raw material-oriented location of Jute and Sugarcane processing versus market-oriented agro-industries.
  • Regur soils possess self-plowing properties and high moisture-holding capacity critical for dry-land cotton farming.
  • Peninsular sugarcane yields surpass northern plains due to frost-free winters and longer crushing seasons.

3. Millets (Nutri-Cereals), Pulses, and Oilseed Geographies

Rainfed agricultural systems dominate marginal lands, utilizing drought-resistant physiological traits.

  • Millets (Jowar, Bajra, Ragi) are hardy, drought-tolerant crops grown in semi-arid regions (Rain shadow areas of Western Ghats and Rajasthan).
  • Bajra thrives on sandy soils in Rajasthan; Jowar dominates Maharashtra and Karnataka on shallow black/mixed red soils.
  • Pulses (Gram, Tur/Arhar, Urad, Mungo) fix atmospheric nitrogen, requiring low moisture and minimal irrigation, serving as vital rotation crops.
  • Madhya Pradesh, Rajasthan, and Maharashtra lead in total pulse production.
  • Oilseeds (Groundnut, Mustard, Soyabean, Sunflower) are critical for edible oil security; Gujarat leads in groundnut, Rajasthan in rapeseed-mustard.
Comparative Analysis: Contrasts green revolution cereal-centric zones with dryland farming and nutri-cereal distribution in arid/semi-arid tracts.
  • Nutri-cereals form the backbone of food security in dryland zones and are heavily emphasized under the National Food Security Mission.
  • Yellow Revolution initiatives target bridging the domestic edible oil deficit through mustard and soybean expansion.

Spatial Patterns & Regional Distribution

🌐 Global Geographic Belts & Spatial Variation

Global crop distribution follows latitudinal climatic belts: equatorial and tropical zones favor plantation crops and rice; mid-latitudes host extensive wheat and maize belts; Mediterranean zones specialize in viticulture and citrus fruits.

🇮🇳 Indian Subcontinent Context & Regional Corridors

India is divided into 15 Agro-Climatic Zones by the Planning Commission. The Indo-Gangetic Plain serves as the food-grain granary (rice-wheat system); the Deccan Plateau supports cotton, jowar, and oilseeds; the Eastern and North-Eastern regions sustain rice and jute; and the Western arid tracts specialize in bajra and pulses.

📐 Key Geographic Formulas, Scales & Classifications
Weaver's Crop Combination Method
Standard Deviation Formula: S = \sqrt{\frac{\sum (d^2)}{n}}
Parameters: d = difference between actual crop percentage in a given area and the theoretical percentage; n = number of crops in combination.
Exam Focus: Frequently asked in UGC-NET and PGT Geography to determine the minimum number of crops required to account for a cumulative percentage of agricultural land use.
Coppock's Instability Index
V = \frac{\log (X_{t+1} / X_t)}{N}
Parameters: X = area or production of a crop in year t and t+1; N = number of years. Used to measure agricultural risk and yield fluctuations.
Exam Focus: Core statistical tool in agricultural geography questions regarding crop diversification and production volatility.

📚 High-Yield Geographic Terminology & Definitions

Kharif Crops UP TGT/PGT Core

Monsoon-dependent crops sown with the onset of the southwest monsoon in June-July and harvested in September-October.

Rabi Crops UP TGT/PGT Core

Winter season crops sown from October to December and harvested in spring (April-May), requiring mild winters and warm maturity.

Zaid Crops UGC-NET Concept

Short duration summer crops grown between Rabi and Kharif seasons (March to June), mainly comprising watermelons, cucumbers, and fodder.

Aman, Aus, and Boro Regional Geography

The three distinct cropping seasons of rice cultivation in West Bengal and eastern India, corresponding to winter, autumn, and summer rice.

Golden Revolution Economic Geography

A period of phenomenal agricultural production growth in India specifically associated with horticulture, honey, and fruit production.

Operation Flood UGC-NET Core

The world's largest agricultural dairy development program launched in 1970 by NDDB, transforming India into the largest milk producer.

Green Revolution Agricultural Geography

Mid-20th century initiative introducing High Yielding Varieties (HYV) of seeds, chemical fertilizers, and irrigation, chiefly boosting wheat and rice.

Ratooning Agrarian Terminology

An agricultural practice of harvesting a crop by leaving the roots intact to allow the stubble to sprout into a secondary crop, common in sugarcane.

Shifting Cultivation Tribal/Primitive Agri

A primitive system of agriculture where plots of land are cleared, farmed for a few years, and then abandoned for soil fertility restoration.

Commercial Grain Farming Agri-Types

Extensive, mechanized agricultural system where single crops like wheat or maize are grown primarily for commercial export and market sale.

Plantation Agriculture Economic Geography

A form of capital-intensive single-crop commercial farming spanning large estates, focusing on tea, coffee, rubber, and spices.

Minimum Support Price (MSP) Agri-Policy

A form of market intervention by the Government of India to insure agricultural producers against any sharp fall in farm prices.

Buffer Stock Economic Geography

Reserve stock of food grains maintained by the Food Corporation of India (FCI) to ensure food security and manage price volatility.

🧭 Prescribed Scholars, Laws & Spatial Models

Johann Heinrich von Thünen (1826)

Isolated State Model: Explains agricultural land-use patterns based on concentric rings around a central market, where intensity and crop type are governed by transport costs and perishability.

Norman Borlaug (1960s)

High-Yielding Varieties (HYV) Paradigm: Developed semi-dwarf, disease-resistant, high-responsive wheat and rice varieties that catalyzed the Green Revolution across developing nations.

M. S. Swaminathan (2004-2006)

National Commission on Farmers (NCF): Recommended comprehensive agrarian reforms, including fixing MSP at 50% above the comprehensive cost of production (C2) and sustainable farming practices.

E. Willard Miller & J. F. Hart (Mid-20th Century)

Agro-climatic Regionalization: Classification of agricultural landscapes based on physical parameters (temperature, rainfall, soil) and socio-economic variables guiding regional planning.

Question 1 of 18
Which among the following is the leading producer of sugarcane in India?

Rationale: Uttar Pradesh is the largest producer of sugarcane in India, followed by Maharashtra and Karnataka. UP's Gangetic plain provides fertile alluvial soil and adequate irrigation suitable for sugarcane.

Question 2 of 18
Aman, Aus, and Boro are traditional varieties of which crop in India?

Rationale: Aman (winter), Aus (autumn), and Boro (summer) are the three cropping seasons of rice cultivated extensively in eastern states like West Bengal, Odisha, and Assam.

Question 3 of 18
Which crop requires high temperature, high humidity, and annual rainfall above 100 cm, primarily grown in tropical and sub-tropical regions?

Rationale: Rice is a tropical crop that requires high temperatures (above 25°C), high humidity, and heavy rainfall (above 100 cm) or assured irrigation.

Question 4 of 18
Which of the following states is the largest producer of wheat in India?

Rationale: Uttar Pradesh consistently ranks as the top producer of wheat in India, followed closely by Madhya Pradesh and Punjab in total volume.

Question 5 of 18
Which state is known as the 'Granary of India' due to its high wheat productivity and contribution to the central pool?

Rationale: Punjab is known as the granary of India because of its exceptionally high agricultural productivity per hectare and massive marketable surplus of wheat and rice.

Question 6 of 18
Which soil type is most ideal for the cultivation of cotton in the peninsular region of India?

Rationale: Black cotton soil, also known as Regur soil, has high moisture retention capacity and is rich in lime, iron, magnesium, and alumina, making it perfect for cotton cultivation.

Question 7 of 18
Tea cultivation requires which of the following environmental conditions?

Rationale: Tea is a plantation crop that thrives on well-drained, rolling hill slopes where water stagnation is avoided, combined with frequent, distributed heavy rainfall.

Question 8 of 18
Which of the following is categorized as a coarse grain or 'Shree Anna' (Millets) highlighted for climate-resilient agriculture?

Rationale: Bajra, along with Jowar and Ragi, is a millet requiring low moisture and hardy soil conditions, making it an ideal climate-resilient crop.

Question 9 of 18
Jute cultivation in India is predominantly concentrated in which river basin?

Rationale: The Ganga-Brahmaputra delta, particularly West Bengal, produces the bulk of India's jute due to fertile flood-replenished alluvial soils and humid climate.

Question 10 of 18
Which Indian state is the leading producer of coffee?

Rationale: Karnataka accounts for over 70% of India's total coffee production, followed by Kerala and Tamil Nadu.

Question 11 of 18
Which oilseed crop covers the largest area and forms a major kharif oilseed in India?

Rationale: Groundnut is a major kharif oilseed crop in India, extensively grown in Gujarat, Andhra Pradesh, and Tamil Nadu.

Question 12 of 18
Operation Flood is most directly associated with which of the following?

Rationale: Operation Flood, launched in 1970, established the milk grid across India, making it the world's largest agricultural dairy development program.

Question 13 of 18
Which crop is known as 'Golden Fibre' in Indian agriculture?

Rationale: Jute is known as the 'Golden Fibre' due to its color, high cash value, and versatile utility in packaging and textiles.

Question 14 of 18
Which agro-climatic zone in India is the primary belt for rubber cultivation?

Rationale: Rubber requires equatorial conditions—high temperature (above 25°C) and heavy rainfall (above 200 cm)—making the Western Ghats (especially Kerala) ideal.

Question 15 of 18
What is the primary objective of introducing Minimum Support Prices (MSP) in India?

Rationale: MSP acts as a safety net for farmers, assuring a guaranteed price by the government regardless of market gluts or price crashes.

Question 16 of 18
Which of the following pulses is cultivated during the rabi season in India?

Rationale: Gram (chana) is the most dominant rabi pulse in India, whereas tur, moong, and urad are predominantly kharif or summer pulses.

Question 17 of 18
Which state leads in the production of soybean in India?

Rationale: Madhya Pradesh is famously known as the 'Soy State' of India, accounting for the highest production and acreage of soybean.

Question 18 of 18
Which type of farming involves growing crops and raising livestock simultaneously on the same farm?

Rationale: Mixed farming combines crop cultivation with livestock rearing, providing economic stability and fodder-crop rotation benefits.

In which Indian state is the 'Boro' variety of rice predominantly cultivated?

Official Benchmark Explanation: Boro rice is the summer rice crop cultivated in the low-lying wet lands (chars or bils) of West Bengal and Assam during the dry months with intensive irrigation.

Question 1 of 10
Consider the following statements regarding Indian agriculture: 1. Rice is grown under rainfed conditions in eastern India but requires intensive irrigation in northwestern states. 2. Wheat is a temperature-sensitive rabi crop requiring bright sunshine during ripening. Which of the statements is/are correct?

Solution: Both statements are correct. Eastern India receives heavy monsoon rainfall making rice rainfed, whereas Punjab/Haryana grow rice using extensive groundwater irrigation. Wheat requires cool growing seasons and bright sunshine during harvest.

Question 2 of 10
Which of the following pairs of Crop and its major producing state is incorrectly matched?

Solution: Jute is predominantly grown in West Bengal and the Ganga-Brahmaputra delta region, not in Maharashtra. Maharashtra is prominent in cotton, sugarcane, and onion.

Question 3 of 10
What is the primary reason behind the success of wheat cultivation in northwestern India (Punjab and Haryana) compared to peninsular India?

Solution: Northwestern India benefits from winter rains brought by Western Disturbances and extensive canal networks originating from Himalayan rivers, ideal for rabi wheat.

Question 4 of 10
Which geographical factor is most critical for the localization of tea plantations in the Darjeeling and Assam hills?

Solution: Tea bushes cannot tolerate stagnant water at their roots; hence, hill slopes with well-drained acidic soil, high humidity, and abundant rainfall are essential.

Question 5 of 10
Millets (Jowar, Bajra, Ragi) are gaining prominence in Indian agricultural policy primarily because:

Solution: Millets ('Shree Anna') are hardy, drought-tolerant crops that thrive in dryland farming conditions with minimal inputs, enhancing nutritional and climate security.

Question 6 of 10
Which of the following correctly describes 'Ratooning' in sugarcane cultivation?

Solution: Ratooning involves harvesting the cane by cutting it close to the ground, leaving the underground rootstock intact to produce subsequent ratoon crops.

Question 7 of 10
Which crop zone forms the core of the 'Green Revolution Belt' in India during its initial phase in the 1960s?

Solution: The initial phase of the Green Revolution was concentrated in Punjab, Haryana, and Western Uttar Pradesh due to assured canal irrigation and responsive farmers.

Question 8 of 10
In the context of Indian cropping seasons, which of the following falls under the 'Zaid' category?

Solution: Zaid crops are grown during the summer months between Rabi and Kharif, primarily featuring watermelons, cucumbers, muskmelons, and green vegetables.

Question 9 of 10
Which of the following states is the leading producer of pulses in India?

Solution: Madhya Pradesh is the largest producer of total pulses in India, followed by Maharashtra, Rajasthan, and Uttar Pradesh.

Question 10 of 10
Which agro-climatic zone classification is widely used by the Planning Commission of India for agricultural planning?

Solution: The Planning Commission of India classified the country into 15 broad agro-climatic zones based on topography, soil, climate, and water resources.
