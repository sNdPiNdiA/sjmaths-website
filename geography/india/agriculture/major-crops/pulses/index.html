India — Agriculture

Pulses: Comprehensive Geographic Analysis, Physical & Human Mechanisms, and Regional Distribution

High-yield conceptual breakdown and physical/human geographical foundations of Pulses tailored for competitive exam mastery.

UP PGT Geography UP TGT Social Science B.Sc. / M.Sc. Level

Pulses constitute the foundational grain legumes of Indian agriculture, playing a critical dual role in nutritional security and ecological sustainability via biological nitrogen fixation (BNF). Geographically, India stands as the world's largest producer, consumer, and importer of pulses, cultivated predominantly under rainfed conditions (nearly 85% of total area) with high spatial vulnerability to monsoon aberrations. The spatial distribution exhibits distinct cropping zones aligned with semi-arid and sub-humid tropical climates, primarily concentrated in the peninsular plateau, central highlands, and the northern plains. From an examination perspective (UP TGT/PGT Geography and UGC-NET), questions frequently interrogate agro-climatic requirements, soil-moisture relationships, the paradox of low yields vis-a-vis high total production, and shifting regional concentration towards Madhya Pradesh, Rajasthan, and Maharashtra. Understanding the physiological drought tolerance of crops like chickpea (Gram) and pigeonpea (Tur) provides crucial insight into dryland agricultural geography, marginal land utilization, and sustainable cropping intensity frameworks.

1. Agro-Climatic Requirements and Physiological Mechanisms

Pulses are drought-tolerant, thermo-sensitive crops requiring moderate rainfall and well-drained light soils, operating primarily under dryland farming paradigms.

  • Optimum temperature range: 20°C to 30°C across growing phases.
  • Rainfall requirement: Low to moderate (400mm to 750mm annually); highly sensitive to waterlogging.
  • Soil preference: Light loamy to heavy clay soils; neutral pH (6.5 to 7.5) ideal for Rhizobium symbiosis.
  • Photoperiod sensitivity: Most pulses are short-day or day-neutral plants influencing flowering periods.
  • Root system: Deep taproot system enables utilization of residual soil moisture from lower horizons.
Comparative Analysis: Unlike water-guzzling cereals (Paddy and Wheat) that depend on assured canal or tube-well irrigation, pulses act as low-water-footprint crops utilizing residual moisture in post-monsoon (Rabi) or pre-monsoon (Kharif) seasons.
  • Pulses cannot tolerate water stagnation or frost during flowering.
  • Excess nitrogen fertilizer inhibits symbiotic nitrogen fixation, requiring phosphorus-heavy applications instead.

2. Production, Trends, and Spatial Distribution Dynamics in India

Pulse production in India is characterized by structural shifts from traditional rainfed northern plains to central-western peninsular plateaus, driven by technological interventions and MSP policies.

  • India accounts for ~25% of global pulse production and ~27% of global consumption.
  • Major seasons: Rabi pulses contribute over 60% of total production (led by Gram/Chickpea), while Kharif contributes ~35% (led by Tur/Pigeonpea).
  • Geographic shift: Madhya Pradesh emerges as the top producing state, followed by Rajasthan, Maharashtra, Uttar Pradesh, and Karnataka.
  • Irrigation deficit: Less than 20% of the total pulse area has assured irrigation access, creating high yield elasticity to monsoon behavior.
  • Yellow Revolution: Strategic initiatives like NFSM (National Food Security Mission-Pulses) successfully bridged the productivity gap post-2010.
Comparative Analysis: Contrasts with the Green Revolution bias towards wheat and rice in northwestern India; pulses represent the core pillar of dryland agricultural diversification and sustainable crop rotation.
  • Madhya Pradesh dominates total acreage and production.
  • Gram (Chickpea) is the single largest pulse crop grown in India.

3. Ecological Role, Cropping Systems, and Agronomic Integration

Pulses function as biological fertilizers and risk-mitigation buffers in intensive cropping systems through atmospheric nitrogen fixation and stubble nutrient recycling.

  • Biological Nitrogen Fixation (BNF): Fixes 30-150 kg N/ha via symbiotic Rhizobium bacteria in root nodules.
  • Crop rotation synergy: Breaks pest-disease cycles of cereal monocultures and improves soil aggregation.
  • Intercropping models: Widely integrated with millets, sorghum, and cotton to maximize land equivalent ratios (LER).
  • Stubble management: Post-harvest residue leaves behind organic carbon and labile nitrogen, reducing chemical fertilizer dependency.
  • Climate resilience: Acts as an insurance crop against aberrant monsoon failures due to deep root architecture.
Comparative Analysis: Compares favorably with chemical nitrogenous fertilizers which cause groundwater nitrate leaching and greenhouse gas emissions (N2O).
  • Essential component of sustainable low-input sustainable agriculture (LISA).
  • Significantly enhances soil microbiology and enzymatic activity.

Spatial Patterns & Regional Distribution

🌐 Global Geographic Belts & Spatial Variation

Pulses are cultivated extensively across semi-arid tropics and temperate dry zones. South Asia (India) is the primary global hub, followed by Sub-Saharan Africa (Eastern and Southern dry belts), North America (Canada for dry peas and lentils), and Australia. Lentils and chickpeas dominate Mediterranean and West Asian agricultural landscapes, while dry beans thrive in Latin America.

🇮🇳 Indian Subcontinent Context & Regional Corridors

The Vindhyan and Malwa plateaus of Madhya Pradesh, the semi-arid western plains of Rajasthan, the Deccan Plateau tracts of Maharashtra and Karnataka, and the Gangetic alluvial plains of Uttar Pradesh constitute the core pulse-producing regions. Gram dominates the central and northern tablelands, whereas Tur (Pigeonpea) and Urad/Mung occupy peninsular and inter-mountainous valley systems.

📐 Key Geographic Formulas, Scales & Classifications
Land Equivalent Ratio (LER)
LER = (Intercrop Yield A / Sole Yield A) + (Intercrop Yield B / Sole Yield B)
Parameters: Yield A & B represent individual crop yields in intercropping vs sole cropping systems. LER > 1 indicates biological advantage of pulse integration.
Exam Focus: Frequently tested in UGC-NET Geography and Agriculture Geography sections to evaluate intercropping efficiency involving pulses and cereals.
Cropping Intensity Index (CII) as applied to Double-Cropping with Pulses
CII = (Gross Cropped Area / Net Sown Area) × 100
Parameters: Gross Cropped Area includes area sown more than once in an agricultural year; Net Sown Area is the physical land area cultivated.
Exam Focus: Used in UP PGT to analyze how short-duration catch-crops like Greengram (Moong) raised during the Zaid season elevate regional cropping intensity.

Mnemonics, Memory Shortcuts & Exam Traps

💡 Top Pulse Producing States in India
MR UPU KA

M - Madhya Pradesh (1st), R - Rajasthan (2nd), UP - Uttar Pradesh, U - Karnataka (or Uttarakhand context), KA - Maharashtra/Karnataka shifting ranks.

⚠️ Exam Trap: Examiners often trap students by listing historical data where UP was leading; Madhya Pradesh is currently the undisputable leader in total pulse production.
💡 Kharif vs Rabi Pulse Classification
Kharif = T-U-M (Tur, Urad, Moong); Rabi = C-L (Chickpea/Gram, Lentil)

TUM represents major monsoon-season pulses requiring warm temperatures; CL represents cool-season dry winter pulses.

⚠️ Exam Trap: Do not confuse Gram as a Kharif crop; Chickpea (Gram) is exclusively a Rabi season crop dependent on winter retreating monsoon or residual soil moisture.

📚 High-Yield Geographic Terminology & Definitions

Leguminous Crops UP PGT Geography

Plants belonging to the Fabaceae family that form a symbiotic relationship with Rhizobium bacteria to fix atmospheric nitrogen into the soil.

Gram (Chana) UGC-NET Geography

The most dominant pulse crop in India, accounting for nearly 40-50% of total pulse production, thriving best in semi-arid and rainfed conditions.

Pigeon Pea (Arhar/Tur) UP TGT Geography

A major Kharif pulse crop characterized by a deep root system, making it highly drought-resistant and suitable for intercropping with millets or cotton.

Moong Bean UP PGT Geography

A short-duration Kharif and summer pulse crop valued for its high digestibility, quick maturing cycle, and role in green manuring.

Urad Bean (Black Gram) UGC-NET Geography

A kharif and zaid season pulse extensively cultivated in heavy clay soils of river floodplains, crucial for preparing traditional dishes like dal makhani.

Masur (Lentil) UP TGT Geography

A major Rabi season pulse crop grown widely in the Indo-Gangetic plains as a relay crop or after the harvest of Kharif paddy.

Symbiotic Nitrogen Fixation UGC-NET Geography

The biological process where Rhizobium bacteria residing in root nodules of pulses convert atmospheric nitrogen into plant-usable ammonia.

Minimum Support Price (MSP) UP PGT Geography

An agricultural product price floor set by the Government of India to safeguard pulse producers against sudden market price crashes.

National Food Security Mission (NFSM-Pulses) UP TGT Geography

A centrally sponsored scheme launched in 2007-08 to enhance the production of pulses through area expansion and productivity enhancement.

Dryland Agriculture UGC-NET Geography

Crop production systems relying entirely on natural rainfall without irrigation, highly characteristic of India's rainfed pulse-producing regions.

Intercropping UP PGT Geography

The agricultural practice of cultivating two or more crops simultaneously on the same field, commonly practiced with pulses and cereals.

Protein Security UP TGT Geography

The concept emphasizing the dietary availability of affordable plant-based proteins, where pulses serve as the primary source for vegetarian populations.

Rainfed Agro-Ecosystem UGC-NET Geography

Farming zones dependent on monsoon precipitation where pulses act as climate-resilient insurance crops against drought.

🧭 Prescribed Scholars, Laws & Spatial Models

Von Thünen's Isolated State Model (1826)

Explains agricultural land use patterns based on transportation costs and market distance, where extensive crops like pulses and grains occupy outer concentric rings.

Weaver's Crop Combination Method (1954)

A statistical technique used by geographers to determine regional agricultural specialization, highlighting districts where pulses form dominant secondary or tertiary crop combinations.

Bhatia's Agricultural Efficiency Index (1965)

Measures agricultural productivity by comparing local crop output per hectare with national averages, heavily influenced by the adoption of high-yielding pulse varieties.

Green Revolution Spatial Diffusion Model (Based on Hägerstrand) (1968)

Demonstrates how agricultural innovations spread spatially; while cereals experienced rapid diffusion, pulses lagged behind due to lack of high-yielding dwarf varieties in early phases.

Question 1 of 18
Which state is the leading producer of pulses in India as per recent agricultural statistics?

Rationale: Madhya Pradesh consistently accounts for the highest share of total pulse production in India, followed closely by Maharashtra and Rajasthan.

Question 2 of 18
Which of the following pulse crops is predominantly grown during the Kharif season in India?

Rationale: Pigeon pea (Arhar/Tur) is a long-duration Kharif crop sown with the onset of the monsoon and harvested around December-January.

Question 3 of 18
Why are pulses considered ideal for sustainable and climate-resilient agriculture?

Rationale: Pulses harbor Rhizobium bacteria in their root nodules which fix atmospheric nitrogen, reducing the need for synthetic fertilizers and conserving soil health.

Question 4 of 18
Which Indian state ranks first in the production of Gram (Chana)?

Rationale: Madhya Pradesh is the largest producer of Gram (Chickpea) in India, benefiting from favorable black soils and semi-arid climatic conditions.

Question 5 of 18
The National Food Security Mission (NFSM) for pulses was launched in which academic/financial year?

Rationale: NFSM was launched in 2007-08 by the Government of India to boost the production of rice, wheat, and pulses through area expansion and productivity enhancement.

Question 6 of 18
Which agro-climatic zone in India accounts for the largest concentration of pulse cultivation?

Rationale: Pulses are primarily rainfed crops thriving in the semi-arid, dryland tracts of Central and Western India including Madhya Pradesh, Rajasthan, and Maharashtra.

Question 7 of 18
Which pulse is commonly known as 'Red Gram'?

Rationale: Pigeon pea, scientifically known as Cajanus cajan, is widely referred to as Red Gram or Arhar/Tur.

Question 8 of 18
What is the primary reason India remains a net importer of pulses despite being a leading global producer?

Rationale: India is the world's largest consumer and producer of pulses, but high domestic demand driven by dietary preferences requires supplementing production with imports from Canada, Myanmar, and African nations.

Question 9 of 18
Which of the following is a Zaid season pulse crop?

Rationale: Moong can be cultivated as a short-duration catch crop during the Zaid (summer) season between the Rabi harvest and Kharif sowing.

Question 10 of 18
In terms of root system architecture, why are pulses well-suited for dryland farming?

Rationale: Many pulses, especially pigeon pea and chickpea, have deep taproot systems that penetrate deep soil profiles to tap residual moisture during dry spells.

Question 11 of 18
Which state is a leading producer of Tur (Pigeon pea) in peninsular India?

Rationale: Karnataka, along with Maharashtra and Madhya Pradesh, is a major producer of Tur in the southern peninsular plateau region.

Question 12 of 18
Which organism forms a mutualistic symbiotic association with pulse crops for nitrogen fixation?

Rationale: Rhizobium bacteria infect the roots of leguminous plants, forming nodules where atmospheric nitrogen is reduced to ammonia.

Question 13 of 18
What percentage approximately of global pulse production does India contribute?

Rationale: India is the largest producer (roughly 25% of global production), consumer (27%), and importer (14%) of pulses in the world.

Question 14 of 18
Which soil type is generally considered most suitable for cultivating Gram (Chana)?

Rationale: Gram thrives best in well-drained loamy to clayey alluvial soils and regur (black cotton soils) that retain adequate moisture.

Question 15 of 18
Which scheme was restructured to merge with NFSM-Pulses to provide comprehensive coverage?

Rationale: ISOPOM was merged into the National Food Security Mission to streamline funding and boost pulse and oilseed production across India.

Question 16 of 18
Which of the following is NOT a Rabi pulse crop?

Rationale: Urad is primarily a Kharif crop (though grown in some southern regions during Rabi or summer), whereas Gram, Lentil, and Peas are quintessential Rabi crops.

Question 17 of 18
What is the significance of crop rotation involving pulses?

Rationale: Rotating cereals with pulses replenishes soil nitrogen biologically and helps interrupt weed, insect, and fungal pathogen cycles.

Question 18 of 18
Which country is a major exporter of pulses to India?

Rationale: Canada (along with Myanmar, Australia, and African countries like Tanzania and Mozambique) is a major trading partner exporting yellow peas and lentils to India.

Which of the following states is the leading producer of pulses in India?

Official Benchmark Explanation: Madhya Pradesh holds the top rank in pulse production in India, contributing around 23-25% of the national total. UP PGT aspirants must memorize top state rankings for commercial and food crops.

Official Benchmark Explanation: Both statements are factually sound. Pulses thrive in rainfed/dryland zones precisely because of their drought resistance and symbiotic nitrogen fixation ability, explaining their agronomic importance.

UGC-NET Geography (Previous Year Pattern)
Match List-I (Pulse Crop) with List-II (Leading State Producer): A. Gram (1. Maharashtra), B. Tur/Arhar (2. Madhya Pradesh), C. Urad (3. Uttar Pradesh), D. Lentil (4. Rajasthan). Choose the correct code:

Official Benchmark Explanation: Gram is led by Madhya Pradesh (A-2), Tur by Maharashtra/Karnataka/MP (B-1), Urad by UP/MP (C-3), and Lentil by UP/MP/Rajasthan (D-4). Matching regional crop dominance is a recurring UGC-NET pattern.

UP TGT Geography (Previous Year Pattern)
Which season is most associated with the cultivation of Gram (Chana) in Northern India?

Official Benchmark Explanation: Gram is a premier Rabi crop sown after the withdrawal of the southwest monsoon (October-November) and harvested in spring (March-April).

UGC-NET Geography (Previous Year Pattern)
In the context of Indian agriculture, what is the primary agronomic benefit of incorporating pulses into cereal-based cropping systems?

Official Benchmark Explanation: Legumes/pulses fix atmospheric nitrogen, reducing synthetic fertilizer dependence and improving soil structure, making them vital for sustainable cropping systems.

UP PGT Geography
Which of the following pairs of Pulse Crop and its botanical classification/common name is incorrectly matched?

Official Benchmark Explanation: Vigna mungo refers to Black Gram (Urad), whereas Lentil is scientifically known as Lens culinaris. Precision in scientific naming is tested in advanced PGT exams.

Question 1 of 10
Consider the following statements regarding pulse production in India: 1. India is the world's largest producer and consumer of pulses. 2. Among pulses, Gram has the highest share in total production. 3. Rajasthan is the top producer of Tur. Which of the statements given above are correct?

Solution: Statement 1 and 2 are correct: India is the top producer and consumer, and Gram leads production (~40%). Statement 3 is incorrect because Maharashtra, Karnataka, and Madhya Pradesh lead in Tur production, not Rajasthan.

Question 2 of 10
Which geographical factor primarily restricts the extensive expansion of pulse cultivation in canal-irrigated alluvial plains of Punjab and Haryana?

Solution: The Green Revolution transformed Punjab and Haryana into intensive wheat-paddy belts. Abundant canal irrigation favors water-guzzling cereals over drought-hardy pulses.

Question 3 of 10
What is the principal climatic requirement for harvesting good quality pulse crops?

Solution: While pulses need moderate moisture during growth, clear skies and dry weather during maturity and harvesting are essential to prevent fungal infections and grain spoilage.

Question 4 of 10
Which of the following correctly describes 'Relay Cropping' involving pulses in the Indo-Gangetic plain?

Solution: Relay cropping involves sowing the succeeding crop (like lentils) before harvesting the standing crop (like paddy) to utilize residual soil moisture and time efficiently.

Question 5 of 10
Which government initiative specifically targets self-sufficiency in pulse production through cluster demonstrations and seed hubs?

Solution: NFSM-Pulses focuses on cluster-frontline demonstrations, distribution of high-yielding seed varieties, and setting up seed hubs to bridge the demand-supply gap.

Question 6 of 10
Why are pulse yields in India generally lower per hectare compared to countries like Canada or Myanmar?

Solution: A major portion of pulses in India is grown under rainfed conditions on marginal soils with minimal fertilizers and irrigation, leading to lower average yields.

Question 7 of 10
Which of the following states is known for the cultivation of 'Arhar' as a major intercrop with cotton or millets?

Solution: In Maharashtra and parts of the Deccan plateau, pigeon pea (Arhar) is traditionally intercropped with cotton or sorghum (jowar) to optimize land use and hedge against crop failure.

Question 8 of 10
How do pulse crops contribute to mitigating global climate change?

Solution: Biological nitrogen fixation by legumes reduces the reliance on Haber-Bosch synthetic nitrogen fertilizers, which are major greenhouse gas emitters during production.

Question 9 of 10
Which pulse is often referred to as 'Horse Gram' and is widely grown in southern India for both human consumption and cattle feed?

Solution: Macrotyloma uniflorum is the scientific name for Horse Gram (Kulthi), an extremely hardy pulse cultivated in arid and semi-arid tracts of southern India.

Question 10 of 10
In geographical analysis of crop concentration, high location quotient (LQ) for pulses in a district indicates:

Solution: Location Quotient measures regional specialization; an LQ greater than 1 for pulses indicates that the district specializes in pulse production compared to the broader geographic benchmark.
