Resource Geography — Agriculture

Major Crops: Comprehensive Geographic Analysis, Physical & Human Mechanisms, and Regional Distribution

High-yield conceptual breakdown and physical/human geographical foundations of Major Crops tailored for competitive exam mastery.

UP PGT Geography UP TGT Social Science B.Sc. / M.Sc. Level

Agricultural geography examines the spatial distribution of cultivated plants in relation to environmental constraints and socio-economic frameworks. The global pattern of major crops is dictated by the interaction of lithospheric, atmospheric, and hydrological parameters—principally thermal units, precipitation regimes, and edaphic characteristics—modified heavily by technological inputs, agrarian policies, and market forces. In the context of UP TGT/PGT and UGC-NET Geography, mastery over agricultural systems requires an understanding of Köppen's climatic classifications, Von Thünen's spatial land-use mechanics, and localized Indian agro-climatic zones. Major staple and commercial crops exhibit distinct geographical belts, such as the tropical monsoon rice-paddy systems, mid-latitude temperate wheat belts, and specialized equatorial plantation corridors. The spatial organization of these crops is intrinsically tied to green revolution technologies, irrigation infrastructure, and shifting dietary transitions. Exam questions frequently probe precise thermal thresholds, soil requirements, leading producing nations/states, and the environmental externalities of mono-cropping versus poly-culture systems.

1. Agro-Climatic Determinants and Environmental Controls of Crop Distribution

Liebig's Law of the Minimum governs the yield and spatial boundaries of major crops based on limiting physical factors.

  • Temperature requirements: Rice requires high temperatures ($21^circ\text{C}$ to $37^circ\text{C}$) and high humidity throughout its growth cycle.
  • Thermal indices: Wheat is a micro-thermal, temperate-adapted crop requiring $10^circ\text{C}$ to $15^circ\text{C}$ during sowing and $21^circ\text{C}$ to $26^circ\text{C}$ at ripening.
  • Precipitation parameters: Paddy rice demands $100\text{ cm}$ to $200\text{ cm}$ of rainfall; wheat thrives in moderate regimes of $50\text{ cm}$ to $75\text{ cm}$.
  • Edaphic controls: Alluvial and clay-loam soils are optimal for water-retaining paddy cultivation, while well-drained loamy soils suit wheat and maize.
  • Photoperiodism: Long-day plants (wheat, barley) vs. short-day plants (rice, millets) dictate global latitudinal cropping limits.
Comparative Analysis: Contrasts with livestock ranching and forestry by showing absolute dependence of arable crops on short-term seasonal meteorological anomalies.
  • Rice dominates tropical/subtropical monsoonal lowlands with high standing water.
  • Wheat replaces rice in semi-arid, continental interiors with winter-spring precipitation cycles.

2. Global Spatial Patterns, Production Belts, and Trade Geographies

Von Thünen's Isolated State model modified by globalization explains commercial grain farming and intensive subsistence zones.

  • Rice Belt: Monsoon Asia (China, India, Bangladesh, Southeast Asia) accounts for over 90% of global production.
  • Wheat Belt: North American Prairies, Eurasian Steppes, Argentine Pampas, and Australian Downs (Extensive Commercial Grain Farming).
  • Maize (Corn) Belt: US Midwest, North China Plain, and parts of Sub-Saharan Africa, serving primarily as feed and industrial input.
  • Beverage & Plantation Crops: Tea (Assam, Sri Lanka, Kenya) and Coffee (Brazil's Terra Roxa soils, Colombia, Vietnam) concentrated in tropical highlands.
  • Fiber Crops: Cotton distribution is bounded by subtropical arid/semi-arid environments with frost-free periods of 200 days.
Comparative Analysis: Subsistence intensive agriculture of Monsoon Asia contrasts sharply with mechanized extensive commercial grain farming of the New World.
  • China and India lead aggregate cereal production due to demographic scale and intensive double-cropping.
  • Global trade flows heavily favor wheat and maize from temperate exporters to tropical importers.

3. Indian Agrarian Geography: Regionalization and Cropping Seasons

India's physiographic diversity enables a tri-season agricultural calendar (Kharif, Rabi, Zaid) regulated by the SW and NE Monsoons.

  • Kharif crops (Monsoon): Rice, maize, jowar, bajra, cotton, and soybean sown in June-July and harvested in Sept-October.
  • Rabi crops (Winter): Wheat, barley, gram, mustard, and peas sown in October-November and harvested in March-April.
  • Zaid crops (Summer): Watermelon, muskmelon, cucumber, and green fodder cultivated during dry summer months with irrigation.
  • Rice Distribution: West Bengal, Uttar Pradesh, Punjab, Andhra Pradesh, and Tamil Nadu via deltaic and canal irrigation.
  • Wheat Distribution: Indo-Gangetic Plain, with Punjab, Haryana, and Western UP forming the core surplus zone.
Comparative Analysis: Contrast between rainfed dryland farming of the Deccan Plateau (millets/pulses) and irrigated intensive farming of the northern plains (rice/wheat).
  • Punjab and Haryana exhibit the highest cropping intensity and commercialization ratios in India.
  • Eastern India possesses high potential for second-green-revolution rice expansion.

Spatial Patterns & Regional Distribution

🌐 Global Geographic Belts & Spatial Variation

Major crop belts align closely with climatic zones: Wet equatorial and monsoonal regimes support paddy rice and plantation agriculture; humid continental and mid-latitude steppe regions support wheat and maize; Mediterranean belts support citrus fruits, olives, and viticulture.

🇮🇳 Indian Subcontinent Context & Regional Corridors

The Indo-Gangetic alluvial plain is dominated by Rice-Wheat systems. The peninsular plateau features black cotton soil (Regur) regions ideal for cotton and jowar, while the Western Ghats and North-East hills specialize in plantation crops (tea, coffee, rubber, spices).

📐 Key Geographic Formulas, Scales & Classifications
Crop Concentration Index (Bhatia's Method)
C_i = \sum \left( \frac{x_i}{t_i} \right) / \sum \left( \frac{X}{T} \right) \times 100
Parameters: x_i = Area of crop i in a spatial unit, t_i = Total cropped area in that unit, X = Total area of crop i in the region, T = Total cropped area in the region.
Exam Focus: Frequently tested in UGC-NET Geography for measuring regional specialization and spatial distribution concentration.
Agricultural Efficiency Index (Kendall's Ranking Coefficient)
I_e = \frac{\sum R}{n}
Parameters: R = Rank of a spatial unit in per-hectare yield of various crops, n = Total number of crops considered.
Exam Focus: Used in UP PGT quantitative geography sections to evaluate comparative agricultural productivity across districts.

Mnemonics, Memory Shortcuts & Exam Traps

💡 Top Rice Producing Nations
C-I-B-V (Can I Buy Vegetables?)

C = China, I = India, B = Bangladesh, V = Vietnam (Top global producers of rice in descending order).

⚠️ Exam Trap: Do not confuse production volume with export volume; Thailand and India often lead global rice export markets while China consumes most domestically.
💡 Major Rabi Crops in India
W-B-G-M-P (We Buy Good Mustard Peas)

W = Wheat, B = Barley, G = Gram, M = Mustard, P = Peas (Core winter-season Rabi crops).

⚠️ Exam Trap: Examiners frequently trap students by listing Kharif crops (like maize or cotton) under Rabi options.

📚 High-Yield Geographic Terminology & Definitions

Isohypse UGC-NET Concept

A contour line connecting points of equal elevation, critical in determining the altitudinal zonation of plantation crops like tea and coffee.

Isohyet UP PGT Mapping

A line on a map connecting points that receive equal amounts of rainfall, vital for delineating rainfed cropping regions such as coarse cereals.

Ratooning TGT/PGT Agriculture

An agricultural practice of harvesting a crop by leaving the roots intact to allow the stubble to sprout for a subsequent harvest, extensively used in sugarcane cultivation.

Shifting Cultivation Resource Geography

An agricultural system in which plots of land are cultivated temporarily, then abandoned and allowed to revert to natural vegetation while the cultivator moves to another plot.

Truck Farming Economic Geography

The horticultural practice of growing one or more vegetable crops on a large scale for shipment to distant markets, often located near urban centers.

Green Revolution Indian Agriculture

A large-scale transformation in agricultural production between the 1950s and late 1960s characterized by high-yielding varieties (HYV) of cereals, expanded irrigation infrastructure, and synthetic fertilizers.

Intensive Subsistence Agriculture UGC-NET Core

A form of subsistence agriculture in which farmers expend a relatively high amount of effort to produce the maximum possible yield from a parcel of land, common in monsoon Asia.

Extensive Commercial Grain Farming UP PGT Concept

Agriculture practiced in the interior semi-arid lands of the mid-latitudes, characterized by large farm sizes, high mechanization, and low per-acre yields with high per-capita output.

Plantation Agriculture UP TGT Geography

A form of commercial farming where a single crop (e.g., tea, coffee, rubber, banana) is grown on a large estate using cheap labor and capital-intensive management.

Mixed Farming Economic Geography

An agricultural system in which a farmer conducts different agricultural practices together, such as cash crops and livestock cultivation, primarily found in the highly developed parts of the world.

Mediterranean Agriculture World Agriculture

Specialized agriculture practiced in regions with a Mediterranean climate, characterized by viticulture (grape cultivation), citrus fruits, and olive production.

Dryland Farming Agrarian Systems

A technique used to cultivate crops in regions with limited annual rainfall without the aid of irrigation, relying on moisture-conservation practices.

Sericulture Specialized Crops

The cultivation of silkworms to produce silk, strongly tied to mulberry cultivation in specific climatic pockets of India and China.

Horticulture UP PGT Agriculture

The branch of agriculture that deals with the art, science, technology, and business of growing plants, including fruits, vegetables, nuts, and ornamental plants.

🧭 Prescribed Scholars, Laws & Spatial Models

Johann Heinrich von Thünen (1826)

Isolated State model explaining agricultural location based on land rent, transport costs, and perishability of crops, concentric rings around a central market town.

David Grigg (1974)

Comprehensive global classification of agricultural systems based on intensity, land tenure, methods, and crop-livestock combinations.

Carl Sauer (1952)

Theory of agricultural origins and dispersals, arguing that agriculture began in areas of high biodiversity with sedentary populations rather than under environmental stress.

Ester Boserup (1965)

Population growth forces an increase in agricultural intensity through technological innovation and shorter fallow periods (Population drives agricultural change).

Interactive Concept Practice

Instant evaluation with detailed spatial & physical explanations for each question.

Question 1 of 19
Which of the following climatic conditions is most ideal for the cultivation of Rubber (Hevea brasiliensis)?

Rationale: Rubber requires an equatorial or tropical rainforest climate with high temperatures (above 25°C) and heavy, well-distributed rainfall exceeding 200 cm annually.

Question 2 of 19
Von Thünen's agricultural land-use model places which of the following activities in the innermost ring closest to the central market?

Rationale: In Von Thünen's model, market gardening and dairying occupy the innermost ring due to the high perishability of products and high transport costs to the central market.

Question 3 of 19
Which Indian state is the leading producer of raw jute?

Rationale: West Bengal accounts for the majority of India's raw jute production, benefited by the fertile alluvial soils of the Ganga-Brahmaputra delta and abundant water for retting.

Question 4 of 19
Which of the following crops is classified as a Kharif crop in North India?

Rationale: Paddy is a quintessential Kharif (summer/monsoon) crop requiring high water availability, whereas wheat, barley, and mustard are Rabi (winter) crops.

Question 5 of 19
Who propounded the thesis that population growth is the autonomous driver of agricultural intensification?

Rationale: Ester Boserup argued against Malthus, asserting that population pressure forces agricultural innovation and the reduction of fallow periods to increase output.

Question 6 of 19
Tea cultivation thrives best on which type of topography?

Rationale: Tea bushes require abundant rainfall but cannot tolerate water stagnation at their roots, making well-drained hill slopes ideal for preventing waterlogging.

Question 7 of 19
Which country is the world's largest producer of Paddy Rice?

Rationale: China is the largest producer of rice globally, followed closely by India as the second largest producer.

Question 8 of 19
Operation Flood, launched in India in 1970, was primarily associated with the increase in production of which major commodity?

Rationale: Operation Flood was the world's largest agricultural dairy development program, spearheading India's White Revolution under the guidance of Verghese Kurien.

Question 9 of 19
Which of the following is NOT a plantation crop?

Rationale: Wheat is a cereal grain cultivated under commercial or subsistence farming systems, not a plantation crop, which are typically perennial cash crops grown on large estates.

Question 10 of 19
The process of 'Retting' is associated with the preparation of which crop fiber?

Rationale: Retting is a microbiological process used in jute processing where bundles of jute stalks are immersed in slow-moving water to loosen the fibers from the woody core.

Question 11 of 19
Which type of agriculture is characterized by slash-and-burn methods and nomadic shifting?

Rationale: Primitive subsistence agriculture involves shifting cultivation (Jhum in Northeast India, Milpa in Central America) utilizing slash-and-burn techniques.

Question 12 of 19
Which state in India is the leading producer of Coffee?

Rationale: Karnataka accounts for over 70% of India's total coffee production, primarily centered around the Western Ghats districts like Kodagu, Chikkamagaluru, and Hassan.

Question 13 of 19
Which soil type is most suitable for the extensive cultivation of Cotton in peninsular India?

Rationale: Regur (Black Cotton Soil), derived from basaltic lava traps, has high moisture retention capacity and high clay content, making it ideal for cotton cultivation.

Question 14 of 19
Which of the following agricultural regions is noted for citriculture, viticulture, and orchard farming?

Rationale: Mediterranean agriculture is uniquely specialized in citrus fruits, olives, and grapes (viticulture) adapted to dry summers and mild, wet winters.

Question 15 of 19
What is the primary objective of Mixed Farming?

Rationale: Mixed farming integrates crop production and animal husbandry, ensuring that income is diversified and farm waste is recycled as manure.

Question 16 of 19
Which Indian state leads in the production of Sugarcane?

Rationale: Uttar Pradesh is the leading state in sugarcane production in India, followed by Maharashtra and Karnataka.

Question 17 of 19
What is 'Sericulture' directly linked with?

Rationale: Sericulture involves rearing silkworms, predominantly feeding on mulberry leaves, to produce raw silk.

Question 18 of 19
Which crop is known as 'Golden Fiber' in agricultural geography?

Rationale: Jute is commonly referred to as the 'Golden Fiber' due to its golden-brown color and high cash-crop value.

Question 19 of 19
Which of the following is a Rabi crop requiring a cool growing season and bright sunshine at ripening?

Rationale: Wheat is a principal Rabi crop that needs a cool, moist climate during early growth and bright sunny weather at harvest/ripening time.

UP TGT Geography (Previous Year Pattern)
The term 'Regur' is synonymous with which of the following soil types?

Official Benchmark Explanation: Regur is the local name for black cotton soils found extensively in the Deccan trap region of India, highly suitable for cotton production.

UP PGT Geography (Previous Year Pattern)
Which scholar formulated the concentric ring model of agricultural land use based on transport costs and spatial location?

Official Benchmark Explanation: J.H. von Thünen developed the isolated state agricultural location model in 1826.

UGC-NET Geography (Previous Year Pattern)
Which of the following climatic phenomena is vital for the successful harvesting of Rabi wheat crop in Northern India?

Official Benchmark Explanation: Western Disturbances bring winter rainfall (Mahawat) to Northwest India, which is extremely beneficial for the ripening and growth of Rabi crops like wheat.

Question 1 of 10
Consider the following statements regarding plantation agriculture: 1. It is a form of commercial farming practiced in tropical regions. 2. It requires large capital investment and skilled managerial support. 3. Tea, coffee, rubber, and sugarcane are typical plantation crops. Which of the statements given above are correct?

Solution: All statements are correct. Plantation agriculture is characterized by monoculture, large estates, heavy capital, migrant labor, and scientific management in tropical/sub-tropical zones.

Question 2 of 10
In the context of agricultural typology, 'Truck Farming' derives its name from:

Solution: In North America, 'truck' historically meant 'barter' or 'vegetables grown for market', leading to the term truck farming for horticulture near cities.

Question 3 of 10
Which of the following pairs of Crop and its geographical requirement is incorrectly matched?

Solution: Tea requires well-drained hill slopes, not waterlogged flat plains, as water stagnation damages its root system.

Question 4 of 10
According to D. Whittlesey's agricultural classification, which criteria were primarily used to demarcate world agricultural regions?

Solution: Whittlesey used functional and empirical criteria including crop-livestock combinations, farming techniques, capital input, and land-use intensity.

Question 5 of 10
Which Indian state is the largest producer of Millets (Shree Anna) following recent national promotional initiatives?

Solution: Rajasthan is among the leading producers of millets (such as Bajra) in India, leveraging arid and semi-arid climatic conditions.

Question 6 of 10
What is the primary reason why shifting cultivation (Jhum) is facing a decline in environmental sustainability?

Solution: Traditionally, shifting cultivation relied on long fallow periods (10-15 years) for soil rejuvenation. Population pressure has shortened these cycles, leading to severe soil erosion and nutrient depletion.

Question 7 of 10
Which climatic factor limits the northward extension of commercial grain farming in the Canadian Prairies and Russian steppes?

Solution: High latitudes feature short frost-free growing seasons and low temperatures, which prevent cereal crops like wheat from maturing properly.

Question 8 of 10
Which agricultural system is characterized by the production of flowers, ornamental plants, and vegetables in high-value green-houses near metropolitan centers?

Solution: Floriculture and intensive market gardening are high-value, capital-intensive forms of agriculture supplying urban demands directly.

Question 9 of 10
In Von Thünen's model, forestry (firewood and timber) was placed in the second ring primarily because:

Solution: Before widespread fossil fuels and modern transport, wood was heavy and bulky; high transport costs dictated that forestry be located relatively close to the city.

Question 10 of 10
The 'Green Revolution' in India was most successful in significantly boosting the production of which two food grains?

Solution: The Green Revolution introduced High Yielding Varieties (HYV) of dwarf wheat and rice, drastically boosting their production, particularly in Punjab, Haryana, and Western UP.
