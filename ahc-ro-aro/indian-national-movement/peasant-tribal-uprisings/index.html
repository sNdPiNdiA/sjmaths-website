Peasant and Tribal Uprisings in India Core Study Notes

Peasant and tribal uprisings form a crucial part of the Indian National Movement, highlighting the grassroots resistance against British colonial exploitation, heavy revenue demands, and landlord oppression. For the Allahabad High Court RO/ARO and UPPSC examinations, questions frequently test specific dates, leaders, regions, and the socio-economic causes behind these movements.

Causes and Character of Early Uprisings

The British land revenue systems (Permanent Settlement, Ryotwari, and Mahalwari) disrupted the traditional agrarian economy and spawned widespread resistance.

  • Destruction of Traditional Economy: Commercialization of agriculture, heavy taxation, and influx of revenue collectors turned self-sufficient peasants into heavily indebted landless laborers.
  • Role of Moneylenders (Dikus): New judicial and administrative systems favored moneylenders, leading to widespread alienation of tribal lands through fraudulent debt traps.
  • Forest Regulations and Encroachment: Colonial forest laws restricted traditional tribal rights over shifting cultivation, grazing, and collection of minor forest produce.
  • Localized and Isolated Nature: Early uprisings were generally localized, lacking a pan-Indian vision or modern political ideology, fighting primarily against immediate oppressors.

Key Peasant Movements of the 19th & 20th Century

The transition from localized resistance to organized agrarian movements supported by the nationalist leadership.

  • Indigo Revolt (1859-60): Began in Govindpur village of Nadia district (Bengal). Peasants successfully united to refuse indigo cultivation, leading to the appointment of the Indigo Commission in 1860.
  • Pabna Agrarian Leagues (1873-85): Pabna peasants in Bengal formed agrarian leagues to resist arbitrary rent hikes by landlords through legal recourse and peaceful protests, supported by Bankim Chandra Chatterjee and R.C. Dutt.
  • The Deccan Riots (1875): Agrarian unrest in Poona and Ahmednagar against heavy debt burdens imposed by moneylenders, prompting the passage of the Deccan Agriculturists' Relief Act of 1879.
  • Champaran Satyagraha (1917): Mahatma Gandhi's first civil disobedience movement in India against the exploitative 'Tinkathia' system (compulsory cultivation of indigo on 3/20th of land).
  • Awadh Kisan Sabha and Ekta Movement: Formed in 1920 under Baba Ramchandra and later Jawaharlal Nehru; the Ekta movement in UP (1921-22) protested high rents and illegal cesses.
  • All India Kisan Sabha (AIKS): Established in 1936 at the Lucknow session of the Indian National Congress; Swami Sahajanand Saraswati was elected its first president.

Significant Tribal Revolts

Tribal communities mounted fierce armed resistance against British expansion and interference in their socio-cultural and economic systems.

  • Bhil Uprisings (1817-19): Occurred in Khandesh and Western Ghats; Bhils revolted against British occupation and the fear of agrarian changes introduced by the East India Company.
  • Kol Mutiny (1831-32): Chotanagpur tribals (Kols, Mundas, Oraons) rose in revolt after their lands were transferred to outsiders (Sikhs, Muslims, and British agents) and heavy taxes were imposed.
  • Khond Uprising (1837-56): Led by Chakra Bisoi in Odisha and parts of Madras Presidency against British attempts to suppress human sacrifice (Mariah) and new taxes.
  • Rampa Rebellion (1879): Hill tribes of the Godavari agency in Andhra Pradesh revolted against the new forest laws, restrictions on toddy tapping, and exploitation by local mansabdars.
  • Munda Ulgulan (1899-1900): Birsa Munda organized the 'Great Tumult' (Ulgulan) to establish Munda Raj and eliminate Dikus and British rule through guerrilla warfare.
