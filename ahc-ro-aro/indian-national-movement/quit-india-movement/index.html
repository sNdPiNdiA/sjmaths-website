Quit India Movement Core Study Notes

The Quit India Movement, launched in August 1942, was the final mass struggle initiated by Mahatma Gandhi for complete independence from British rule. It holds immense significance for Allahabad High Court RO/ARO exams due to parallel governments, underground networks, and UP-specific revolutionary participation.

Background and Causes

The immediate catalyst for the Quit India Movement was the total breakdown of constitutional negotiations and wartime economic hardships.

  • Failure of Cripps Mission (1942): Sir Stafford Cripps arrived with proposals offering Dominion Status only after the war and the right of provinces to secede, which was rejected by all parties ('Post-dated cheque on a crashing bank').
  • Wartime Hardships and Inflation: Unchecked rise in prices of essential commodities, shortages of rice and salt, and the British 'scorched earth policy' in Bengal and Assam alienated the masses.
  • Fear of Japanese Invasion: Japanese forces were advancing towards India's borders through Southeast Asia. Gandhi believed that an orderly British withdrawal was essential to save India from Japanese occupation and to enable Indians to defend themselves.
  • Shift in Gandhi's Stance: Disillusioned with British stubbornness, Gandhi moved from conditional cooperation to absolute defiance, declaring 'angrezon Bharat chhodo' (Quit India).

Nature and Methods of the Movement

Unlike earlier Gandhian movements, Quit India was characterized by spontaneity, underground resistance, and militant public participation.

  • Spontaneous Upheaval: With the arrest of all frontline leaders on August 9, the movement became leaderless and spontaneous, unleashing pent-up public anger.
  • Attacks on Symbols of Authority: Crowds attacked police stations, post offices, railway tracks, and telegraph wires, severely disrupting British communications across UP, Bihar, and Bengal.
  • Underground Networks: Leaders like Jayaprakash Narayan, Ram Manohar Lohia, Achyut Patwardhan, and Sucheta Kripalani operated underground to coordinate sabotage and propaganda.
  • Usha Mehta's Underground Radio: Usha Mehta ran a clandestine Congress Radio transmitter in Bombay, broadcasting nationalist news and updates across India until late 1942.

Parallel Governments (Prati Sarkar)

A unique feature of the Quit India Movement was the establishment of parallel national governments in various parts of India.

  • Ballia (Uttar Pradesh - High Yield for AHC RO/ARO): Chittu Pandey led a successful revolt, forcing local authorities to surrender and establishing a parallel government that released arrested Congress leaders.
  • Tamluk (Midnapore, Bengal): Jatiya Sarkar functioned effectively, establishing its own arbitration courts, cyclone relief work, and Vidyut Vahini (armed militia).
  • Satara (Maharashtra): The longest-lasting parallel government ('Prati Sarkar') was established under leaders like Nana Patil, featuring Nyayadan Mandals (people's courts) and prohibition campaigns.
  • Talcher (Odisha) and Other Pockets: Parallel administrative structures also emerged in parts of Bihar, Karnataka, and Orissa, challenging British sovereignty directly.

Repression and British Response

The British administration unleashed ruthless repression to crush the Quit India uprising within weeks.

  • Viceroy Linlithgow's Policy: Viceroy Lord Linlithgow adopted a policy of violent suppression, using police firing, lathi charges, and even machine-gunning crowds from aircraft.
  • Casualties and Imprisonment: Over 10,000 people were killed in police and military firing, and more than 100,000 activists were arrested without trial.
  • Collective Fines: Heavy collective fines were imposed on villages and towns where anti-government sabotage took place, crushing local economies.
  • Censorship and Press Control: Strict press censorship was enforced; nationalist newspapers like 'Harijan' and 'National Herald' were forced to suspend publication.
