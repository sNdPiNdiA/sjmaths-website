Indian Architecture: Temple and Islamic Styles Core Study Notes

This topic covers the evolution of Indian temple architecture (Nagara, Dravida, and Vesara styles) and Indo-Islamic architecture (Delhi Sultanate, Mughal styles). It is crucial for AHC RO/ARO and UPPSC examinations as questions frequently test structural terms, regional variations, and architectural components.

Nagara Style (North Indian Temple Architecture)

Characterized by a beehive-shaped tower called Shikhara and built on a high stone platform.

  • Panchayatana Style: A central shrine surrounded by four subsidiary shrines, forming a cross-like layout (e.g., Dashavatara Temple, Deogarh).
  • Mandapa and Garbhagriha: Garbhagriha houses the principal deity, while the assembly hall in front is called the Mandapa.
  • Amalaka and Kalasha: The crowning elements of the Shikhara: a ribbed disk-like stone called Amalaka, topped by a vase-like Kalasha.
  • Absence of Boundary Walls: Unlike South Indian temples, classic Nagara temples generally lack elaborate boundary walls and large gateway towers (gopurams).

Dravida Style (South Indian Temple Architecture)

Developed under dynasties like the Pallavas, Cholas, and Pandyas, marked by enclosure walls and imposing gateways.

  • Vimana and Gopuram: The main tower is called Vimana (stepped pyramid). Grand entrance gateways are known as Gopurams.
  • Boundary Walls and Mandapams: Temples are enclosed within high compound walls containing large pillared halls (Mandapams) and sacred water tanks.
  • Pallava Phase: Initiated rock-cut architecture at Mahabalipuram (Rathas) by Mahendravarman I and Narasimhavarman I.
  • Chola Peak: Brihadeshwara Temple at Thanjavur (Rajaraja I) represents the zenith of Dravida architecture with a massive 66m vimana.

Indo-Islamic Architecture: Delhi Sultanate

Fusion of Islamic geometrical concepts with Indian decorative traditions during the Sultanate period.

  • True Arch and Dome: Introduction of the keystoned true arch and true dome, replacing the earlier trabeate (beam and lintel) weight-bearing system.
  • Mortar and Calligraphy: Use of high-quality mortar as a cementing agent and Quranic verses inscribed in Arabic calligraphy for decoration.
  • Qutb Minar Complex: Started by Qutb-ud-din Aibak and completed by Iltutmish; includes the Quwwat-ul-Islam Mosque and the Alai Darwaza.
  • Tughlaq Style: Characterized by sloping walls (batter) and austere grey stone buildings, reflecting sober structural austerity (e.g., Tughlaqabad Fort).

Mughal Architecture

Magnificent synthesis of Persian, Timurid, and Indian architectural styles under the Great Mughals.

  • Akbar's Red Sandstone Era: Fatehpur Sikri, Buland Darwaza, and Humayun's Tomb (first garden-tomb with a double dome) mark the architectural renaissance.
  • Shah Jahan's White Marble Era: Transition to white marble, extensive use of Pietra Dura (semi-precious stone inlay), and symmetrical garden layouts (Taj Mahal, Red Fort).
  • Charbagh Layout: Quadrrilateral garden layout divided by walkways and water channels, symbolizing Islamic paradise gardens.
  • Decline Phase under Aurangzeb: Reduction in ornamentation and grand scale; Bibi ka Maqbara in Aurangabad mimics the Taj Mahal with restricted grandeur.
