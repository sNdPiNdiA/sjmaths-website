Indian Music Paintings Core Study Notes

Indian Music Paintings, predominantly known as Ragamala paintings, represent a unique synthesis of classical Indian music (Ragas and Raginis) and visual art (Miniature Painting Schools). This topic is highly scoring in Allahabad High Court RO/ARO and UPPSC examinations, particularly for questions testing school categorizations, iconography, and historical patronage.

Concept and Structure of Ragamala Paintings

Understanding the core philosophy linking musical notes, seasons, times of day, and visual imagery.

  • Definition of Ragamala: Ragamala translates to 'Garland of Ragas'. It is a series of illustrative paintings depicting various classical Indian musical modes known as Ragas and their female counterparts, Raginis.
  • Personification of Ragas: In Indian tradition, abstract musical notes are personified as human characters—gods, heroes (Nayakas), and heroines (Nayikas)—engaged in specific emotional or romantic moods.
  • Classification Systems (Melvargkas): Two primary classification systems exist: the Hanuman system (six primary Ragas, each with five or six Raginis) and the Kedar system, widely adapted across various regional painting schools.
  • Association with Time and Seasons: Each Raga is strictly prescribed for a specific time of day (morning, dusk, midnight) or season (monsoon, spring), which is vividly captured through background landscapes and color schemes in the paintings.
Painting School Notable Patron/Period Key Characteristics
Mewar School Chawand, 1605 (Nisaruddin) Bold use of primary red and yellow colors, vibrant emotional expression, simple architectural borders.
Bundi School Rao Ratan Singh, 17th Century Lush vegetation, naturalistic animal depictions (ducks, monkeys), rich blues and greens, starry nights.
Deccani School (Bijapur/Ahmednagar) Ibrahim Adil Shah II, c. 1590 Rich Persian influence, luminous gold skies, refined facial profiles, mystical Sufi elements.
Pahari School (Basohli / Kangra) Raja Kirpal Pal / Raja Sansar Chand Intense brilliance in Basohli; lyrical grace, delicate naturalism, and poetic mood in Kangra.

Iconography and Representation of Prominent Ragas

Detailed analysis of how specific Ragas and Raginis are visually translated onto canvas.

  • Bhairava Raga: Personified as Lord Shiva (Mahadeva), depicted with matted hair, a crescent moon, a trident (trishula), and snakes around his neck, usually set against an early dawn backdrop.
  • Megha Raga (Megh Malhar): Represents the monsoon season. Portrayed as Lord Krishna with dark blue complexion, playing the flute, surrounded by black storm clouds, lightning, and dancing peacocks.
  • Todi Ragini: Depicted as a graceful woman standing in a forest playing the Vina, which enchants wild animals like deer and antelopes that gather around her trustingly.
  • Basant Raga: Celebrates the arrival of spring (Vasant). Shown as Kamadeva (God of Love) or Krishna holding mango blossoms, dressed in yellow garments amidst blooming mustard fields.

Literary and Cultural Texts Associated with Ragamala

Key Sanskrit and vernacular treatises that provided the poetic framework for visual artists.

  • Sangeet Ratnakar: Authored by Sharangadeva in the 13th century in Devagiri (Deccan); one of the most authoritative historical treatises on Indian music and dance.
  • Raga Darpana: A Persian translation of Fakirullah (compiled during the reign of Mughal Emperor Aurangzeb) based on Koutuhala's Man Kautuhal, detailing music and modal structures.
  • Ragamala of Pundarika Vitthala: Written in the late 16th century by a musicologist from Karnataka who worked in North Indian royal courts, classifying ragas into specific male and female hierarchies.
