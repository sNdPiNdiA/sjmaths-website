Industrialization Under British: De-industrialization and Drain of Wealth Core Study Notes

This topic is vital for AHC RO/ARO and UPPSC examinations as it covers the systematic destruction of traditional Indian handicrafts and cottage industries under British colonial rule. Aspirants must understand the economic theories, particularly Dadabhai Naoroji's Drain Theory, and the uneven pattern of modern industrial growth in British India.

De-industrialization: Ruin of Traditional Industries

Examines the systematic dismantling of India's world-renowned textile and handicraft sectors due to discriminatory tariff policies and unequal competition.

  • One-Way Free Trade: While British machine-made goods entered India duty-free or at nominal rates, heavy protective duties were levied on Indian textiles entering Britain.
  • Impact of Railways: Railways introduced by Lord Dalhousie primarily served colonial interests by connecting internal raw material hubs directly to ports, penetrating remote markets with British goods.
  • Displacement of Artisans: Unemployed weavers and artisans had no alternative industrial sector to absorb them, leading to extreme ruralization and pressure on agricultural land.
  • Loss of Princely Patronage: Annexation of Indian princely states by the British eliminated local courts and Nawabs who were traditional buyers of luxury handicrafts and fine textiles.

Drain of Wealth Theory (धन की निकासी का सिद्धांत)

Understanding the economic mechanism through which wealth was continuously siphoned off from India to Britain without any economic return.

  • Dadabhai Naoroji's Exposition: Dadabhai Naoroji propounded the Drain Theory in his paper 'England's Debt to India' (1867) and his seminal book 'Poverty and Un-British Rule in India' (1901).
  • Components of the Drain: The drain included Home Charges (pensions of civil and military officers in UK, interest on public debt, military expenses in India/abroad), profits of British capitalists, and shipping/banking charges.
  • Other Nationalist Economists: R.C. Dutt ('Economic History of India'), M.G. Ranade, and G.K. Gokhale further analyzed and popularized the exploitation of Indian resources through economic critique.
  • Political Significance: The Drain Theory served as a powerful economic weapon for the early nationalists to prove that British rule was the root cause of India's poverty and famines.

Growth of Modern Industries & Factory Legislation

Tracing the lopsided growth of modern factories and the enactment of early labor laws driven largely by British commercial pressures.

  • Lopsided Industrialization: Industrial growth was limited mainly to consumer goods like cotton and jute. Capital goods industries (machinery, heavy engineering, chemicals) were virtually absent.
  • First Factory Act (1881): Passed under Lord Ripon, it prohibited employment of children under 7, limited working hours for children (7-12 years), and mandated a weekly holiday, but was influenced by Lancashire lobby fearing cheap Indian competition.
  • Second Factory Act (1891): Introduced a weekly holiday for all workers, raised the minimum age for children to 9, and limited working hours for women to 11 hours a day.
  • Managed Agency System: British managing agency houses controlled most of the tea plantations, coal mines, and jute mills, dominating corporate finance and management with minimal Indian control initially.
