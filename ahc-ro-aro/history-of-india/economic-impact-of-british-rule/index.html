Economic Impact Of British Rule Core Study Notes

The economic impact of British rule in India represents a systematic transformation of a self-sufficient agrarian economy into a colonial supplier of raw materials and a market for British manufactured goods. This topic is exceptionally vital for Allahabad High Court RO/ARO and UPPSC examinations as it forms the bedrock of modern Indian history questions, frequently testing drain of wealth theories, land revenue settlements, and de-industrialization.

De-industrialization and Ruin of Handicrafts

The deliberate destruction of traditional Indian cottage industries and handicrafts through free trade policy and unequal competition.

  • One-Way Free Trade: British goods entered India duty-free or at nominal tariffs, while heavy discriminatory duties were imposed on Indian textiles entering Britain.
  • Destruction of Urban Centers: Traditional manufacturing hubs like Dacca, Murshidabad, and Surat suffered catastrophic decline, forcing artisans back to agriculture.
  • Commercialization of Agriculture: Farmers were forced to shift from food crops to cash crops (indigo, cotton, opium, jute) to feed British industries, triggering severe food grain shortages.

Land Revenue Systems

The three major colonial land revenue systems designed to extract maximum agricultural surplus for the British Empire.

  • Permanent Settlement (Zamindari): Covered 19% of British India. Lord Cornwallis made zamindars the owners of land, fixing 10/11th share for the company and 1/11th for zamindars.
  • Ryotwari System: Covered 51% of British India (Madras, Bombay, Assam). Direct settlement between government and ryot (cultivator), with revenue revision every 20-30 years.
  • Mahalwari System: Covered 30% of British India (North-West Provinces, Punjab, Central India). Introduced by Holt Mackenzie; revenue settlement was made with the village community (Mahal) jointly.

Drain of Wealth Theory

The continuous transfer of India's wealth and economic resources to Britain without any adequate economic or material return.

  • Pioneered by Dadabhai Naoroji: Famously expounded in his book 'Poverty and Un-British Rule in India' (1901), calculating the exact magnitude of capital drain.
  • Components of Drain: Included Home Charges (pensions of British officers, interest on public debt, military expenses in Britain), railway guaranteed returns, and private remittances.
  • Other Proponents: R.C. Dutt ('Economic History of India'), G. Subramania Iyer, and M.G. Ranade actively exposed the economic exploitation in colonial journals.

Growth of Commercial Infrastructure and Railways

The strategic establishment of railways, post, telegraph, and modern banking primarily to serve British imperial and commercial interests.

  • Railway Introduction: First passenger train ran in 1853 between Bombay and Thane under Lord Dalhousie. Built under 'Guarantee System' ensuring 5% minimum return to British investors.
  • Impact on Internal Market: Railways connected remote hinterlands to ports, facilitating the rapid export of raw materials and influx of cheap British manufactured goods into Indian villages.
