Land Revenue Systems Core Study Notes

The evolution of land revenue systems under British colonial rule forms a high-yielding core area for Allahabad High Court RO/ARO and UPPSC examinations. Aspirants must master the structural mechanics, geographical distribution, key architects, and socioeconomic impacts of the Permanent Settlement, Ryotwari, and Mahalwari systems.

Permanent Settlement (Zamindari System / Ismerari)

Covering roughly 19% of British India, this system transformed zamindars from traditional revenue collectors into legal landowners.

  • Geographical Extent: Implemented in Bengal, Bihar, Orissa, parts of modern Uttar Pradesh (Varanasi division), and northern districts of Madras presidency.
  • Architects & Enactment: Conceived by Sir John Shore and executed by Lord Cornwallis in 1793 through the Permanent Settlement Act.
  • Revenue Distribution Ratio: Out of the total collection, 1/11th was retained by the Zamindar as commission, while 10/11th was remitted to the British East India Company.
  • Sunset Law: Stipulated that if the zamindar failed to pay the fixed revenue by sunset on the specified date, their zamindari rights would be auctioned off.
  • Socio-Economic Impacts: Created an absentee landlordism class, heavily exploited actual cultivators (tenants), and caused massive rural indebtedness.

Ryotwari System

Covering around 51% of British India, this system established a direct relationship between the government and the individual cultivator.

  • Geographical Spread: Predominantly applied in Madras Presidency, Bombay Presidency, parts of Assam, and Coorg provinces.
  • Pioneering Experiment: First tried on a small scale by Alexander Read in Baramahal district, later expanded across Madras by Thomas Munro.
  • Ownership Status: Ryots were recognized as the actual owners of land, with rights to sell, mortgage, or lease their plots as long as they paid revenue.
  • High Assessment Rates: Revenue demand was excessively high—fixing 45% to 50% of gross or net produce—and was subject to periodic revision every 20-30 years.
  • Role of Moneylenders: Rigid cash payment terms forced cultivators into the clutches of moneylenders (Sahukars), leading to agrarian riots like the Deccan Riots of 1875.

Mahalwari System

Formulated for the North-Western Provinces and Punjab, this system recognized the village or estate (Mahal) as the revenue assessment unit.

  • Regions Covered: Applied extensively in the North-Western Provinces (modern UP), parts of Central India, and Punjab.
  • Key Architects: Initially designed by Holt Mackenzie in 1822 and comprehensively reformed by Robert Merttins Bird in 1833.
  • Collective Responsibility: The village community, through village headmen (Lumberdars), was collectively responsible for paying the revenue assessed on the entire Mahal.
  • Saharanpur Rules (1855): Under Lord Dalhousie, the Saharanpur Rules reduced the state's share of rental value down to 50%, setting a standardized benchmark for northern India.

Drain of Wealth & Economic Impact

British land revenue policies fundamentally dismantled the traditional self-sufficient agrarian economy of pre-colonial India.

  • Commercialization of Agriculture: High cash revenue demands forced farmers to grow cash crops (indigo, cotton, opium, jute) instead of food crops, triggering severe famines.
  • Impoverishment of Peasantry: Extreme tax burdens led to chronic indebtedness, transfer of land to non-cultivating moneylenders, and widespread rural poverty.
  • Dadabhai Naoroji's Drain Theory: Articulated in his book 'Poverty and Un-British Rule in India', exposing how land revenue was drained to Britain without economic returns.
