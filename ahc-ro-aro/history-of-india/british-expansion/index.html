British Expansion in India Core Study Notes

The British expansion in India transformed the East India Company from a trading body into a supreme imperial power. This topic is crucial for Allahabad High Court RO/ARO and UPPSC examinations as it covers critical battles, treaties, annexations, and administrative policies that shaped modern Indian history.

Conquest of Bengal

Bengal was the richest province in India. Its conquest laid the solid foundation of the British Empire.

  • Misuse of Dastaks: Company officials misused trade permits (dastaks) for private trade, depriving the Bengal Nawab of legitimate revenue.
  • Battle of Plassey (23 June 1757): Siraj-ud-Daulah was defeated due to the betrayal of Mir Jafar and Jagat Seth. Robert Clive became the master of Bengal.
  • Battle of Buxar (22 October 1764): Hector Munro defeated the combined forces of Mir Qasim, Shuja-ud-Daulah (Awadh), and Shah Alam II (Mughal Emperor).
  • Treaty of Allahabad (1765): Concluded in two parts by Robert Clive; granted Diwani rights of Bengal, Bihar, and Orissa to the East India Company.
  • Dual Government in Bengal: Introduced by Robert Clive (1765-1772), where the Company held Diwani (revenue) and the Nawab held Nizamat (police/judicial) powers.

Subjugation of Mysore and Marathas

Mysore and the Maratha Confederacy were the most formidable military hurdles in the path of British supremacy.

  • Rise of Haidar Ali: Modernized the Mysore army with French assistance and captured Dindigul in 1755, later overthrowing the Wodeyar dynasty.
  • Fourth Anglo-Mysore War (1799): Tipu Sultan died fighting heroically at Seringapatam. Wellesley restored a minor of the Wodeyar dynasty via a Subsidiary Alliance.
  • First Anglo-Maratha War (1775-1782): Triggered by the succession dispute involving Raghunath Rao. Ended with the Treaty of Salbai, maintaining status quo for 20 years.
  • Second Anglo-Maratha War (1803-1805): Treaty of Bassein (1802) signed by Peshwa Baji Rao II, effectively surrendering Maratha independence to Lord Wellesley.
  • Third Anglo-Maratha War (1817-1819): Led by Lord Hastings, the Maratha confederacy was dissolved, the office of Peshwa was abolished, and Baji Rao II was exiled to Bithur.

Policies of Expansion: Subsidiary Alliance & Doctrine of Lapse

The British expanded their empire not only through direct wars but also via ingenious political and legal doctrines.

  • Subsidiary Alliance System: Conceived by Lord Wellesley, Indian states had to maintain a British army contingent, expel other Europeans, and station a British Resident.
  • First to Accept Subsidiary Alliance: Nizam of Hyderabad was the first to accept it in 1798, followed by Mysore (1799), Awadh (1801), and Peshwa (1802).
  • Doctrine of Lapse (Paramountcy): Applied by Lord Dalhousie, stating that dependent states without a natural male heir would lapse to the British company.
  • States Annexed under Doctrine of Lapse: Satara (1848), Jaitpur & Sambalpur (1849), Baghat (1850), Udaipur (1852), Jhansi (1853), and Nagpur (1854).
  • Annexation of Awadh (1856): Annexed by Lord Dalhousie on the grounds of 'misgovernance' (misrule) based on the Outram Report, deeply angering Sepoys.

Conquest of Sindh and Punjab

The final phase of expansion completed the natural borders of the British Indian Empire towards the northwest.

  • Conquest of Sindh (1843): Annexed by Charles Napier under Governor-General Ellenborough, driven by Russophobia (fear of Russian invasion).
  • Treaty of Amritsar (1809): Signed between Maharaja Ranjit Singh and Charles Metcalfe, fixing the Sutlej River as the boundary between British and Sikh territories.
  • First Anglo-Sikh War (1845-1846): Ended with the humiliating Treaty of Lahore (1846) and Treaty of Bhairowal, where a British resident was appointed for minor Duleep Singh.
  • Second Anglo-Sikh War (1848-1849): Fought under Lord Dalhousie; ended with the annexation of Punjab and pensioning off of Maharaja Duleep Singh to England.
  • Koh-i-Noor Diamond: Following the annexation of Punjab in 1849, the Koh-i-Noor diamond was surrendered by Duleep Singh to Queen Victoria.
