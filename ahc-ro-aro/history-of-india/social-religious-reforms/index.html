Social Religious Reforms in 19th Century India Core Study Notes

The socio-religious reform movements of the 19th century were foundational to the Indian Renaissance, aimed at eradicating rigid caste hierarchies, superstitious practices, and regressive social customs like Sati and child marriage while fostering modern rationalism. These movements hold immense significance for Allahabad High Court RO/ARO and UPPSC examinations due to recurring factual questions on founding personalities, foundational texts, and legislations.

Early Reform Movements & Brahmo Samaj

Raja Ram Mohan Roy, often called the 'Father of Indian Renaissance', initiated organized socio-religious reform through foundational institutional setups.

  • Atmiya Sabha (1815): Started by Ram Mohan Roy in Calcutta to propagate monotheistic ideals of Vedanta and campaign against idol worship and meaningless rituals.
  • Brahmo Samaj Foundation (1828): Originally launched as Brahmo Sabha, later renamed Brahmo Samaj; focused on purification of Hinduism and preaching monotheism, forbidding idol worship and priesthood.
  • Abolition of Sati (1829): Due to relentless crusade by Raja Ram Mohan Roy, Lord William Bentinck enacted Regulation XVII, declaring Sati illegal and punishable in British India.
  • Split in Brahmo Samaj (1866): The Samaj split into Adi Brahmo Samaj led by Debendranath Tagore and Brahmo Samaj of India led by Keshab Chandra Sen over issues like child marriage and radical reforms.
  • Sadharan Brahmo Samaj (1878): Formed by Ananda Mohan Bose, Shib Chandra Deb, and others after Keshab Chandra Sen flouted Brahmo principles by marrying his minor daughter to the Maharaja of Cooch Behar.

Western India Reforms & Prarthana Samaj

Maharashtra witnessed a distinct socio-religious awakening led by intellectuals focusing on social reform, education, and caste upliftment.

  • Paramahansa Mandali (1849): Secret socio-religious society founded in Poona by Dadoba Pandurang and Mehtaji Dharamaram; aimed at breaking caste rules and promoting widow remarriage.
  • Prarthana Samaj (1867): Established by Atmaram Pandurang under the guidance of Keshab Chandra Sen; prominent members included M.G. Ranade, R.C. Bhandarkar, and N.G. Chandavarkar.
  • Mahadev Govind Ranade (MG Ranade): Referred to as the 'Socrates of Maharashtra', he founded the Widow Marriage Association (1861), Deccan Education Society (1884), and Poona Sarvajanik Sabha (1870).
  • Satyashodhak Samaj (1873): Founded by Jyotirao Phule in Maharashtra to liberate lower castes/Dalits from Brahminic exploitation and promote education among Shudras and Ati-Shudras.
  • Gopal Hari Deshmukh ('Lokahitawadi'): Advocated western education, social equality, and attacked caste privileges through his weekly letters published in 'Prabhakar' under the pen name 'Lokahitawadi'.

Arya Samaj, Ramakrishna Mission & Theosophical Society

Revivalist and nationalist movements that sought to instill national pride by looking back at India's ancient cultural heritage while undertaking internal reforms.

  • Arya Samaj (1875): Founded by Swami Dayanand Saraswati in Bombay (later shifted to Lahore in 1877); gave the slogan 'Back to the Vedas' and rejected Puranic Hinduism, polytheism, and untouchability.
  • Satyarth Prakash: Magnum opus of Swami Dayanand Saraswati outlining the philosophical, social, and moral principles of Arya Samaj and critiquing orthodox religious practices.
  • Ramakrishna Mission (1897): Founded by Swami Vivekananda at Belur Math to propagate the teachings of Sri Ramakrishna Paramahamsa, emphasizing social service, altruism, and universal brotherhood.
  • Theosophical Society (1875): Founded in New York by Madame H.P. Blavatsky and Colonel H.S. Olcott; shifted international headquarters to Adyar (Madras) in 1882; Annie Besant popularized it in India.
  • Shuddhi Movement: Initiated by Arya Samaj to reconvert individuals who had converted to Islam or Christianity back to Hinduism, creating intense political and religious ripples.

Muslim, Sikh & Parsi Reform Movements

Minority communities also witnessed reformative zeal, balancing modern Western education with the purification of religious traditions.

  • Aligarh Movement: Led by Sir Syed Ahmed Khan; established Muhammadan Anglo-Oriental (MAO) College in 1875 at Aligarh to promote modern western sciences and English education among Muslims.
  • Deoband Movement: Started as a revivalist school in 1866 by Mohammad Qasim Nanautawi and Rashid Ahmad Gangohi; aimed at moral and religious regeneration of Muslims through traditional Islamic teachings.
  • Rahnumai Mazdayasnan Sabha (1851): Parsi reform movement founded by Dadabhai Naoroji, Naoroji Furdonji, and S.S. Bengali to uplift the Zoroastrian community and improve the status of Parsi women.
  • Singh Sabha Movement (1873): Founded at Amritsar to restore purity of Sikhism, counteract Christian proselytizing and Arya Samaj criticisms, and promote Western education through Khalsa schools.

Major Social Legislation & Acts

Colonial legislation enacted under pressure from Indian reformers to eradicate inhuman social practices across British India.

  • Bengal Sati Regulation (1829): Regulation XVII declared the practice of immolating Hindu widows illegal throughout British territories.
  • Hindu Widows' Remarriage Act (1856): Act XV of 1856 drafted by Lord Dalhousie and passed largely due to the tireless efforts of Ishwar Chandra Vidyasagar, legalizing the marriage of Hindu widows.
  • Native Marriage Act / Civil Marriage Act (1872): Prohibited child marriage; set minimum marriage age at 14 for girls and 18 for boys; penalised polygamy and applied across inter-caste marriages.
  • Age of Consent Act (1891): Passed due to efforts of B.M. Malabari; raised the age of sexual consent for girls from 10 to 12 years, triggering intense conservative backlash.
  • Sarda Act (1930): Child Marriage Restraint Act spearheaded by Harbilas Sarda; fixed the minimum marriage age at 18 for boys and 14 for girls.
