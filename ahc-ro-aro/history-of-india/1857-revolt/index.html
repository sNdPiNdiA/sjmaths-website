1857 Revolt Core Study Notes

The 1857 Revolt was a major turning point in modern Indian history, marking the first widespread armed resistance against British colonial rule. For Allahabad High Court RO/ARO and UPPSC aspirants, questions frequently focus on regional leaders, key centers in Uttar Pradesh, causes, and British officials involved in suppression.

Causes of the Revolt

The uprising was a cumulative result of multi-dimensional grievances against British imperialist policies.

  • Political Causes: Lord Dalhousie's Doctrine of Lapse (Satara 1848, Jhansi 1853, Nagpur 1854) and annexation of Awadh in 1856 on grounds of misgovernance alienated native rulers and soldiers.
  • Economic Causes: Heavy land revenue systems (Zamindari, Ryotwari, Mahalwari), destruction of traditional handicrafts, and commercialization of agriculture ruined peasants and artisans.
  • Socio-Religious Causes: Interference in social customs (Abolition of Sati 1829, Widow Remarriage Act 1856), activities of Christian missionaries, and the Religious Disabilities Act of 1850 created widespread panic.
  • Military Causes: Racial discrimination in promotions and pay, General Service Enlistment Act of 1856 (mandatory overseas service), and the immediate trigger of the Enfield Rifle using greased cartridges.

Nature and Character of the Revolt

Historians hold diverse opinions regarding the classification and exact nature of the 1857 uprising.

  • First War of Independence: V.D. Savarkar famously termed the revolt as the 'First War of Indian Independence' in his book published in 1909.
  • Sepoy Mutiny View: British historians like Sir John Seeley and Charles Lawrence characterized it merely as a 'selfish and unpatriotic Sepoy Mutiny'.
  • Clash of Civilizations: L.E.R. Rees viewed the revolt as a religious war between fanaticism (Islam/Hinduism) and Christianity.
  • Feudal Reaction: Marxist historians and modern analysts view it as a popular peasant revolt backed by dispossessed feudal elements fighting to restore the old order.

Causes of Failure

Despite massive participation, the revolt failed due to several organizational and structural shortcomings.

  • Lack of Centralized Leadership: The movement lacked a unified supreme commander; Bahadur Shah Zafar was old and weak, and regional leaders fought independently without coordination.
  • Limited Territorial Spread: The revolt remained largely confined to North and Central India. South India, Punjab, and Rajputana largely remained unaffected or loyal to the British.
  • Superior British Resources: The British possessed superior telegraphic communication, modern weaponry, naval power, and uninterrupted military reinforcements from Britain.
  • Lack of Support from Intelligentsia: Modern educated Indians, prominent merchants, and most Indian princes (e.g., Holkar of Indore, Scindia of Gwalior) sided with or aided the British.

Aftermath and Consequences

The suppression of the 1857 revolt fundamentally transformed the administrative architecture of British India.

  • Government of India Act 1858: Abolished Company rule, transferred governance to the British Crown, and created the office of the Secretary of State for India assisted by a 15-member council.
  • Queen Victoria's Proclamation: Read out by Lord Canning at Allahabad on November 1, 1858, promising non-interference in religious matters, equality in public services, and respect for native princes' rights.
  • Reorganization of the Army: The 'Peel Commission' recommended increasing the ratio of European troops to Indian troops and strategically keeping artillery strictly under British control.
  • End of Expansionist Policy: The Doctrine of Lapse was officially abandoned, and the British began using Indian princes as a 'breakwater' (buffer) against popular national uprisings.
