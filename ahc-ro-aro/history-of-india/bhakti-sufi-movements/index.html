Bhakti and Sufi Movements Core Study Notes

The Bhakti and Sufi movements represent a spiritual revolution in medieval Indian history, emphasizing personal devotion, universal brotherhood, and social equality. For Allahabad High Court RO/ARO and UPPSC examinations, questions frequently focus on key saints, their philosophical doctrines, foundational texts, and regional centers in Uttar Pradesh.

South Indian Bhakti Saints and Philosophies

The Bhakti movement originated in South India, spearheaded by the Alvars and Nayanars, and later shaped by Vedantic philosophers.

  • Alvars and Nayanars: Alvars were 12 Vaishnavite saints (including Andal, the female saint), while Nayanars were 63 Shaivite saints who composed devotional hymns compiled as 'Tirumurai'.
  • Shankracharya (Advaita): Propounded Advaita Vedanta (Non-dualism), stating that Brahma is the only reality (Sat) and the world is an illusion (Maya). Established four mathas across India.
  • Ramanujacharya (Vishishtadvaita): Propounded Vishishtadvaita (Qualified Monism), arguing that individual souls are part of Brahman and salvation is attainable through intense devotion (Prapatti).
  • Madhvacharya (Dvaita): Propounded Dvaita (Dualism), emphasizing absolute difference between God (Vishnu) and the human soul, opposing Shankaracharya's non-dualism.
  • Basaveshwara and Lingayats: Initiated the Virashaiva (Lingayat) movement in Karnataka in the 12th century, strongly rejecting the caste system, child marriage, and supporting widow remarriage.

Northern Bhakti Movement: Saguna and Nirguna Saints

The North Indian movement split into Nirguna (formless devotion) and Saguna (devotion to avatars) streams, transforming society and vernacular languages.

  • Ramananda: The bridge between Southern devotion and Northern propagation. He preached in Hindi and accepted disciples from all castes, including lower castes and Muslims.
  • Kabir Das: A premier Nirguna saint, weaver by profession, who vehemently criticized ritualism, idol worship, and caste discrimination in his couplets (Dohas) collected in 'Bijak'.
  • Guru Nanak Dev: Founder of Sikhism, propagated Nirguna Bhakti, universal brotherhood, rejection of caste hierarchy, and introduced the concept of Langar and Ek Onkar.
  • Tulsidas: A prominent Saguna Rama-bhakta, author of 'Ramcharitmanas', 'Vinaya Patrika', and 'Kavitavali', promoting righteous living and devotion in Awadhi.
  • Surdas and Mirabai: Surdas (blind poet of Agra) composed 'Sur Sagar' dedicated to Lord Krishna in Brajbhasha. Mirabai, the Rajput princess, composed passionate bhajans for Krishna.

Sufi Movement in India: Silsilas and Teachings

Sufism is the mystical dimension of Islam that entered India around the 12th century, promoting love, tolerance, and service to humanity.

  • Chishti Silsila: Most popular Sufi order in India, founded by Khwaja Moinuddin Chishti in Ajmer. Promoted 'Sama' (musical gatherings) and kept away from state politics.
  • Shaikh Nizamuddin Auliya: Famous Chishti saint of Delhi, popularly known as 'Mahbub-i-Ilahi'. Witnessed the reign of seven Sultans but never visited the royal court.
  • Suhrawardi Silsila: Founded by Shihabuddin Umar al-Suhrawardi, prominent in Sindh and Multan. Unlike Chishtis, they accepted state patronage, jagirs, and wealth to help the poor.
  • Naqshbandi Silsila: Introduced in India by Khwaja Baqi Billah and popularized by Shaikh Ahmad Sirhindi (Mujaddid). Known for orthodox views and opposing Akbar's liberal religious policies.
  • Qadiri Silsila: Founded by Abdul Qadir Gilani, brought to India by Shah Niamatullah. Prince Dara Shikoh was a devout follower of this order and translated the Upanishads.

Key Terminology and Administrative Impact

A thorough understanding of Sufi and Bhakti terms is essential for matching and statement-based questions in UPPSC and RO/ARO exams.

  • Pir and Murid: 'Pir' refers to the Sufi master/spiritual guide, and 'Murid' refers to the disciple or seeker who undergoes spiritual training.
  • Khanqah: The hospice or residential center where Sufi saints lived, taught, and distributed food to visitors, acting as centers of socio-cultural exchange.
  • Urs: The death anniversary festival of a Sufi saint, celebrated at their shrine (Dargah), symbolizing the soul's wedding union with God.
  • Ziyarat: Pilgrimage to Sufi shrines (such as Khwaja Moinuddin Chishti's Dargah in Ajmer or Nizamuddin Auliya's Dargah in Delhi) to seek blessings.
  • Sama: Mystical musical sessions involving recitation of devotional poetry (Qawwali) to induce spiritual ecstasy, pioneered by Amir Khusrau in the Chishti tradition.
