Dr Samuel Johnson: Comprehensive Literary & Grammatical Analysis

High-yield conceptual breakdown and foundational study notes of Dr Samuel Johnson tailored for competitive exam mastery.

UP PGT English UP TGT English B.A. / M.A. Level

Dr Samuel Johnson (1709–1784), often referred to simply as Dr Johnson, was a towering figure of 18th-century English literature—a poet, essayist, moralist, literary critic, biographer, and lexicographer. As the quintessential representative of the Age of Sensibility and the Augustan transition, Johnson shaped English letters through monumental achievements like 'A Dictionary of the English Language' (1755), his critical prefaces in 'Lives of the Most Eminent English Poets' (1779–1781), and his edition of the plays of William Shakespeare. For UP TGT/PGT and UGC-NET aspirants, Johnson's biographical positioning by James Boswell, his neoclassic aesthetic preferences, his moral conservatism, and his critique of poetic diction are vital core areas frequently tested.

1. Lexicography and the Monumental Dictionary

Standardization of the English language through empirical citation and prescriptive lexicography.

  • Took nine years (1746–1755) to compile A Dictionary of the English Language single-handedly with minimal secretarial help.
  • Included 42,773 entries with extensive illustrative literary quotations drawn from authors spanning the Elizabethan era to the 18th century.
  • Featured iconic, often idiosyncratic definitions reflecting Johnson's robust Tory politics, robust common sense, and wit (e.g., 'Oats: A grain, which in England is generally given to horses, but in Scotland supports the people').
  • Initially hoped to fix the English language and arrest its corruption, though he later conceded in the 1755 Preface that language is inherently organic and subject to change ('languages are not invariants, they are like leaves on trees').
  • Dedicated the Dictionary initially to Lord Chesterfield, later castigating him in a famous 1755 letter for patronage neglect ('Is a patron, my Lord, one who looks with unconcern on a man struggling for life in the water...').
Comparative Analysis: Contrasts with modern descriptive lexicography (like the Oxford English Dictionary) which records usage rather than prescribing a normative standard.
  • Published in 1755, establishing British English spelling and usage norms.
  • Marked a massive leap forward from previous glossaries by using contextual literary citations.

2. Literary Criticism and Shakespearean Scholarship

Neoclassical critical principles balanced by empirical moralism and psychological realism.

  • The Preface to Shakespeare (1765) defends Shakespeare against strict French neoclassical rules of the Unities (Time and Place), arguing that the mind easily bridges spatial and temporal gaps in dramatic illusion.
  • Famous critical dictum on Shakespeare: 'He that accuses him of wanting heart, has himself no heart.'
  • Critiqued Shakespeare's moral laxity ('he sacrifices virtue to convenience') and his occasional descent into 'fatal Cleopatra' puns and conceits.
  • Lives of the Most Eminent English Poets (commonly known as Lives of the Poets, 1779–1781) covers 52 poets from Cowley to Lyttelton.
  • Famous controversial critiques within Lives include his harsh dismissal of John Milton's Lycidas ('easy, vulgar, and therefore disgusting') and his censure of metaphysical conceits in Abraham Cowley and John Donne.
Comparative Analysis: Compares with Matthew Arnold's 'touchstone method' and John Dryden's neoclassical critical prefaces.
  • Defended dramatic verisimilitude over pedantic adherence to Aristotle's unities of time and place.
  • Established biographical-critical evaluation as a dominant paradigm in English literary criticism.

3. Major Non-Fictional Prose and Periodical Essays

Moralistic periodical essays upholding Augustan rationality, Christian stoicism, and human limitation.

  • The Rambler (1750–1752): A twice-weekly periodical essay series characterized by heavy, Latinate prose style ('Johnsonese') and deep moral seriousness.
  • The Idler (1758–1760): Lighter, more varied periodical essays published in the Universal Chronicle.
  • The History of Rasselas, Prince of Abissinia (1759): A philosophical fable written in evenings over one week to pay for his mother's funeral; explores the universal 'choice of life' and the vanity of human wishes.
  • The Vanity of Human Wishes (1749): Johnson's masterly imitation of Juvenal's Satire X, reflecting on the futility of ambition across historical figures (Wolsey, Charles XII of Sweden).
  • Rambler No. 4: A foundational text of early English literary realism and the novel, warning against depicting unrealistic moral extremes in fiction.
Comparative Analysis: Mirrors the earlier journalistic successes of Joseph Addison and Richard Steele in The Spectator, but with a darker, more melancholic Christian-stoic depth.
  • Rasselas emphasizes that human happiness is an illusion and true fulfillment is found only in the afterlife.
  • The Vanity of Human Wishes is hailed by critics like T.S. Eliot as a supreme masterpiece of poetic satire.

4. Biography, Boswell, and Cultural Persona

The transition of Johnson from literary author to immortalized literary icon through James Boswell's biography.

  • James Boswell published 'The Life of Samuel Johnson' in 1791, widely considered the greatest biography in the English language.
  • Captured Johnson's idiosyncratic physical traits, conversational brilliance, Tory-Jacobite sympathies, profound religious melancholy, and deep humanitarianism.
  • Formed 'The Club' (later Literary Club) in 1764 with Joshua Reynolds, Edmund Burke, Oliver Goldsmith, and David Garrick.
  • Celebrated journey to the Western Islands of Scotland (1773), chronicled both by Johnson (A Journey to the Western Islands of Scotland) and Boswell (Journal of a Tour to the Hebrides).
  • Affectionately dubbed 'The Great Cham of Literature' by Tobias Smollett.
Comparative Analysis: Contrasts standard hagiographic biographies with Boswell's psychologically intimate, warts-and-all observational technique.
  • Boswell's biography immortalized Johnson's conversational persona far beyond his written works.
  • Brought unprecedented empirical detail and personal anecdote into the biographical genre.

Literary Periods & Regional Context

🌐 Global/Historical Context

Belongs firmly to the Augustan Age / Age of Sensibility (mid-to-late 18th century in Britain). Stood against the rising tide of early Romantic individualism, defending universal human nature, decorum, and classical models.

🇮🇳 Indian Literature in English

Crucial part of the British Literature core syllabus across all Indian universities (BA, MA, UGC-NET) and UP TGT/PGT English examinations, serving as the benchmark for 18th-century neoclassicism.

📐 Key Literary Devices & Grammar Rules
Heroic Couplet (Johnsonian Versification)
AABB... iambic pentameter rhymed couplets with strong caesura and balanced antithesis.
Parameters: Meter: Strict iambic pentameter; Structure: Closed couplets containing complete grammatical thoughts; Rhetorical devices: Antithesis, parallel structure, balance.
Exam Focus: Frequently tested in questions regarding The Vanity of Human Wishes and Johnson's mastery of closed neoclassical verse forms.
Neoclassical Critical Evaluation Model
Empirical Observation + Moral Utility + Universal Human Nature = Canonical Standing
Parameters: Evaluation criteria prioritizing verisimilitude, moral instruction, general human truths over local eccentricities, and rejection of extravagant conceits.
Exam Focus: Essential framework for answering analytical questions on Preface to Shakespeare and Lives of the Poets in UGC-NET and PGT.

Mnemonics, Memory Shortcuts & Exam Traps

💡 J-R-L-P: Major Works of Dr Samuel Johnson
J - R - L - P

J = Dictionary (Johnson's/Journal); R = Rasselas; L = Lives of the Poets; P = Preface to Shakespeare.

⚠️ Exam Trap: Candidates often confuse the publication year of the Dictionary (1755) with Rasselas (1759) and Preface to Shakespeare (1765). Memorize the chronological gap of 10 years from Dictionary to Shakespeare.
💡 Periodical Essay Distinctions
Rambler = Hard / Idler = Idle

The Rambler (1750-52) uses heavy Latinate prose ('Johnsonese') and deep moral rigor. The Idler (1758-60) is lighter, more satirical, and relaxed.

⚠️ Exam Trap: Do not mistake Johnson's periodicals with Addison & Steele's The Spectator or The Tatler; always verify the author and exact dates.

📚 High-Yield Literary & Grammatical Terminology

Preface to Shakespeare UP PGT 2021

A seminal 1765 critical essay by Dr. Samuel Johnson where he defends Shakespeare against neoclassical unities and lauds him as the poet of nature.

Biographia Literaria UGC-NET

Coleridge's work featuring a famous critique of Wordsworth's theory of poetic diction and Johnson's prose style.

The Lives of the Poets UP TGT

Johnson's massive biographical and critical collection (1779-1781), officially titled 'Prefaces, Biographical and Critical, to the Works of the English Poets'.

The Rambler UP PGT

A periodical essay paper written chiefly by Samuel Johnson, published bi-weekly from 1750 to 1752, known for its moral seriousness and Latinate prose.

The Idler UGC-NET

A series of weekly essays written by Samuel Johnson for the Universal Chronicle between 1758 and 1760, lighter in tone than The Rambler.

A Dictionary of the English Language UP PGT/TGT

Published in 1755 by Johnson, one of the most influential dictionaries in history, noted for its rich literary citations and occasional eccentric definitions.

Rasselas, Prince of Abissinia UP TGT

A philosophical novella written by Johnson in 1759 to defray the cost of his mother's funeral, exploring the theme of 'the choice of life'.

Neoclassicism UGC-NET

An 18th-century literary movement emphasizing reason, order, decorum, and classical restraint, of which Johnson was the supreme English arbiter.

The Unities UP PGT

The neoclassical dramatic rules of time, place, and action, which Johnson famously critiqued Shakespeare for breaking, arguing audiences always know they are in a theater.

Boswell's Life of Johnson UP TGT

Published in 1791 by James Boswell, widely considered the greatest biography in the English language, capturing Johnson's conversational brilliance.

Metaphysical Poets UGC-NET

A term coined (and largely disparaged) by Samuel Johnson in his Life of Cowley to describe 17th-century poets who 'Yoked by violence together' heterogeneous ideas.

London: A Poem UP PGT

Johnson's first major poem published in 1738, an imitation of Juvenal's Third Satire reflecting political corruption and urban decay.

The Vanity of Human Wishes UP PGT

Johnson's masterpiece in verse (1749), an imitation of Juvenal's Tenth Satire, contemplating the emptiness of worldly ambitions.

🧭 Prescribed Authors, Critics & Rules

Dr. Samuel Johnson (On Shakespeare) (1765 (Neoclassical Age))

Shakespeare is the poet of nature who holds up to his readers a faithful mirror of manners and of life; units of time and place are unnecessary because the spectator's mind makes allowance for theatrical fiction.

Dr. Samuel Johnson (On Metaphysical Wit) (1779 (Life of Cowley))

Definition of metaphysical wit as a kind of discordia concors—a combination of dissimilar images, or discovery of occult resemblances in things apparently unlike.

Dr. Samuel Johnson (On Poetic Language) (18th Century)

Insistence on general nature over particular eccentricities; literature must instruct by pleasing and adhere to moral universalism.

Interactive Concept Practice

Instant evaluation with detailed explanations for each question.

Question 1 of 18
In which work did Dr. Johnson coin or popularize the critique of the 'Metaphysical Poets'?

Rationale: Johnson used the term 'metaphysical' in his 'Life of Abraham Cowley' (part of Lives of the Poets) to describe the 17th-century poets who showed more learning than affection and yoked heterogeneous ideas by violence together.

Question 2 of 18
What is the subtitle of Dr. Johnson's famous philosophical tale 'Rasselas'?

Rationale: 'Rasselas, Prince of Abissinia' has the subtitle 'The Choice of Life', examining various lifestyles to determine which yields true happiness, concluding with 'the conclusion in which nothing is concluded'.

Question 3 of 18
In 'Preface to Shakespeare', Johnson defends Shakespeare against which neoclassical rule?

Rationale: Johnson famously defended Shakespeare's violation of the unities of time and place, and his blending of tragedy and comedy (tragicomedy), arguing that it reflects real life.

Question 4 of 18
When was Dr. Samuel Johnson's monumental 'A Dictionary of the English Language' published?

Rationale: Johnson's Dictionary was published in 1755 after nearly nine years of single-handed toil, establishing a standard for English orthography and lexicography.

Question 5 of 18
Which periodical essay series was written by Samuel Johnson between 1750 and 1752?

Rationale: The Rambler was published twice a week by Johnson from March 1750 to March 1752, dealing largely with serious moral, religious, and literary themes.

Question 6 of 18
Whn Johnson wrote 'A short-lived tyranny', to which literary group was he predominantly referring?

Rationale: Though not phrased precisely as a short-lived tyranny here, Johnson's critique of the Metaphysical school notes that their style flourished briefly before giving way to Waller and Denham.

Question 7 of 18
Which poem of Johnson is an imitation of Juvenal's Tenth Satire?

Rationale: 'The Vanity of Human Wishes' (1749) is Johnson's profound imitation of Juvenal's Tenth Satire, surveying historical examples of the futility of ambition.

Question 8 of 18
Who famously remarked that 'A dictionary is like a watch; bad if it doesn't go, and true even if it goes'?

Rationale: This is a reflection of Samuel Johnson's wit regarding lexicography, though he also famously defined a lexicographer in his dictionary as 'a writer of dictionaries, a harmless drudge'.

Question 9 of 18
What did Johnson mean by 'discordia concors' in relation to the Metaphysical poets?

Rationale: Johnson defined metaphysical wit as 'discordia concors: a combination of dissimilar images, or discovery of occult resemblances in things apparently unlike'.

Question 10 of 18
Which of Shakespeare's plays did Dr. Johnson particularly dislike for its lack of poetic justice regarding Cordelia's death?

Rationale: Johnson was so distressed by the death of Cordelia in 'King Lear' that he confessed he could not bear to read the last act until his edition forced him to do so.

Question 11 of 18
In what year did Dr. Johnson receive his pension from King George III, relieving him of financial distress?

Rationale: Johnson was awarded a royal pension of 300 pounds a year in 1762 through the influence of Lord Bute, which secured his financial independence.

Question 12 of 18
Which Scottish author wrote the definitive biography of Dr. Samuel Johnson?

Rationale: James Boswell's 'Life of Samuel Johnson, LL.D.' published in 1791, is regarded as the pinnacle of biographical writing.

Question 13 of 18
What was the title of Johnson's critical edition of William Shakespeare's plays published in 1765?

Rationale: Johnson published 'The Plays of William Shakespeare' in 8 volumes in 1765, prefaced by his monumental 'Preface to Shakespeare'.

Question 14 of 18
Which of the following journals was NOT edited or contributed to by Dr. Samuel Johnson?

Rationale: The Tatler was founded and primarily written by Richard Steele and Joseph Addison in 1709, long before Johnson's active journalistic career.

Question 15 of 18
According to Dr. Johnson, what is the ultimate end of poetry?

Rationale: Reflecting neoclassical tenets, Johnson maintained that the end of writing is to instruct, and the end of poetry is to instruct by pleasing.

Question 16 of 18
Which major literary figure did Dr. Johnson attack in his 'Life of Milton' for his political beliefs, while still praising 'Paradise Lost' as a poem that 'none ever wished longer'?

Rationale: Johnson detested Milton's republican politics and regicide support, but his critical integrity forced him to accord high praise to 'Paradise Lost'.

Question 17 of 18
What is the central theme of Johnson's periodical 'The Idler'?

Rationale: The Idler essays were shorter and generally lighter in tone than The Rambler, featuring characters like Dick Minim and Sober.

Question 18 of 18
Who was Dr. Johnson's beloved friend and patron who established 'The Club' (later Literary Club) in 1764?

Rationale: Sir Joshua Reynolds proposed the formation of 'The Club' in 1764, which included Johnson, Burke, Goldsmith, Boswell, and Gibbon.

Question 1 of 10
Which of the following statements about Dr. Johnson's view on Shakespeare's characters is correct?

Solution: Johnson praised Shakespeare's representation of general human nature, noting that his characters are not modified by local customs of particular times and places, but represent universal humanity.

Question 2 of 10
In 'Preface to Shakespeare', how does Johnson justify Shakespeare's blending of tragic and comic elements?

Solution: Johnson argued that Shakespeare's plays mirror life, and in real life, 'the extremes of pleasure and pain are easily confounded in misery and joy'.

Question 3 of 10
Which 18th-century writer famously created the character of 'Imlac' in 'Rasselas'?

Solution: Imlac is the poet and philosopher mentor to Rasselas and Nekayah in Johnson's 'Rasselas', often considered to voice Johnson's own views on poetry and human experience.

Question 4 of 10
What is the primary significance of Johnson's definition of a lexicographer as 'a writer of dictionaries, a harmless drudge'?

Solution: This definition is one of Johnson's most famous witticisms, reflecting his self-deprecating humor regarding the tedious, unglamorous labor of compiling his dictionary.

Question 5 of 10
How did Dr. Johnson evaluate John Milton's 'Lycidas'?

Solution: Johnson was notoriously harsh on 'Lycidas', stating in his 'Life of Milton' that 'Where there is leisure for fiction, there is little grief', condemning its artificial pastoral conventions.

Question 6 of 10
Which neoclassic principle does Johnson uphold when he criticizes Shakespeare for sacrificing virtue to convenience?

Solution: Johnson criticized Shakespeare for writing without moral purpose and sometimes failing to uphold poetic justice, noting that Shakespeare 'carries his persons indifferently through right and wrong'.

Question 7 of 10
What was the initial reception and impact of Johnson's 1755 Dictionary on the English language?

Solution: Johnson's Dictionary was hailed as a monumental triumph of scholarship, setting standards for spelling and usage that endured for generations.

Question 8 of 10
In 'The Vanity of Human Wishes', what overarching moral lesson does Johnson convey?

Solution: The poem surveys soldiers, scholars, statesmen, and beauties, showing the vanity of all earthly desires, and concludes that faith and virtue provide the only genuine shelter.

Question 9 of 10
Which of the following characterizes Johnson's prose style in 'The Rambler' and his other major critical essays?

Solution: Johnson's prose style is famous for its Latinate vocabulary, balanced periodic sentences, and formal antithesis, which became known as the 'Johnsonian' style.

Question 10 of 10
Who said of Samuel Johnson, 'He has more wit than any man I ever knew, and what is so extraordinary is, it is never on the surface'?

Solution: Edmund Burke paid this high tribute to Samuel Johnson's intellectual depth and conversational brilliance during their gatherings at The Club.
