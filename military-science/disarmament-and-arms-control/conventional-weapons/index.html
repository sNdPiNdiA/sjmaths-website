Disarmament and Arms Control

Conventional Weapons in Disarmament and Arms Control

Conventional weapons encompass all non-biological, non-chemical, and non-nuclear armaments used in warfare.

UP PGT Military Science6 concepts42 quiz questions10-question test

Topic overview

  • Conventional weapons encompass all non-biological, non-chemical, and non-nuclear armaments used in warfare.
  • Arms control frameworks aim to regulate the production, transfer, stockpiling, and deployment of conventional armaments.
  • International humanitarian law restricts conventional weapons that cause indiscriminate harm or superfluous injury.
  • India maintains a robust indigenous conventional military industrial base while adhering to global transparency norms.
Concept 1

Small Arms and Light Weapons (SALW)

SALW constitute the primary drivers of internal conflicts and transnational armed violence globally.

Explanation

  • Small arms are designed for personal use, including revolvers, pistols, assault rifles, and sub-machine guns.
  • Light weapons are designed for use by several persons serving as a crew, including heavy machine guns and anti-tank guided missiles.
  • The illicit trafficking of SALW undermines state authority, fuels insurgencies, and destabilizes regional security architectures.
  • The United Nations Programme of Action (UN PoA) serves as the primary global framework to combat illicit SALW trade.
  • India actively participates in international efforts against illicit SALW transfers while securing its porous borders.

Key points

  • SALW are characterized by portability, low cost, durability, and ease of concealment.
  • They account for the vast majority of casualties in modern asymmetric conflicts and civil wars.
  • Global control mechanisms focus on marking, record-keeping, and strict end-user certification.
  • State-sponsored illicit supply chains often bypass international arms embargoes and monitoring mechanisms.
Examples / applications:
  • The AK-47 assault rifle used extensively by regular forces and non-state actors worldwide.
  • Portable rocket-propelled grenade (RPG) launchers utilized in asymmetric urban combat zones.
Compare:
  • Unlike weapons of mass destruction, SALW are legally possessed and manufactured by sovereign states for self-defense.
  • Unlike heavy artillery, SALW do not require complex logistical chains for tactical deployment in the field.
Common misconceptions:
  • Students often confuse SALW control with total disarmament of national defense forces.
  • Many assume international treaties ban the manufacture of all small arms rather than targeting illicit trafficking.
Exam focus:
  • Memorize the definition and operational scope of the UN Programme of Action on SALW.
  • Understand the distinction between individual small arms and crew-served light weapons.
  • Analyze the security implications of SALW proliferation for internal security in South Asia.
Concept 2

Convention on Certain Conventional Weapons (CCW)

The CCW restricts or prohibits specific conventional weapons deemed excessively injurious or indiscriminate.

Explanation

  • Formally known as the Convention on Prohibitions or Restrictions on the Use of Certain Conventional Weapons.
  • The framework operates through multiple specialized protocols addressing distinct categories of battlefield armaments.
  • Protocol I completely prohibits weapons whose primary injury effect relies on undetectable fragments in the human body.
  • Protocol II and its amended version regulate the use and transfer of landmines, booby-traps, and other explosive devices.
  • India is a signatory to the CCW and several of its constituent protocols, balancing humanitarian law with military necessity.

Key points

  • The treaty entered into force internationally in December 1983 under United Nations auspices.
  • It applies international humanitarian law principles directly to battlefield conventional weapon systems.
  • Protocol IV specifically bans the use and transfer of blinding laser weapons designed to cause permanent loss of sight.
  • Periodic review conferences update the protocols to address emerging technological developments in weapon design.
Examples / applications:
  • Prohibition of plastic fragment munitions under CCW Protocol I that evade X-ray medical detection.
  • Regulation of anti-personnel landmines to prevent indiscriminate civilian casualties under Amended Protocol II.
Compare:
  • Unlike the Arms Trade Treaty, the CCW focuses directly on battlefield deployment restrictions rather than commercial transfers.
  • Unlike nuclear non-proliferation treaties, the CCW regulates weapon design characteristics rather than fissile material stockpiles.
Common misconceptions:
  • Candidates frequently think the CCW imposes a blanket ban on all types of landmines.
  • Some confuse CCW protocols with chemical weapons conventions managed by the OPCW.
Exam focus:
  • Learn the specific prohibitions outlined in CCW Protocols I through V.
  • Identify India's official stance and ratification status regarding amended protocol provisions.
  • Distinguish between weapons regulation on the battlefield and global export control regimes.
Concept 3

Arms Trade Treaty (ATT)

The ATT establishes international standards for regulating the global commercial trade in conventional arms.

Explanation

  • Adopted by the United Nations General Assembly in April 2013, the ATT regulates international transfers of conventional weapons.
  • It covers battle tanks, armored combat vehicles, large-caliber artillery systems, combat aircraft, and warships.
  • Exporting states must assess whether proposed arms transfers could facilitate human rights abuses or terrorism.
  • The treaty mandates detailed record-keeping and national export control systems to prevent diversion into illicit markets.
  • India has chosen not to sign the ATT, citing concerns over national security interests and biased export criteria.

Key points

  • The ATT aims to reduce human suffering caused by illegal and irresponsible conventional arms transfers.
  • It establishes the golden rule that states must not authorize exports if there is an overriding risk of violation of humanitarian law.
  • State parties are required to submit annual reports on their import and export activities to the ATT secretariat.
  • The treaty provides a framework for international cooperation and assistance to strengthen national regulatory authorities.
Examples / applications:
  • Blocking an international sale of combat aircraft to a regime accused of committing war crimes.
  • Requiring rigorous end-user certification to prevent legal arms imports from being diverted to rebel groups.
Compare:
  • Unlike the UN PoA which targets small arms specifically, the ATT covers all major conventional weapon categories.
  • Unlike bilateral defense pacts, the ATT establishes multilateral legally binding criteria for global arms commerce.
Common misconceptions:
  • Students often assume India is a foundational member state of the Arms Trade Treaty.
  • Many believe the ATT completely outlaws the global arms manufacturing industry.
Exam focus:
  • Understand the core provisions and scope of weapon categories covered under the ATT.
  • Analyze the strategic reasons behind India's non-signatory stance on the ATT.
  • Evaluate the effectiveness of risk assessment criteria in international arms transfer authorizations.
Concept 4

Cluster Munitions and Anti-Personnel Landmines

Unexploded ordnance from cluster munitions and landmines pose severe long-term humanitarian and developmental threats.

Explanation

  • Cluster munitions disperse multiple smaller submunitions over a wide area, frequently failing to detonate on impact.
  • Anti-personnel landmines remain active long after conflicts end, maiming civilians and inhibiting post-war economic recovery.
  • The Convention on Cluster Munitions (CCM) prohibits the use, production, stockpiling, and transfer of cluster bombs.
  • The Ottawa Treaty (Mine Ban Treaty) completely bans the use, stockpiling, and production of anti-personnel landmines.
  • India is not a party to either the CCM or the Ottawa Treaty due to perceived operational requirements along disputed borders.

Key points

  • Humanitarian disarmament campaigns successfully mobilized global public opinion against indiscriminate explosive remnants.
  • Dud rates of cluster submunitions turn affected agricultural lands into hazardous minefields for decades.
  • State non-signatories often argue that tactical landmine deployment remains vital for defensive territorial integrity.
  • Victim assistance and clearance operations form core pillars of modern international humanitarian mine action.
Examples / applications:
  • Deployment of cluster bomb units by air-to-surface delivery systems during high-intensity interstate conflicts.
  • Use of fenced barrier minefields along fortified international borders to deter unauthorized infiltration.
Compare:
  • Unlike guided missiles which target specific points, cluster munitions create broad, indiscriminate hazard zones.
  • Unlike temporary fortifications, landmines act as indiscriminate permanent hazards for civilian populations.
Common misconceptions:
  • Candidates often believe international treaties ban all types of landmines, overlooking anti-tank variants.
  • Many assume India adheres to the Ottawa Treaty despite its reliance on border minefields.
Exam focus:
  • Memorize the key prohibitions of the Ottawa Treaty and the Convention on Cluster Munitions.
  • Examine India's national security rationale for remaining outside these specific humanitarian treaties.
  • Understand the operational impact of unexploded ordnance on post-conflict reconstruction.
Concept 5

United Nations Register of Conventional Arms (UNROCA)

UNROCA promotes transparency in international conventional arms transfers to build mutual trust and regional stability.

Explanation

  • Established in 1991 by the UN General Assembly, UNROCA functions as a voluntary global transparency mechanism.
  • Member states report annual data on imports and exports of seven major conventional weapon categories.
  • The reporting categories include battle tanks, armored combat vehicles, large-caliber artillery, and combat aircraft.
  • Some states also voluntarily report on military holdings, procurement through national production, and transfers of small arms.
  • India actively participates in UNROCA submissions, demonstrating transparency while maintaining sovereign defense preparedness.

Key points

  • Transparency serves as a confidence-building measure designed to reduce misperceptions of aggressive military buildup.
  • The registry relies entirely on voluntary reporting by sovereign member states rather than intrusive inspections.
  • Data submitted to UNROCA is publicly accessible, facilitating academic research and parliamentary oversight.
  • Periodic group of governmental experts evaluates registry operations to incorporate emerging military technologies.
Examples / applications:
  • Reporting annual import quantities of foreign naval frigates and submarines to the UN registry.
  • Submitting national defense production figures for domestically manufactured main battle tanks.
Compare:
  • Unlike the ATT which enforces legal restrictions, UNROCA focuses solely on voluntary reporting and transparency.
  • Unlike IAEA safeguards, UNROCA does not involve on-site verification or intrusive inspections of military facilities.
Common misconceptions:
  • Students often mistake UNROCA for a binding disarmament treaty that limits military acquisitions.
  • Many believe reporting to the registry is legally mandatory for all United Nations member states.
Exam focus:
  • Learn the establishment year and primary objectives of the UN Register of Conventional Arms.
  • Identify the seven major weapon categories tracked under the standard UNROCA reporting format.
  • Evaluate the role of transparency measures as confidence-building tools in international security.
Concept 6

India's Conventional Arms Policy and Security Dynamics

India balances strategic autonomy with robust conventional modernization and selective multilateral arms control engagement.

Explanation

  • India maintains a massive conventional military capability to deter simultaneous threats along its western and northern borders.
  • The national defense acquisition strategy emphasizes 'Atmanirbhar Bharat' (Self-Reliant India) to reduce import dependency.
  • India supports global non-discriminatory disarmament but rejects treaties that compromise its sovereign security imperatives.
  • New Delhi engages constructively with transparency initiatives like UNROCA while abstaining from the Ottawa Treaty and ATT.
  • India participates in select multilateral export control regimes like the Wassenaar Arrangement to govern sensitive dual-use goods.

Key points

  • Conventional deterrence remains the cornerstone of India's grand strategy in a multi-aligned geopolitical environment.
  • Domestic defense manufacturing initiatives seek to transform India from a top global arms importer into an exporter.
  • Regional security challenges necessitate the retention of versatile conventional forces across mountain, desert, and maritime domains.
  • India's export controls align with international standards to prevent proliferation to non-state actors and rogue states.
Examples / applications:
  • Indigenously developing and producing the Arjun main battle tank and Tejas light combat aircraft.
  • Adopting the SCOMET (Special Chemicals, Organisms, Materials, Equipment and Technologies) export control list.
Compare:
  • Unlike Western allies bound by strict bloc-level arms pacts, India pursues independent multi-source defense procurement.
  • Unlike nations relying purely on humanitarian disarmament, India prioritizes operational territorial defense along active borders.
Common misconceptions:
  • Aspirants often assume India's opposition to certain treaties stems from disregard for international law.
  • Many confuse India's nuclear doctrine restraint with a lack of conventional military modernization programs.
Exam focus:
  • Analyze the intersection of India's strategic autonomy doctrine and conventional arms procurement policies.
  • Examine the objectives of the Wassenaar Arrangement and India's participation status.
  • Evaluate the strategic implications of domestic defense indigenization programs for regional stability.

Concept 1

Small Arms and Light Weapons (SALW)

Definition and scope

  • SALW comprise portable firearms designed for individual or crew-served tactical military and police use.
  • They are categorized primarily into small arms (pistols, rifles) and light weapons (heavy machine guns, mortars).
  • They represent the primary instrumentation of violence in modern asymmetric warfare and internal insurgencies.

Must remember

  • UN Programme of Action (UN PoA) is the principal global framework for SALW control.
  • SALW proliferation directly fuels transnational crime, terrorism, and civil conflict.
  • Marking and record-keeping are vital technical measures for tracing illicit weapons.
  • Light weapons require crew service, unlike individual small arms.
  • India emphasizes strict border management to counter illicit SALW inflows.

Concept-specific exam traps

  • Do not confuse SALW control with the total prohibition of state military arsenals.
  • Remember that SALW are not classified as weapons of mass destruction.
Concept 2

Convention on Certain Conventional Weapons (CCW)

Definition and scope

  • The CCW is a framework convention restricting specific conventional weapons under international humanitarian law.
  • It operates through specialized protocols targeting weapons causing indiscriminate or superfluous injury.
  • It bridges military necessity with humanitarian protections on the active battlefield.

Must remember

  • Protocol I bans undetectable X-ray fragments in the human body.
  • Amended Protocol II regulates landmines and booby-traps.
  • Protocol IV completely prohibits blinding laser weapons.
  • The convention entered into force internationally in December 1983.
  • India is a state party to the CCW and several key protocols.

Concept-specific exam traps

  • Avoid confusing CCW protocols with chemical or biological weapons conventions.
  • Do not assume the CCW bans all landmines without noting protocol exemptions.
Concept 3

Arms Trade Treaty (ATT)

Definition and scope

  • The ATT establishes international standards for regulating the global commercial trade in conventional arms.
  • It covers major conventional weapon systems including battle tanks, warships, and combat aircraft.
  • It mandates human rights and counter-terrorism risk assessments prior to export authorization.

Must remember

  • Adopted by the UN General Assembly in the year 2013.
  • Requires robust national export control systems and end-user verification.
  • India is a non-signatory to the ATT due to sovereignty and security concerns.
  • Focuses primarily on commercial international transfers rather than domestic production.
  • Aims to prevent diversion of legal arms into illicit trafficking networks.

Concept-specific exam traps

  • Never state that India is a founding member of the ATT.
  • Remember the ATT regulates trade flows rather than banning weapon manufacturing.
Concept 4

Cluster Munitions and Anti-Personnel Landmines

Definition and scope

  • Cluster munitions disperse submunitions over wide areas with high unexploded ordnance failure rates.
  • Anti-personnel landmines remain hazardous long after active hostilities cease.
  • Humanitarian treaties seek their complete abolition to protect civilian populations.

Must remember

  • The Ottawa Treaty completely bans anti-personnel landmines.
  • The Convention on Cluster Munitions prohibits cluster bomb stockpiles and use.
  • India is not a party to either the Ottawa Treaty or the CCM.
  • Border defense requirements drive India's continued retention of landmines.
  • Unexploded ordnance clearance is a critical component of post-conflict reconstruction.

Concept-specific exam traps

  • Do not confuse anti-personnel landmines with anti-tank mines under the Ottawa Treaty.
  • Do not assume India has signed the Convention on Cluster Munitions.
Concept 5

United Nations Register of Conventional Arms (UNROCA)

Definition and scope

  • UNROCA is a voluntary global transparency mechanism established in 1991.
  • It tracks international transfers across seven major conventional weapon categories.
  • It functions as a confidence-building measure to reduce regional security misperceptions.

Must remember

  • Established by UN General Assembly resolution in 1991.
  • Participation and reporting by member states are entirely voluntary.
  • Data submitted is publicly available for academic and strategic analysis.
  • India regularly submits national reports to UNROCA.
  • It does not involve intrusive on-site verification inspections.

Concept-specific exam traps

  • Do not mistake UNROCA for a legally binding disarmament or arms reduction treaty.
  • Remember that reporting is voluntary, not mandatory for UN members.
Concept 6

India's Conventional Arms Policy and Security Dynamics

Definition and scope

  • India's policy centers on maintaining robust conventional deterrence along active contested borders.
  • It combines strategic autonomy in procurement with indigenous manufacturing drives ('Atmanirbhar Bharat').
  • It engages selectively with international transparency and export control mechanisms.

Must remember

  • India is a participating member of the Wassenaar Arrangement.
  • New Delhi rejects discriminatory treaties that limit sovereign defense preparedness.
  • SCOMET lists govern India's national dual-use export controls.
  • Conventional modernization is essential for addressing simultaneous continental threats.
  • Domestic defense production aims to transform India into an arms exporter.

Concept-specific exam traps

  • Do not confuse India's nuclear no-first-use posture with its conventional military doctrine.
  • Remember that India participates in select export control regimes while avoiding restrictive disarmament treaties.

Quick facts

  • The UN Programme of Action on SALW was adopted in the year 2001.
  • The CCW entered into force internationally in December 1983.
  • The Arms Trade Treaty was adopted by the UN General Assembly in 2013.
  • UNROCA was established as a voluntary transparency register in 1991.
  • The Ottawa Treaty for landmine bans was concluded in 1997.
  • India is a participating state in the Wassenaar Arrangement on Export Controls.
  • CCW Protocol IV explicitly prohibits blinding laser weapons.

Glossary

Small ArmsPortable firearms designed for personal use such as pistols, rifles, and sub-machine guns.
Light WeaponsCrew-served portable armaments including heavy machine guns, grenade launchers, and portable anti-tank missiles.
Cluster MunitionA container that ejects multiple smaller submunitions over a wide target area.
Transparency MeasureA confidence-building tool such as data reporting designed to reduce military suspicions between states.
End-User CertificateAn official document verifying the final recipient and intended use of transferred military equipment.

Important distinctions

Arms Trade Treaty (ATT)vsUN Register of Conventional Arms (UNROCA)
  • ATT establishes legally binding standards and transfer criteria for state exports.
  • UNROCA operates strictly as a voluntary transparency and data-reporting mechanism without binding prohibitions.
CCW Protocol IVvsConvention on Cluster Munitions
  • CCW Protocol IV specifically prohibits blinding laser weapons.
  • The Convention on Cluster Munitions targets explosive submunitions that create unexploded ordnance hazards.

Memory hooks

  • Remember UNROCA as the voluntary 1991 transparency register for major weapon imports and exports.
  • Recall the CCW through its Roman numeral protocols banning blinding lasers and regulating landmines.
  • Keep India's stance clear: cooperative on transparency and export controls, cautious on binding disarmament treaties.

Exam traps and cautions

  • Do not confuse the Arms Trade Treaty with the UN Programme of Action on Small Arms.
  • Do not assume India has signed the Ottawa Treaty or the Convention on Cluster Munitions.
  • Never describe UNROCA as an intrusive on-site inspection regime like the IAEA.
  • Remember that light weapons are crew-served, whereas small arms are individual personal weapons.
