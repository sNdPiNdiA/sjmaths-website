Current Foreign and Defence Policies

Current Defence Policy of India for UP PGT Military Science

India's current defence policy integrates proactive deterrence, strategic autonomy, and domestic industrial capacity to secure national interests.

UP PGT Military Science6 concepts42 quiz questions10-question test

Topic overview

  • India's current defence policy integrates proactive deterrence, strategic autonomy, and domestic industrial capacity to secure national interests.
  • The policy addresses a complex two-front threat matrix involving both continental land borders and maritime domains.
  • Defence preparedness increasingly emphasizes indigenization, advanced technology absorption, and multi-service theater command structures.
  • Strategic partnerships and minilateral security architectures play a vital role in balancing regional power dynamics in the Indo-Pacific.
Concept 1

Strategic Autonomy and Multi-Alignment

India maintains independent foreign and defence policies by engaging diverse global powers without entering formal military alliances.

Explanation

  • Strategic autonomy prevents external subordination of national security decision-making processes.
  • Multi-alignment enables constructive security and economic cooperation with competing global powers simultaneously.
  • India collaborates through minilateral frameworks like the Quad while fiercely protecting its independent strategic voice.
  • Non-alignment evolved into multi-alignment to maximize national gains in a multipolar international system.
  • Defence diplomacy is leveraged to build security partnerships without compromising core sovereign prerogatives.

Key points

  • Rejects historical bloc politics in favor of issue-based coalitions.
  • Maintains defense supply-chain diversification across western and eastern partners.
  • Upholds independent voting positions in multilateral security forums.
  • Enhances geopolitical leverage through diplomatic flexibility.
Examples / applications:
  • Simultaneous participation in the Quad security dialogue and BRICS multilateral summits.
  • Maintaining robust defense trade with both Russia and Western nations like the United States and France.
Compare:
  • Unlike Cold War non-alignment which favored passivity, multi-alignment pursues active and pragmatic engagement.
  • Unlike formal alliance systems, multi-alignment avoids mutual defense obligations and automatic entanglements.
Common misconceptions:
  • Multi-alignment is frequently misinterpreted by critics as diplomatic indecision or fence-sitting.
  • Some assume strategic autonomy prevents India from entering interoperability agreements with partner nations.
Exam focus:
  • Explain the evolution from non-alignment to contemporary multi-alignment in Indian grand strategy.
  • Evaluate how strategic autonomy shields India from external pressures in defence procurement.
  • Analyze the role of minilateralism within the broader framework of Indian foreign policy.
Concept 2

Atmanirbharta in Defence Production

Self-reliance in military manufacturing seeks to reduce heavy import dependence through domestic design, development, and production.

Explanation

  • Atmanirbharta aims to transform India from the world's leading arms importer into a net defense exporter.
  • Positive Indigenization Lists legally mandate embargoes on importing specific military platforms and sub-systems.
  • Corporatization of the Ordnance Factory Board into seven distinct public sector undertakings improved accountability and efficiency.
  • Defence Acquisition Procedure prioritizes Buy Indian and IDDM categories to incentivize domestic research and development.
  • Innovations for Defence Excellence scheme fosters startups, MSMEs, and individual innovators in military technology.

Key points

  • Reduces strategic vulnerability to foreign supply chain disruptions and geopolitical sanctions.
  • Stimulates domestic high-tech manufacturing ecosystems and generates specialized employment.
  • Encourages joint ventures and foreign direct investment through progressive policy relaxations.
  • Focuses on critical domains like aerospace, naval shipbuilding, and combat vehicles.
Examples / applications:
  • Indigenously designed and manufactured Light Combat Aircraft Tejas inducted into the Indian Air Force.
  • Commissioning of Indigenous Aircraft Carrier INS Vikrant built at Cochin Shipyard Limited.
Compare:
  • Unlike historical license-production models, current policy mandates indigenous intellectual property generation.
  • Unlike pure public sector monopolies, current indigenization policy actively promotes private sector participation.
Common misconceptions:
  • Atmanirbharta implies complete autarky and total cessation of all foreign defense imports immediately.
  • Domestic manufacturing is mistakenly assumed to compromise military quality compared to imported hardware.
Exam focus:
  • Examine the significance of Positive Indigenization Lists in driving domestic defence manufacturing.
  • Discuss the role of IDDM categories under the Defence Acquisition Procedure in promoting research.
  • Analyze the structural impact of corporatizing ordnance factories on India's defense supply chain readiness.
Concept 3

Two-Front War Doctrine and Deterrence

Indian military planning accounts for the complex challenge of simultaneous active conflict on both western and northern borders.

Explanation

  • The two-front contingency requires force structuring to handle coordinated hostile actions by Pakistan and China.
  • Deterrence posture relies on conventional military superiority, nuclear deterrence credibility, and border infrastructure development.
  • Force posture adjustments focus on rapid redeployment capabilities from the western front to the northern plateau.
  • Intelligence, surveillance, and reconnaissance assets provide early warning along contested Himalayan and desert frontiers.
  • Integrated logistics nodes and reserve ammunition stockpiles are prioritized to sustain high-intensity dual-theater combat.

Key points

  • Demands seamless tri-service operational synergy and optimized resource allocation.
  • Requires heavy mountain strike corps and high-altitude warfare specialization.
  • Elevates the importance of cyber, space, and electronic warfare capabilities across both borders.
  • Integrates diplomatic signaling with military readiness to deter opportunistic escalation.
Examples / applications:
  • Upgradation of forward airbases and construction of strategic tunnels like Atal Tunnel for round-the-year connectivity.
  • Deployment of advanced surveillance drones and mountain infantry divisions along the Line of Actual Control.
Compare:
  • Unlike single-front Cold War planning, current doctrine prepares for simultaneous high-intensity conventional engagements.
  • Unlike purely defensive posture shifts, modern deterrence incorporates offensive-defense and proactive response options.
Common misconceptions:
  • The two-front doctrine is often misconstrued as an aggressive policy of launching pre-emptive simultaneous invasions.
  • Some believe nuclear weapons alone eliminate the requirement for robust conventional warfighting capability.
Exam focus:
  • Analyze the structural challenges posed by the two-front threat matrix to Indian defense budgeting.
  • Evaluate the role of border infrastructure development in enhancing conventional deterrence along the LAC.
  • Discuss the operational implications of multi-domain warfare in managing simultaneous border crises.
Concept 4

Military Jointness and Theater Commands

Reforms in higher defense organization aim to integrate Army, Navy, and Air Force under unified geographic commands for optimized warfare.

Explanation

  • The creation of the Department of Military Affairs under the Chief of Defence Staff institutionalizes tri-service integration.
  • Theater commands replace single-service commands to ensure synchronized application of military power in specific theaters.
  • Joint logistics nodes streamline supply chains, repair facilities, and fuel distribution across all three services.
  • Integrated communication networks facilitate real-time data sharing and common operational picture awareness.
  • Joint doctrine development eliminates service silos and optimizes procurement to prevent duplication of military assets.

Key points

  • Transitions from coordinated service operations to fully integrated joint combat actions.
  • Enhances cost-efficiency by pooling administrative and support resources across the armed forces.
  • Streamlines the chain of command for faster decision-making during high-tempo military operations.
  • Focuses on theater-specific threats rather than isolated service-specific operational priorities.
Examples / applications:
  • Establishment of the permanent office of the Chief of Defence Staff to act as the single-point military advisor.
  • Creation of specialized tri-service agencies for cyber, space, and special operations.
Compare:
  • Unlike legacy systems where individual service chiefs operated independently, theater commands enforce unified operational control.
  • Unlike allied joint commands such as US CENTCOM, Indian theater commands are tailored strictly to immediate continental and maritime security geography.
Common misconceptions:
  • Theaterization is mistakenly believed to abolish the individual identity and service traditions of the Army, Navy, and Air Force.
  • Critics often assume the Chief of Defence Staff exercises direct operational command over combat units.
Exam focus:
  • Explain the rationale behind establishing the Department of Defence Production and the Department of Military Affairs.
  • Assess the strategic benefits and administrative hurdles of implementing integrated theater commands in India.
  • Discuss the role of the Chief of Defence Staff in fostering inter-service synergy and joint planning.
Concept 5

Indo-Pacific Strategy and Maritime Security

India projects naval power and builds security partnerships to maintain a free, open, and inclusive rules-based order in the Indo-Pacific.

Explanation

  • The Indo-Pacific framework expands India's strategic horizon from the Indian Ocean to the Western Pacific rim.
  • SAGAR doctrine emphasizes Security and Growth for All in the Region, positioning India as a preferred security partner.
  • Net security provider role involves counter-piracy patrols, humanitarian assistance, and disaster relief operations.
  • Information Fusion Centre for Indian Ocean Region enhances maritime domain awareness through collaborative intelligence sharing.
  • Strategic naval exercises with friendly navies build tactical interoperability and deter maritime hegemony.

Key points

  • Protects vital sea lines of communication critical for national energy security and international trade.
  • Counters extra-regional naval footprints and strategic encirclement concerns in the maritime domain.
  • Leverages island territories like Andaman and Nicobar and Lakshadweep as strategic power projection hubs.
  • Promotes adherence to UNCLOS and freedom of navigation principles across international waters.
Examples / applications:
  • Active participation in the Malabar naval exercises involving India, US, Japan, and Australia.
  • Bilateral hydrographic surveys and capacity-building assistance extended to littoral states in the Indian Ocean.
Compare:
  • Unlike historical inward-looking naval policies, current strategy actively asserts blue-water power projection capabilities.
  • Unlike exclusive alliance frameworks, India's Indo-Pacific vision emphasizes inclusive minilateral cooperation.
Common misconceptions:
  • The Indo-Pacific strategy is often mischaracterized as an Asian NATO designed solely for military containment.
  • Some confuse maritime security cooperation with formal mutual defense treaties obligating combat intervention.
Exam focus:
  • Analyze the strategic objectives of the SAGAR doctrine in Indian maritime security policy.
  • Evaluate the role of the Quad and bilateral naval partnerships in maintaining stability in the Indo-Pacific.
  • Discuss the importance of Maritime Domain Awareness and the Information Fusion Centre for Indian Ocean Region.
Concept 6

Nuclear Doctrine and Credible Deterrence

India maintains a nuclear weapons program anchored on No First Use and massive retaliation to ensure credible minimum deterrence.

Explanation

  • The No First Use posture dictates that India will never use nuclear weapons first in any armed conflict scenario.
  • Retaliation to a nuclear attack will be massive and designed to inflict unacceptable damage on the adversary.
  • Authorized nuclear retaliation can only be commanded by the civilian political leadership through the Nuclear Command Authority.
  • Credible minimum deterrence ensures the maintenance of a survivable nuclear arsenal capable of second-strike delivery.
  • Strategic triad capability across land, air, and sea guarantees survivability and unbreakable deterrence stability.

Key points

  • Rejects pre-emptive nuclear strike doctrines while maintaining a high state of operational readiness.
  • Reserves the right to use nuclear weapons in response to chemical or biological attacks against India or its forces.
  • Separates political authorization from military execution to ensure absolute civilian control over strategic assets.
  • Focuses on survivability through ballistic missile submarines and dispersed mobile land launchers.
Examples / applications:
  • Operationalization of the nuclear triad with the induction of Arihant-class nuclear-powered ballistic missile submarines.
  • Development and successful testing of Agni series intercontinental ballistic missiles with strategic reach.
Compare:
  • Unlike the declared first-use policies of certain nuclear weapon states, India's policy strictly adheres to no first use.
  • Unlike minimum deterrence doctrines that rely on small arsenals, credible minimum deterrence adapts dynamically to emerging missile defense technologies.
Common misconceptions:
  • No First Use is frequently misunderstood as a sign of military weakness rather than a calculated political deterrent posture.
  • Some analysts incorrectly assume India's nuclear command structure allows immediate battlefield-level tactical nuclear weapon use.
Exam focus:
  • Critically examine the core tenets of India's Nuclear Doctrine and the strategic logic of No First Use.
  • Explain the institutional mechanism of the Nuclear Command Authority in governing strategic weapons.
  • Discuss the importance of the nuclear triad and second-strike capability in maintaining credible deterrence.

  • Positive Indigenization Lists legally restrict import of specified military hardware.
  • SAGAR stands for Security and Growth for All in the Region.
  • The Information Fusion Centre for Indian Ocean Region is located in Gurugram.
  • The Nuclear Command Authority consists of a Political Council and an Executive Council.

Glossary

AtmanirbhartaState policy objective of achieving economic and military self-reliance.
IDDMIndigenously Designed, Developed, and Manufactured equipment category.
SAGARSecurity and Growth for All in the Region maritime policy framework.
No First UsePolicy committing a nuclear power not to use nuclear weapons unless first attacked.

Important distinctions

Non-AlignmentvsMulti-Alignment
  • Non-alignment focused on maintaining passive distance from Cold War power blocs.
  • Multi-alignment pursues active, pragmatic, and simultaneous engagement with multiple global powers.

Memory hooks

  • Remember the nuclear doctrine pillars using the triad: No First Use, Massive Retaliation, Civilian Control.
  • Recall Indo-Pacific maritime security through SAGAR: Security and Growth for All.
  • Associate Atmanirbharta with IDDM and Positive Indigenization Lists for self-reliance.

Exam traps and cautions

  • Never confuse India's multi-alignment strategy with Cold War non-alignment.
  • Do not state that India has abandoned No First Use in official defense doctrine.
  • Avoid assuming theater commands eliminate the individual service branches.
  • Do not treat Atmanirbharta as an immediate ban on all foreign defense imports.
