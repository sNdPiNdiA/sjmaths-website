Question 1

For data 2, 2, 3, 5, 8, the mode is:

  1. 2
  2. 3
  3. 4
  4. 8
Show step-wise answer

(a) 2

  1. The mode is the value with the greatest frequency.
  2. 2 occurs twice; every other listed value occurs once.

Weighted averages · 1 practice mark

Question 2

Two equally large groups have means 12 and 18. Their combined mean is:

  1. 6
  2. 12
  3. 15
  4. 30
Show step-wise answer

(c) 15

  1. The weights are equal.
  2. The weighted mean reduces to (12 + 18)/2 = 15.

Question 3

A 100% stacked bar graph always makes each complete bar represent:

  1. The same absolute count
  2. 100% of its own group
  3. Only its largest category
  4. The arithmetic mean
Show step-wise answer

(b) 100% of its own group

  1. Each group’s category frequencies are converted to percentages of that group.
  2. The complete bar is 100%, even when the group sizes differ.

Weighted mean · 1 practice mark

Question 4

10 students average 50 marks and 20 average 80 marks. Their combined average is:

  1. 65
  2. 70
  3. 75
  4. 130
Show step-wise answer

(b) 70

  1. Total marks = 10 × 50 + 20 × 80 = 2100.
  2. Divide by 30 students: 2100/30 = 70.

Weighted averages · 3 practice marks

Question 5

A class of 20 students has average score 60; another class of 30 has average 80. Find the combined average and explain why averaging 60 and 80 directly is wrong.

Show step-wise answer

Combined average 72.

  1. The total scores are 20 × 60 = 1200 and 30 × 80 = 2400.
  2. Combined average = (1200 + 2400)/(20 + 30) = 3600/50 = 72.
  3. The classes have different sizes; weight each average by its student count instead of treating the averages equally.

Weighted averages · 3 practice marks

Question 6

Rice costing ₹40/kg and ₹60/kg is mixed in quantities 3 kg and 2 kg. Find the average cost per kilogram and verify it by total cost.

Show step-wise answer

₹48/kg.

  1. Total cost is 3 × 40 + 2 × 60 = ₹240.
  2. Total mass is 3 + 2 = 5 kg.
  3. The weighted mean cost is 240/5 = ₹48/kg, not the unweighted average ₹50/kg.

Central tendency · 3 practice marks

Question 7

For 4, 6, 6, 8, 11, find the mean, median and mode. Explain why the three measures need not be equal.

Show step-wise answer

Mean 7, median 6, mode 6.

  1. The sum is 35; dividing by 5 gives mean 7.
  2. The third value in this sorted list is 6, so median 6.
  3. 6 occurs most often, giving mode 6. The measures use different features of the data.

Question 8

For travel times 10, 15, 15, 20 and 40 minutes, compute mean, median and mode. Explain which measure best describes a typical trip if 40 minutes was an exceptional traffic delay, and suggest an investigative question.

Show step-wise answer

Mean 20, median 15, mode 15 minutes; the median is less affected by the exceptional delay.

  1. The total is 100 minutes for 5 trips, so the mean is 20.
  2. The middle sorted value is 15, giving the median; 15 also occurs most often, giving the mode.
  3. The unusually long trip raises the mean. The median gives a useful typical value here, though the right measure depends on the purpose.
  4. An investigative question could be “How does travel time change by day or route?” Record comparable trips and organise them in a table.

Stacked and 100% stacked bar graphs · 4 practice marks

Question 9

Class A has 12 students travelling by bus and 8 walking; Class B has 15 by bus and 15 walking. Describe ordinary stacked and 100% stacked bar graphs for these data. Which graph compares proportions more directly?

Show step-wise answer

Ordinary bar totals 20 and 30; percentage bars show A 60%/40% and B 50%/50%.

  1. An ordinary stacked bar combines category counts: A = 12 + 8 = 20, B = 15 + 15 = 30.
  2. Divide each category by its own class total to form percentage shares.
  3. For A, bus share 12/20 = 60%, walking 40%; for B the shares are 50% each.
  4. 100% stacked bars have equal total height and directly compare composition; ordinary bars retain absolute totals.

Question 10

A survey records daily reading times 5, 10, 10, 15, 20, 60 minutes. Find mean, median and mode. Explain one sampling issue to consider before using this survey to represent the whole school.

Show step-wise answer

Mean 20 min; median 12.5 min; mode 10 min.

  1. The sum is 120, so the mean is 120/6 = 20.
  2. There are six sorted values; average the third and fourth: (10 + 15)/2 = 12.5.
  3. 10 occurs most frequently, so it is the mode.
  4. A small or selected group may not represent all ages/classes. Use a fair sampling plan, a clear question and a consistent measurement period.

Question 11

School X reports 30 bus, 10 bicycle and 10 walking journeys; School Y reports 36 bus, 12 bicycle and 12 walking journeys. Compare their stacked and 100% stacked bars and explain what can and cannot be concluded.

Show step-wise answer

X total 50, Y total 60; both have proportions 60% bus, 20% bicycle, 20% walking.

  1. Ordinary stacked bars show different totals: 50 and 60.
  2. For X divide each count by 50; for Y divide by 60.
  3. Both percentage bars have segments 60%, 20% and 20%, hence identical composition.
  4. Equal composition does not imply equal absolute numbers. Nor does this observational table alone establish the cause of transport choices.

Percentage bars and counts · 4 practice marks

Question 12

Group A has 12 cycling and 18 walking students; B has 16 cycling and 24 walking students. Draw stacked and 100% stacked bars. Explain why identical percentage bars do not mean equal counts.

Show step-wise answer

Totals 30 and 40; both percentage bars are 40% cycling and 60% walking.

  1. Ordinary stacked bars have heights 30 and 40, divided into the stated counts.
  2. A cycling share is 12/30 = 40%; B cycling share is 16/40 = 40%.
  3. Both walking shares are 60%; percentage bars therefore look identical.
  4. Equal proportions can belong to different total sizes. Ordinary bars preserve the distinction in counts.
