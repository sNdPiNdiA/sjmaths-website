Question 1

Which number cannot be a probability?

  1. 0
  2. 0.4
  3. 1
  4. 1.2
Show step-wise answer

(d) 1.2

  1. Probabilities lie on the scale from 0 to 1 inclusive.
  2. 1.2 is larger than 1 and therefore cannot be a probability.

Question 2

For one throw of a fair six-sided die, the probability of a prime number is:

  1. 1/6
  2. 1/3
  3. 1/2
  4. 2/3
Show step-wise answer

(c) 1/2

  1. The equally likely sample space is {1,2,3,4,5,6}.
  2. Prime outcomes are 2,3,5, so probability = 3/6 = 1/2.

Question 3

An impossible event has probability:

  1. −1
  2. 0
  3. 1/2
  4. 1
Show step-wise answer

(b) 0

  1. An impossible event contains no possible outcomes.
  2. Its probability is 0. A certain event has probability 1.

Equally likely outcomes · 1 practice mark

Question 4

A fair die is thrown. The probability of a number greater than 4 is:

  1. 1/6
  2. 1/3
  3. 1/2
  4. 2/3
Show step-wise answer

(b) 1/3

  1. The favourable outcomes are 5 and 6.
  2. There are six equally likely outcomes, giving 2/6 = 1/3.

Question 5

A spinner lands on red 47 times in 100 trials. Estimate its probability of red. Does this prove its theoretical probability is exactly 0.47?

Show step-wise answer

Empirical estimate 0.47; it does not prove an exact theoretical probability.

  1. Relative frequency = red outcomes/total trials = 47/100.
  2. Finite experiments fluctuate randomly, so the observed fraction need not equal an exact theoretical value.
  3. A theoretical model requires information about the spinner and outcome probabilities; more trials can help assess the estimate.

Experimental estimate · 3 practice marks

Question 6

A coin gives 84 heads in 150 tosses. Estimate P(head). Explain why this does not prove the coin is unfair.

Show step-wise answer

Empirical estimate 84/150 = 14/25 = 0.56.

  1. Divide the head count by the total trials.
  2. Reduce 84/150 by 6 to get 14/25.
  3. A finite sample can fluctuate even for a fair coin. Further evidence is needed to judge fairness; one observed frequency is not an exact theoretical probability.

Question 7

Two fair coins are tossed independently. Use a tree or table to list the sample space and find probabilities of exactly one head and at least one head.

Show step-wise answer

Sample space {HH, HT, TH, TT}; probabilities 1/2 and 3/4.

  1. Branch H or T for the first coin, then H or T for the second.
  2. Independence gives each ordered outcome probability (1/2)(1/2) = 1/4.
  3. Exactly one head is {HT,TH}, so its probability is 2/4 = 1/2.
  4. At least one head is {HH,HT,TH}, so its probability is 3/4.

Question 8

Write a step-by-step simulation of 1000 fair die throws to estimate the probability of an even outcome. State the theoretical target and why a simulation may differ from it.

Show step-wise answer

Choose uniform random integers 1–6; estimated probability = even count/1000; theoretical target 1/2.

  1. Initialise an even-outcome counter to zero.
  2. Repeat 1000 times: sample one of 1,2,3,4,5,6 with equal probability and increment the counter for 2,4 or 6.
  3. Divide the final counter by 1000.
  4. Three of six equally likely outcomes are even, so theoretical probability is 1/2. Random fluctuations mean a finite estimate need not be exactly 0.5.

Question 9

A bag contains 3 red and 2 blue balls. Two balls are drawn without replacement. Draw a probability tree and find the probability that the colours differ.

Show step-wise answer

3/5.

  1. First branches have probabilities R = 3/5 and B = 2/5.
  2. After R, the next blue probability is 2/4. After B, the next red probability is 3/4.
  3. P(RB) = (3/5)(2/4) = 3/10 and P(BR) = (2/5)(3/4) = 3/10.
  4. The paths are disjoint, so add them: 3/10 + 3/10 = 3/5. Without replacement the second probabilities change.

Question 10

A player wins 18 of 30 games and then 12 of 20 games. Find the combined empirical win probability. Can we conclude the next 10 games must contain exactly 6 wins?

Show step-wise answer

Estimate 30/50 = 0.6; no exact future count is guaranteed.

  1. Add successes: 18 + 12 = 30. Add trials: 30 + 20 = 50.
  2. Combined relative frequency is 30/50 = 0.6.
  3. This is an empirical estimate under comparable playing conditions, not a guaranteed theoretical probability.
  4. Even if a suitable model had probability 0.6, future random outcomes need not match 6 out of 10 exactly.

Question 11

A learner lists sums 2,3,…,12 for two fair dice and assumes all sums are equally likely. Correct the model using a table and describe a simulation for estimating P(sum = 7).

Show step-wise answer

Use 36 equally likely ordered pairs; P(sum = 7) = 6/36 = 1/6.

  1. Make a 6-by-6 table: rows identify the first die and columns the second, giving 36 ordered pairs.
  2. Sum 7 occurs at (1,6), (2,5), (3,4), (4,3), (5,2), (6,1), so its probability is 1/6.
  3. Sums are not equally likely: sum 2 has only one ordered pair, while sum 7 has six.
  4. Simulate independent uniform throws of each die repeatedly, count sums of 7, and divide by total trials. Compare this relative frequency with 1/6.

With and without replacement · 4 practice marks

Question 12

A bag has 4 red and 3 blue balls. Find the probability of two red draws (i) with replacement, (ii) without replacement. Explain the changed branch probabilities.

Show step-wise answer

With replacement 16/49; without replacement 2/7.

  1. With replacement both red probabilities are 4/7; multiply to obtain 16/49.
  2. Without replacement, after a red ball is removed, 3 red balls remain among 6 balls.
  3. Multiply (4/7)(3/6) = 12/42 = 2/7.
  4. Replacement restores the initial composition; without it the second branch depends on the first result.
