Government of India Act 1919

UPSC Study Notes - Brief and Comprehensive

Government of India Act 1919 (Montagu-Chelmsford Reforms)

  • Also known as the Montagu-Chelmsford Reforms.
  • Enacted in 1919 and came into force in 1921.
  • Based on the Montagu Declaration (August Declaration) of 1917, which aimed for "gradual development of self-governing institutions" in India.

Key Provisions and Features

1. Provincial Government: Introduction of Dyarchy

  • Most significant feature.
  • Divided provincial subjects into two lists:
    • Transferred Subjects: Administered by the Governor with the aid of Ministers responsible to the legislative council (e.g., education, health, local self-government, agriculture, public works).
    • Reserved Subjects: Administered by the Governor and his Executive Council, without responsibility to the legislative council (e.g., law and order, finance, land revenue, irrigation).
  • Ministers were chosen from elected members of the provincial legislature.
  • The Governor retained overriding powers over both transferred and reserved subjects.

2. Central Government: No Dyarchy, Bicameralism Introduced

  • The central government remained unitary and centralized, with no dyarchy.
  • Executive:
    • The Governor-General (Viceroy) remained the head of the executive.
    • His Executive Council members were not responsible to the legislature.
    • Three out of six members (excluding the Commander-in-Chief) of the Viceroy's Executive Council were to be Indians.
    • The Viceroy retained extensive powers, including veto power, ordinance-making power, and the power to certify bills.
  • Legislature:
    • Replaced the Imperial Legislative Council with a bicameral legislature:
      • Upper House (Council of State): 60 members (34 elected).
      • Lower House (Legislative Assembly): 145 members (104 elected).
    • Introduced direct elections for both houses.
    • The legislature had limited powers; it could discuss the budget, pass resolutions, and ask questions, but had no control over the executive.
    • The Viceroy's assent was mandatory for any bill to become law.

3. Franchise and Communal Representation

  • The franchise was extended, but remained very limited (approximately 3% of the population).
  • Extended the principle of separate electorates to Sikhs, Indian Christians, Anglo-Indians, and Europeans (first introduced for Muslims by the Morley-Minto Reforms 1909).

4. Other Important Provisions

  • Secretary of State for India: Some functions were transferred to the newly created High Commissioner for India (appointed by the Government of India). The Secretary of State's salary was now paid from the British exchequer.
  • Public Service Commission: Provided for the establishment of a Central Public Service Commission for recruiting civil servants (established in 1926 based on the Lee Commission recommendations).
  • Statutory Commission: Mandated the appointment of a Statutory Commission after 10 years to inquire into and report on the working of the Act (led to the appointment of the Simon Commission in 1927).
  • Budget: Separated provincial budgets from the central budget, empowering provinces to enact their own budgets.

Significance and Impact

  • Marked the first step towards responsible government in the provinces.
  • Introduced direct elections and bicameralism at the central level, increasing Indian participation in the legislative process.
  • Led to increased political awareness and experience among Indians.
  • Dyarchy was largely unsuccessful due to practical difficulties, financial constraints, lack of clear demarcation of subjects, and the Governor's overriding powers.
