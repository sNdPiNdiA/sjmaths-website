Features of Semi Presidential System

UPSC Study Notes - Brief and Comprehensive

Key Characteristics

  • Dual Executive: Features both a directly elected President and a Prime Minister who heads the government.
  • Hybrid System: Blends elements of both presidential and parliamentary systems, aiming to combine stability with accountability.

Roles of the Executive

  • President:
    • Directly Elected: By universal adult suffrage for a fixed term, serving as the Head of State.
    • Significant Powers: Often holds powers over foreign policy, defense, national security, and emergency provisions.
    • Appoints Prime Minister: Appoints the Prime Minister, though the choice is usually dictated by the majority party/coalition in the legislature.
  • Prime Minister:
    • Head of Government: Leads the cabinet and manages domestic policy.
    • Accountable to Legislature: Must command the confidence of the legislature and can be removed by a vote of no-confidence.
    • Forms Cabinet: Selects and leads the cabinet, which is collectively responsible to the parliament.

Accountability Mechanisms

  • Prime Minister and Cabinet:
    • Subject to parliamentary oversight and can be removed by a vote of no-confidence by the legislature (e.g., France's National Assembly).
    • Collectively responsible to the legislature for government policy.
  • President:
    • Generally not subject to a vote of no-confidence by the legislature due to a fixed term.
    • Can typically only be removed through a rigorous impeachment process for grave constitutional violations.

Concept of Co-habitation

Co-habitation occurs when the President and the Prime Minister belong to different political parties, especially when the President's party does not hold a majority in the legislature. This situation can lead to:

  • Power struggles and policy disagreements between the two executive heads.
  • A division of responsibilities, where the President focuses on foreign affairs and the Prime Minister on domestic policy.
  • Notable in France, where it has occurred several times since the **Fifth Republic (1958)**.

Advantages

  • Stability: The fixed term of the President provides a degree of executive stability.
  • Flexibility: The government (PM and cabinet) can be changed without affecting the head of state, allowing for adaptation to legislative majorities.
  • Broader Mandate: Direct election of the President can provide a strong popular mandate for the Head of State.

Disadvantages

  • Potential for Conflict: Inherent tension and power struggles between the President and Prime Minister, particularly during co-habitation.
  • Unclear Lines of Responsibility: Can lead to confusion over who is ultimately responsible for policy outcomes, especially in times of crisis.
  • Power Imbalance: Depending on constitutional provisions, power can heavily tilt towards either the President (e.g., Russia) or the Prime Minister (e.g., Portugal).

Examples

  • France (classic example, particularly under the **Fifth Republic**).
  • Russia (strong presidential powers).
  • Portugal (more parliamentary-leaning).
  • Sri Lanka, Egypt, Ukraine.
