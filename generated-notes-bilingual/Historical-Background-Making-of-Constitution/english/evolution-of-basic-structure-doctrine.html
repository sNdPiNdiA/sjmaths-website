Evolution of Basic Structure Doctrine

The Basic Structure Doctrine is a judicial innovation that limits the Parliament's power to amend the Constitution under Article 368, ensuring that the core identity of the Constitution remains intact.

Key Judicial Landmarks

  • Shankari Prasad Case (1951): The Supreme Court held that the Parliament's power to amend under Article 368 includes the power to amend Fundamental Rights.
  • Golaknath Case (1967): The SC reversed its stance, ruling that Fundamental Rights are "sacrosanct" and cannot be abridged or taken away by the Parliament.
  • 24th Constitutional Amendment Act (1971): Enacted by Parliament to nullify the Golaknath verdict; it affirmed that Parliament has the power to amend any part of the Constitution.
  • Kesavananda Bharati Case (1973): The landmark verdict. The SC upheld the validity of the 24th Amendment but introduced the Basic Structure Doctrine. It ruled that while Parliament can amend any part, it cannot alter the "basic structure" or "essential features" of the Constitution.
  • 42nd Constitutional Amendment Act (1976): Attempted to restrict judicial review by adding clauses to Article 368, stating there is no limitation on the constituent power of Parliament.
  • Minerva Mills Case (1980): The SC struck down the clauses of the 42nd Amendment, declaring that the limited amending power of Parliament is itself a basic feature of the Constitution.
  • Waman Rao Case (1981): The SC clarified that the doctrine would apply to amendments enacted after the date of the Kesavananda Bharati judgment (April 24, 1973).

Elements of Basic Structure

The Constitution does not define "Basic Structure." It has been evolved by the Judiciary through various judgments. Key elements include:

  • Supremacy of the Constitution
  • Sovereign, democratic, and republican nature of the Indian polity
  • Secular character of the Constitution
  • Separation of powers between the Legislature, Executive, and Judiciary
  • Federal character of the Constitution
  • Unity and integrity of the nation
  • Welfare state (socio-economic justice)
  • Judicial Review
  • Freedom and dignity of the individual
  • Parliamentary system
  • Rule of law
  • Harmony and balance between Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles
  • Free and fair elections
  • Limited power of Parliament to amend the Constitution

Significance for UPSC

  • Checks and Balances: Prevents the misuse of the amending power by a brute majority in Parliament.
  • Constitutionalism: Ensures that the Constitution remains a living document while preserving its original spirit.
  • Judicial Activism: Represents the zenith of judicial power in interpreting the Constitution.
