Modern India (1858 to 1958 AD.)

Contribution of Mahatma Gandhi in the National Movement

Core study module, theoretical overview, and exam revision checklist for Contribution of Mahatma Gandhi in the National Movement under the History curriculum.

Appears in: UP TGT Social Science Tracker → 35 quiz questions10-question mini test

Topic overview

  • Mahatma Gandhi transformed the Indian national movement from an elite-led petitioning campaign into a mass-based revolutionary struggle.
  • He introduced unique socio-political philosophies anchored in Satya (Truth) and Ahimsa (Non-violence), redefining political morality.
  • His leadership spanned three watershed mass movements: Non-Cooperation, Civil Disobedience, and Quit India, fundamentally altering the trajectory of modern Indian history.
  • He systematically targeted social evils like untouchability, promoted communal harmony, and advanced village self-sufficiency through the constructive programme.
  • His methods successfully galvanized diverse socio-economic strata, including peasants, workers, women, and the urban middle class, into a unified anti-imperialist front.
Concept 1

Early Experiments and Political Evolution in South Africa (1893-1914)

Gandhi developed his philosophy of Satyagraha and mass mobilization techniques during his two decades in South Africa.

Detailed explanation

  • Mohandas Karamchand Gandhi arrived in Durban, South Africa, in 1893 on a legal case for the firm Dada Abdulla & Co.
  • He faced intense racial discrimination, exemplified by his ejection from a train compartment at Pietermaritzburg railway station in 1893.
  • He founded the Natal Indian Congress in 1894 to unite the Indian community against discriminatory legislation.
  • He established the weekly journal 'Indian Opinion' in 1903 to articulate the grievances of Indians and propagate his ideas.
  • He founded Phoenix Farm in 1904 near Durban and Tolstoy Farm in 1910 near Johannesburg as communal living and training grounds for Satyagraha.
  • He led the historic campaign against the £3 poll tax, the invalidation of non-Christian marriages, and the Asiatic Registration Act (Pass Laws).

Key points

  • First application of Satyagraha: 1906 against the Transvaal Asiatic Ordinance.
  • The Great March: Gandhi led over 2,000 striking Indian miners from Newcastle across the Transvaal border in November 1913.
  • Smuts-Gandhi Agreement: Concluded in 1914, addressing major Indian demands regarding poll tax and marriage recognition.
  • Return to India: Gandhi returned to India permanently on January 9, 1915, commemorated today as Pravasi Bharatiya Divas.
Examples and evidence
  • The Transvaal Indian Association resistance against compulsory finger-printing and registration certificates.
  • The protest march of indentured laborers from the mines of Natal into Transvaal without permits in 1913.
Important distinctions
  • Contrast with Moderate methods: While Moderates relied on petitions and prayers, Gandhi employed direct action and self-suffering.
  • Contrast with extremist methods: While extremists occasionally condoned secret violent action, Gandhi strictly adhered to absolute non-violence.
Common misconceptions
  • Misconception: Gandhi went to South Africa solely as a political leader. Fact: He went as a professional lawyer and was radicalized by institutional racism.
  • Misconception: Satyagraha was formulated instantly. Fact: It evolved gradually through years of experimentation, borrowing from Thoreau, Ruskin, and Tolstoy.
Exam focus
  • Chronology of South African institutions like Phoenix Farm and Tolstoy Farm.
  • The exact year of Gandhi's return to India and the context of the Smuts-Gandhi agreement.
  • Significance of Indian Opinion and Natal Indian Congress in early mobilization.
Concept 2

Early Regional Satyagrahas in India (1917-1918)

Champaran, Ahmedabad, and Kheda served as the laboratory for Gandhi's leadership within the Indian mainland.

Detailed explanation

  • Champaran Satyagraha (1917) was Gandhi's first civil disobedience movement in India, taken up at the invitation of Rajkumar Shukla.
  • The core issue in Champaran was the Tinkathia system, which forced peasants to cultivate indigo on 3/20th of their land.
  • Ahmedabad Mill Strike (1918) involved a dispute between mill owners and workers over the discontinuation of the Plague Bonus.
  • Gandhi undertook his first official hunger strike in India during the Ahmedabad mill dispute to secure a 35 percent wage hike for workers.
  • Kheda Satyagraha (1918) was organized due to crop failure and the British government's refusal to remit land revenue under the Revenue Code.
  • In Kheda, Gandhi was assisted by Vallabhbhai Patel and Indulal Yagnik in urging peasants to withhold taxes despite government coercion.

Key points

  • Champaran outcome: The Tinkathia system was abolished and a partial refund was granted to exploited peasants.
  • Ahmedabad outcome: Workers achieved a 35 percent wage increase through arbitration.
  • Kheda outcome: The government issued secret instructions that revenue should be collected only from those who could afford to pay.
  • Emergence of leadership: Sardar Vallabhbhai Patel emerged as a major national figure during the Kheda campaign.
Examples and evidence
  • Rajkumar Shukla persistently following Gandhi to the Lucknow Congress of 1916 to bring attention to Champaran.
  • Anasuya Sarabhai seeking Gandhi's intervention in the labor dispute between Ahmedabad mill owners and weavers.
Important distinctions
  • Champaran addressed agrarian exploitation by European planters against tenant farmers.
  • Kheda addressed state extraction of land revenue from land-owning peasants during times of famine and crop failure.
Common misconceptions
  • Misconception: Champaran was Gandhi's first-ever political agitation. Fact: It was his first in India, but he had already led major campaigns in South Africa.
  • Misconception: Gandhi fasted in Champaran. Fact: His first hunger strike in India occurred during the Ahmedabad Mill Strike.
Exam focus
  • The exact names of local leaders like Rajkumar Shukla and Vallabhbhai Patel.
  • The precise economic grievances behind Tinkathia and the Plague Bonus.
  • Chronological ordering of Champaran, Ahmedabad, and Kheda.
Concept 3

Non-Cooperation and Khilafat Movement (1920-1922)

Gandhi orchestrated a historic alliance between national political aspirations and pan-Islamist grievances against British rule.

Detailed explanation

  • The movement was formally launched on August 1, 1920, combining the grievances of the Khilafat wrong with the Punjab wrongs and Swaraj.
  • The Nagpur Session of the Congress in December 1920 endorsed the Non-Cooperation program and reorganized the Congress Working Committee.
  • The program included the surrender of titles, boycott of civil services, law courts, legislative councils, and foreign goods.
  • Constructive aspects emphasized the promotion of Swadeshi, hand-spinning of Khadi, establishment of national schools, and removal of untouchability.
  • The movement witnessed massive participation from peasants, students, women, and the working class across various provinces.
  • Gandhi suspended the movement abruptly following the Chauri Chaura incident on February 5, 1922, where a mob burned a police station, killing 22 policemen.

Key points

  • Khilafat leaders: Ali Brothers (Maulana Shaukat Ali and Maulana Mohammad Ali), Maulana Azad, and Hakim Ajmal Khan.
  • Congress response: Moderate leaders like Surendranath Banerjea left the Congress over the adoption of extra-constitutional mass struggle.
  • Arrest of Gandhi: Gandhi was arrested in March 1922 and sentenced to six years imprisonment.
  • Impact: The movement fundamentally transformed the Congress into a mass political organization with grassroots reach.
Examples and evidence
  • Renunciation of legal practices by eminent lawyers like Chittaranjan Das, Motilal Nehru, and Jawaharlal Nehru.
  • Burning of foreign cloth in public bonfires across major cities like Bombay and Calcutta.
Important distinctions
  • Contrast with Rowlatt Satyagraha: Non-Cooperation was a nationwide comprehensive withdrawal of cooperation, whereas the Rowlatt agitation was localized and brief.
  • Contrast with Moderate tactics: Shift from constitutional agitation within legislatures to extra-parliamentary mass action.
Common misconceptions
  • Misconception: The Khilafat movement was a purely religious agitation. Fact: It was an anti-imperialist political alliance against the dismemberment of the Ottoman Empire.
  • Misconception: Chauri Chaura was the sole reason for suspension. Fact: Gandhi was also deeply concerned about widespread outbreaks of violence elsewhere and the exhaustion of the masses.
Exam focus
  • Resolutions passed at the Calcutta Special Session (1920) and Nagpur Annual Session (1920).
  • Exact date and location of the Chauri Chaura incident.
  • Institutional changes within the Congress, such as the creation of the 15-member CWC.
Concept 4

Civil Disobedience Movement and Salt Satyagraha (1930-1934)

Gandhi defied the colonial state through the symbolic breaking of the salt laws, precipitating a nationwide anti-tax campaign.

Detailed explanation

  • The movement was preceded by the Lahore Congress session of December 1929, where the Purna Swaraj (Complete Independence) resolution was passed.
  • Gandhi issued an ultimatum of Eleven Demands to Lord Irwin on February 31, 1930, which was ignored by the Viceroy.
  • The Salt March (Dandi March) began on March 12, 1930, with 78 chosen followers marching 240 miles from Sabarmati Ashram to Dandi.
  • Gandhi broke the salt law at Dandi on April 6, 1930, marking the formal inauguration of the Civil Disobedience Movement.
  • The movement spread rapidly, witnessing widespread defiance of forest laws in the Central Provinces, no-chowkidari tax in Bihar, and picketing of liquor shops.
  • The movement was temporarily halted following the Gandhi-Irwin Pact of March 1931, leading to Gandhi's participation in the Second Round Table Conference.

Key points

  • Karachi Session (1931): Endorsed the Gandhi-Irwin Pact and passed landmark resolutions on Fundamental Rights and National Economic Programme.
  • Second Round Table Conference (1931): Gandhi attended as the sole representative of the Indian National Congress; talks broke down over separate electorates.
  • Communal Award (1932): Announced by Ramsay MacDonald, granting separate electorates to depressed classes; countered by Gandhi's epic fast unto death.
  • Poona Pact (1932): Signed between B.R. Ambedkar and Madan Mohan Malaviya (on behalf of Gandhi), increasing reserved seats for Dalits instead of separate electorates.
Examples and evidence
  • Khan Abdul Ghaffar Khan organizing the Khudai Khidmatgars (Red Shirts) in the North-West Frontier Province.
  • C. Rajagopalachari leading a salt march from Trichy to Vedaranyam on the Coromandel coast.
Important distinctions
  • Contrast with Non-Cooperation: Civil Disobedience involved active non-payment of taxes and open violation of laws, not merely non-cooperation.
  • Participation profile: Significantly higher involvement of women and business groups compared to the Non-Cooperation movement.
Common misconceptions
  • Misconception: The Poona Pact was signed by Gandhi himself. Fact: Gandhi was fasting in Yerwada Jail; the pact was signed by Ambedkar and Dalit/Congress leaders like M.M. Malaviya.
  • Misconception: The Dandi March was an isolated event. Fact: It served as a synchronized signal for nationwide law-breaking across different provinces.
Exam focus
  • Route, duration, and participant count of the Dandi March.
  • Provisions and consequences of the Gandhi-Irwin Pact and Poona Pact.
  • Role of regional leaders like Rani Gaidinliu, Sarojini Naidu, and Khan Abdul Ghaffar Khan.
Concept 5

Quit India Movement and Final Phase (1942-1948)

Gandhi issued the ultimate call for immediate British withdrawal during the Second World War with the slogan 'Do or Die'.

Detailed explanation

  • The failure of the Cripps Mission in April 1942 convinced Gandhi that compromise with British imperialism was impossible.
  • The All India Congress Committee passed the historic Quit India resolution at Gowalia Tank in Bombay on August 8, 1942.
  • Gandhi gave his famous 'Do or Die' (Karenge ya Marenge) speech, urging Indians to act as free men and women.
  • The British government launched Operation Zero Hour in the early hours of August 9, 1942, arresting almost all top-tier Congress leaders.
  • In the absence of recognized leadership, the movement spontaneously transformed into an underground resistance and localized uprisings.
  • Parallel governments were established in several regions, such as Ballia in Uttar Pradesh, Tamluk in Bengal, and Satara in Maharashtra.

Key points

  • Character of the movement: Leaderless, highly militant, and unprecedented in terms of spontaneous mass participation and disruption of communication lines.
  • Role of underground leaders: Ram Manohar Lohia, Jayaprakash Narayan, Aruna Asaf Ali, and Usha Mehta (who operated an underground radio station).
  • Gandhi's 21-day fast in 1943: Undertaken in Aga Khan Palace to protest government violence and state repression.
  • Release: Gandhi was unconditionally released from detention in May 1944 due to failing health.
Examples and evidence
  • Chitu Pandey leading the establishment of a parallel national government in Ballia for a brief period in August 1942.
  • Satara witnessing the longest-running parallel government (Prati Sarkar) led by Nana Patil.
Important distinctions
  • Contrast with earlier movements: Quit India was characterized by widespread violence and sabotage, despite Gandhi's creed of non-violence, due to the sudden arrest of leadership.
  • Attitude towards war: Shift from conditional support for the British war effort against fascism to immediate unconditional transfer of power.
Common misconceptions
  • Misconception: Gandhi planned and directed the violent sabotage of 1942. Fact: The violence was spontaneous and leaderless following the sudden arrest of all principal leaders.
  • Misconception: Gandhi actively participated in the post-war Cabinet Mission negotiations as a primary negotiator. Fact: While consulted, he was increasingly sidelined as negotiations shifted to constitutional power-transfer dynamics.
Exam focus
  • Exact date and venue of the Quit India resolution (August 8, 1942, Gowalia Tank, Bombay).
  • Locations of parallel governments (Ballia, Satara, Tamluk).
  • Role of underground activists like Usha Mehta and Jayaprakash Narayan.
Concept 6

Constructive Programme, Social Ideology and Harijan Uplift

Gandhi viewed social reform and economic self-reliance as essential prerequisites for political swaraj.

Detailed explanation

  • Gandhi formulated the Constructive Programme as a systematic blueprint for socio-economic transformation from the grassroots.
  • The promotion of Khadi and village industries was designed to eradicate rural poverty, unemployment, and economic dependence on British textiles.
  • He established the All India Spinners Association (1925) and All India Village Industries Association (1934) to institutionalize rural reconstruction.
  • The crusade against untouchability became central to his mission; he founded the All India Anti-Untouchability League in 1932, later renamed Harijan Sevak Sangh.
  • He published the weekly journal 'Harijan' starting in 1933 to advocate for the social, educational, and economic rights of depressed classes.
  • He championed Hindu-Muslim unity throughout his life, undertaking numerous fasts to quell communal riots during the partition trauma of 1946-1947.

Key points

  • Concept of Trusteeship: Wealthy individuals should hold their property as trustees for the benefit of society rather than as absolute owners.
  • Basic Education (Nai Talim): Formulated at the Wardha Conference in 1937, emphasizing learning through productive manual work and crafts.
  • Empowerment of Women: Gandhi strongly advocated for women's education, abolition of child marriage, purdah, and the dowry system, bringing women into the public sphere.
  • Gram Swaraj: Envisioned independent, self-contained village republics practicing participatory democracy and economic self-sufficiency.
Examples and evidence
  • Gandhi's Harijan Yatra across India in 1933-1934 to campaign against untouchability.
  • His lone peace mission through Noakhali and Calcutta in 1946-1947 to restore communal harmony during partition violence.
Important distinctions
  • Contrast with Ambedkar's approach: While Gandhi sought internal purification of Hinduism and social reform (temple entry, uplift), Ambedkar advocated for radical political safeguards, conversion, and legal annihilation of caste.
  • Contrast with socialist approach: While socialists emphasized class war and state ownership, Gandhi proposed trusteeship and decentralized village cooperatives.
Common misconceptions
  • Misconception: Gandhi supported the continuation of the caste system. Fact: While he initially defended Varna as a division of labor, he later rejected caste hierarchy entirely and advocated for inter-caste dining and marriage.
  • Misconception: Constructive work was secondary to political agitations. Fact: Gandhi considered constructive work the true foundation of Swaraj, viewing mass movements as intermittent expressions.
Exam focus
  • Names of journals founded by Gandhi (Indian Opinion, Young India, Navajivan, Harijan).
  • Key tenets of the Wardha Scheme of Basic Education (1937).
  • Organizations established for social reform such as Harijan Sevak Sangh and All India Spinners Association.

Concept 1

Early Experiments and Political Evolution in South Africa (1893-1914)

Point-wise rapid recall

  • Spent 21 formative years (1893-1914) in South Africa, initially arriving on a legal case for Dada Abdulla & Co.
  • Founded Natal Indian Congress (1894) and published 'Indian Opinion' newspaper to unite Indians against racial discrimination.
  • Established communal farms like Phoenix Settlement (1904) and Tolstoy Farm (1910) to foster self-reliance and collective living.
  • Developed and refined the concepts of Satyagraha (truth-force/soul-force), Ahimsa (non-violence), and civil disobedience against the Transvaal Asiatic Registration Act and poll tax.

Mnemonic

  • S-A-N-P: South Africa, Natal, Phoenix, Tolstoy

Tips

  • Remember the chronology: Natal Indian Congress came first (1894), followed by Phoenix (1904), Transvaal struggle, and Tolstoy Farm (1910).

Exam tricks

  • If a question asks where Satyagraha was first conceptualized and tested, the answer is South Africa, not Champaran.

Common traps

  • Confusing South African organizational milestones with early Indian regional satyagrahas.
Concept 2

Early Regional Satyagrahas in India (1917-1918)

Point-wise rapid recall

  • Champaran Satyagraha (1917): First civil disobedience in India; fought against the oppressive 'Tinkathia' system forcing peasants to cultivate indigo on 3/20th of land.
  • Ahmedabad Mill Strike (1918): First hunger strike by Gandhi; fought for 'Plague Bonus' dispute between mill owners and workers, resulting in a 35% wage hike.
  • Kheda Satyagraha (1918): First non-cooperation movement; peasants faced crop failure but officials demanded full revenue; Gandhi urged withholding revenue until relief was granted.
  • These three regional campaigns established Gandhi's mass appeal and grassroots leadership style in mainland India.

Mnemonic

  • C-A-K: Champaran (Indigo), Ahmedabad (Mill/Hunger Strike), Kheda (Revenue/Tax).

Tips

  • Link each Satyagraha to its specific issue: Champaran = Tinkathia/Indigo; Ahmedabad = Plague Bonus; Kheda = Revenue withholding.

Exam tricks

  • Remember that Ahmedabad was Gandhi's FIRST hunger strike, whereas Champaran was his FIRST civil disobedience movement in India.

Common traps

  • Inverting the years of Ahmedabad and Kheda; both occurred in 1918, but Ahmedabad strike happened before the Kheda harvest assessment.
Concept 3

Non-Cooperation and Khilafat Movement (1920-1922)

Point-wise rapid recall

  • Orchestrated a historic alliance between the Indian National Congress and pan-Islamist Khilafat leaders (Ali brothers) against British betrayal of the Ottoman Caliphate.
  • Officially launched on August 1, 1920; program included surrender of titles, boycott of legislature, law courts, foreign goods, and educational institutions.
  • Emphasized constructive programs like hand-spinning Khadi, removal of untouchability, and Hindu-Muslim unity.
  • Abruptly suspended by Gandhi in February 1922 following the Chauri Chaura incident where a police station was burnt down, resulting in police casualties.

Mnemonic

  • K-N-C: Khilafat, Non-Cooperation, Chauri Chaura suspension.

Tips

  • Note that the Khilafat issue was religious/pan-Islamic, but Gandhi cleverly merged it with Swaraj to broaden nationalist mass base.

Exam tricks

  • When asked why Gandhi suspended the movement, look for 'violence breaking out at Chauri Chaura' rather than government repression.

Common traps

  • Assuming the Khilafat movement was solely an Indian agrarian movement; it was primarily tied to international Islamic geopolitics.
Concept 4

Civil Disobedience Movement and Salt Satyagraha (1930-1934)

Point-wise rapid recall

  • Commenced with the historic 240-mile Dandi March from Sabarmati Ashram to Dandi, breaking the colonial salt law on April 6, 1930.
  • Expanded into a nationwide anti-tax campaign, forest law violations, picketing of foreign liquor and cloth shops, and refusal to pay chowkidar taxes.
  • Led to the Gandhi-Irwin Pact (1931), temporary suspension of the movement, and Gandhi's participation in the Second Round Table Conference in London.
  • Resumed in 1932 after failure of talks, finally withdrawn formally in 1934 as mass energy fatigued.

Mnemonic

  • D-G-R: Dandi march, Gandhi-Irwin pact, Round Table Conference.

Tips

  • Salt was chosen because it touched every single Indian across caste, class, and gender lines, making it the ultimate unifying symbol.

Exam tricks

  • Remember Gandhi attended only ONE Round Table Conference—the Second (1931).

Common traps

  • Confusing the First Round Table Conference (boycotted by INC) with the Second (attended by Gandhi).
Concept 5

Quit India Movement and Final Phase (1942-1948)

Point-wise rapid recall

  • Launched in August 1942 at the Bombay session of the AICC following the failure of the Cripps Mission.
  • Issued the historic 'Do or Die' (Karenge ya Marenge) mantra, demanding immediate and complete British withdrawal from India.
  • Marked by 'Operation Zero Hour', leading to immediate pre-dawn arrest of all top-tier national leaders, leaving the movement leaderless and spontaneously violent.
  • Gandhi undertook a 21-day fast in 1943 in detention; dedicated his final months to communal harmony during Partition violence before his assassination on January 30, 1948.

Mnemonic

  • Q-I-D: Quit India, Do or die, 1942.

Tips

  • Quit India was characterized by underground resistance and leaderless mass upsurge due to the sudden imprisonment of the entire leadership.

Exam tricks

  • If an exam question mentions 'Do or Die', immediately link it to the Quit India Movement of 1942, not Non-Cooperation.

Common traps

  • Believing Gandhi endorsed the widespread violence during Quit India; he maintained that non-violence remained the core creed even while calling for uncompromising struggle.
Concept 6

Constructive Programme, Social Ideology and Harijan Uplift

Point-wise rapid recall

  • Gandhi viewed social reform and economic self-reliance (constructive programme) as vital foundational prerequisites for political swaraj.
  • Promoted Khadi and village industries (Charkha) to achieve economic decentralization and eradicate rural poverty.
  • Fought relentlessly against untouchability, coining the term 'Harijans' (Children of God) and founding the Harijan Sevak Sangh in 1932.
  • Advocated Trusteeship theory for capitalists, urging them to act as trustees rather than absolute owners of wealth for the welfare of workers.

Mnemonic

  • C-H-T: Charkha (Khadi), Harijans (Uplift), Trusteeship (Economy).

Tips

  • Never separate Gandhi's political campaigns from his social constructive work; they were two sides of the same coin in his philosophy.

Exam tricks

  • Trusteeship does not advocate violent expropriation of private property; it relies on moral change of heart of the wealthy.

Common traps

  • Mistaking Gandhi's Trusteeship theory for a form of Marxist state socialism.

Quick facts

  • Gandhi returned to India from South Africa on January 9, 1915 (celebrated as Pravasi Bharatiya Divas).
  • The Champaran Satyagraha in 1917 was Gandhi's first civil disobedience movement on Indian soil.
  • Ahmedabad Mill Strike in 1918 was Gandhi's first hunger strike.
  • The Non-Cooperation Movement was launched alongside the Khilafat Movement on August 1, 1920.
  • The Dandi March covering 240 miles began on March 12, 1930, and broke the salt law on April 6, 1930.
  • Gandhi attended the Second Round Table Conference in London in 1931 after signing the Gandhi-Irwin Pact.
  • The Quit India Movement resolution was passed at Bombay on August 8, 1942, featuring the 'Do or Die' slogan.
  • Gandhi founded the Harijan Sevak Sangh in 1932 to eradicate untouchability and uplift depressed classes.

Topic mnemonics

  • S-E-N-C-Q: South Africa (1915) -> Early Regional (1917-18) -> Non-Cooperation (1920) -> Civil Disobedience (1930) -> Quit India (1942).
  • C-A-K-D: Champaran (Indigo), Ahmedabad (Bonus), Kheda (Tax), Dandi (Salt).
  • F-H-T: Fasting (moral weapon), Harijan uplift (social unity), Trusteeship (economic ethics).

Revision tips

  • Always trace Gandhi's movements chronologically to avoid mixing up regional agitations with national-level mass movements.
  • Pay close attention to the distinction between the goals of Non-Cooperation (Swaraj + Khilafat redressal) and Civil Disobedience (Complete independence / Purna Swaraj context).
  • Understand the dual nature of Gandhian strategy: mass direct action alternating with constructive social work.
  • Remember that Gandhi was a pragmatist as well as an idealist; he suspended movements when violence flared up.

Exam tricks

  • If a question asks for the 'first' of anything regarding Gandhi, check the modifiers: first Satyagraha in SA vs first in India vs first hunger strike vs first civil disobedience.
  • Link year '1918' to both Ahmedabad (early year) and Kheda (later harvest season).
  • Identify 'Chauri Chaura' instantly as the death knell of the Non-Cooperation Movement.
  • Match 'Dandi' with 'Salt' and 'August Kranti' with 'Quit India'.

Important comparisons

Non-Cooperation Movement (1920-22)vsCivil Disobedience Movement (1930-34)
  • NCM focused on non-cooperating with unjust colonial institutions; CDM focused on actively breaking specific colonial laws (like Salt and tax laws).
  • NCM was launched on pan-Islamist Khilafat grievances plus Swaraj; CDM explicitly targeted Purna Swaraj aspirations.
  • NCM ended abruptly due to local violence at Chauri Chaura; CDM went through structured truces, Round Table participation, and a phased withdrawal.

Exam traps and cautions

  • Confusing Champaran (indigo planters' coercion) with Kheda (land revenue assessment after crop failure).
  • Assuming Gandhi participated in all three Round Table Conferences (he attended only the Second).
  • Attributing the outbreak of Quit India violence directly to Gandhi's active instruction, ignoring that the immediate arrest of all leaders caused a leaderless uprising.
  • Believing Gandhi was a member of the Indian National Congress at the time of India's independence in 1947 (he had resigned from active membership earlier to focus on constructive work).
  • Mixing up the dates of the Gandhi-Irwin Pact (1931) with the Poona Pact (1932).
