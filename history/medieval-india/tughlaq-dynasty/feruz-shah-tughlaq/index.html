Tughlaq and Later Sultanate

Feruz Shah Tughlaq

Core study module, theoretical overview, and exam revision checklist for Feruz Shah Tughlaq under the History curriculum.

Appears in: UP TGT Social Science Tracker → 35 quiz questions10-question mini test

Topic overview

  • Firoz Shah Tughlaq ruled the Delhi Sultanate from 1351 to 1388 AD, succeeding his cousin Muhammad bin Tughlaq.
  • His reign is marked by extensive administrative reforms, welfare measures, and a departure from imperial expansion to consolidation.
  • He was the third ruler of the Tughlaq dynasty and came to power through an election by the nobles and the army led by Khan-i-Jahan Maqbul.
  • Contemporary and later chroniclers like Barani, Afif, and Sirhindi provide comprehensive accounts of his socio-economic and military policies.
Concept 1

Accession and Early Challenges

Firoz Shah Tughlaq ascended the throne during a severe crisis at Thatta in Sindh following Muhammad bin Tughlaq's death.

Detailed explanation

  • Muhammad bin Tughlaq died at Thatta in Sind in March 1351 AD during a campaign against Taghi.
  • The camp faced internal chaos, Mongol attacks, and rebellion by local chiefs.
  • Khwaja Jahan initially placed a boy named Malik Mahmud on the throne in Delhi.
  • Senior nobles and the army under Firoz Shah rejected Delhi's move and persuaded Firoz to accept the crown.
  • Firoz marched to Delhi, where Khwaja Jahan submitted peacefully without bloodshed.
  • Firoz forgave Khwaja Jahan but later executed him due to pressure from rival nobles led by Khan-i-Jahan Maqbul.

Key points

  • Ascension Year: 1351 AD
  • Predecessor: Muhammad bin Tughlaq
  • Key Supporter: Khan-i-Jahan Maqbul
  • Location of Accession: Thatta, Sind
Examples and evidence
  • Peaceful surrender of Khwaja Jahan in Delhi
  • Negotiated settlement with Bengal under Ilyas Shah
Important distinctions
  • Unlike Muhammad bin Tughlaq who faced constant rebellions due to radical policies, Firoz sought conciliation.
  • Unlike his predecessor's aggressive expansionism, Firoz focused on defensive consolidation.
Common misconceptions
  • Firoz Shah did not seize power through a bloody civil war.
  • He was not the immediate biological son of Ghiyas-ud-din Tughlaq but his nephew.
Exam focus
  • Circumstances of his ascension at Thatta
  • Role of Khan-i-Jahan Maqbul
  • Fate of Khwaja Jahan
Concept 2

Revenue and Taxation Reforms

Firoz Shah abolished unauthorized taxes and strictly implemented Islamic canonical taxation.

Detailed explanation

  • He abolished 24 harsh and illegal taxes known as abwabs that burdened the peasantry and merchants.
  • He restricted state demands to four canonical Shariat-approved taxes: Kharaj, Zakat, Jizya, and Khums.
  • Jizya was levied strictly on non-Muslims as a separate tax, and even Brahmins were brought under its purview.
  • He commissioned a fresh revenue settlement based on the assessment of actual produce by Khwaja Hisam-ud-din Junaid.
  • Agricultural prosperity was revived by constructing an extensive network of canals for irrigation.
  • He introduced a special irrigation tax called Haqq-i-Sharb, fixed at ten percent of the produce from canal-irrigated lands.

Key points

  • Abolished Taxes: 24 abwabs
  • Approved Taxes: Kharaj, Zakat, Jizya, Khums
  • Irrigation Tax: Haqq-i-Sharb
  • Revenue Assessor: Khwaja Hisam-ud-din Junaid
Examples and evidence
  • Imposition of Jizya on Brahmins who previously enjoyed exemption
  • Digging of the Western Yamuna Canal and Ghaggar Canal
Important distinctions
  • Unlike Ala-ud-din Khilji who maximized state share to fifty percent, Firoz adopted a moderate revenue demand to encourage agriculture.
  • Unlike Muhammad bin Tughlaq's failed Doab tax hike, Firoz linked taxation directly to verifiable irrigation facilities.
Common misconceptions
  • Firoz did not eliminate all taxes; he replaced arbitrary cesses with Shariat-compliant ones.
  • Haqq-i-Sharb was legally sanctioned by contemporary Muslim theologians.
Exam focus
  • Number of abolished abwabs
  • Introduction and rate of Haqq-i-Sharb
  • Status of Jizya under Firoz
Concept 3

Public Works and Infrastructure

Firoz Shah is renowned in medieval Indian history as a prolific builder of canals, towns, hospitals, and charitable institutions.

Detailed explanation

  • He constructed five major canal systems to supply water to arid regions of Haryana and Delhi.
  • The longest canal was dug from the Sutlej river to Hansi, a distance of over 150 miles.
  • He founded several new towns including Firozabad, Jaunpur, Hissar, Fatehabad, and Firozpur.
  • He established the Dar-ul-Shifa, a free state hospital for the treatment of the poor and needy.
  • He repaired older architectural landmarks such as the Qutub Minar and the Shamsi tank.
  • He transported two Ashokan pillars from Topra and Meerut to Delhi and erected them with great fanfare.

Key points

  • New Towns Founded: Firozabad, Jaunpur, Hissar, Fatehabad
  • Hospital Established: Dar-ul-Shifa
  • Notable Relocated Monuments: Ashokan Pillars from Topra and Meerut
  • Major Canal: Sutlej to Hansi
Examples and evidence
  • Placement of the Topra Ashokan pillar inside the citadel of Firozabad
  • Construction of numerous sarais, gardens, and public baths
Important distinctions
  • Unlike his predecessors who built mostly forts and mosques for military or religious display, Firoz prioritized public utility infrastructure.
  • Unlike Muhammad bin Tughlaq's wasteful shifting of the capital, Firoz's urban foundations endured and flourished.
Common misconceptions
  • The Ashokan pillars were not newly carved by Firoz; they were ancient monuments relocated by him.
  • Jaunpur was founded in memory of his cousin Muhammad bin Tughlaq, whose real name was Jauna Khan.
Exam focus
  • Names of cities founded by Firoz Shah
  • Origins of the Ashokan pillars in Delhi
  • Function of Dar-ul-Shifa
Concept 4

Military Organization and Nobility

Firoz Shah's military administration introduced hereditary assignments and weakened central control over the army.

Detailed explanation

  • He revived the Jagir system and made military commands and land revenue assignments hereditary.
  • He declared that upon a soldier's retirement, his son, son-in-law, or slave would succeed to his office and salary.
  • He stopped the practice of branding horses (dagh) and maintaining descriptive rolls of soldiers (chehra).
  • He treated his nobles and slaves with extreme leniency, allowing the concentration of power in the hands of ministers like Khan-i-Jahan Maqbul.
  • He amassed a massive corps of slaves (bandagan), numbering around 180,000, who were employed in royal workshops and administration.
  • His military campaigns were limited and largely unsuccessful, failing to reconquer Bengal or independent Deccan territories.

Key points

  • Hereditary Principle: Applied to military posts and jagirs
  • Abolished Practices: Dagh and Chehra
  • Slave Corps Size: Approximately 180,000 slaves
  • Chief Minister: Khan-i-Jahan Maqbul
Examples and evidence
  • Failure of his two military expeditions to Bengal against Ilyas Shah and Sikandar Shah
  • Unsuccessful siege of Jajnagar in Orissa
Important distinctions
  • Unlike Ala-ud-din Khilji who strictly enforced dagh and chehra to maintain a professional standing army, Firoz promoted hereditary feudalism.
  • Unlike Balban who ruthlessly crushed the nobility, Firoz pampered the nobles and made their positions hereditary.
Common misconceptions
  • The large slave corps did not strengthen the Sultanate; instead, it became a destabilizing faction after his death.
  • Firoz was not a successful military conqueror; his reign was characterized by military stagnation.
Exam focus
  • Hereditary army service under Firoz
  • Abolition of Dagh and Chehra
  • Role and size of the slave (bandagan) department
Concept 5

Religious Policy and Patronage

Firoz Shah governed as an orthodox Sunni Muslim ruler, closely aligning state policy with the dictates of the Ulema.

Detailed explanation

  • He was deeply pious and sought the sanction of the Caliph of Egypt to legitimize his rule.
  • He actively patronized orthodox Sunni Islam and suppressed practices he deemed un-Islamic or heretical.
  • He banned public processions of Shia Muslims and persecuted heterodox sects like the Mahdvis and Sufi saints who violated orthodoxy.
  • He established the Diwan-i-Khairat to provide financial aid for the marriage of poor Muslim girls.
  • He set up a translation bureau to translate Sanskrit medical and astrological texts into Persian.
  • His court chronicler, Shams-i-Siraj Afif, and Ziya-ud-din Barani strongly praised his pious devotion to Islam.

Key points

  • Charity Department: Diwan-i-Khairat
  • Legitimacy Source: Caliph of Egypt
  • Literary Work: Fatuhat-i-Firozshahi (autobiography)
  • Key Chroniclers: Barani and Afif
Examples and evidence
  • Translation of Sanskrit scientific texts under the title Dalail-i-Firozshahi
  • Destruction of newly built Hindu temples where idols were worshipped publicly
Important distinctions
  • Unlike Ala-ud-din Khilji who separated religion from politics, Firoz made religious orthodoxy a pillar of statecraft.
  • Unlike Akbar who later championed religious tolerance, Firoz enforced strict orthodox Hanafi jurisprudence.
Common misconceptions
  • Firoz Shah's religious policy was entirely driven by personal bigotry, though it also served political consolidation.
  • His autobiography Fatuhat-i-Firozshahi is a short inscription rather than a massive historical chronicle.
Exam focus
  • Diwan-i-Khairat and Diwan-i-Bandagan
  • Authorship of Fatuhat-i-Firozshahi
  • Attitude towards the Ulema and Islamic orthodoxy

Concept 1

Accession and Early Challenges

Point-wise rapid recall

  • Ascended the throne in 1351 during a severe administrative and political crisis.
  • Took power at Thatta in Sindh following the sudden death of Muhammad bin Tughlaq.
  • Faced immediate rebellions and a demoralized royal army.
  • Emphasized conciliation and compromise to stabilize the fractured Delhi Sultanate.

Mnemonic

  • AT-TS: Accession Thatta Tughlaq Sindh

Tips

  • Remember that Firoz came to power through succession crisis management rather than conquest.

Exam tricks

  • If a question mentions Thatta and 1351 regarding accession, it is always Firoz Shah.

Common traps

  • Confusing his accession site (Thatta, Sindh) with Muhammad bin Tughlaq's death campaign.
Concept 2

Revenue and Taxation Reforms

Point-wise rapid recall

  • Abolished 24 harsh and unauthorized taxes (abwabs) collected by predecessors.
  • Strictly implemented Islamic canonical taxation aligned with Sharia.
  • Levied only four approved taxes: Kharaj, Zakat, Jizya, and Khams.
  • Imposed irrigation tax (Haq-i-Sharb) on lands watered by state canals.

Mnemonic

  • KZJK: Kharaj, Zakat, Jizya, Khams

Tips

  • Link the abolition of 24 taxes directly to his benevolent or orthodox image.

Exam tricks

  • Haq-i-Sharb is unique to Firoz; look for canal irrigation tax questions.

Common traps

  • Assuming he reduced overall tax burden without noting the introduction of the new canal water tax.
Concept 3

Public Works and Infrastructure

Point-wise rapid recall

  • Renowned as a prolific builder of canals, towns, hospitals, and charitable institutions.
  • Constructed an extensive network of irrigation canals from Yamuna and Sutlej.
  • Founded major urban centers like Firozabad, Hissar, and Jaunpur.
  • Established Dar-ul-Shifa (hospitals) and Diwan-i-Khairat (marriage bureau for poor).

Mnemonic

  • FHJ: Firozabad, Hissar, Jaunpur

Tips

  • Match public works like canals and hospitals directly to Firoz's welfare state model.

Exam tricks

  • Towns starting with 'Firoz' or built as new administrative bases usually trace back to him.

Common traps

  • Attributing canal construction to Muhammad bin Tughlaq; Firoz is the master builder of canals.
Concept 4

Military Organization and Nobility

Point-wise rapid recall

  • Introduced hereditary assignments for military personnel and nobles.
  • Weakened central control over the army by allowing soldiers to pass offices to sons.
  • Revived the Iqta system and made land grants permanent.
  • Increased the number of slaves (Diwan-i-Bandagan), which bloated state expenditure.

Mnemonic

  • HSB: Hereditary, Slaves, Bureaucracy

Tips

  • Note that hereditary military posts directly led to the decline of central military efficiency.

Exam tricks

  • Hereditary assignments = Firoz Shah Tughlaq. Temporary/checking assignments = Alauddin Khilji.

Common traps

  • Confusing his generous treatment of nobility with strong centralized military command.
Concept 5

Religious Policy and Patronage

Point-wise rapid recall

  • Governed as an orthodox Sunni Muslim ruler closely aligned with the Ulema.
  • Imposed Jizya strictly, including on Brahmins who were previously exempt.
  • Translated Sanskrit texts into Persian and patronized orthodox Islamic scholarship.
  • Banned un-Islamic practices and public worship displays by non-Muslims.

Mnemonic

  • Ulema-Sunni-Jizya

Tips

  • Contrast his orthodox religious policy with Akbar's later liberal approach.

Exam tricks

  • If Brahmins paying Jizya is mentioned, the ruler is always Firoz Shah Tughlaq.

Common traps

  • Assuming his welfare works were secular; they were deeply tied to Islamic charity concepts.

Quick facts

  • Firoz Shah ascended the throne in 1351 at Thatta.
  • Abolished 24 unauthorized taxes (abwabs).
  • Introduced irrigation tax known as Haq-i-Sharb.
  • Founded the city of Jaunpur in memory of Muhammad bin Tughlaq (Jauna Khan).
  • Established Diwan-i-Khairat for poor Muslim girls' marriages.
  • Set up Diwan-i-Bandagan department specifically for slaves.
  • Made military and administrative offices hereditary.
  • Imposed Jizya on Brahmins for the first time in Delhi Sultanate history.

Topic mnemonics

  • C-T-W-S: Canals, Towns, Welfare, Slaves
  • J-H-F-D: Jizya on Brahmins, Haq-i-Sharb, Firozabad, Diwan-i-Bandagan
  • A-R-P-M-R: Accession, Revenue, Public works, Military, Religious policy

Revision tips

  • Always associate canal building and founding towns with Firoz Shah.
  • Keep track of his revenue changes: abolished 24 taxes but added Haq-i-Sharb.
  • Remember his administrative weakness: hereditary assignments ruined military discipline.
  • Connect his welfare departments (Khairat, Bandagan, Shifa) directly to his name.

Exam tricks

  • Eliminate options attributing severe military reforms to Firoz; he was conciliatory and lax.
  • Spot 'Haq-i-Sharb' as the instant giveaway for Firoz's economic policy questions.
  • Look for 'Brahmins and Jizya' to instantly identify Firoz Shah Tughlaq.
  • Check whether a town starts with 'Firoz' (Firozabad, Firozpur) before guessing the founder.

Important comparisons

Alauddin KhiljivsFeruz Shah Tughlaq
  • Alauddin maintained strict centralized control and abolished hereditary posts, whereas Firoz made military assignments hereditary.
  • Alauddin kept religion separate from state policy; Firoz governed strictly as an orthodox Sunni aligned with the Ulema.
  • Alauddin taxed heavily to maintain a massive standing army; Firoz abolished 24 unauthorized taxes and focused on public infrastructure.

Exam traps and cautions

  • Believing Firoz Shah was a great military conqueror rather than an administrator and builder.
  • Confusing the Diwan-i-Khairat (marriage bureau) with Diwan-i-Bandagan (slaves department).
  • Forgetting that Firoz levied Jizya on Brahmins.
  • Assuming all his tax reforms reduced state revenue, ignoring the new canal water tax.
  • Mistaking the accession location of Thatta for Delhi.
