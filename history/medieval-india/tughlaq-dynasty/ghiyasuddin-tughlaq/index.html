Tughlaq and Later Sultanate

Ghiyasuddin Tughlaq

Core study module, theoretical overview, and exam revision checklist for Ghiyasuddin Tughlaq under the History curriculum.

Appears in: UP TGT Social Science Tracker → 35 quiz questions10-question mini test

Topic overview

  • Ghiyasuddin Tughlaq, also known as Ghazi Malik, was the founder of the Tughlaq dynasty of the Delhi Sultanate, reigning from 1320 to 1325 CE.
  • He ascended the throne after overthrowing Nasir-ud-din Khusrau Shah, ending the brief and turbulent Khilji dynasty restoration period.
  • His administrative policies aimed at restoring fiscal health, agricultural prosperity, and internal security after the chaotic rule of Mubarak Shah Khilji.
  • He was the first Sultan of Delhi to assume the title of 'Ghazi' or slayer of infidels, emphasizing his military career against the Mongols.
  • His brief five-year reign laid the systemic foundations for the expansionist policies later executed by his son and successor, Muhammad bin Tughlaq.
Concept 1

Accession and Rise to Power

Ghazi Malik's transition from a frontier governor to the founder of a new imperial dynasty.

Detailed explanation

  • Ghazi Malik originally served as the governor of Dipalpur under the reign of Alauddin Khilji.
  • He successfully defended the north-western frontiers of the Delhi Sultanate against repeated Mongol invasions multiple times.
  • Following the assassination of Qutb-ud-din Mubarak Shah, Nasir-ud-din Khusrau Shah seized the throne of Delhi in 1320 CE.
  • Ghazi Malik mobilized regional governors and military commanders under the banner of avenging the Khiljis and restoring orthodox Islamic governance.
  • He defeated Khusrau Shah at the Battle of Kahravan near Delhi in 1320 CE and was subsequently invited by nobles to occupy the throne.

Key points

  • Original name: Ghazi Malik.
  • Title assumed: Ghiyas-ud-din Tughlaq Shah.
  • Year of accession: 1320 CE.
  • Predecessor: Nasir-ud-din Khusrau Shah.
Examples and evidence
  • Mobilization of the military forces of Multan and Sindh against Khusrau Shah.
  • Securing support from provincial governors like Ayn-ul-Mulk Mulani before marching on Delhi.
Important distinctions
  • Unlike Alauddin Khilji who seized power through patricide and usurpation, Ghiyasuddin came to power through military coalition and elimination of an unpopular usurper.
  • Unlike the Khilji rulers of Turkish-Afghan extraction, the Tughlaqs are traditionally identified by historians as belonging to the Qarauna Turk tribe.
Common misconceptions
  • Misconception that Ghiyasuddin was a direct blood relative of the Khilji dynasty.
  • Belief that he came to power through palace intrigue rather than open military confrontation.
Exam focus
  • Name of the battle against Khusrau Shah.
  • Tribal origins of the Tughlaq dynasty as discussed by Ibn Battuta.
  • Exact year of establishment of the Tughlaq dynasty.
Concept 2

Agricultural and Revenue Reforms

Moderate revenue policies designed to encourage peasant cultivation and stabilize state finances.

Detailed explanation

  • Ghiyasuddin reversed the harsh agrarian measures of Alauddin Khilji, opting for a system of moderation and conciliation.
  • He formulated the principle that the revenue demand should not be increased exorbitantly at once, but rather raised gradually over years.
  • Land revenue was fixed between one-tenth and one-eleventh of the produce, avoiding the oppressive half-produce demand of Alauddin.
  • He instructed revenue collectors (amils and muqtis) not to ruin cultivators by excessive taxation and to protect agricultural assets.
  • Special concessions and tax exemptions were granted to restore lands that had gone out of cultivation due to previous misrule.

Key points

  • Policy principle: Moderation in revenue collection.
  • Exemption of wasteland developers from immediate taxation.
  • Protection of village headmen (Muqaddams, Chaudharis, and Khuts) whose rights were restored.
  • Strict audit of provincial revenue accounts to prevent embezzlement.
Examples and evidence
  • Restoration of hereditary rights and perquisites to local revenue officials known as Khuts and Muqaddams.
  • Remission of arrears of land revenue in provinces suffering from natural calamities.
Important distinctions
  • Contrasts sharply with Alauddin Khilji's policy of heavy taxation, abolition of intermediary privileges, and state-controlled market pricing.
  • Differs from Muhammad bin Tughlaq's later ill-fated experiment of heavy taxation in the Doab region.
Common misconceptions
  • Assuming Ghiyasuddin continued the centralized agrarian exploitation of Alauddin Khilji without modification.
  • Belief that village headmen were completely eliminated during his reign.
Exam focus
  • Treatment of local intermediaries like Khuts, Muqaddams, and Chaudharis.
  • Proportion of land revenue demanded during his reign.
  • Core philosophy of gradual increase in revenue assessment.
Concept 3

Public Works and Infrastructure

Extensive developmental initiatives focusing on irrigation, communication, and urban planning.

Detailed explanation

  • Ghiyasuddin placed strong emphasis on the construction of canals for irrigation to boost agricultural yields.
  • He ordered the excavation of several new canals and the repair of older silted water channels.
  • A robust postal and intelligence network (mrid and barid systems) was revitalized to ensure swift communication across the vast empire.
  • He prioritized the construction of fortified towns and defensive architecture to safeguard against internal rebellions and external invasions.
  • Horticulture was encouraged, and royal gardens were established around administrative centers.

Key points

  • Pioneer in state-sponsored canal irrigation within the Delhi Sultanate.
  • Enhancement of the horse and foot-runner postal relay system.
  • Focus on border security through fortification.
  • Encouragement of internal trade routes by ensuring safety from highway robbers.
Examples and evidence
  • Construction of the fortified city of Tughlaqabad near Delhi.
  • Digging of irrigation canals in various agrarian tracts of the Doab.
Important distinctions
  • Unlike his successor Muhammad bin Tughlaq who frequently shifted capitals, Ghiyasuddin built a single formidable new stronghold.
  • His irrigation works served as the foundational blueprint for the extensive canal network later expanded by Firoz Shah Tughlaq.
Common misconceptions
  • Crediting Firoz Shah Tughlaq with the initiation of all Sultanate canal systems.
  • Viewing Tughlaqabad merely as a palace rather than a comprehensive fortified administrative city.
Exam focus
  • Foundation of the city of Tughlaqabad.
  • Role in early canal irrigation development.
  • Maintenance of communication and intelligence networks.
Concept 4

Military Campaigns and Imperial Consolidation

Aggressive pacification of rebellious provinces and strategic frontier defense.

Detailed explanation

  • Ghiyasuddin undertook military expeditions to reassert central authority over distant provinces that had slipped away during the Khilji succession crisis.
  • He sent his eldest son, Jauna Khan (later Muhammad bin Tughlaq), to reconquer the rebellious regions of Warangal in the Deccan.
  • Initial campaigns against Prataparudra II of Warangal faced setbacks, but subsequent campaigns resulted in the annexation and renaming of Sultanpur.
  • He personally led a successful expedition against the ruler of Bengal, Shamsuddin Bahadur Shah, restoring imperial control over Lakhnauti.
  • The north-western frontier was heavily garrisoned, successfully repelling renewed Mongol incursions through proactive military deployment.

Key points

  • Key commander in Deccan: Prince Jauna Khan.
  • Deccan target: Kakatiya kingdom of Warangal.
  • Eastern target: Bengal Sultanate under Shamsuddin Bahadur Shah.
  • Frontier policy: Aggressive deterrence against Mongol incursions.
Examples and evidence
  • The siege and subjugation of Warangal in 1323 CE.
  • The Bengal campaign of 1324-1325 CE which divided Bengal into administrative spheres.
Important distinctions
  • Unlike Alauddin Khilji who maintained indirect tributary control over the Deccan, Ghiyasuddin and Jauna Khan began the process of direct annexation.
  • His military approach combined diplomatic conciliation with swift punitive strikes against defiant regional governors.
Common misconceptions
  • Assuming Ghiyasuddin remained a passive ruler focused solely on domestic administration.
  • Confusing his Deccan policy with the later radical transfer of capital initiatives by his son.
Exam focus
  • Role of Prince Jauna Khan in the Warangal campaign.
  • Outcome and reorganization of Bengal administration.
  • Treatment of defeated regional dynasties.
Concept 5

Administrative and Judicial Policy

Establishment of a rule of law, bureaucratic efficiency, and orthodox administrative balance.

Detailed explanation

  • Ghiyasuddin restored the prestige of the judiciary by appointing competent and honest Qazis to administer Islamic and customary law impartially.
  • He curbed the arbitrary corruption of nobles and military commanders by enforcing strict administrative accountability.
  • Torture and harsh mutilation punishments introduced during earlier reigns were curtailed, favoring a more humane yet deterrent penal code.
  • He maintained cordial relations with the influential Sufi saints of the Chishtiyya and Suhrawardiyya orders, though occasional friction occurred.
  • The spy network was reorganized to keep the Sultan informed of bureaucratic malfeasance and potential conspiracies.

Key points

  • Judicial reform: Empowerment of regular courts and Qazis.
  • Administrative motto: Justice combined with benevolence.
  • Relation with nobility: Balancing old Turkish nobility with capable new commanders.
  • Anti-corruption drives across provincial governance.
Examples and evidence
  • Enforcement of strict audit rules on revenue officials who embezzled state funds.
  • Arbitration of local disputes through established judicial channels rather than arbitrary royal fiat.
Important distinctions
  • Contrasts with the draconian and deeply intrusive market regulations and spy systems of Alauddin Khilji.
  • Differs from the eccentric and unpredictable judicial edicts issued later by Muhammad bin Tughlaq.
Common misconceptions
  • Belief that Ghiyasuddin ruled as an absolute autocrat without respect for established legal norms.
  • Assumption that he persecuted non-Muslim subjects systematically.
Exam focus
  • Reforms in judicial administration and penal codes.
  • Approach towards curbing bureaucratic corruption.
  • Balance of power between the crown and the nobility.
Concept 6

Death and Controversy at Afghanpur

The mysterious circumstances surrounding the sudden demise of the Sultan in 1325 CE.

Detailed explanation

  • Ghiyasuddin died in February 1325 CE under the collapsing wooden pavilion (köshk) at Afghanpur near Delhi.
  • The pavilion was specially erected by his son, Prince Jauna Khan, to welcome the Sultan back from his successful Bengal campaign.
  • Contemporary chroniclers like Ibn Battuta and Isami recorded strong suspicions of patricide orchestrated by Jauna Khan with the help of Sufi saint Nizamuddin Auliya.
  • Modern historians debate whether the collapse was a pre-planned assassination conspiracy or a tragic accidental structural failure caused by an elephant.
  • Following his sudden death, Prince Jauna Khan seamlessly ascended the throne as Muhammad bin Tughlaq without immediate armed opposition.

Key points

  • Year of death: 1325 CE.
  • Location: Afghanpur pavilion near Delhi.
  • Primary contemporary accounts: Ibn Battuta (Rihla) and Isami (Futuh-us-Salatin).
  • Successor: Prince Jauna Khan (Muhammad bin Tughlaq).
Examples and evidence
  • Ibn Battuta's detailed account of the architect Ahmad b. Ayaz designing the pavilion to collapse under specific mechanical stress.
  • The popular historical anecdote involving Nizamuddin Auliya and the phrase 'Huzur Dilli dur ast' (Delhi is still far off).
Important distinctions
  • Unlike natural deaths of prior founding monarchs, Ghiyasuddin's death remains one of the most controversial assassination mysteries in medieval Indian history.
  • Contrasts with the orderly successions established by strong rulers through formal nominations.
Common misconceptions
  • Accepting the Afghanpur pavilion collapse as an undisputed historical assassination without acknowledging counter-arguments of accidental structural collapse.
  • Believing Nizamuddin Auliya directly participated in the physical murder of the Sultan.
Exam focus
  • Authors and texts documenting the Afghanpur incident (Ibn Battuta, Isami).
  • Exact year of Ghiyasuddin's death.
  • Immediate successor to the throne.

Concept 1

Accession and Rise to Power

Point-wise rapid recall

  • Ghazi Malik started his career as a frontier governor under the Khalji dynasty.
  • He rose to power by defeating Khusrau Khan in 1320 CE, ending the brief usurpation.
  • He founded the Tughlaq dynasty, adopting the title Ghiyasuddin Tughlaq.
  • He was the first Sultan of Delhi to use the title 'Ghazi' (slayer of infidels).

Mnemonic

  • Ghiyasuddin's Rise: 'G-K-F' -> Ghazi Malik (G) defeated Khusrau Khan (K) and Founded Tughlaq (F).

Tips

  • Remember that Ghiyasuddin came to power through popular and aristocratic support after removing an unpopular usurper.

Exam tricks

  • Look for keywords like 'Ghazi Malik' and 'Khusrau Khan' to identify questions related to his accession.

Common traps

  • Trap: Assuming he was a general of Alauddin Khalji from the very beginning without tracking his regional prominence in Dipalpur.
Concept 2

Agricultural and Revenue Reforms

Point-wise rapid recall

  • Adopted a moderate revenue policy based on the principle of 'concilation and moderation'.
  • Fixed land revenue as a fraction of produce rather than exorbitant arbitrary demands.
  • Exempted barren lands and encouraged peasant cultivation to expand the agrarian base.
  • Restricted local revenue collectors (Muqtis and Walis) from over-taxing peasants.

Mnemonic

  • Revenue rule: 'M-M-E' -> Moderate revenue, Maximize cultivation, Exempt barren lands.

Tips

  • Contrast his moderate stance with Alauddin's harsh 50% land tax and market controls.

Exam tricks

  • Whenever a question mentions 'concilation' or 'encouraging peasants' in early Tughlaq era, it refers to Ghiyasuddin.

Common traps

  • Trap: Confusing Ghiyasuddin's moderate revenue rates with Muhammad bin Tughlaq's disastrous Doab taxation experiment.
Concept 3

Public Works and Infrastructure

Point-wise rapid recall

  • Initiated extensive canal construction for irrigation to support agriculture.
  • Improved communication by maintaining efficient postal and courier systems.
  • Founded the fortified city of Tughlaqabad, featuring a massive stone fortress.
  • Emphasized urban planning and infrastructural safety against Mongol incursions.

Mnemonic

  • Public Works: 'ICU' -> Irrigation, Communication, Urban planning (Tughlaqabad).

Tips

  • Keep in mind that while Firoz Shah Tughlaq expanded canals vastly, Ghiyasuddin was the pioneer of canal irrigation among Tughlaq Sultans.

Exam tricks

  • Associate 'Tughlaqabad fort' instantly with Ghiyasuddin Tughlaq.

Common traps

  • Trap: Attributing all canal-building activities of the Tughlaq period solely to Firoz Shah.
Concept 4

Military Campaigns and Imperial Consolidation

Point-wise rapid recall

  • Aggressively pacified rebellious provinces that tried to break away after Khalji decline.
  • Sent his eldest son, Jauna Khan (later Muhammad bin Tughlaq), to conquer Warangal (Kakatiyas).
  • Maintained strict frontier defense measures against ongoing Mongol threats.
  • Ensured firm military control over Bengal through direct expedition and strategic interference.

Mnemonic

  • Military focus: 'P-F-W' -> Pacification of rebels, Frontier defense, Warangal expedition.

Tips

  • Note that Jauna Khan led the southern campaigns on behalf of his father.

Exam tricks

  • Remember that Ghiyasuddin's military policy was defensive-offensive: securing borders while crushing internal revolts.

Common traps

  • Trap: Crediting Ghiyasuddin with deep southern imperial annexations; his southern campaigns were mostly punitive and tributary.
Concept 5

Administrative and Judicial Policy

Point-wise rapid recall

  • Established a clear rule of law, curbing corruption among state officials.
  • Restored bureaucratic efficiency by respecting older, capable administrative personnel.
  • Maintained an orthodox administrative balance without extreme religious rigidity.
  • Ensured justice was swift and impartial to win public confidence.

Mnemonic

  • Admin pillars: 'B-J-O' -> Bureaucratic efficiency, Justice swift, Orthodox balance.

Tips

  • Focus on how he reversed the extreme administrative centralization of Alauddin Khalji.

Exam tricks

  • Think of Ghiyasuddin as a restorer of administrative normalcy rather than an innovator of radical policies.

Common traps

  • Trap: Assuming his administration was as autocratic and oppressive as his predecessor's.
Concept 6

Death and Controversy at Afghanpur

Point-wise rapid recall

  • Died suddenly in 1325 CE under mysterious and controversial circumstances.
  • An iconic wooden pavilion (pandal) collapsed upon him at Afghanpur near Delhi.
  • The pavilion was allegedly engineered by his son and successor, Jauna Khan.
  • Historians like Ibn Battuta and Ziauddin Barani recorded suspicions of patricregime/parricide.

Mnemonic

  • Death event: 'A-W-P' -> Afghanpur wooden pavilion collapse.

Tips

  • Remember the year 1325 CE as the end of his brief 5-year reign (1320-1325 CE).

Exam tricks

  • If a question mentions 'wooden pavilion' or 'Afghanpur', the answer directly points to Ghiyasuddin's death.

Common traps

  • Trap: Confusing his natural-looking accidental death with illness or battlefield defeat.

Quick facts

  • Ghiyasuddin Tughlaq reigned from 1320 to 1325 CE.
  • He was the founder of the Tughlaq dynasty.
  • His original name or pre-accession title was Ghazi Malik.
  • He constructed the fortified city of Tughlaqabad.
  • His son Jauna Khan later became Muhammad bin Tughlaq.
  • He followed a moderate land revenue policy of conciliation.
  • He died in 1325 CE due to the collapse of a wooden pavilion at Afghanpur.
  • Ibn Battuta and Barani provided accounts of his mysterious death.

Topic mnemonics

  • Tughlaq Founders: 'G-J-F' -> Ghiyasuddin, Jauna (Muhammad), Firoz.
  • Reign Span: 'Five to Twenty' -> 1320 to 1325 CE (5 years).
  • Major Achievements: 'R-I-M-A' -> Revenue moderation, Infrastructure canals, Military pacification, Administrative rule of law.

Revision tips

  • Always connect Ghiyasuddin's early career to the northwestern frontier defense against Mongols.
  • Distinguish his moderate revenue approach from Muhammad bin Tughlaq's erratic experiments.
  • Memorize the exact year of his accession (1320) and death (1325).
  • Note the role of architectural legacy, specifically the Tughlaqabad fort.

Exam tricks

  • When questions mention 'Ghazi Malik', immediately think of the transition from Khalji to Tughlaq.
  • Look for 'Afghanpur pavilion' as a dead giveaway for Ghiyasuddin's demise.
  • Associate 'moderate revenue' with Ghiyasuddin and 'harsh price control' with Alauddin.
  • Treat his 5-year rule as a period of consolidation and stabilization.

Important comparisons

Ghiyasuddin TughlaqvsMuhammad bin Tughlaq
  • Ghiyasuddin followed a cautious and moderate revenue policy; Muhammad bin Tughlaq introduced radical and often disastrous fiscal experiments like Doab taxation.
  • Ghiyasuddin ruled briefly and stably for 5 years (1320-1325 CE); Muhammad bin Tughlaq ruled for 26 years marked by massive expansion and rebellions.
  • Ghiyasuddin died in an accidental/suspicious pavilion collapse; Muhammad bin Tughlaq died of illness during a campaign in Sindh.

Exam traps and cautions

  • Trap: Attributing the token currency or capital transfer to Ghiyasuddin Tughlaq instead of his son Muhammad bin Tughlaq.
  • Trap: Confusing the founder of the dynasty with Firoz Shah Tughlaq regarding extensive canal networks.
  • Trap: Assuming Ghiyasuddin established a harsh, tyrannical regime similar to late Khalji rule.
  • Trap: Forgetting that Ghiyasuddin was a frontier governor (Dipalpur) before seizing the throne.
  • Trap: Misidentifying the year of his death as 1351 CE (which is Firoz Shah's death year).
