Delhi Sultanate

Allauddin Khilji — Market System

Core study module, theoretical overview, and exam revision checklist for Allauddin Khilji — Market System under the History curriculum.

Appears in: UP TGT Social Science Tracker → 35 quiz questions10-question mini test

Topic overview

  • Allauddin Khilji instituted a comprehensive and strict market control system in the Delhi Sultanate to maintain a large standing army at low salaries.
  • The primary contemporary source for this economic experiment is Ziauddin Barani's Tarikh-i-Firuz Shahi, supplemented by Amir Khusrau's Khaza'in-ul-Futuh and Isami's Futuh-us-Salatin.
  • The market regulations extended across Delhi, fixing prices of essential commodities ranging from food grains to horses, slaves, and luxury items.
  • Special judicial and administrative offices, notably the Shahna-i-Mandi and Diwan-i-Riyasat, were created to enforce strict compliance and punish violators.
Concept 1

Objectives and Causes of Market Control

Strategic military necessity was the fundamental driver behind the price control policy.

Detailed explanation

  • Allauddin Khilji wanted to maintain a massive standing army of 475,000 cavalrymen without increasing the state treasury burden.
  • Soldiers demanded higher pay due to rising inflation, but the Sultan reasoned that lowering commodity prices was more sustainable than raising salaries.
  • Frequent Mongol invasions necessitated a permanent and well-equipped military apparatus stationed constantly around Delhi and the frontiers.
  • Preventing hoarding and black-marketing by grain merchants (karwanis) was essential to ensure steady supplies for the urban populace and military camps.
  • The Sultan aimed to impoverish the Hindu nobility and agrarian intermediaries (khuts, muqaddams, and chaudhuris) to prevent rebellions.

Key points

  • Primary objective: Maintenance of a large, cheap permanent army.
  • Primary source: Tarikh-i-Firuz Shahi by Ziauddin Barani.
  • Military size: 475,000 permanent cavalrymen.
  • Targeted groups: Grain merchants, nobility, and rural intermediaries.
Examples and evidence
  • Fixing the salary of a fully armed soldier at 233.5 tankas per year with an additional 78 tankas for two-horse troopers.
  • Banning the free accumulation of wealth among nobles to curb potential political conspiracies.
Important distinctions
  • Contrast with modern market economies where prices are determined by market forces of demand and supply.
  • Contras with previous Sultans who relied on temporary feudal levies rather than a centrally maintained standing army.
Common misconceptions
  • Misconception: The market regulations were introduced out of welfare motives for the general public peasants.
  • Correction: The policy was strictly a military and political measure designed for state survival and expansion.
Exam focus
  • Identify the exact military size maintained under this system.
  • Recognize Ziauddin Barani as the chief chronicler of these economic reforms.
  • Understand the socio-political motive of suppressing the khuts and muqaddams.
Concept 2

The Four Distinct Markets (Mandis)

Delhi was divided into four specialized market zones to regulate every category of goods.

Detailed explanation

  • The first market was the Sarl-i-Adl, exclusively established for food grains like wheat, barley, rice, pulses, and sugar.
  • The second market dealt with cloth, silk, dry fruits, butter, and oil, housed centrally inside the Badaun Gate.
  • The third market regulated the trade of horses, cattle, and pack animals to ensure quality cavalry remounts.
  • The fourth market was designated for slaves and concubines, strictly checking prices based on utility and skills.
  • Each market operated under meticulous supervision with fixed quotas and daily inspections by imperial officials.

Key points

  • Sar-i-Adl: Dedicated grain market.
  • Cloth market location: Inside the Badaun Gate.
  • Animal market focus: Horses for cavalry units.
  • Slave market: Controlled prices for domestic and skilled laborers.
Examples and evidence
  • Grain prices fixed at rates such as wheat at 7 jitals per maund and barley at 4 jitals per maund.
  • Cloth grades strictly categorized with fixed prices per yard to prevent merchant profiteering.
Important distinctions
  • Distinction between Sar-i-Adl (generalized manufactured and cloth goods) and the central grain mandi.
  • Differentiation in animal grading where high-grade Arab and Turkish horses were priced differently from native ponies.
Common misconceptions
  • Misconception: Only food items were subject to price control in Delhi.
  • Correction: The control covered all essential consumer goods, luxury textile items, livestock, and slaves.
Exam focus
  • Match the four markets with their respective commodities (Sar-i-Adl for cloth/manufactured goods).
  • Recall specific grain prices recorded by Barani.
  • Identify the location of the cloth market near the Badaun Gate.
Concept 3

Administrative Machinery and Officials

A robust bureaucracy and strict surveillance network ensured flawless implementation of market edicts.

Detailed explanation

  • Diwan-i-Riyasat was the overarching ministry established to oversee all commercial transactions and trade regulations.
  • Shahna-i-Mandi was the superintendent or market controller appointed to enforce price schedules in each respective market.
  • Barids were the intelligence officers who secretly reported market conditions, weight discrepancies, and merchant violations directly to the Sultan.
  • Munhiyans served as secret spies who mingled in the markets and verified the reports submitted by the official barids.
  • Harsh corporal punishments, including mutilation and banishment, awaited any merchant or official found violating the fixed rates.

Key points

  • Diwan-i-Riyasat: Supreme ministry of commerce headed by officers like Malik Yaqub.
  • Shahna-i-Mandi: Superintendent of the grain and general markets.
  • Barids and Munhiyans: Imperial intelligence and spy network.
  • Punitive mechanism: Use of physical torture and banishment for underweight goods.
Examples and evidence
  • Malik Yaqub appointed as Naib-i-Riyasat to oversee the enforcement of market regulations.
  • Inspectors severing flesh equal to the underweight amount from a merchant's body as punishment.
Important distinctions
  • Comparison between Barids (official intelligence reporters) and Munhiyans (secret undercover agents).
  • Contrast with ordinary judicial administration (Qazi courts) which handled civil and religious disputes rather than specialized market enforcement.
Common misconceptions
  • Misconception: Merchants willingly adopted the price cuts due to moral persuasion.
  • Correction: Total compliance was achieved exclusively through extreme terror, espionage, and brutal punishments.
Exam focus
  • Identify the functions of Diwan-i-Riyasat and Shahna-i-Mandi.
  • Differentiate between Barids and Munhiyans.
  • Recall the harsh nature of punishments meted out for short-weighing.
Concept 4

Grain Supply, Rationing, and State Granaries

The state maintained buffer stocks and regulated grain distribution to survive droughts and famines.

Detailed explanation

  • Multani merchants were given financial advances by the state to procure and transport grain from surrounding rural areas into Delhi.
  • Caravan merchants (banjaras or karwanis) were registered and placed under state surveillance to ensure continuous grain transport.
  • Government granaries were established across Delhi to store vast quantities of surplus food grains as buffer stock.
  • In times of drought or scarcity, grain was rationed to citizens based on household size to prevent hoarding and starvation.
  • Hoarding grain beyond specified limits was treated as a capital offense, and hidden stocks were forcibly confiscated.

Key points

  • Multani traders: Financed by the state for long-distance grain procurement.
  • Karwanis / Banjaras: Nomadic grain-transporting merchant community.
  • State granaries: Buffer reserves maintained directly by the imperial government.
  • Rationing policy: Implemented strictly during scarcity periods.
Examples and evidence
  • Forcible seizure of hoarded grain from nobles and merchants who violated storage limits.
  • Issuing fixed quantity quotas per family during lean agricultural seasons.
Important distinctions
  • Comparison between Multani merchants (state-subsidized urban traders) and independent rural grain producers.
  • Contrast between open-market grain trading before Khilji and state-monitored supply chains under his regime.
Common misconceptions
  • Misconception: Farmers were allowed to sell their surplus grain freely to anyone offering the highest price.
  • Correction: Agricultural produce was heavily regulated, and forced sales to state-approved merchants at fixed prices were mandatory.
Exam focus
  • Name the merchant community (Multanis and Karwanis) responsible for grain transport.
  • Recognize the role of state granaries in food security.
  • Understand the mechanics of grain rationing during shortages.
Concept 5

Control of Horses, Slaves, and Luxury Goods

The market system extended beyond daily subsistence to control military animals and high-value commodities.

Detailed explanation

  • The horse market was closely monitored to eliminate middlemen (dalals) who manipulated prices and sold substandard animals to the army.
  • Brokers and horse dealers were registered, and corrupt practices were severely punished by Diwan-i-Riyasat.
  • The slave market was strictly regulated because slaves served as domestic servants, artisans, and concubines in elite households.
  • Slaves were graded into distinct categories based on utility, and their prices were fixed accordingly to prevent exorbitant speculation.
  • Luxury goods like silk, velvet, brocade, and dry fruits were permitted for sale only with special permits issued to wealthy nobility.

Key points

  • Horse trade reform: Elimination of dishonest dalals (middlemen/brokers).
  • Slave market regulation: Classification based on utility and labor capability.
  • Luxury goods restriction: Access limited strictly to permitted elites.
  • Impact on cavalry: Ensured top-quality war horses for the imperial army.
Examples and evidence
  • Banning dishonest horse brokers from entering Delhi markets permanently.
  • Fixing slave prices ranging from 10 to 20 tankas for ordinary domestic servants up to higher rates for skilled artisans.
Important distinctions
  • Distinction between ordinary consumer markets (grain and cloth) and specialized military supply markets (horses and weapons).
  • Comparison of slave grading systems with general commodity price fixation.
Common misconceptions
  • Misconception: Slaves were banned entirely under Allauddin Khilji.
  • Correction: Slavery remained legal and active, but the slave market was strictly price-controlled and supervised.
Exam focus
  • Identify the role of horse brokers (dalals) in market manipulation.
  • Understand why horse market control was crucial for military success.
  • Recognize the stratification of slave pricing under the regulations.
Concept 6

Impact and Historiographical Assessment

The market system was a short-lived administrative marvel that collapsed immediately after the Sultan's death.

Detailed explanation

  • The economic controls successfully enabled Allauddin Khilji to maintain an invincible army and repel successive Mongol invasions.
  • The system crippled the economic power of Hindu rural elites (khuts, muqaddams) and urban merchants, ensuring absolute royal despotism.
  • The rigidity of the controls caused immense hardship to the trading community and created widespread resentment among merchants and nobles.
  • Barani praises the system as a miraculous administrative achievement despite its severe, terror-based enforcement.
  • Modern historians view the system as a localized, authoritarian wartime economy rather than a permanent structural economic reform.

Key points

  • Lifespan: Strictly limited to the reign of Allauddin Khilji (ended with his death in 1316 AD).
  • Successor reversal: Qutubuddin Mubarak Shah repealed all market regulations immediately upon accession.
  • Historiographical consensus: Viewed as a draconian wartime measure, not a lasting economic model.
  • Primary chronicler view: Ziauddin Barani lauded its efficacy while noting the oppressive terror.
Examples and evidence
  • Immediate breakdown of price controls and resurgence of inflation under Qutubuddin Mubarak Shah.
  • Barani's detailed descriptions of merchants hiding their goods in fear of royal inspectors.
Important distinctions
  • Contrast with Muhammad bin Tughlaq's later economic experiments (token currency) which failed due to counterfeiting and lack of trust.
  • Comparison between Barani's praise for military strength and modern economic critique of state-controlled pricing.
Common misconceptions
  • Misconception: Allauddin's market reforms survived as a permanent institution of the Delhi Sultanate.
  • Correction: The entire system collapsed immediately after his death as his successors lacked his iron will.
Exam focus
  • Identify which successor abolished the market regulations (Qutubuddin Mubarak Shah).
  • Analyze Ziauddin Barani's perspective on the market control system.
  • Understand the long-term unsustainability of state-mandated price controls.

Concept 1

Objectives and Causes of Market Control

Point-wise rapid recall

  • Fundamental driver was strategic military necessity, not public welfare.
  • Sultan needed to maintain a large standing army within limited financial resources.
  • Low salaries dictated that commodity prices had to be kept artificially low.
  • Prevented rebellions by impoverishing nobles and curtailing economic power.

Mnemonic

  • M-A-S-P: Military Army Size Protected by prices.

Tips

  • Remember that Alauddin's market control was driven by state defense, not consumer socialism.

Exam tricks

  • If a question links market control to benevolence, it is an exam trap.

Common traps

  • Confusing the market system as an early form of welfare economics.
Concept 2

The Four Distinct Markets (Mandis)

Point-wise rapid recall

  • First market: Sarai-i-Adl for manufactured goods, textiles, and imported items.
  • Second market: Grain market (Mandi) supervised by Shah-i-Mandi.
  • Third market: Horses, cattle, and slaves regulated strictly to curb middlemen.
  • Fourth market: General merchandise, horses, and daily utility goods.

Mnemonic

  • S-G-H-G: Sarai, Grain, Horses, General goods.

Tips

  • Focus on Sarai-i-Adl as the textile and luxury cloth market.

Exam tricks

  • Match markets with their specific administrators during matching questions.

Common traps

  • Mixing up Sarai-i-Adl with the general grain market.
Concept 3

Administrative Machinery and Officials

Point-wise rapid recall

  • Diwan-i-Riyasat monitored the overall commercial setup and registered merchants.
  • Shah-i-Mandi was the superintendent in charge of the grain market.
  • Barids (intelligence officers) and Munhifnas (secret spies) reported directly to the Sultan.
  • Punishments for short-weighing were extremely brutal, involving cutting flesh equal to the deficit.

Mnemonic

  • D-S-B-M: Diwan, Shah, Barid, Munhifna.

Tips

  • Keep track of Barids (intelligence) vs Munhifnas (secret agents).

Exam tricks

  • Brutal punishments were used primarily to deter corruption among merchants.

Common traps

  • Thinking that merchants voluntarily cooperated without fear of the spy network.
Concept 4

Grain Supply, Rationing, and State Granaries

Point-wise rapid recall

  • State maintained vast buffer stocks and granaries in Delhi.
  • Peasants were forced to pay land revenue directly in kind (grain).
  • Caravanis (traders/banjaras) were registered to continuously bring grain from villages.
  • Rationing system was deployed during droughts and food shortages.

Mnemonic

  • P-C-S-R: Peasants, Caravanis, Stores, Rationing.

Tips

  • Note that revenue in kind was a major pillar ensuring granary stocks.

Exam tricks

  • Identify Caravanis as the mobile transport backbone of the grain supply.

Common traps

  • Assuming grain distribution was open to all free-market fluctuations.
Concept 5

Control of Horses, Slaves, and Luxury Goods

Point-wise rapid recall

  • Middlemen (dalals) were strictly banned from horse and slave markets.
  • Horse trade was monopolized to prevent black marketing of military mounts.
  • Slave prices were regulated based on utility, ranging from domestic help to skilled laborers.
  • High-value luxury commodities required special permits to purchase.

Mnemonic

  • H-S-D-L: Horses, Slaves, Dalals banned, Luxury permits.

Tips

  • Focus on the elimination of middlemen as the key to price fixation.

Exam tricks

  • Remember that horses were vital for countering the Mongol threat.

Common traps

  • Assuming slaves were traded freely according to open market dynamics.
Concept 6

Impact and Historiographical Assessment

Point-wise rapid recall

  • Described by contemporary chronicler Barani as a unique historical miracle.
  • Extremely successful during Alauddin's lifetime but restricted strictly to Delhi and surroundings.
  • Collapsed immediately after the Sultan's death under Qutbuddin Mubarak Shah.
  • Proved to be an artificial administrative imposition rather than a lasting structural evolution.

Mnemonic

  • B-S-C-A: Barani, Success, Collapse, Artificial.

Tips

  • Always cite Ziauddin Barani when discussing contemporary accounts of the market.

Exam tricks

  • Remember that the reforms did not extend across the entire empire, only around the capital.

Common traps

  • Believing the market system survived into the Tughlaq dynasty.

Quick facts

  • Alauddin Khilji's market control policy was detailed extensively by Ziauddin Barani in Tarikh-i-Firuz Shahi.
  • Sarai-i-Adl was the exclusive market established for cloth and manufactured goods.
  • Diwan-i-Riyasat was the central office supervising the entire market regulatory apparatus.
  • Shah-i-Mandi was the specific officer appointed to oversee the grain market.
  • Barids and Munhifnas acted as the secret service network reporting directly to the Sultan.
  • Caravanis were the nomadic grain-carrying traders registered by the state.
  • Punishments for short-weighing involved cutting flesh equal to the missing weight.
  • The entire market system collapsed immediately after Alauddin's death in 1316 AD.

Topic mnemonics

  • Four Markets: SG-HG (Sarai, Grain, Horses, General)
  • Officials: DSM (Diwan, Shah, Munhifna)
  • Causes: MSLA (Military Size Low Allowance)

Revision tips

  • Always link market reforms directly to the financial sustainability of a large standing army.
  • Memorize the distinct names of officials like Barid and Diwan-i-Riyasat.
  • Remember that the market rules were geographically confined primarily to Delhi.
  • Distinguish between revenue paid in cash versus grain assessment.

Exam tricks

  • Eliminate options that suggest public welfare was the primary goal of Alauddin.
  • Look for 'Barani' when questions ask for primary sources on the market system.
  • Treat 'Dalals' (middlemen) as banned entities in the horse and slave markets.
  • Remember that agricultural land revenue was raised to 50% of the produce.

Important comparisons

Sarai-i-AdlvsGrain Mandi
  • Sarai-i-Adl handled manufactured goods, textiles, and imports; Grain Mandi handled cereals and food grains.
  • Sarai-i-Adl required merchants to register in a special permit office; Grain Mandi operated under Shah-i-Mandi.
  • Sarai-i-Adl dealt with high-value luxury and dry goods; Grain Mandi dealt with essential subsistence rations.

Exam traps and cautions

  • Assuming the market control system was implemented across the entire Delhi Sultanate empire.
  • Confusing Barids (intelligence reporters) with Munhifnas (secret inspectors).
  • Believing that merchants supported and voluntarily sustained the price controls.
  • Thinking the market system survived past the Khilji dynasty into subsequent reigns.
  • Viewing the price fixation policy as a progressive measure for general consumer welfare.
