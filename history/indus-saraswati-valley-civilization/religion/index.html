Indus-Saraswati Valley Civilization

Religion

Appears in: UP PGT History Tracker →

Topic overview

  • The religion of the Indus-Saraswati Valley Civilization is primarily reconstructed through archaeological artifacts, seals, figurines, and architectural remains due to the undeciphered script.
  • Excavations across major urban centers like Harappa, Mohenjodaro, and Kalibangan reveal a pervasive element of ritualistic and symbolic practices.
  • Prominent features include the worship of a Mother Goddess, a proto-Shiva figure, sacred animals, trees, and an emphasis on ritual purity.
  • There is a notable absence of monumental temples or dedicated places of worship, suggesting domestic, communal, or open-air ritual practices.
  • Many religious motifs and traditions of the Indus-Saraswati civilization laid the foundation for later Hindu religious beliefs and practices.
Concept 1

Mother Goddess and Female Deities

Terracotta figurines indicate a widespread fertility and Mother Goddess cult across Harappan sites.

Details

  • Numerous terracotta female figurines have been recovered from sites like Mohenjodaro, Harappa, and Kalibangan.
  • These figurines typically feature elaborate headdresses, heavy jewelry, and scant clothing, highlighting fertility iconography.
  • The presence of soot marks on some figurines suggests they were used with oil lamps during household rituals.
  • Similar figurines found in Baluchistan and Kulli culture sites indicate regional interaction and shared belief systems.
  • John Marshall identified these figures as early representations of the Mother Goddess or Shakti.

Remember

  • Primary material used is terracotta, molded by hand with pinched details.
  • Associated with fertility, agriculture, and protection of the household.
  • Found in domestic contexts rather than monumental public shrines.
  • Widely distributed across mature Harappan phase settlements.
Examples & sources
  • Terracotta female figurines from Mohenjodaro depicting fan-shaped headdresses.
  • Figurines recovered from Harappan stratum layers showing pierced nostrils for ornaments.
Compare
  • Unlike Vedic religion where male deities like Indra and Agni dominate, Harappan religion emphasizes female fertility symbols.
  • Unlike later Hindu temples with established deity shrines, Harappan goddess worship occurred in domestic environments.
Common confusions
  • Misconception that elaborate brick temples were dedicated to the Mother Goddess.
  • Assumption that all female figurines served religious purposes, ignoring potential secular toys.
Exam points
  • Identify the primary material of Mother Goddess figurines (terracotta).
  • Recall John Marshall's attribution of the figures.
  • Note the absence of monumental temples for these deities.
Concept 2

Pashupati Seal and Proto-Shiva Worship

The famous Pashupati seal depicts a seated three-faced deity surrounded by animals, interpreted as an early form of Shiva.

Details

  • Seal number 420 from Mohenjodaro depicts a seated figure in a yogic posture, identified by John Marshall as Pashupati.
  • The central figure has three faces, wears horned headdresses, and is surrounded by animals including a rhino, buffalo, elephant, and tiger.
  • Two deer are shown standing beneath the multi-headed deity's seat.
  • The yogic posture resembles Mulabandhasana or lotus position, indicating early traditions of yoga and meditation.
  • Numismatic and sigillographic evidence confirms the seal's high status in Harappan iconography.

Remember

  • Discovered at the Mohenjodaro excavation site.
  • Depicts a proto-type of the later Puranic deity Shiva in his aspect as Lord of Animals.
  • Shows early synthesis of asceticism and animal symbolism.
  • Engraved on a steatite seal.
Examples & sources
  • Steatite seal from Mohenjodaro featuring the horned yogic figure.
  • Similar horned deity motifs found on pottery shards at various sites.
Compare
  • Differs from Vedic Indra who commands armies in battle; Pashupati represents mastery over nature and inner asceticism.
  • Unlike later anthropomorphic Shiva idols, the Harappan representation is sigillographic and symbolic.
Common confusions
  • Assuming the seal proves the existence of formal Shaivism in the Bronze Age.
  • Confusing the horned headdress with actual anatomical horns on the deity.
Exam points
  • Identify the specific animals surrounding the Pashupati figure (elephant, tiger, rhino, buffalo, two deer).
  • Note John Marshall's identification of the figure.
  • Recognize the steatite material of the seal.
Concept 3

Animal and Pipal Tree Worship

Harappans held deep reverence for specific animals and trees, establishing enduring zoolatry and phytolatry traditions.

Details

  • The humped bull appears frequently on seals, indicating its high ritual and economic value in society.
  • The mythical unicorn-like creature depicted on numerous seals represents the most common animal motif in Harappan art.
  • Trees, particularly the Pipal (Ficus religiosa), were venerated as sacred entities, often shown enclosed by railings.
  • Serpent worship (Naga cult) is indicated by snake motifs on pottery and seals.
  • Composite animals, such as human-headed bulls and tiger-bodied beasts, reflect complex mythological frameworks.

Remember

  • Humped bull (Zebu) was the most revered domestic animal.
  • Unicorn motif appears on over 60 percent of seals.
  • Pipal tree leaves and branches frequently decorated painted pottery.
  • Sacred enclosures around trees mirror later Indian sacred grove traditions.
Examples & sources
  • Unicorn seals from Harappa and Mohenjodaro.
  • Pipal leaf motifs painted on polychrome pottery from Chanhu-daro.
Compare
  • Unlike Egyptian animal worship involving mummification of entire species, Harappan animal reverence was artistic and sigillographic.
  • Unlike Vedic sacrifice centered on horses and cows, Harappan animal motifs emphasize symbolic protection and nobility.
Common confusions
  • Believing the unicorn represents a real zoological discovery rather than a composite mythic beast.
  • Assuming animal seals were mere commercial trademarks without religious significance.
Exam points
  • Identify the unicorn as the most frequent seal motif.
  • Recognize the sacred status of the Pipal tree.
  • Note the ritual importance of the humped Zebu bull.
Concept 4

Great Bath and Fire Altars

Architectural features like the Great Bath and fire altars point to organized ritual purification and public fire cults.

Details

  • The Great Bath at Mohenjodaro features a large watertight brick pool with steps, changing rooms, and an elaborate drainage system.
  • Scholars like D.D. Kosambi and John Marshall associate the Great Bath with ritual purification and communal bathing festivals.
  • Fire altars (vedikas) have been excavated at several sites, notably Kalibangan, Lothal, and Banawali.
  • These fire altars are often arranged in a series within residential complexes, suggesting domestic fire worship.
  • Animal bones and ash found inside some fire altars suggest sacrificial offerings.

Remember

  • Great Bath uses bitumen lining for waterproofing.
  • Fire altars found at Kalibangan are raised brick platforms containing ash and terracotta cakes.
  • Lothal fire altars are linked by some researchers to early Vedic-style yajnas.
  • Indicates a strong focus on ritual cleanliness and public hygiene tied to religion.
Examples & sources
  • The Great Bath citadel complex at Mohenjodaro.
  • Row of seven fire altars discovered on the citadel mound at Kalibangan.
Compare
  • Unlike later massive temple tank complexes, the Harappan Great Bath is integrated into an urban civic layout.
  • Unlike complex Vedic yajna altars with strict geometric rules, Harappan fire altars are simpler domestic or platform structures.
Common confusions
  • Viewing the Great Bath as a mere swimming pool for recreation.
  • Conflating Harappan fire altars directly with later Vedic sacrificial systems without chronological caution.
Exam points
  • Identify the site of the Great Bath (Mohenjodaro).
  • List sites where fire altars were discovered (Kalibangan, Lothal).
  • Recognize the role of bitumen in waterproofing the Great Bath.
Concept 5

Burial Practices and Eschatology

Harappan funerary customs reveal complex beliefs about death, afterlife, and social stratification.

Details

  • Primary burial in extended north-south orientation was the most common funerary practice across sites like Harappa and Rakhigarhi.
  • Graves typically contained grave goods such as pottery, beads, copper mirrors, and shell ornaments for use in the afterlife.
  • Urn burials (fractional burials) involving secondary interment of bones after exposure to scavengers were also practiced.
  • Cemeteries like R-37 at Harappa provide crucial data on demographic profiles and burial goods.
  • Joint burials discovered at Lothal, where a male and female were interred together, suggest practices akin to later sati or joint mortality.

Remember

  • North-south skeletal orientation reflects astronomical or religious alignment.
  • Cemetery H at Harappa represents a later post-urban cultural phase with distinctive painted pottery urns.
  • Kalibangan shows pit burials alongside urn burials and brick-lined graves.
  • Absence of royal pyramids or grand megaliths suggests an egalitarian or mercantile death culture.
Examples & sources
  • Cemetery R-37 at Harappa containing numerous extended inhumations.
  • Double burial at Lothal featuring two skeletons in a single brick-lined pit.
Compare
  • Unlike Egyptian burials with mummies and massive tombs, Harappan graves are simple earthen pits with modest goods.
  • Unlike Vedic cremation traditions dominant in later periods, Harappan practice favored inhumation.
Common confusions
  • Assuming all grave goods indicate extreme wealth inequality rather than standard ritual provisions for the afterlife.
  • Confusing Cemetery H pottery with mature Harappan ceramics.
Exam points
  • Identify Cemetery R-37 and Cemetery H at Harappa.
  • Recall the dominant north-south skeletal orientation.
  • Note the evidence of double burials at Lothal.
Concept 6

Lingam, Yoni, and Amulets

Stone ringstones and phallic symbols indicate early fertility cults and apotropaic magic against evil forces.

Details

  • Conical and cylindrical stone objects recovered from Harappan sites have been identified by archaeologists as early lingams (phallic symbols).
  • Ringstones (perforated circular stones ranging from small amulets to large architectural rings) symbolize the yoni or female reproductive principle.
  • Amulets and talismans were commonly worn to ward off evil spirits, disease, and malevolent deities.
  • The swastika and trishula-like symbols on seals demonstrate early use of sacred geometric signs.
  • Terracotta masks and horned deity representations suggest apotropaic rituals performed by priests or shamans.

Remember

  • Stone ringstones found at Mohenjodaro and Harappa.
  • Phallic worship criticized in early Vedic texts (Rigveda mentions Shishnadeva) likely refers to non-Vedic indigenous practices.
  • Apotropaic magic played a major role in everyday protective rituals.
  • Geometric symbols on seals show continuity into historic Indian religions.
Examples & sources
  • Miniature ringstones from Mohenjodaro.
  • Stone phallic-like cylinders discovered in urban housing strata.
Compare
  • Unlike institutionalized Vedic sacrifices, ringstone and amulet use reflects folk and household religion.
  • Unlike later abstract philosophical treatises, Harappan phallic symbols were direct fertility and protective markers.
Common confusions
  • Assuming all ringstones served as architectural bases for pillars, ignoring miniature devotional examples.
  • Rejecting the link between Harappan 'Shishnadeva' objects and later Shaivite lingam worship.
Exam points
  • Connect Harappan stone cylinders to the Rigvedic term 'Shishnadeva'.
  • Identify the symbolic meaning of ringstones (yoni/fertility).
  • Recognize the use of amulets for apotropaic protection.

Compare

Indus-Saraswati ReligionvsVedic Religion
  • Indus religion lacked monumental temples; Vedic religion centered on open-air sacrificial altars.
  • Indus religion emphasized female fertility and Mother Goddess; Vedic religion was patriarchal with dominant male gods like Indra and Agni.
  • Indus burial practice favored extended inhumation; Vedic practice favored cremation.

  • Believing that fire altars at Kalibangan prove the authors were exclusively Vedic Aryans.
  • Neglecting to remember the two deer seated beneath the Pashupati throne.
