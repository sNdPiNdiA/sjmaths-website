Indus-Saraswati Valley Civilization

Trade

Appears in: UP PGT History Tracker →

Topic overview

  • Trade formed the backbone of the urban economy in the Indus-Saraswati Valley Civilization.
  • Internal and external commerce operated across vast distances, connecting regional resource zones to major urban centers.
  • Exchange networks involved both terrestrial caravan routes and maritime coastal navigation.
  • The civilization lacked a coinage system, relying instead on barter and standardized weights and measures.
  • Archaeological evidence of trade includes seals, weights, imported raw materials, and dockyard structures.
Concept 1

Internal Trade Networks and Regional Exchange

Extensive domestic commerce linked metropolitan centers with resource-rich hinterlands.

Details

  • Major cities like Harappa, Mohenjo-daro, and Dholavira served as primary hubs for redistribution and manufacturing.
  • Raw materials moved from peripheral mining and pastoral zones to core urban workshops.
  • Terrestrial transport utilized bullock carts with solid wheels and pack animals like oxen, donkeys, and camels.
  • Riverine networks along the Indus and Saraswati river systems facilitated bulk transport of heavy goods.
  • Specialized craft production centers emerged near source regions, such as bead-making at Chanhudaro and shell-working at Lothal.

Remember

  • Copper was sourced primarily from Khetri in Rajasthan.
  • Lapis lazuli came from Shortugai in Badakhshan, Afghanistan.
  • Steatite for seals was procured from sources in Rajasthan and Gujarat.
  • Chalcedony and carnelians originated from Gujarat and Saurashtra.
Examples & sources
  • Transport of Khetri copper ore to Kalibangan and Harappa for metallurgical processing.
  • Distribution of marine shells from Balakot and Lothal to interior northern settlements.
Compare
  • Internal trade relied on riverine and short-haul bullock transport, whereas external trade utilized maritime and long-distance caravan routes.
  • Internal networks focused on raw material redistribution, while external trade centered on luxury and prestige items.
Common confusions
  • Students often assume money was used in transactions, ignoring the complete absence of coins.
  • Many believe trade was purely state-controlled, underestimating the role of private merchant guilds.
Exam points
  • Identify specific source locations for raw materials like copper, lapis lazuli, and tin.
  • Recognize the role of specialized regional production centers.
  • Understand the mechanics of internal bulk transport.
Concept 2

External and Foreign Trade with Western Asia

Harappan merchants maintained active maritime and overland commercial links with Mesopotamia and the Persian Gulf.

Details

  • Mesopotamian cuneiform texts mention trade with regions named Dilmun, Makan, and Meluhha.
  • Meluhha is widely identified by historians and epigraphists as the Indus-Saraswati region.
  • Harappan seals, weights, and etched carnelians have been discovered at Mesopotamian sites like Ur, Kish, and Lagash.
  • Mesopotamian cylindrical seals and goods have been recovered from Harappan sites, indicating reciprocal exchange.
  • Persian Gulf seals found at Lothal and Ur demonstrate the intermediary role of Dilmun (modern Bahrain).

Remember

  • Meluhha traded timber, ivory, lapis lazuli, gold, and carnelian beads to Mesopotamia.
  • Dilmun acted as the primary trading entrepot or middleman in the Persian Gulf.
  • Makan is generally identified with the copper-rich region of modern Oman.
  • Sargon of Akkad's inscriptions boast of Meluhhan ships docking at his capital.
Examples & sources
  • Discovery of Indus-type square stamp seals at Ur and Umma in Mesopotamia.
  • Finds of circular Persian Gulf seals at the port city of Lothal.
Compare
  • External trade involved high-value prestige goods, whereas internal trade handled bulk raw materials and daily necessities.
  • Mesopotamian trade used maritime sea routes via the Persian Gulf, while northern overland routes connected with Central Asia.
Common confusions
  • Examiners frequently test whether Meluhha refers to Egypt; students must remember it denotes the Indus civilization.
  • Misunderstanding Dilmun as a Harappan colony rather than an independent trading station.
Exam points
  • Memorize Mesopotamian textual references to Meluhha, Dilmun, and Makan.
  • Identify archaeological markers of foreign trade, such as Gulf seals.
  • Understand the nature of exports from the Indus region to Western Asia.
Concept 3

Weights and Measures System

A highly standardized, binary and decimal system of weights regulated commercial transactions.

Details

  • Weights were typically made of chert, jasper, agate, and limestone, shaped into polished cubes without markings.
  • The system was largely binary in the lower denominations and decimal in the higher denominations.
  • The standard base unit weighed approximately 13.63 grams, following a ratio of 1:2:4:8:16:32 up to 64, and then in decimal multiples.
  • Standardized bronze scale pans and graduated rulers made of shell and bronze indicate precise linear measurement.
  • Uniformity of weights across distant sites demonstrates central administrative control or widespread commercial consensus.

Remember

  • Chert was the most common material used for manufacturing cubical weights.
  • Mohenjo-daro yielded shell scales with precision markings.
  • Lothal produced a unique ivory-made graduated scale.
  • The precision of weights reflects an advanced standardized market economy.
Examples & sources
  • Discovery of a graded series of chert weights at Harappa and Mohenjo-daro.
  • Ivory scale recovered from archaeological excavations at Lothal.
Compare
  • Harappan weights followed a consistent binary-decimal progression, unlike contemporary Egyptian or Mesopotamian systems which varied regionally.
  • Harappan weights were un-inscribed and purely functional, unlike later historical Indian punch-marked coin systems.
Common confusions
  • Assuming weights had religious or ritual significance due to their careful finish.
  • Confusing Harappan linear measurements with later Vedic units of length.
Exam points
  • Identify chert as the primary material for weights.
  • Recall the binary-decimal ratio structure of the weight system.
  • Note the discovery sites of graduated scales like Lothal and Mohenjo-daro.
Concept 4

Maritime Trade and Coastal Port Facilities

Advanced maritime infrastructure enabled active sea-borne commerce along the Arabian Sea and Persian Gulf.

Details

  • Lothal in Gujarat featured a massive brick-built basin identified by archaeologists as a tidal dockyard.
  • The Lothal dockyard connected to the Gulf of Khambhat via a tidal channel, allowing ships to enter at high tide.
  • Sutkagendor and Sotka-koh served as strategic coastal trading outposts along the Makran coast.
  • Balakot and Allahdino functioned as coastal settlements engaged in shell-working and maritime exchange.
  • Harappan sailors utilized monsoon wind patterns to navigate across the Arabian Sea to the Persian Gulf.

Remember

  • Lothal is universally cited in competitive exams for its dockyard structure.
  • The Makran coast outposts monitored land and sea routes connecting to Iran.
  • Dockyard engineering at Lothal utilized sophisticated burnt bricks and channels for water regulation.
  • Terracotta models of boats and depictions of ships on seals confirm maritime technology.
Examples & sources
  • The brick basin structure at Lothal measuring over 214 meters in length.
  • Terracotta ship amulet recovered from Mohenjo-daro.
Compare
  • Coastal port sites like Lothal specialized in maritime crafts and foreign exchange, whereas inland agrarian sites focused on crop production.
  • Maritime transport handled bulk luxury items and precious stones, complementing overland caravan trade.
Common confusions
  • Some early scholars disputed the dockyard function of Lothal, though current consensus reaffirms its tidal basin nature.
  • Believing Harappans were exclusively land-locked farmers.
Exam points
  • Identify Lothal as the premier maritime port and dockyard site.
  • Memorize coastal outposts like Sutkagendor on the Makran coast.
  • Recognize material evidence of seafaring, such as boat models and seal motifs.
Concept 5

Trade Mechanisms, Seals, and Barter Economy

Commerce operated without metallic coinage through barter, credit systems, and sealing practices.

Details

  • Transactions relied primarily on barter, supplemented by standardized commodities and bullion.
  • Steatite seals served primarily as administrative tokens or markers of ownership rather than currency.
  • Merchants applied seal impressions on wet clay tags attached to bales of goods to prevent tampering during transit.
  • The iconography and Indus script on seals likely recorded merchant names, ownership, or the nature of goods.
  • The standardization of weights and measures provided a reliable equivalent value framework for exchange.

Remember

  • Absence of metallic coins is a defining economic feature of the civilization.
  • Steatite was the most favored material for carving intaglio trade seals.
  • Unicorn and humped bull motifs were most common on commercial seals.
  • Clay sealings with rope impressions prove the physical use of seals in transit security.
Examples & sources
  • Finding of clay bullae (sealings) bearing cord marks at Harappa.
  • Unicorn seals discovered in large numbers at urban trade centers.
Compare
  • Harappan seals functioned as security and administrative tokens, whereas later Mauryan punch-marked coins functioned as standardized currency.
  • Barter exchange dominated the Indus economy, unlike later monetized market economies of the historical period.
Common confusions
  • Mistaking Indus seals for coins or currency tokens used in direct purchases.
  • Assuming lack of coins implies a primitive economy, overlooking the sophisticated credit and sealing systems.
Exam points
  • Remember that the civilization had no coinage.
  • Understand the administrative and security function of sealings on cargo.
  • Identify steatite as the primary seal material.
Concept 6

Export and Import Commodity Profile

A diverse array of raw materials and finished manufactured goods moved across regional and international borders.

Details

  • Exports from the Indus region included finished crafts like carnelian beads, shell ornaments, ivory items, and cotton textiles.
  • Agricultural surplus, timber, and high-quality pottery also formed significant export categories to Western Asia.
  • Imports comprised essential metals such as copper, tin, gold, silver, and precious stones.
  • Lapis lazuli and turquoise were imported from Central Asia and Persia to satisfy elite demands for luxury ornaments.
  • Craft specialization ensured high value addition to imported raw materials before re-export.

Remember

  • Cotton textiles were a premier export commodity, noted in Mesopotamian records as 'Sindhu'.
  • Carnelian bead industry at Chanhudaro and Lothal produced items highly prized in foreign markets.
  • Tin was imported from Central Asia or Afghanistan to alloy with copper for bronze production.
  • Silver was sourced from Afghanistan and Iran.
Examples & sources
  • Etched carnelian beads found in Mesopotamian royal graves.
  • Imports of raw copper blocks from Oman discovered at various urban sites.
Compare
  • Exports emphasized value-added manufactured goods and crafts, whereas imports focused on raw metallurgical inputs and rare stones.
  • Luxury imports served elite consumption, while imported metals sustained urban toolmaking and weaponry.
Common confusions
  • Believing the civilization was self-sufficient in all minerals, ignoring extensive long-distance metal procurement.
  • Assuming cotton was only grown for domestic use, overlooking international textile trade.
Exam points
  • List key export items like carnelian beads and cotton fabrics.
  • List key import items like tin, copper, and lapis lazuli.
  • Connect specific craft production sites to export items.

Compare

Internal TradevsExternal Trade
  • Internal trade handled bulk raw materials and daily necessities, whereas external trade focused on luxury items.
  • Internal transport relied on riverboats, bullock carts, and pack animals, while external trade utilized maritime sea routes.
  • Internal exchange linked peripheral mines to urban workshops, while foreign trade connected Harappa to Mesopotamia and the Persian Gulf.

Watch for

  • Assuming the Indus civilization had a monetary coinage system.
  • Confusing Meluhha with Egypt or other ancient river valley civilizations.
  • Believing the Harappans were self-sufficient in metals and minerals.
  • Mistaking seals for currency tokens rather than administrative security markers.
  • Attributing the dockyard discovery to Harappa or Mohenjo-daro instead of Lothal.
