Indus-Saraswati Valley Civilization

Society

Appears in: UP PGT History Tracker →

Topic overview

  • The Indus-Saraswati Valley Civilization society was urban, highly organized, and egalitarian in material culture compared to contemporary civilizations of Mesopotamia and Egypt.
  • Archaeological excavations across major sites like Harappa, Mohenjo-daro, Dholavira, and Rakhigarhi reveal a sophisticated social stratification without overt signs of royal palaces or grand standing armies.
  • Material remains indicate a diversified occupational structure encompassing merchants, artisans, agriculturists, administrators, and labourers.
  • The social framework prioritized civic hygiene, urban planning, standardization of weights, and extensive trade networks over monumental religious structures or royal glorification.
Concept 1

Social Stratification and Class Structure

Analysis of urban layout and material culture reveals a class-divided yet cohesive society.

Details

  • Settlement patterns in major cities like Harappa and Mohenjo-daro often feature a raised citadel and a lower town.
  • The citadel typically housed administrative or ritual structures, suggesting a ruling elite, priest-kings, or merchant oligarchs.
  • The lower town contained multi-roomed houses of varying sizes, indicating a diverse middle class of traders and craftsmen.
  • Small single-room quarters near the granaries at Harappa point towards a distinct working-class or labourer stratum.
  • Burial practices, such as variations in grave goods and coffin types at Ropar, Kalibangan, and Lothal, reflect social differentiation.

Remember

  • Absence of explicit royal palaces or large monumental temples distinguishing the power elite.
  • Uniformity of bricks, weights, and pottery suggesting strong administrative control or guild regulations.
  • Citadel vs Lower Town spatial segregation as the primary indicator of socio-spatial hierarchy.
  • Existence of specialized workers' quarters near industrial areas.
Examples & sources
  • The Granary workmen quarters at Harappa.
  • The varied brick sizes and multi-roomed residences in the Lower Town of Mohenjo-daro.
Compare
  • Unlike Mesopotamia and Egypt, Harappan society did not glorify monarchs or construct massive royal tombs.
  • Unlike the later Vedic society, the Indus society lacked a rigid, codified Varna system rooted in ritual purity.
Common confusions
  • Believing the society was completely classless and entirely egalitarian.
  • Assuming absolute theocratic rule solely based on the Priest-King limestone sculpture from Mohenjo-daro.
Exam points
  • Identification of citadel and lower town features.
  • Interpretation of workmen's quarters at Harappa.
  • Nature of elite control without royal monuments.
Concept 2

Position of Women and Matricentric Elements

Terracotta figurines and burial findings suggest a significant societal role for women and mother Goddess worship.

Details

  • Discovery of numerous terracotta figurines of female deities points to the prevalence of Mother Goddess worship.
  • The abundance of female figurines relative to male ones suggests a society with strong matricentric or mother-centric elements.
  • Burial evidence from sites like Kalibangan and Lothal shows joint or individual graves where women were buried alongside men or with distinct ornaments.
  • Ornaments such as bangles, girdles, and necklaces found extensively on female skeletons indicate personal autonomy and status.
  • The absence of weapons of warfare associated with women or men implies a relatively peaceful domestic sphere.

Remember

  • Dominance of terracotta female figurines across diverse sites.
  • Significance of Mother Goddess in domestic and communal rituals.
  • Personal ornamentation indicating social visibility of women.
  • Lack of direct literary records leaves social status open to archaeological interpretation.
Examples & sources
  • The Terracotta Mother Goddess figurines found at Mohenjo-daro and Harappa.
  • Ornaments recovered from female burials at Lothal and Ropar.
Compare
  • Unlike the later Vedic period where women's status gradually declined in public assemblies, Indus material culture highlights high visibility in domestic rituals.
  • Unlike Mesopotamian society where legal codes explicitly subjugated women, Indus legal structures remain uninterpreted but visually egalitarian.
Common confusions
  • Assuming the civilization was strictly a matriarchy based on female figurines.
  • Confusing religious prominence of the Mother Goddess with political matriarchy.
Exam points
  • Significance of terracotta female figurines in UP TGT/PGT exams.
  • Association of Mother Goddess worship with fertility and agriculture.
  • Evidence from burials regarding gender parity.
Concept 3

Occupational Groups and Craft Specialization

A highly diversified economy supported specialized artisans, traders, farmers, and administrators.

Details

  • Craft specialization was a hallmark of Indus society, evident in bead-making, metallurgy, pottery, and shell-working.
  • Chanhudaro was a dedicated industrial site renowned for bead-making factories and shell ornamentation.
  • Lothal featured specialized brick dockyards and bead-making facilities, catering to maritime and inland trade.
  • Metallurgists worked extensively with copper, bronze, lead, and tin, producing tools, weapons, and ornaments.
  • Potters utilized advanced wheel-made techniques to produce standardized red and black painted pottery.

Remember

  • Chanhudaro as the primary center for bead-making and craft workshops.
  • Standardization of weights and measures indicating centralized trade supervision.
  • Guild-like or family-based hereditary transmission of specialized skills.
  • Integration of rural agricultural producers with urban craft specialists.
Examples & sources
  • Bead-making workshops at Chanhudaro and Lothal.
  • Bronze casting using the lost-wax technique, exemplified by the Dancing Girl of Mohenjo-daro.
Compare
  • Unlike pastoral societies, the Indus economy was anchored in advanced urban craft production and metallurgy.
  • Unlike independent craft units in later periods, Indus craft production showed high standardization in dimensions and weights.
Common confusions
  • Assuming iron was widely used by Indus craftsmen.
  • Believing all pottery was hand-made rather than mass-produced on fast wheels.
Exam points
  • Specific industrial sites like Chanhudaro and Lothal.
  • Lost-wax method of metal casting (Cire Perdue).
  • Standardization of weights and measures.
Concept 4

Diet, Dress, and Ornamentation

Diverse food habits, varied textiles, and elaborate personal adornments defined everyday material life.

Details

  • Dietary patterns included cultivated cereals like wheat and barley, supplemented by pulses, millets, sesame, and dates.
  • Animal bones found at sites indicate the consumption of beef, mutton, pork, poultry, fish, and freshwater molluscs.
  • Textile remains, including carbonized cotton and impressions on pottery, confirm the use of both cotton and wool for clothing.
  • Both men and women wore ornaments, including necklaces, bangles, finger rings, and anklets made of gold, silver, copper, terracotta, and semi-precious stones.
  • Cosmetics were popular, as evidenced by bronze mirrors, collyrium sticks, and vanity cases found in excavations.

Remember

  • Rabi and Kharif cropping patterns supporting a rich urban diet.
  • Early cultivation and use of cotton in the ancient world.
  • Widespread use of semi-precious stones like carnelian, lapis lazuli, agate, and jasper for jewelry.
  • Grooming accessories reflecting aesthetic refinement.
Examples & sources
  • Carbonized grains recovered from Banawali and Mehrgarh.
  • Carnelian beads and gold necklaces found in hoards at Mohenjo-daro and Lothal.
Compare
  • Unlike contemporary Egypt where linen dominated, Indus society relied heavily on cotton textiles.
  • Unlike nomadic diets focused strictly on dairy and meat, Indus diet was remarkably plant- and cereal-centric with balanced protein sources.
Common confusions
  • Assuming the population was exclusively vegetarian based on animal protection theories.
  • Believing clothing was primitive due to tropical climate assumptions.
Exam points
  • Evidence of cotton cultivation.
  • Types of semi-precious stones used in jewelry.
  • Dietary staples identified via archaeobotanical studies.
Concept 5

Burial Customs and Belief Systems

Mortuary practices reveal diverse post-funeral rites, social beliefs, and attitudes toward the afterlife.

Details

  • Extended inhumation was the dominant burial practice across major Harappan cemeteries.
  • Bodies were typically laid out in a north-south orientation, accompanied by pottery vessels containing food and water.
  • Ropar and Kalibangan exhibit varied burial types, including urn burials, fractional burials, and brick-lined graves.
  • Surkotada provides evidence of pot burials containing cremated remains, pointing to multiple funeral traditions.
  • Lothal features paired burials containing male and female skeletons, though sati cannot be definitively proven.

Remember

  • North-south skeletal orientation as a common mortuary norm.
  • Inclusion of grave goods indicating belief in an afterlife.
  • Coexistence of extended inhumation, fractional burial, and post-cremation urn burial.
  • Absence of grand pyramids or royal tombs, indicating an egalitarian death culture.
Examples & sources
  • Cemetery R-37 at Harappa containing extended inhumations.
  • Double burials at Lothal.
Compare
  • Unlike Egyptian civilization, Indus burials lacked massive pyramids or elaborate mummification processes.
  • Unlike Vedic cremation traditions, Indus society predominantly practiced skeletal inhumation alongside secondary burial forms.
Common confusions
  • Interpreting Lothal double burials as definitive proof of historical Sati.
  • Assuming uniform burial customs across all distant provincial sites.
Exam points
  • Cemetery R-37 identification and significance.
  • Variety of burial methods (inhumation, fractional, urn).
  • Geographical variations in mortuary practices.
Concept 6

Amusements, Toys, and Social Life

Rich recreational culture, games, and child-centric toys highlight a peaceful and vibrant urban community.

Details

  • Excavations have uncovered numerous terracotta toys, indicating a strong focus on child welfare and play.
  • Popular toys included wheeled carts, animal figurines with movable heads, whistles shaped like birds, and clay rattles.
  • Board games and dice found at sites like Lothal and Harappa point to indoor gaming culture among adults.
  • Hunting, fishing, and dancing served as prominent outdoor recreational activities.
  • The discovery of the bronze Dancing Girl figurine highlights the cultural importance of music and dance in social gatherings.

Remember

  • Terracotta wheeled carts as evidence of early transport toys.
  • Presence of dice indicating games of chance.
  • Musical instruments like pottery drums and stringed instruments suggested by iconography.
  • Peaceful nature of toys indicating a non-militaristic social environment.
Examples & sources
  • Terracotta bullock carts with moveable pins from Chanhudaro and Harappa.
  • Terracotta dice from Lothal.
Compare
  • Unlike societies with martial toys, Indus toys emphasize domestic animals, carts, and peaceful interaction.
  • Unlike contemporary cultures with elite-only sports, gaming items were widespread across ordinary households.
Common confusions
  • Viewing terracotta figurines purely as religious idols without recognizing their role as children's toys.
  • Assuming urban life was devoid of leisure and purely administrative.
Exam points
  • Toys and games as indicators of social life.
  • Significance of the Dancing Girl for artistic and social history.
  • Evidence of dice and board games.

  • Standardized weights and measures reveal strict economic control.

Question cues

Compare

Indus Valley SocietyvsLater Vedic Society
  • Indus society was urban and merchant-centric; Vedic society was rural and pastoral/agrarian.
  • Indus society lacked the codified Varna system; Vedic society was strictly organized around the four Varnas.
  • Indus architecture featured advanced drainage and civic planning; Vedic architecture utilized wood, thatch, and mud-brick without complex urban plans.

Watch for

  • Assuming the Priest-King sculpture indicates an absolute monarch or religious dictator.
  • Confusing the Harappan script direction with social customs.
  • Believing that the presence of weapons implies a large standing army or aggressive empire.
  • Generalizing burial customs across all peripheral sites based solely on Harappa's Cemetery R-37.
  • Overlooking the role of craft guilds in maintaining standard weights and brick sizes.
