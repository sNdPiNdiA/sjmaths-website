Post-Mauryan Period

Bharhut Art

Appears in: UP PGT History Tracker →

Topic overview

  • Bharhut art represents a foundational phase of early Indian monumental stone architecture and sculpture belonging to the Sunga period.
  • Located in the Satna district of Madhya Pradesh, the Bharhut stupa site was discovered by Alexander Cunningham in 1873.
  • The surviving architectural remains, primarily railings (vedika) and gateways (torana), are housed predominantly in the Indian Museum, Kolkata.
  • The art style of Bharhut is characterized by low-relief carvings, narrative medallions, and an early use of visual iconography to depict Jatakas and episodes from the life of Buddha.
  • Inscriptions on the railing pillars provide invaluable epigraphical evidence regarding donors, guilds, and the chronological framework of the Sunga dynasty.
Concept 1

Architectural Evolution of the Bharhut Vedika

The transition from wooden prototypes to stone railing construction at Bharhut.

Details

  • The Bharhut stupa features an extensive stone railing or vedika that enclosed the central hemispherical mound.
  • The architectural design meticulously mimics wooden construction techniques, demonstrating structural transition from timber to stone.
  • Uprights (stambhas), cross-bars (sucia), and top coping stones (ushnisha) are tenoned and mortised together without mortar.
  • The inner and outer surfaces of the vedika are densely carved with medallions, half-medillons, and narrative reliefs.
  • Inscriptions are frequently engraved directly onto the vedika pillars, recording the names of donors and their native towns.

Remember

  • Material used is predominantly red sandstone sourced from local quarries.
  • The circular path enclosed by the vedika functioned as the ritual circumambulation (pradakshina) path.
  • Coping stones feature continuous rolling floral scrolls interspersed with auspicious symbols and figures.
  • Architectural fragments confirm patronage not by a single monarch, but through collective public donations.
Examples & sources
  • The surviving upright pillar depicting the Yakshi Chulakoka.
  • The coping stone fragment displaying a continuous lotus scroll with human figures.
Compare
  • Unlike later structural stone railings like Sanchi, Bharhut retains a heavier wooden aesthetic in its mortise-and-tenon joints.
  • Unlike Mauryan monolithic pillars, Bharhut architecture is essentially additive and sculptural in assembly.
Common confusions
  • Students often confuse the Bharhut vedika as belonging to the Mauryan period due to Brahmi script similarities.
  • Many assume the entire stupa mound survives intact, whereas only the railing and gateway fragments remain.
Exam points
  • Identify the architectural components: Stambha, Suchi, and Ushnisha.
  • Note the role of public donations recorded via dedicatory inscriptions.
  • Recall Alexander Cunningham's discovery of the site in 1873.
Concept 2

Aniconic Representation of the Buddha

The symbolic depiction of the Buddha prior to human anthropomorphic representation.

Details

  • Bharhut art strictly adheres to aniconism, avoiding any human representation of the historical Buddha.
  • The Buddha's presence is indicated exclusively through sacred symbols such as footprints (paduka), the empty throne (asana), the wheel of law (dharmachakra), and the umbrella (chhatra).
  • Bodhi trees associated with specific past Buddhas are marked with railings and worshipped as focal points of devotion.
  • This stylistic constraint reflects early Buddhist theology prohibiting the representation of the transcendent Tathagata in physical human form.
  • Narrative panels depict major life events of the Buddha by leaving his designated seat or path physically vacant while other figures react to him.

Remember

  • Aniconic symbols serve as visual equivalents for the living presence of the Master.
  • Past Buddhas are similarly represented through their respective designated Bodhi trees.
  • The worship of symbols demonstrates a transitional phase toward devotional (bhakti) Buddhist practices.
  • Reliefs clearly label these symbols with Brahmi inscriptions to prevent ambiguity for the viewer.
Examples & sources
  • The relief depicting the Enlightenment showing only an empty throne beneath the Bodhi tree.
  • The panel showing the Buddha's footprint worshipped by devotees.
Compare
  • Unlike Kushan-period Mathura and Gandhara art, Bharhut completely lacks anthropomorphic statues of the Buddha.
  • Unlike abstract symbols in modern art, Bharhut symbols carry precise, narrative-driven theological identities.
Common confusions
  • Beginners often misinterpret empty spaces in Bharhut panels as unfinished work rather than deliberate aniconic theology.
  • It is incorrect to assume that aniconism meant the Buddha was considered a non-historical deity.
Exam points
  • Define aniconism in the context of Sunga-period Buddhist art.
  • List key aniconic symbols: footprints, throne, wheel, and Bodhi tree.
  • Contrast Bharhut aniconism with Mathura anthropomorphism.
Concept 3

Yakshas, Yakshis, and Indigenous Folk Deities

The incorporation of pre-Buddhist local fertility cults into the Buddhist architectural program.

Details

  • Bharhut railings feature large-scale high-relief figures of guardian deities known as Yakshas and Yakshis (or Devatas).
  • These figures are explicitly identified by Brahmi inscriptions carved near them, providing crucial epigraphical data.
  • Notable inscribed figures include Yaksha Kubera, Yaksha Supavasu, and Yakshis such as Chulakoka and Sudarsana.
  • The artistic style emphasizes voluptuous bodily forms, heavy ornamentation, and direct eye contact with the viewer.
  • Their integration signifies the assimilation of popular folk religion and nature worship into institutional Buddhism.

Remember

  • Deities stand on crouching dwarfs, animals, or complex vegetative motifs, symbolizing mastery over nature.
  • Female figures (Yakshis) often grasp tree branches in a gesture associated with fertility and the salabhanjika motif.
  • Iconography reflects contemporary indigenous fashion, hairstyles, heavy earrings, and waistbands.
  • Their placement on outer pillars acts as apotropaic guardians protecting the sacred precinct.
Examples & sources
  • The pillar figure of Yakshi Chulakoka standing on an elephant.
  • The inscribed figure of Yaksha Kupira (Kubera).
Compare
  • Unlike Mauryan court art which favored imperial grandeur, Bharhut folk art celebrates robust local agrarian deities.
  • Unlike later Gupta perfection, Bharhut figures possess a flat, frontal, two-dimensional volume.
Common confusions
  • Students often mistake Bharhut Yaksha figures for representations of the Buddha or Bodhisattvas.
  • It is incorrect to view the presence of non-Buddhist deities in a stupa as a corruption of early Buddhism; it represents popular synthesis.
Exam points
  • Memorize specific inscribed names: Chulakoka, Sudarsana, and Kupira.
  • Understand the role of Yakshas as protective guardian spirits in Buddhist architecture.
  • Identify the salabhanjika (tree-holding maiden) motif.
Concept 4

Jataka Narratives and Visual Storytelling Techniques

The early application of continuous narration to depict past lives of the Buddha.

Details

  • Bharhut medallions and cross-bars feature numerous visual narratives illustrating Jataka tales and major biographical events.
  • Artists employed continuous narration, where the same protagonist appears multiple times within a single static frame to show chronological progress.
  • Spatial depth is compressed; all narrative elements are pushed forward toward the picture plane in low relief.
  • Crucial scenes are explicitly identified with descriptive Brahmi inscriptions etched directly above or below the medallion.
  • Animals are depicted with strong anthropomorphic empathy, reflecting their roles as bodhisattvas in previous births.

Remember

  • Prominent Jatakas depicted include the Ruru Jataka, Ekapada Jataka, and Miga Jataka.
  • Visual cues such as trees, rivers, and architectural huts divide narrative episodes within a single circle.
  • The storytelling style is didactic, aimed at educating illiterate pilgrims visiting the stupa.
  • Compositional layouts rely heavily on symmetry, circular framing, and clear narrative focal points.
Examples & sources
  • The Ruru Jataka medallion showing the golden deer saving a drowning man.
  • The depiction of the dream of Queen Maya before the birth of Siddhartha.
Compare
  • Unlike Sanchi narratives which show greater spatial fluidity and dynamic movement, Bharhut narratives are rigidly compartmentalized within circular medallions.
  • Unlike Gandhara narrative friezes, Bharhut uses explicit text labels to clarify complex story sequences.
Common confusions
  • Many assume Bharhut reliefs depict purely decorative secular court scenes rather than sacred Buddhist moral tales.
  • It is a mistake to view multi-figure panels as chaotic; they follow strict traditional narrative rules.
Exam points
  • Identify specific Jataka tales illustrated at Bharhut, particularly the Ruru Jataka.
  • Understand the technique of continuous narration in early Indian art.
  • Recognize the use of Brahmi descriptive labels accompanying narrative reliefs.
Concept 5

Epigraphical Evidence and Donor Inscriptions

The sociological history of Sunga society revealed through Bharhut inscriptions.

Details

  • Bharhut is unique for the high volume of dedicatory inscriptions carved across its architectural members.
  • The script used is early Brahmi, providing a secure paleographic benchmark for the Sunga period (c. 2nd century BCE).
  • Inscriptions record donations made by ordinary monks, nuns, laymen, and merchant guilds rather than solely royal patrons.
  • Geographic origins of donors are frequently mentioned, indicating that pilgrims traveled long distances to visit the stupa.
  • References to the 'Sunga kingdom' (Suganam rajye) on specific pillars offer direct historical correlation to political chronology.

Remember

  • Donors include inhabitants of towns such as Karahakata, Pataliputra, and Nasik.
  • Guilds (shrenis) of artisans and merchants funded major architectural components.
  • Female patronage is prominently visible, with numerous individual nuns (bhikkhunis) recording independent gifts.
  • Epigraphs confirm the widespread popular base of Buddhism during the post-Mauryan Sunga era.
Examples & sources
  • The pillar inscription mentioning the rule of the Sungas (Suganam rajye).
  • Dedicatory records noting gifts by specific female lay devotees and monastics.
Compare
  • Unlike Mauryan edicts which broadcast royal imperial decrees, Bharhut inscriptions document decentralized democratic public patronage.
  • Unlike later Gupta inscriptions written in ornate Sanskrit kavya style, Bharhut epigraphs are in early Prakrit/Brahmi prose.
Common confusions
  • Students often attribute the construction of Bharhut entirely to Emperor Pushyamitra Sunga, ignoring the collective public donor inscriptions.
  • It is incorrect to view Sunga rule as universally hostile to Buddhism, given the extensive Buddhist patronage at Bharhut during their reign.
Exam points
  • Note the presence of the phrase 'Suganam rajye' in Bharhut epigraphs.
  • Recognize the dominance of merchant, guild, and female donor participation.
  • Recall that inscriptions provide the primary dating framework for Sunga art.
Concept 6

Stylistic Features of Bharhut Low Relief Carving

The formal artistic conventions defining the Bharhut sculptural idiom.

Details

  • Bharhut sculpture is characterized by shallow low relief (bas-relief) where figures do not detach fully from the stone background.
  • Compositional space is strictly two-dimensional; perspective is absent, and figures are arranged hierarchically or register-wise.
  • Human figures display rigid frontality in their torsos while faces are frequently shown in profile.
  • Costumes and ornaments are rendered with meticulous linear detail, reflecting contemporary textile and jewelry design.
  • Floral motifs, lotuses, and geometric borders frame the narrative medallions with precise symmetry.

Remember

  • Facial expressions are often neutral or archaic, lacking the dynamic emotional range seen in later periods.
  • The anatomy of figures is somewhat stocky and heavy, prioritizing clear narrative legibility over naturalistic proportions.
  • Relief carving techniques rely on deep incisions to separate planes rather than rounded modeling.
  • The carvers transferred wooden relief-cutting techniques directly onto sandstone blocks.
Examples & sources
  • The medallion borders featuring intricate, repetitive lotus petal patterns.
  • The flat, linear depiction of narrative figures in the royal procession panels.
Compare
  • Unlike the high-relief and dynamic roundness of Sanchi gateway sculptures, Bharhut relies on flat, low-relief planes.
  • Unlike Mauryan polished sandstone finish (chinar), Bharhut surfaces are matte and carved from coarse local stone.
Common confusions
  • Beginners often mistake the flat, two-dimensional style of Bharhut as crude or unskilled rather than an intentional early aesthetic phase.
  • It is incorrect to expect complex foreshortening or realistic perspective in post-Mauryan Sunga reliefs.
Exam points
  • Identify bas-relief and low-relief carving as the primary technique of Bharhut.
  • Note the absence of deep three-dimensional modeling.
  • Contrast Bharhut flatness with Sanchi's developing sculptural roundness.

Concept 1

Architectural Evolution of the Bharhut Vedika

Quick recall

  • Represents the earliest phase of monumental stone architecture in Central India during the Sunga period.
  • Directly mimics wooden architectural prototypes, imitating mortise-and-tenon joints in stone.
  • Comprises upright pillars (thabuda/stambha), crossbars (suci), and a coping stone (ushnisha).
  • Demonstrates structural transition from carpentry techniques to durable sandstone masonry.

Memory aid

  • Pillars, Crossbars, Coping: 'PCC' = Prototypical Carpentry Carried-over.

Review tips

  • Focus on the technical shift from wood to red sandstone.

Quick checks

  • If a question mentions 'mortise-and-tenon in stone', instantly link it to Bharhut vedika.

Watch for

  • Confusing Bharhut vedika joinery with later highly-polished Mauryan pillar monolithic shafts.
Concept 2

Aniconic Representation of the Buddha

Quick recall

  • The Buddha is never shown in human form at Bharhut; he is depicted strictly through symbols.
  • Common symbols include footprints (paduka), empty throne (asana), wheel (dharmachakra), and umbrella (chhatra).
  • Reflects the early Buddhist convention of avoiding anthropomorphic representation before Gandhara/Mathura schools.
  • Emphasizes the presence of the Master rather than his physical likeness.

Memory aid

  • Symbols of Buddha: 'FEW' = Footprints, Empty throne, Wheel.

Review tips

  • Memorize the exact aniconic symbols used on Bharhut medallions.

Quick checks

  • Whenever an option suggests 'seated Buddha statue' for Bharhut, eliminate it immediately.

Watch for

  • Assuming anthropomorphic Buddha images existed in Sunga art because of later Gupta/Kushan developments.
Concept 3

Yakshas, Yakshis, and Indigenous Folk Deities

Quick recall

  • Incorporates pre-Buddhist local fertility cults into the overarching Buddhist architectural program.
  • Features labeled figures like Chulakoka Devata, Sudarsana Yakshi, and Kupira (Kubera).
  • Sculptures display heavy volumetric forms, abundant jewelry, and direct engagement with nature (salabhanjika motif).
  • Demonstrates Buddhism's inclusive strategy of absorbing popular indigenous religious beliefs.

Memory aid

  • Deities: 'CSK' = Chulakoka, Sudarsana, Kupira.

Review tips

  • Remember that Yakshas/Yakshis at Bharhut are explicitly identified by Brahmi inscriptions.

Quick checks

  • Look for 'labeled figures' in the stem; it usually points directly to Bharhut Yaksha/Yakshi inscriptions.

Watch for

  • Mistaking Bharhut guardian deities as exclusively Brahmanical gods due to their rich ornamentation.
Concept 4

Jataka Narratives and Visual Storytelling Techniques

Quick recall

  • Early application of continuous narration to depict past lives (Jatakas) and life events of the Buddha.
  • Multiple episodes of a story are compressed within a single medallion without strict linear compartmentalization.
  • Key stories include Ruru Jataka, Vidura Pandita Jataka, and Mahabodhi Jataka.
  • Relies on viewer familiarity with the narrative text to decode spatial progression.

Memory aid

  • Jatakas at Bharhut: 'RVM' = Ruru, Vidura, Mahabodhi.

Review tips

  • Study how circular medallions force artists to wrap stories around curves.

Quick checks

  • If a narrative relief lacks human depiction of the Buddha, it is a Bharhut Jataka scene.

Watch for

  • Expecting separated comic-strip panels; Bharhut uses integrated continuous narration within medallions.
Concept 5

Epigraphical Evidence and Donor Inscriptions

Quick recall

  • Provides profound sociological history of Sunga society through extensive Brahmi donor inscriptions.
  • Records contributions from ordinary people, nuns, monks, guilds, and royalty (e.g., King Dhanabhuti).
  • Shows decentralized patronage where common folk funded specific pillars and crossbars.
  • Written in early Brahmi script, serving as a primary linguistic and historical anchor for Indian paleography.

Memory aid

  • Patronage: 'RUG' = Royalty, Upasakas (lay followers), Guilds.

Review tips

  • Note that King Dhanabhuti is explicitly named as a patron of the gateway/vedika.

Quick checks

  • Match Brahmi script + patron names + Sunga period directly with Bharhut epigraphs.

Watch for

  • Believing that only royal Mauryan or Sunga kings financed major Buddhist structures.
Concept 6

Stylistic Features of Bharhut Low Relief Carving

Quick recall

  • Characterized by low relief carving (bas-relief) where figures are flattened against the background plane.
  • Figures are depicted with stiff frontal torsos and profile faces, showing archaic stiffness.
  • Lacks deep spatial recession or advanced perspective; crowded composition fills every available inch (horror vacui).
  • Emphasis on clear narrative readability over naturalistic anatomical accuracy.

Memory aid

  • Style: 'FFL' = Flat, Frontal, Low relief.

Review tips

  • Contrast Bharhut low relief with later high relief sculptures of Sanchi and Amaravati.

Quick checks

  • If the description says 'low relief with crowded horror vacui', it's Bharhut.

Watch for

  • Confusing Bharhut's stiff, flattened bas-relief with the dynamic, high-relief voluptuousness of Sanchi Stupa 1.

Quick facts

  • Bharhut is located in the Satna district of Madhya Pradesh.
  • The site was discovered by Alexander Cunningham in 1873.
  • Most surviving Bharhut sculptures are housed in the Indian Museum, Kolkata.
  • The railing (vedika) is made entirely of red sandstone.
  • Inscriptions are in the Brahmi script and Prakrit language.
  • King Dhanabhuti is recorded as a key royal donor of the gateway/railing.
  • The Buddha is represented entirely through aniconic symbols at Bharhut.
  • Jataka stories are carved inside circular medallions (chakra medallions).

Memory aids

  • Site Discovery: 'Cunningham in Satna' = CS.
  • Core Elements: 'PCC' = Pillar, Crossbar, Coping.
  • Art Style: 'FFL' = Flat, Frontal, Low relief.

Review tips

  • Always associate Bharhut with the Sunga period and red sandstone bas-relief.
  • Remember that labels are a signature feature of Bharhut sculptures.
  • Distinguish Bharhut's low relief from Sanchi's deep relief.
  • Keep track of aniconic symbols as they are classic exam favorites.

Question cues

  • Look for the keyword 'bas-relief' or 'low relief' to quickly identify Bharhut questions.
  • If a question mentions 'labeled Yakshis like Chulakoka', it is definitely Bharhut.
  • Remember that wood-to-stone transition applies to the Bharhut vedika railing.
  • Eliminate options suggesting Gupta-period human Buddha statues when analyzing Bharhut.

Compare

Bharhut Stupa ArtvsSanchi Stupa 1 Art
  • Bharhut features strictly low relief carvings; Sanchi features deep, high relief carvings with dynamic volume.
  • Bharhut relies heavily on explanatory Brahmi labels for its deities; Sanchi features extensive narrative gates without labels.
  • Bharhut represents the early, stiffer Sunga phase; Sanchi represents a more mature, fluid narrative style continuing into the Satavahana period.

Watch for

  • Do not attribute human Buddha statues to the Bharhut sculpture collection.
  • Do not confuse Bharhut's Brahmi donor labels with Maurya royal edicts.
  • Do not assume Bharhut railing was built from imported marble; it is local red sandstone mimicking wood.
  • Do not forget that ordinary citizens and guilds contributed majorly alongside King Dhanabhuti.
  • Do not mistake Bharhut's continuous narrative for modern multi-panel comic strips.
