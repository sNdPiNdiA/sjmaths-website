Post-Mauryan Period

Sanchi Art

Core study module, theoretical overview, and exam revision checklist for Sanchi Art under the History curriculum.

Appears in: UP PGT History Tracker → 35 quiz questions10-question mini test

Topic overview

  • Sanchi is a prominent Buddhist complex located in the Raisen district of Madhya Pradesh, near the city of Bhopal.
  • The site represents the pinnacle of Post-Mauryan art and architecture, flourishing particularly between the 3rd century BCE and the 12th century CE.
  • It is world-renowned for its magnificent stupas, monolithic pillars, toranas (gateways), and early monastic architecture.
  • The monuments at Sanchi were designated as a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1989 due to their exceptional universal value in Buddhist art history.
Concept 1

Mauryan Origins and Ashokan Foundation

Sanchi was established as a Buddhist center by Mauryan Emperor Ashoka in the 3rd century BCE.

Detailed explanation

  • Emperor Ashoka chose Sanchi for its proximity to Vidisha, the native city of his principal queen, Devi.
  • Ashoka commissioned the construction of the Great Stupa (Stupa No. 1) using brick and mud mortar.
  • An Ashokan pillar featuring the classic lustrous Mauryan polish was erected near the Southern gateway of the Great Stupa.
  • The Schism Edict (Sanchi Pillar Edict) was inscribed on the Ashokan pillar to warn monks and nuns against creating divisions within the Sangha.
  • Mahinda, the son of Ashoka, is traditionally believed to have visited Sanchi on his way to Sri Lanka to propagate Buddhism.

Key points

  • Founded by Emperor Ashoka in the 3rd century BCE.
  • Original core of Stupa No. 1 was roughly half the present size and made of brick.
  • Houses a fragmentary Ashokan Pillar with Brahmi script.
  • Features the Brahmi Schism Edict concerning monastic discipline.
Examples and evidence
  • Stupa No. 1 original brick core
  • Sanchi Ashokan Pillar fragments with mirror-like polish
Important distinctions
  • Contrast with later Sunga additions: Mauryan phase emphasized brick core and monolithic pillars, whereas Sunga phase added stone casing and elaborate gateways.
  • Contrast with Bharhut: Sanchi maintained continuous patronage well into the Gupta period, unlike Bharhut which declined earlier.
Common misconceptions
  • Misconception: All structures at Sanchi were built during the Mauryan period. Correction: Only the core of Stupa 1 and the pillar date to Ashoka; most visible art belongs to the Sunga and Satavahana periods.
  • Misconception: Ashoka built the carved toranas. Correction: The stone gateways were added during the later Sunga period.
Exam focus
  • Association with Queen Devi of Vidisha
  • Content and purpose of the Sanchi Schism Edict
  • Mauryan brick core dimensions versus later expansions
Concept 2

Sunga Period Expansion and Architectural Evolution

The Sunga dynasty oversaw the massive enlargement and stone-casing of the Great Stupa.

Detailed explanation

  • During the 2nd century BCE under Sunga patronage, the original brick stupa was doubled in size.
  • The brick core was encased in thick layers of dressed sandstone blocks.
  • A terraced berm (medhi) and a double staircase (sopana) were added to allow ritual circumambulation (pradakshina) at an upper level.
  • A wooden-style stone railing (vedika) with crossbars (suchis) and upright pillars (thabhas) was constructed around the stupa.
  • Inscriptions on the railing pillars record numerous individual donations made by commoners, guilds, and bhikkhus.

Key points

  • Dated to the 2nd century BCE under Sunga rule.
  • Stupa doubled in diameter using stone masonry.
  • Introduction of the circumambulatory path (pradakshina patha) at two levels.
  • Extensive use of donor inscriptions (danas) on railings.
Examples and evidence
  • Stupa No. 2 with its early uncarved railing
  • Stupa No. 1 stone casing and ground railing
Important distinctions
  • Contrast with Mauryan plain architecture: Sunga architecture introduced extensive narrative reliefs and standardized stone railings.
  • Contrast between Stupa 1 and Stupa 2: Stupa 1 features elaborate gateways, while Stupa 2 is noted for its early medallion carvings on railings without toranas.
Common misconceptions
  • Misconception: The Sunga rulers were hostile to Buddhism and destroyed Buddhist monuments. Correction: Epigraphic evidence at Sanchi proves that Buddhist art flourished significantly under Sunga rule.
  • Misconception: Railings were funded solely by royal treasuries. Correction: Most Sanchi railings were funded by popular collective donations recorded in Brahmi.
Exam focus
  • Structural modifications from brick to stone
  • Significance of donor inscriptions for social history
  • Evolution of the vedika and pradakshina patha
Concept 3

Satavahana Dynasty and the Four Toranas

The four magnificent stone gateways (toranas) were added during the rule of the Satavahanas in the 1st century BCE.

Detailed explanation

  • Four elaborately carved gateways facing the cardinal directions were added to Stupa No. 1 around the 1st century BCE.
  • An inscription on the southern gateway records that it was carved by the ivory-carvers (dantakaras) of Vidisha.
  • Each torana consists of two square upright pillars supporting three curved architraves with volute ends.
  • The spaces between architraves are filled with upright balusters, dwarf figures, elephants, and horse riders.
  • The craftsmanship reflects the translation of perishable wood and ivory carving techniques into permanent stone architecture.

Key points

  • Dated to the 1st century BCE to 1st century CE.
  • Patronized by the Satavahana dynasty (specifically mentioning Satakarni).
  • Crafted by the ivory workers' guild of nearby Vidisha.
  • Features double-curved architraves and elaborate bracket figures (salabhanjikas).
Examples and evidence
  • Southern Torana of Stupa No. 1 with ivory carvers' inscription
  • Northern Torana featuring best-preserved elephant brackets
Important distinctions
  • Contrast with Bharhut gateways: Sanchi toranas are much more dynamic, deeply undercut, and structurally complex with three architraves instead of two.
  • Contrast between Sunga railings and Satavahana toranas: Railings are relatively flat and formal, whereas toranas exhibit high-relief, volumetric, and narrative-heavy compositions.
Common misconceptions
  • Misconception: Toranas were built at the same time as the original Ashokan stupa. Correction: They represent the final major architectural phase, added roughly two centuries later.
  • Misconception: The toranas serve a structural load-bearing purpose for the dome. Correction: They are freestanding ceremonial entrance arches independent of the hemispherical dome.
Exam focus
  • Vidisha ivory workers' guild inscription
  • Structural components of a Sanchi torana (pillars, architraves, brackets)
  • Satavahana chronological attribution
Concept 4

Aniconic Representation of the Buddha

Sanchi art famously portrays the Buddha through symbols rather than in human form.

Detailed explanation

  • Early Buddhist art at Sanchi adheres strictly to the aniconic tradition, avoiding direct anthropomorphic depictions of Siddhartha Gautama.
  • The Buddha's presence is indicated by a rich vocabulary of auspicious symbols and sacred footprints.
  • The Bodhi tree represents his Enlightenment (Bodhimanda).
  • The Dharmachakra (Wheel of Law) symbolizes his First Sermon at Sarnath (Dharmachakra Pravartana).
  • The Horse (Kanthaka) signifies the Great Departure (Mahabhinishkramana), and the Stupa represents his Parinirvana.

Key points

  • Absence of human images of the Buddha in early reliefs.
  • Use of symbolic vocabulary: Footprints (paduka), Triratna, Umbrella (chhatra), and Empty Throne.
  • Narrative panels depict scenes from Jataka tales and Buddha's life aniconically.
  • Reflects early doctrinal reverence and psychological distance from humanizing the Master.
Examples and evidence
  • Panel showing worshippers adoring an empty throne beneath the Bodhi tree
  • Relief depicting the Miracle of Walking on Water symbolized by footprints on the river
Important distinctions
  • Contrast with Gandhara and Mathura art: Post-Mauryan Sanchi is strictly aniconic, whereas Kushana art introduced the human Buddha image.
  • Contrast with later Mahayana developments: Sanchi represents Hinayana/Early Buddhist phase of revering symbols over idols.
Common misconceptions
  • Misconception: Sanchi artists did not know how to carve human figures. Correction: Yakshis, devatas, and royal patrons are carved with extreme realism; only the Buddha was intentionally left aniconic.
  • Misconception: Sanchi contains no Buddha images at all. Correction: Later Gupta period shrines added standing anthropomorphic Buddha statues to the stupa base.
Exam focus
  • Identification of specific symbols for Buddha's life events
  • Difference between aniconic Sanchi and iconic Kushana art
  • Philosophical implications of the empty throne motif
Concept 5

Narrative Reliefs and Jataka Stories

The architraves and pillars are densely packed with continuous narrative reliefs illustrating Jatakas and historical events.

Detailed explanation

  • Relief carvings on the gateways narrate episodes from the previous lives of the Buddha (Jataka tales) and key events from his final earthly life.
  • The continuous narrative technique is employed, where the same character appears multiple times within a single frame to show temporal progression.
  • Popular Jatakas depicted include the Vessantara Jataka, Chhadanta Jataka, and Shyama Jataka.
  • Secular and royal scenes are also depicted, such as King Ashoka visiting the Ramgrama stupa.
  • Animals are portrayed with remarkable naturalism, empathy, and emotional depth, reflecting a deep ecological harmony in early Indian thought.

Key points

  • Continuous narrative method used in stone panels.
  • Depiction of popular moral tales from the Jatakas.
  • Integration of contemporary animal, floral, and architectural motifs.
  • High degree of spatial depth and crowded compositional arrangements.
Examples and evidence
  • Vessantara Jataka panel on the Northern Gateway
  • Chhadanta Jataka panel illustrating the six-tusked elephant
Important distinctions
  • Contrast with Bharhut narratives: Sanchi narratives are more fluid, less stiff, and show advanced spatial perspective and overlapping figures.
  • Contrast with Ajanta murals: Sanchi stone relief predates Ajanta paintings but shares the same continuous storytelling format.
Common misconceptions
  • Misconception: Sanchi art only depicts religious dogmas. Correction: It vividly portrays contemporary secular life, courtly dress, musical instruments, and architecture of the Post-Mauryan era.
  • Misconception: Each panel tells a separate, unrelated story. Correction: Panels are arranged hierarchically to guide the pilgrim through moral and spiritual journeys.
Exam focus
  • Identification of continuous narrative technique
  • Major Jataka stories represented on Sanchi toranas
  • Artistic comparison between Bharhut and Sanchi relief styles
Concept 6

Yakshi Figures and Syncretic Motifs

The sculptural program of Sanchi integrates indigenous fertility cults through voluptuous Yakshi and Salabhanjika figures.

Detailed explanation

  • Bracket figures (salabhanjikas or yakshis) project diagonally from the capitals of the gateway pillars to support the lowest architrave.
  • These female figures are depicted grasping or bending the boughs of flowering trees, symbolizing fertility, abundance, and auspicious growth.
  • The style showcases heavy ornamentation, transparent drapery, full volumetric bodies, and dynamic sway (tribhanga posture).
  • Folk deities like Yakshas, Nagas, and dryads are harmoniously assimilated into the mainstream Buddhist architectural framework.
  • Foreign influences are also traceable in certain decorative details, such as Persian palmettes, Hellenistic acanthus leaves, and foreign soldiers wearing tunics.

Key points

  • Salabhanjika bracket figures function as structural supports and auspicious symbols.
  • Depicts pre-Buddhist folk earth and tree worship (Yakshas/Yakshis).
  • Exemplifies the tribhanga (triple-bend) posture characteristic of classical Indian sculpture.
  • Displays multicultural motifs including Greco-Roman and Achaemenid stylistic elements.
Examples and evidence
  • The famous Eastern Torana bracket figure grasping a mango tree branch
  • Foreign-looking soldier figures wearing tunics and boots on pillar bases
Important distinctions
  • Contrast with Mauryan court art: Mauryan art was official, imperial, and foreign-influenced, whereas Sanchi folk art represents indigenous popular religion and local guilds.
  • Contrast with Mathura Yaksha figures: Sanchi bracket figures are more dynamic, deeply carved, and integrated into architectural frames as structural brackets.
Common misconceptions
  • Misconception: Female bracket figures are secular dancing girls. Correction: They are traditional Yakshis/Salabhanjikas symbolizing auspiciousness and fertility.
  • Misconception: Sanchi art is purely isolationist. Correction: It absorbs West Asian and Hellenistic decorative motifs via trade networks.
Exam focus
  • Function and iconography of salabhanjikas
  • Tribhanga posture in Post-Mauryan sculpture
  • Synthesis of folk religion and Buddhism at Sanchi
Concept 7

Gupta Period Additions and Later Decline

The Gupta period contributed refined temple architecture and anthropomorphic Buddha shrines to the Sanchi complex.

Detailed explanation

  • During the 4th to 6th centuries CE under Gupta patronage, several small stone shrines and temples were constructed at Sanchi.
  • Temple No. 17 at Sanchi is a classic example of early Gupta flat-roofed temple architecture with a pillared porch and sanctum.
  • Standing anthropomorphic Buddha images were carved against the four sides of the Great Stupa's base, marking the shift to Mahayana devotionalism.
  • Inscriptions from the Gupta era document donations made by kings like Chandragupta II.
  • The site experienced a gradual decline after the 7th century CE with the waning of Buddhism in central India, eventually falling into ruins until its rediscovery in 1818 by General Taylor.

Key points

  • Gupta era marked by structural stone temples (e.g., Temple 17).
  • Introduction of iconic Buddha images on Stupa No. 1 drum.
  • Epigraphic records of Gupta monarchs (Chandragupta II).
  • Rediscovery in 1818 by British officer General Henry Taylor.
Examples and evidence
  • Temple No. 17 (classic Gupta pillared portico shrine)
  • Gupta-era seated and standing Buddha images carved onto the Great Stupa
Important distinctions
  • Contrast with Post-Mauryan stupas: Gupta additions introduced enclosed interior shrines for idol worship, whereas early Sanchi focused exclusively on open-air stupa circumambulation.
  • Contrast in sculptural style: Gupta figures exhibit serene spiritual calm, translucent drapery, and subdued ornamentation compared to the robust, dynamic Post-Mauryan figures.
Common misconceptions
  • Misconception: Sanchi was built entirely in one continuous campaign. Correction: It evolved over 1,500 years, spanning Mauryan, Sunga, Satavahana, and Gupta periods.
  • Misconception: Sanchi was never forgotten. Correction: The site was abandoned and overgrown by jungle until its rediscovery in the 19th century.
Exam focus
  • Architectural features of Gupta Temple No. 17
  • Shift from aniconic Sunga/Satavahana art to iconic Gupta art
  • Rediscovery date and officer (1818, General Henry Taylor)

Concept 1

Mauryan Origins and Ashokan Foundation

Point-wise rapid recall

  • Established by Mauryan Emperor Ashoka in the 3rd century BCE.
  • Original brick stupa was constructed during this foundational period.
  • Associated with Ashoka's queen, Devi, who hailed from nearby Vidisha.
  • Served as a primary Buddhist monastic and pilgrimage center.

Mnemonic

  • Ashoka's 3rd Century Foundation: ACF (Ashoka Created Foundation)

Tips

  • Always remember Ashoka initiated the site, but subsequent dynasties built the structural wonders visible today.

Exam tricks

  • If a question attributes the stone gateways to Ashoka, it is an automatic trap.

Common traps

  • Confusing Ashoka's original brick core with the later Sunga stone casing and Satavahana gateways.
Concept 2

Sunga Period Expansion and Architectural Evolution

Point-wise rapid recall

  • The Sunga dynasty oversaw the massive enlargement of the Great Stupa.
  • The original brick stupa was encased in stone and expanded to nearly twice its size.
  • Terraced pathways, railings (vedika), and stone stairs were added.
  • Marked the transition from simple Mauryan brick architecture to elaborate stone construction.

Mnemonic

  • Sunga Stupa Size Super-sized (SSSS)

Tips

  • Link Sunga rule with physical enlargement and stone casing of the core.

Exam tricks

  • Sunga = Stone-casing and Size-doubling.

Common traps

  • Assuming the toranas (gateways) were built by the Sungas instead of the Satavahanas.
Concept 3

Satavahana Dynasty and the Four Toranas

Point-wise rapid recall

  • Four magnificent stone gateways (toranas) added during the Satavahana rule.
  • Constructed around the 1st century BCE.
  • Gateways face the four cardinal directions with elaborate post-and-beam construction.
  • Feature dense sculptural panels depicting royal processions, animal motifs, and auspicious symbols.

Mnemonic

  • Satavahana Seven-Seven: 1st Century BCE Gateways

Tips

  • Visualise the compass: North, South, East, West toranas added by Satavahanas.

Exam tricks

  • Satavahana starts with S, Toranas start with T -> Satavahana Toranas (ST).

Common traps

  • Attributing the toranas to Ashoka or the Guptas.
Concept 4

Aniconic Representation of the Buddha

Point-wise rapid recall

  • Sanchi art famously portrays the Buddha through symbols rather than human form.
  • Common symbols include footprints (paduka), the wheel of law (dharmachakra), the empty throne, and the Bodhi tree.
  • Reflects early Buddhist theological norms before anthropomorphic images became widespread.
  • Demonstrates masterly storytelling without showing the Master's physical body.

Mnemonic

  • Symbols Steer Sanchi: Tree, Throne, Tracks, Wheel

Tips

  • Look for 'aniconic' keywords in questions regarding early Sanchi reliefs.

Exam tricks

  • Aniconic = Absent anthropomorphism, Active art.

Common traps

  • Expecting to find statues of the Buddha on the Sunga-Satavahana gates.
Concept 5

Narrative Reliefs and Jataka Stories

Point-wise rapid recall

  • Architraves and pillars are densely packed with continuous narrative reliefs.
  • Illustrates stories from the Jatakas (previous lives of the Buddha) and key historical events.
  • Serves as a visual pedagogical tool for illiterate pilgrims visiting the stupa.
  • Depicts flora, fauna, celestial beings, and human architecture in exquisite detail.

  • Jatakas Jump Justly in Sanchi reliefs

Tips

  • Remember that continuous narrative means the same character appears multiple times in a single panel.

Exam tricks

  • Mistaking narrative relief panels for isolated decorative borders.

  • Bracket figures (Salabhanjikas) gracefully grasp tree branches, symbolizing life and abundance.
  • Demonstrates the syncretism of folk religion with canonical Buddhism.
  • Exhibits high organic plasticity and dynamic bodily postures (tribhanga).

Concept 7

Gupta Period Additions and Later Decline

Point-wise rapid recall

  • Gupta period contributed refined temple architecture (such as Temple 17) and anthropomorphic Buddha shrines.
  • Four standing Buddha images carved into the base of the Great Stupa facing the four cardinal directions.
  • Represents the transition from aniconic worship to devotional image worship (bhakti).
  • Followed by gradual abandonment and neglect until its modern rediscovery in the 19th century.

Mnemonic

  • Gupta Gives Genuine Gods (Anthropomorphic shrines)

Tips

  • Gupta = Human form of Buddha added to Sanchi.

Exam tricks

  • Gupta gateways? No, Gupta shrines/temples! Gateways are Satavahana.

Common traps

  • Thinking the Gupta period built the main stupa structure.

Quick facts

  • Sanchi is located in the Raisen district of Madhya Pradesh.
  • Stupa No. 1 is the Great Stupa initiated by Ashoka.
  • Ashoka's wife Devi was from Vidisha, near Sanchi.
  • The stone casing of the Great Stupa occurred under the Sunga dynasty.
  • The four ornate toranas were built during the Satavahana period.
  • Sanchi art is primarily aniconic, representing Buddha through symbols.
  • Salabhanjika bracket figures display the tribhanga body posture.
  • Gupta period introduced anthropomorphic Buddha statues to Sanchi.

Topic mnemonics

  • Chronology of Sanchi: Mauryas Mound (Ashoka), Sungas Stone-case, Satavahanas Sculpt (Toranas), Guptas Gods (Shrines).
  • Symbols of Buddha: FTW (Footprints, Throne, Wheel/Tree).
  • Gateway Builders: Satavahana Stones Spread (SSS).

Revision tips

  • Always trace architectural elements chronologically: Brick (Ashoka) -> Stone casing (Sunga) -> Gateways (Satavahana) -> Shrines (Gupta).
  • Pay close attention to aniconic vs. iconic transitions across dynasties.
  • Remember that Jataka stories form the core thematic narrative on the architraves.
  • Connect indigenous fertility motifs (Yakshis) with the inclusive nature of early Buddhist art.

Exam tricks

  • If a question mentions 'Toranas' and '1st century BCE', lock in Satavahana.
  • If a question mentions 'Brick core' and '3rd century BCE', lock in Ashoka / Mauryas.
  • If a question mentions 'Anthropomorphic Buddha images' at Sanchi, lock in the Gupta period.
  • If a question mentions 'Bracket figures grasping tree branches', lock in Salabhanjika / Yakshi.

Important comparisons

Mauryan FoundationvsSunga Expansion
  • Mauryan phase established the original brick core in the 3rd century BCE.
  • Sunga phase encased the brick core in stone and doubled its size.
  • Mauryan focus was imperial foundation; Sunga focus was architectural enlargement and vedika addition.
Satavahana ToranasvsGupta Shrines
  • Satavahanas built the four stone gateways (toranas) featuring dense narrative reliefs and aniconic symbols.
  • Guptas built refined temples (e.g., Temple 17) and anthropomorphic Buddha image shrines.
  • Satavahanas maintained aniconism; Guptas introduced iconic idol worship.

Exam traps and cautions

  • Assuming Ashoka built the stone toranas.
  • Confusing the Sunga dynasty's stone casing of the stupa with the construction of the gateways.
  • Believing that the Buddha is depicted in human form on the Satavahana toranas.
  • Attributing the architectural addition of Temple 17 to the Mauryas or Sungas instead of the Guptas.
  • Forgetting that Yakshis are indigenous folk motifs successfully integrated into Buddhist architecture.
