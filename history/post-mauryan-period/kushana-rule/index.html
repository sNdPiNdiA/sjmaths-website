Post-Mauryan Period

Kushana Rule

Appears in: UP PGT History Tracker → UP TGT Social Science Tracker →

Topic overview

  • The Kushanas were a branch of the Yueh-chi tribe, nomadic people originating from the steppes of northwestern China.
  • They played a pivotal role in the Post-Mauryan Period (c. 2nd century BCE to 3rd century CE) by establishing a vast empire bridging Central Asia and the Indian subcontinent.
  • Kushana rule facilitated unprecedented commercial, cultural, and religious exchange along the Silk Road, connecting the Roman Empire, Parthia, China, and India.
  • Their reign marked the zenith of numismatic art in ancient India, the patronage of Mahayana Buddhism, and the synthesis of Gandhara and Mathura art schools.
Concept 1

Origin and Early Rulers of the Kushanas

The migration and establishment of the Yueh-chi tribes in Bactria and northern India.

Details

  • The Yueh-chi were forced out of northwestern China by the Xiongnu nomads around the 2nd century BCE.
  • They migrated into the Ili River valley and subsequently into Bactria, displacing the Sakas and the Greeks.
  • The Yueh-chi tribe was divided into five principalities, of which the Kuei-shuang (Kushana) became dominant under Kujula Kadphises.
  • Kujula Kadphises united the five principalities, crossed the Hindu Kush, and established Kushana rule in Kabul and Gandhāra.
  • Vima Kadphises succeeded Kujula, expanded the empire deeper into northwestern India, and issued extensive gold coinage.

Remember

  • Parent Tribe: Yueh-chi (Tocharian speakers)
  • First Independent Ruler: Kujula Kadphises (Kadphises I)
  • Second Ruler: Vima Kadphises (Kadphises II)
  • Geographical Base: Bactria, Kabul, and Gandhara before moving into the Indo-Gangetic plains
Examples & sources
  • Kujula Kadphises imitated Roman imperial coins and Augustus's portrait types.
  • Vima Kadphises issued pure gold dinars bearing the image of Shiva and Nandi.
Compare
  • Unlike the Indo-Greeks and Sakas who became rapidly Indianized, the Kushanas retained distinct Central Asian cultural markers longer.
  • While Kujula relied on joint rule and modest titles, Vima assumed grand imperial titles like Mahesvara.
Common confusions
  • The Kushanas were indigenous Indian rulers; they were actually of Central Asian nomadic stock.
  • Kujula Kadphises was the founder of the Saka Era; Kanishka is traditionally associated with the Saka Era.
Exam points
  • Identification of the Yueh-chi migration pattern.
  • Numismatic transitions from Kujula to Vima Kadphises.
  • Chronological sequence of early Kushana monarchs.
Concept 2

Kanishka and Empire Expansion

The greatest Kushana emperor who consolidated a vast Asian empire and patronized Buddhism.

Details

  • Kanishka I was the most celebrated ruler of the Kushana dynasty, ascending the throne traditionally in 78 CE.
  • His empire extended from Central Asia and modern Xinjiang down to Varanasi in the central Gangetic plain.
  • Purushapura (modern Peshawar) served as the primary capital, while Mathura functioned as the southern administrative capital.
  • Kanishka successfully waged campaigns against the Chinese general Ban Chao to secure control over trade routes in Central Asia.
  • He inaugurated the Saka Era in 78 CE, which became a widely used chronological standard in Indian history.

Remember

  • Ascension Year: 78 CE (Saka Era origin)
  • Capitals: Purushapura (Peshawar) and Mathura
  • Military Reach: Controlled Gandhara, Punjab, Sindh, Kashmir, and parts of the Gangetic valley
  • Administration: Retained the Iranian satrapy system with regional governors called Kshatrapas
Examples & sources
  • The Rabatak Inscription discovered in Afghanistan provides crucial genealogical data on Kanishka and his predecessors.
  • Kanishka's headless statue found at Mat near Mathura depicts him wearing a long tunic and heavy felt boots.
Compare
  • Unlike Ashoka whose empire was primarily peninsular and central Indian, Kanishka's empire straddled the Pamir mountains and connected South and Central Asia.
  • While Mauryan administration was heavily bureaucratic and centralized, Kushana governance was decentralized through local satraps.
Common confusions
  • Kanishka founded the Vikrama Era; the Vikrama Era began in 57 BCE, whereas Kanishka's era started in 78 CE.
  • Kanishka ruled exclusively over northern India; his empire included crucial trans-Pamir territories.
Exam points
  • Significance of the Rabatak inscription.
  • Dual capitals of the Kushana empire under Kanishka.
  • Geographical boundaries of Kanishka's realm.
Concept 3

Religion and the Fourth Buddhist Council

Kushana patronage of Buddhism, its schism, and the rise of Mahayana doctrine.

Details

  • Kushana rulers practiced religious syncretism, worshipping Iranian, Greek, and Indian deities depicted on their coins.
  • Kanishka became a fervent patron of Buddhism later in his reign, much like Ashoka.
  • He convened the Fourth Buddhist Council at Kundalavana in Kashmir under the presidency of Vasumitra and vice-presidency of Ashvaghosha.
  • The council compiled commentaries on the Tripitaka known as the Vibhasa shastras on copper plates.
  • The council formally recognized the split of Buddhism into Hinayana (Theravada) and Mahayana branches.

Remember

  • Council Location: Kundalavana, Kashmir
  • Council Leadership: Vasumitra (President) and Ashvaghosha (Vice-President)
  • Major Outcome: Rise and official state patronage of Mahayana Buddhism
  • Pantheon: Introduction of Buddha worship in human form and Bodhisattvas
Examples & sources
  • Coins of Kanishka feature images of the Buddha alongside Iranian deities like Miro (Sun) and Nana.
  • The writings of Ashvaghosha, such as the Buddhacharita, received direct imperial patronage.
Compare
  • While Ashoka patronized the Third Buddhist Council to purge heretics and promote orthodox Theravada, Kanishka patronized the Fourth Council which institutionalized Mahayana Buddhism.
  • Pre-Kushana Buddhism treated the Buddha aniconically; Kushana art popularized the anthropomorphic representation of the Buddha.
Common confusions
  • The Fourth Buddhist Council was held in Pataliputra; that was the Third Council under Ashoka.
  • Kushanas were exclusively Buddhist; their coins prove they practiced polytheism involving Shiva, Helios, and Ardoksho.
Exam points
  • Presidents and venue of the Fourth Buddhist Council.
  • Transition from Hinayana to Mahayana during the Kushana period.
  • Syncretic religious policy of Kushana rulers.
Concept 4

Art and Architecture: Gandhara and Mathura Schools

The flourishing of distinct sculptural traditions under Kushana patronage.

Details

  • The Kushana period witnessed the birth of the anthropomorphic image of the Buddha in Indian art.
  • The Gandhāra School, centered in the northwestern frontier, showed strong Greco-Roman stylistic influence.
  • Gandhara sculptures used blue-grey schist stone and depicted the Buddha with Hellenistic facial features, wavy hair, and heavy drapery.
  • The Mathura School used spotted red sandstone and developed an indigenous Indian artistic idiom without foreign influence.
  • Mathura sculptures portrayed the Buddha with spiritual vigor, shaven head, tight monastic robes, and smiling expressions alongside Yaksha and Yakshi figures.

Remember

  • Gandhara Material: Grey schist, stucco
  • Mathura Material: Spotted red sandstone
  • Gandhara Themes: Life stories of Buddha, Greco-Roman drapery, realistic anatomy
  • Mathura Themes: Jain, Buddhist, and Brahmanical deities, voluptuous human forms, spiritual radiance
Examples & sources
  • The seated Buddha statue from Katra mound in Mathura bearing inscriptions from the reign of Kanishka.
  • Numerous fasting Buddha sculptures produced in the Peshawar and Swat valleys of Gandhara.
Compare
  • Gandhara art is heavily Hellenistic/Roman in aesthetic, whereas Mathura art is deeply rooted in indigenous Indian traditions.
  • Gandhara focused on narrative panels and realistic physical details, while Mathura prioritized divine symbolism, volume, and symbolic hand gestures (mudras).
Common confusions
  • Gandhara art was created by Greeks; it was actually produced by local Indian and foreign artisans under Kushana patronage.
  • Mathura art copied Gandhara styles; Mathura evolved independently from earlier Bharhut and Sanchi traditions.
Exam points
  • Distinction between schist stone (Gandhara) and red sandstone (Mathura).
  • Role of Kushanas in standardizing the image of the Buddha.
  • Major sculptural centers and motifs.
Concept 5

Economy, Trade, and Numismatics

Economic prosperity driven by Silk Road commerce and abundant gold coinage.

Details

  • The Kushana Empire controlled major nodes of the Silk Road, facilitating lucrative trade between China, Central Asia, and the Roman Empire.
  • Indian ports on the western coast traded extensively with Roman ports like Myos Hormos and Berenike in Egypt.
  • Kushanas issued the finest and purest gold coins (dinars) in ancient Indian history, weighing nearly the same as Roman aurei.
  • They also issued a large volume of copper coins, indicating widespread monetisation of the internal agrarian and urban economy.
  • Export commodities included silk, spices, precious stones, cotton textiles, and steel, while gold and wine were imported.

Remember

  • Major Currency: Gold dinar and copper pieces
  • Trade Route: Silk Road (Northern and Southern branches)
  • Economic Impact: Integration of Indian markets with Mediterranean and Chinese commercial networks
  • Urban Centers: Mathura, Taxila, Purushapura, and Begram served as thriving commercial hubs
Examples & sources
  • The archaeological finds at Begram (Afghanistan) yielded Roman glassware, Chinese lacquerware, and Indian ivory plaques.
  • Roman author Pliny the Elder complained about the massive drain of Roman gold to India to pay for spices and luxury goods.
Compare
  • While the Mauryan economy was largely state-regulated and agrarian, the Kushana economy was decentralized, market-driven, and dependent on international trade.
  • Maurya punch-marked coins lacked royal legends and figures, whereas Kushana gold and copper coins featured portraits of kings, deities, and Greek/Kharoshthi legends.
Common confusions
  • Kushanas invented gold coins in India; Indo-Greeks were actually the first to issue gold coins in India, but Kushanas issued them in massive quantities.
  • Kushana gold coins indicate a purely domestic economy; they were primarily minted to facilitate international long-distance trade.
Exam points
  • Purity and significance of Kushana gold dinars.
  • Role of the Silk Road in Kushana fiscal health.
  • Archaeological evidence from the Begram hoard.
Concept 6

Society, Literature, and Science

Cultural synthesis, literary luminaries, and scientific advancements during the Kushana era.

Details

  • Kushana society was highly cosmopolitan, absorbing diverse ethnic groups including Sakas, Parthians, Greeks, and indigenous Indians.
  • Sanskrit gained prominence as a literary medium alongside Prakrit, exemplified by the works of court poets like Ashvaghosha.
  • Mahayana Buddhism stimulated the production of philosophical and devotional literature in Sanskrit.
  • Medicine and science flourished with legendary figures like Charaka and Nagarjuna operating within the cultural milieu of the empire.
  • The administrative titles used by Kushanas, such as Shahi and Shahanshah, reflected Persian and Central Asian political traditions.

Remember

  • Literary Giants: Ashvaghosha (author of Buddhacharita and Saundarananda), Nagarjuna (Madhyamika philosophy)
  • Scientific Figures: Charaka (compiler of Charaka Samhita)
  • Languages Used: Sanskrit, Prakrit, Bactrian (written in Greek script), and Kharoshthi
  • Social Assimilation: Foreign tribal elites were absorbed into the Kshatriya varna through ritual validation
Examples & sources
  • Ashvaghosha's Saundarananda is an ornate Sanskrit kavya poem describing the conversion of Nanda.
  • The Charaka Samhita remains the foundational classical text of Ayurvedic internal medicine.
Compare
  • Unlike the preceding Mauryan era where Prakrit was the universal administrative language, the Kushana era witnessed the resurgence and official patronage of classical Sanskrit.
  • Mauryan society was inward-looking regarding foreign assimilation, whereas Kushana society institutionalized the absorption of foreigners into the caste hierarchy.
Common confusions
  • Sanskrit was replaced by Persian under Kushanas; Persian influence was limited to titles, while Sanskrit dominated high literature.
  • Nagarjuna was a contemporary of the Guptas; he lived during the Satavahana-Kushana period around the 2nd century CE.
Exam points
  • Works of Ashvaghosha and Nagarjuna.
  • Authorship and significance of the Charaka Samhita.
  • Use of Bactrian language and Greek script in Kushana inscriptions.

Rapid revision

Concept 1

Origin and Early Rulers of the Kushanas

Quick recall

  • The Kushanas were a branch of the Yueh-chi nomadic tribe originating from northwest China/Steppes.
  • Driven out by the Xiongnu, they moved into Bactria and eventually crossed the Hindu Kush into northwestern India.
  • Kujula Kadphises united the five Yueh-chi principalities and founded the Kushana dynasty, issuing copper coins.
  • Vima Kadphises expanded territory into northwestern India and was the first to issue large-scale gold coins in India.

Memory aid

  • Yueh-chi Kings Validate Coinage: Y (Yueh-chi), K (Kujula), V (Vima).

Review tips

  • Remember the sequential order: Kujula (unifier/copper) followed by Vima (expander/gold).

Quick checks

  • If an exam question asks who issued the first gold coins among Kushanas, pick Vima Kadphises, not Kanishka.

Watch for

  • Confusing Kanishka as the founder of the dynasty; Kanishka was the third/greatest emperor, not the founder.
Concept 2

Kanishka and Empire Expansion

Quick recall

  • Kanishka was the most celebrated Kushana emperor, ascending the throne in 78 CE (starting the Saka Era).
  • His vast empire spanned from Central Asia (Bactria, parts of China) down to northern and central India.
  • He maintained a dual capital system: Purushapura (Peshawar) as the primary capital and Mathura as the secondary capital.
  • He was a fierce conqueror who extended his sway into Chinese Turkestan (Khotan, Yarkand).

Memory aid

  • Kanishka's Twin Capitals: PM Purushapura Mathura.

Review tips

  • Link the accession year 78 CE directly to Kanishka and the Saka calendar.

Quick checks

  • When questions mention 78 CE or Saka Era, automatically connect it to Kanishka's coronation.

Watch for

  • Assuming Kanishka conquered all of Southern India; his empire was strictly northern, northwestern, and Central Asian.
Concept 3

Religion and the Fourth Buddhist Council

Quick recall

  • Kanishka was a devout patron of Buddhism, comparable to Ashoka.
  • He convened the Fourth Buddhist Council at Kundalavana in Kashmir, presided over by Vasumitra and Ashvaghosha.
  • The council finalized the commentaries on Buddhist texts (Vibhashashastra) and marked the formal rise of Mahayana Buddhism.
  • Kushana coinage features images of various deities including Greek, Iranian, and Indian gods, showing religious tolerance.

Memory aid

  • Council Key: Ku-Va-Ka (Kundalavana, Vasumitra, Kanishka).

Review tips

  • Note that Mahayana Buddhism gained royal backing for the first time on a massive scale under Kanishka.

Quick checks

  • Remember: Ashoka held the Third Council (Pataliputra), Kanishka held the Fourth (Kashmir).

Watch for

  • Thinking Kanishka persecuted non-Buddhists; his coins feature Shiva, Buddha, and Iranian deities equally.
Concept 4

Art and Architecture: Gandhara and Mathura Schools

Quick recall

  • The Gandhara school featured Greco-Roman influence, grey-blue schist stone, and realistic human representations of Buddha.
  • The Mathura school used spotted red sandstone, portrayed indigenous Indian features, and produced both Buddhist, Jain, and Hindu images.
  • Both schools flourished simultaneously under Kushana patronage, depicting Buddha in human form for the first time widely.
  • Kushana art also introduced portrait sculptures of kings (e.g., headless statue of Kanishka at Mathura).

Memory aid

  • Gandhara = Grey/Greek; Mathura = Mud/Red Sandstone.

Review tips

  • Visualize Gandhara as Western-influenced (Apollo-like Buddha) and Mathura as purely indigenous.

Quick checks

  • If the question mentions 'grey schist' or 'Greco-Roman', immediately choose Gandhara.

Watch for

  • Confusing the stone types: Gandhara used slate/schist; Mathura used red sandstone.
Concept 5

Economy, Trade, and Numismatics

Quick recall

  • Control over the Silk Road allowed the Kushanas to reap massive revenues from trans-Asiatic trade between Rome and China.
  • They issued the purest and most abundant gold coins in ancient Indian history, reflecting immense commercial wealth.
  • Extensive trade links connected India with the Roman Empire, exporting textiles, spices, and luxury goods.
  • Their copper currency dominated local daily transactions, proving a monetized economy across social strata.

Memory aid

  • Kushana Cash: Pure Gold on the Silk Road.

Review tips

  • Connect Kushana gold abundance directly to their strategic tax-gatekeeper role on the Silk Road.

Quick checks

  • Guptas issued the *largest number* of gold coins, but Kushanas are famous for issuing the *purest* gold coins in early India.

Watch for

  • Attributing the highest quantity of gold coins to Kushanas; that title belongs to the Imperial Guptas.
Concept 6

Society, Literature, and Science

Quick recall

  • The Kushana era was a melting pot of cultures, absorbing Central Asian, Persian, Greek, and Indian elements into society.
  • Literary luminaries like Ashvaghosha (author of Buddhacharita), Nagarjuna (Madhyamaka philosophy), and Vasumitra adorned Kanishka's court.
  • Charaka, the pioneer of Ayurveda, and mathematician/astronomer/statesman traditions thrived during this period.
  • Sanskrit increasingly became the court language of literature alongside the continued use of Prakrit and Kharosthi/Brahmi scripts.

Memory aid

  • ANV: Ashvaghosha, Nagarjuna, Vasumitra.

Review tips

  • Group court scholars by discipline: Ashvaghosha (poetry/religion), Charaka (medicine), Nagarjuna (philosophy).

Quick checks

  • If asked who wrote Buddhacharita, connect it to Ashvaghosha, Kanishka's spiritual advisor.

Watch for

  • Placing Charaka in the Gupta period; Charaka was active during the Kushana era (often associated with Kanishka).

Quick facts

  • The Kushanas were a branch of the Yueh-chi nomadic tribe.
  • Kujula Kadphises founded the Kushana dynasty.
  • Vima Kadphises introduced the first large-scale gold coinage.
  • Kanishka ascended the throne in 78 CE, initiating the Saka Era.
  • Kanishka's capitals were Purushapura (Peshawar) and Mathura.
  • The Fourth Buddhist Council took place at Kundalavana, Kashmir.
  • Gandhara art was heavily influenced by Greco-Roman styles using grey schist.
  • Mathura art utilized spotted red sandstone with indigenous motifs.

Memory aids

  • Yueh-chi Kings Validate Coinage: Y-K-V (Yueh-chi, Kujula, Vima)
  • Kanishka's Council: Ku-Va-Ka (Kundalavana, Vasumitra, Kanishka)
  • Scholar Trio: A-N-C (Ashvaghosha, Nagarjuna, Charaka)

Review tips

  • Always associate Kanishka with Mahayana Buddhism and the Fourth Council.
  • Differentiate the art schools by their raw materials: Grey schist for Gandhara, Red sandstone for Mathura.
  • Keep track of coin milestones: Kujula (copper), Vima (pure gold introduction), Kanishka (diverse pantheon on coins).
  • Remember that Kushana dominance over the Silk Road was the primary driver of their economic might.

Question cues

  • Saka Era = 78 CE = Kanishka's accession year.
  • Purest gold = Kushanas; Maximum gold = Guptas.
  • Headless statue of a king in armor = Kanishka at Mathura.
  • Buddhacharita author = Ashvaghosha (Kanishka's court poet).

Compare

Gandhara School of ArtvsMathura School of Art
  • Gandhara used grey schist and mud plaster; Mathura used spotted red sandstone.
  • Gandhara shows strong Greco-Roman/Hellenistic influence; Mathura is indigenous and native in style.
  • Gandhara Buddha figures have realistic wavy hair and heavy drapery; Mathura Buddha figures often appear spiritual with shaven heads and tight robes.

Watch for

  • Confusing the founder of the Kushana dynasty (Kujula Kadphises) with its greatest ruler (Kanishka).
  • Attributing the maximum volume of gold coins to Kushanas instead of Guptas.
  • Forgetting that Kushana coins depicted Iranian, Greek, and Hindu deities alongside Buddha, showing syncretism.
  • Placing Charaka or Ashvaghosha in the Maurya or Gupta periods instead of the Kushana era.
  • Assuming Kanishka's empire reached deep into South India.
