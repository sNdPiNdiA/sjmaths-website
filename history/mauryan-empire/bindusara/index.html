Mauryan Empire

Bindusara

Appears in: UP PGT History Tracker → UP TGT Social Science Tracker →

Topic overview

  • Bindusara was the second Mauryan Emperor and the son of Chandragupta Maurya, ruling from approximately 297 BCE to 273 BCE.
  • He is known in Greek sources as Amitrochates, which is considered the Hellenized form of the Sanskrit title Amitraghata, meaning destroyer of foes.
  • His reign bridges the foundational consolidation under Chandragupta and the extensive ideological expansion under his famous son, Ashoka.
  • Buddhist, Jain, and Puranic texts provide diverse and rich accounts of his accession, administration, and personal religious inclinations.
  • During his tenure, the Mauryan Empire maintained active diplomatic and commercial relations with the Hellenistic kingdoms of the West.
Concept 1

Accession and Early Life

The transition of power from Chandragupta Maurya to Bindusara and traditional accounts of his birth.

Details

  • Bindusara succeeded his father Chandragupta Maurya around 297 BCE.
  • According to Jain tradition, Chandragupta abdicated the throne and accompanied Bhadrabahu to Shravanabelagola.
  • Jain texts record that Chanakya fed Chandragupta small doses of poison to build immunity against enemies, and a pregnant queen accidentally ingested it.
  • Chanakya performed a cesarean section to save the child, cutting open the queen's womb, during which a drop of poison touched the baby's head, causing a spot or bindu.
  • This incident allegedly led to his name being established as Bindusara, meaning the strength of the drop.

Remember

  • Predecessor: Chandragupta Maurya.
  • Successor: Ashoka.
  • Estimated Reign: c. 297 BCE – 273 BCE.
  • Primary traditions: Jain and Buddhist accounts.
Examples & sources
  • The story of Chanakya performing surgery to save the unborn child is narrated in the Jain text Parishishtaparvan.
  • The Mahavamsa mentions him as the son of Chandragupta by queen Durdhara.
Compare
  • Chandragupta Maurya founded the empire through military conquest and overthrow of the Nanda dynasty.
  • Bindusara inherited a vast pre-established empire and focused primarily on internal consolidation and southern expansion.
Common confusions
  • Students often confuse Bindusara with Bimbisara of the Haryanka dynasty.
  • Many assume Bindusara personally conquered the entire Indian peninsula, though southern campaigns are debated.
Exam points
  • Sanskrit equivalent of Amitrochates.
  • Role of Chanakya as prime minister during early years.
  • Jain source mentioning his birth details.
Concept 2

Epithets, Names and Foreign Accounts

The various titles bestowed upon Bindusara by indigenous and foreign historical sources.

Details

  • Greek historians refer to Bindusara by the name Amitrochates or Allitrochades.
  • Linguists trace Amitrochates to the Sanskrit epithet Amitraghata, meaning slayer of enemies.
  • Puranas and Brahmanical texts frequently refer to him as Bhadrasara or Varisara.
  • Jain texts mention him as Bindusara, aligning with the legend of the poison drop.
  • Strabo and Athenaeus preserve Greek accounts mentioning diplomatic exchanges with Seleucid rulers.

Remember

  • Greek Name: Amitrochates.
  • Sanskrit Title: Amitraghata.
  • Purana Name: Bhadrasara.
  • Source: Writings of Athenaeus citing earlier Greek ambassadors.
Examples & sources
  • Athenaeus mentions that Antiochus I sent figs, sweet wine, and a Greek philosopher to Amitrochates at his request.
  • Puranic lists place him immediately after Chandragupta Maurya.
Compare
  • Chandragupta Maurya was known to Greeks as Sandrokottos.
  • Bindusara was known to Greeks as Amitrochates, showing continuity in diplomatic nomenclature.
Common confusions
  • Amitraghata is sometimes mistakenly attributed to Ashoka instead of Bindusara.
  • Foreign names like Amitrochates are often confused with Bactrian Greek invaders.
Exam points
  • Match the Greek name Amitrochates with Bindusara.
  • Identify Amitraghata as slayer of enemies.
  • Distinguish Puranic names from Buddhist names.
Concept 3

Foreign Relations and Hellenistic Contacts

Bindusara's diplomatic outreach to Western Hellenistic monarchs and exchange of commodities.

Details

  • Bindusara maintained friendly diplomatic ties with the Hellenistic Seleucid Empire of West Asia.
  • He corresponded with Antiochus I Soter, the ruler of the Seleucid Empire.
  • According to Athenaeus, Bindusara requested Antiochus to send him sweet wine, dried figs, and a Greek philosopher.
  • Antiochus replied that he sent the wine and figs, but noted that Greek law prohibited the trade or export of philosophers.
  • Deimachus was sent as the Seleucid ambassador to Bindusara's court at Pataliputra, succeeding Megasthenes.

Remember

  • Seleucid King: Antiochus I Soter.
  • Greek Ambassador: Deimachus.
  • Requested Items: Wine, figs, and a sophist/philosopher.
  • Diplomatic channel: Pataliputra royal court.
Examples & sources
  • Deimachus resided at Pataliputra as the official ambassador representing the Seleucid court.
  • Correspondence with Ptolemaic Egypt is also suggested by later historical interpretations.
Compare
  • Chandragupta Maurya's foreign relations involved military conflict and matrimonial alliance with Seleucus I Nicator.
  • Bindusara's foreign relations were characterized by peaceful commercial and cultural requests with successor states.
Common confusions
  • Megasthenes is often incorrectly associated with Bindusara's reign instead of Chandragupta's.
  • Students assume the Greek philosopher was actually dispatched to Pataliputra.
Exam points
  • Identify Deimachus as ambassador to Bindusara.
  • Recall the three items requested by Bindusara from Antiochus I.
  • Significance of the title Amitrochates in trade relations.
Concept 4

Administration and Ministerial Council

The continuity of Mauryan administrative machinery and prominent ministers during Bindusara's reign.

Details

  • Bindusara inherited the robust administrative framework designed by Kautilya and Chandragupta Maurya.
  • Chanakya continued to serve as the chief minister or advisor during the early part of Bindusara's reign.
  • Later traditions mention Radhagupta, a minister belonging to a prominent Brahman family, who later played a crucial role in Ashoka's succession.
  • The council of ministers known as Mantriparishad retained immense executive power and advisory roles.
  • Provincial administration continued under royal princes functioning as kumaras or aryaputras.

Remember

  • Chief Advisor: Chanakya (early reign).
  • Key Minister: Radhagupta.
  • Administrative Body: Mantriparishad.
  • Governance Model: Centralized autocracy with provincial autonomy.
Examples & sources
  • Ashoka served as the viceroy or kumar at Ujjain and Taxila during Bindusara's administration.
  • Subandhu is mentioned in Tibetan Buddhist traditions as a minister associated with court intrigues.
Compare
  • Chandragupta's administration was focused on state creation and subduing regional satraps.
  • Bindusara's administration focused on bureaucratic stabilization and regional pacification.
Common confusions
  • Chanakya is often thought to have died immediately after Chandragupta's retirement, ignoring his service under Bindusara.
  • Radhagupta is frequently confused with Kautilya in popular media.
Exam points
  • Continuity of Kautilya's administrative system under Bindusara.
  • Radhagupta's association with the Mauryan court.
  • Role of royal princes as provincial governors.
Concept 5

Revolts at Taxila and Suppression

Internal security challenges and the uprisings in the northwestern frontier province of Taxila.

Details

  • Taxila, situated in the northwestern province, witnessed two major revolts during Bindusara's reign due to misrule by local officials.
  • The first revolt occurred while Bindusara was reigning, and he dispatched his eldest son, Susima, to quell it, but he failed to restore order.
  • Subsequently, Ashoka was sent to Taxila, where he successfully pacified the populace without widespread bloodshed by addressing local grievances.
  • A second revolt broke out later, which was also effectively handled by the imperial administration.
  • These uprisings highlight the fragility of control in distant frontier regions and the strategic importance of royal princes as troubleshooters.

Remember

  • Location of Revolt: Taxila (North-Western Province).
  • First Prince Dispatched: Susima (failed to suppress).
  • Second Prince Dispatched: Ashoka (successfully pacified).
  • Primary Cause: Oppressive local administration of Amatyas.
Examples & sources
  • The Divyavadana provides detailed accounts of the Taxila revolts and the respective missions of Susima and Ashoka.
  • Local citizens in Taxila reportedly complained against wicked officials rather than the imperial crown itself.
Compare
  • Chandragupta Maurya faced external Greek threats like Seleucus.
  • Bindusara faced internal regional revolts from provincial subjects and officials.
Common confusions
  • The Taxila revolt is often mistakenly attributed to foreign invasions rather than internal administrative oppression.
  • Some believe Ashoka led a massive army of slaughter to crush Taxila, whereas Buddhist texts emphasize peaceful resolution.
Exam points
  • Source text for Taxila revolts (Divyavadana).
  • Princes involved: Susima and Ashoka.
  • Strategic location of Taxila in the northwest.
Concept 6

Empire Extent and Southern India

The territorial boundaries of the Mauryan Empire under Bindusara and expansion towards the Deccan.

Details

  • Bindusara inherited an empire that spanned from the Hindu Kush in the northwest to the valleys of the central Deccan.
  • Historians like Taranath and medieval inscriptions suggest his campaigns extended Mauryan influence into the southern peninsula.
  • Taranath records that Chanakya and Bindusara destroyed the nobles and kings of sixteen towns and subjugated the territory between the eastern and western seas.
  • Pliny and Plutarch note the vastness of the Mauryan dominion, which continued uninterrupted through his reign.
  • Mysore and Karnataka regions contain Mauryan administrative footprints that date back to this phase of consolidation.

Remember

  • Geographical Scope: From Northern Afghanistan/Hindu Kush down to Karnataka.
  • Tibetan Historian: Taranath's account of southern conquests.
  • Stretches between two seas: Arabian Sea and Bay of Bengal.
  • Core Regions: Magadha, Gandhara, Avanti, and Kalinga (partially autonomous or contested).
Examples & sources
  • Taranath's history mentions conquest of land between the eastern and western oceans.
  • Distribution of Mauryan punch-marked coins across South India.
Compare
  • Chandragupta conquered North, Northwest, and parts of Central India.
  • Bindusara pushed the southern frontier further into the Deccan plateau.
Common confusions
  • Bindusara is often credited with conquering Kalinga, which actually took place under Ashoka.
  • Many assume South India was entirely independent until Ashoka's Kalinga war.
Exam points
  • Taranath's testimony regarding sixteen towns.
  • Territorial continuity between Chandragupta and Ashoka.
  • Deccan expansion under Bindusara.
Concept 7

Religious Inclinations and Death

Bindusara's personal faith, patronage of non-orthodox sects, and the end of his rule.

Details

  • Unlike his grandson Ashoka, Bindusara was not a follower of Buddhism for most of his life.
  • He showed deep interest in Ajivika ascetics, a heterodox sect founded by Gosala Maskariputra.
  • Divyavadana mentions Brahmanical and Ajivika teachers frequented his court, earning him the title of patron of ascetics.
  • Jain traditions claim he had favorable relations with Jain monks and respected various ascetic orders.
  • Bindusara died around 273 BCE, leading to a four-year war of succession among his sons before Ashoka formally ascended the throne in 269 BCE.

Remember

  • Primary Sect Patronized: Ajivikas.
  • Court Ascetics: Brahmins and heterodox philosophers.
  • Year of Death: c. 273 BCE.
  • Succession Crisis: Four-year civil war prior to Ashoka's coronation.
Examples & sources
  • Divyavadana records his interaction with various religious sects and Brahmins.
  • The war of succession involved the conflict between Ashoka and his elder brother Susima.
Compare
  • Chandragupta Maurya embraced Jainism towards the end of his life.
  • Ashoka embraced Buddhism after the Kalinga War, whereas Bindusara patronized Ajivikas and Brahmins.
Common confusions
  • Bindusara is often wrongly assumed to be a devout Buddhist because he was Ashoka's father.
  • The four-year gap between his death and Ashoka's coronation is frequently ignored.
Exam points
  • Bindusara's association with the Ajivika sect.
  • Four-year succession interregnum before Ashoka's coronation.
  • Death year around 273 BCE.
