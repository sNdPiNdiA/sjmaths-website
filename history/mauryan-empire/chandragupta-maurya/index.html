Mauryan Empire

Chandragupta Maurya

Core study module, theoretical overview, and exam revision checklist for Chandragupta Maurya under the History curriculum.

Appears in: UP PGT History Tracker → UP TGT Social Science Tracker → 35 quiz questions10-question mini test

Topic overview

  • Chandragupta Maurya was the founder of the Maurya Empire in ancient India, establishing his rule around 321 BCE.
  • He overthrew the Nanda dynasty with the strategic counsel of his mentor, Chanakya, also known as Kautilya or Vishnugupta.
  • His vast empire unified most of the Indian subcontinent, stretching from the Himalayas in the north to the Deccan in the south, and from Assam in the east to Afghanistan and Balochistan in the west.
  • Greek and Latin sources refer to him as Sandrokottos or Androkottos, establishing a crucial chronological anchor in ancient Indian history through his interaction with Alexander the Great's successors.
Concept 1

Early Life and Ascendancy to Power

The origins and rise of Chandragupta Maurya from obscure beginnings to the overthrow of the Nanda dynasty.

Detailed explanation

  • Chandragupta Maurya founded the Maurya dynasty around 321 BCE after defeating Dhanananda, the last ruler of the Nanda dynasty.
  • Buddhist texts like the Mahavamsa and Divyavadana state that he belonged to the Moriya clan of Kshatriyas originating from Pippalavana.
  • Brahmanical literature, including the Mudrarakshasa by Vishakhadatta, describes him as Vrishala or Shudra origin.
  • Justin, the Roman historian, mentions that Chandragupta came from a humble background before rising to supreme power.
  • Chanakya played a pivotal role as his mentor, prime minister, and strategist, orchestrating the systematic destabilization and overthrow of the Nanda rule.

Key points

  • Ascendancy date is traditionally accepted as 321 BCE.
  • Key text documenting his rise is Vishakhadatta's Mudrarakshasa.
  • Mentor and chief advisor was Kautilya/Chanakya.
  • Overthrown dynasty was the Nanda dynasty based in Pataliputra.
Examples and evidence
  • The use of guerrilla warfare tactics by Chandragupta and Chanakya against frontier Nanda garrisons before capturing Pataliputra.
  • The diplomatic isolation of King Dhanananda through Chanakya's infiltration of enemy counsels as detailed in the Mudrarakshasa.
Important distinctions
  • Buddhist traditions assign a high Kshatriya status to his lineage via the Moriyas.
  • Brahmanical sources lean toward lower caste origins while acknowledging his extraordinary political capability.
Common misconceptions
  • Assuming Chandragupta established his empire without any strategic or military assistance.
  • Believing the Nanda dynasty fell in a single open battle without protracted guerrilla campaigns.
Exam focus
  • Identification of Chandragupta as Sandrokottos in Greek accounts by William Jones.
  • Authorship and significance of Mudrarakshasa.
  • Role of Chanakya in the formation of the empire.
Concept 2

Extent of Empire and Military Conquests

The territorial expansion of the Mauryan Empire under Chandragupta Maurya across the Indian subcontinent.

Detailed explanation

  • Chandragupta Maurya built the first truly pan-Indian empire by unifying disparate regional polities.
  • His empire encompassed modern-day India, Pakistan, Bangladesh, and parts of eastern Afghanistan.
  • He conquered the Indus river valley and the Punjab region following the power vacuum left by the retreat of Alexander the Great's generals.
  • He engaged in a conflict with Seleucus I Nicator around 305 BCE, resulting in a treaty that ceded vast territories including Aria, Arachosia, Gedrosia, and Paropamisadae to the Mauryas.
  • Jain texts suggest his rule extended deep into the southern peninsula, as far as Karnataka, supported by his later ascetic retirement.

Key points

  • Treaty with Seleucus Nicator occurred in 305 BCE.
  • Acquired territories included Kabul, Kandahar, Herat, and Baluchistan.
  • Megasthenes was dispatched as an ambassador to Chandragupta's court by Seleucus.
  • Southern expansion is attested by Jain tradition linking him to Shravanabelagola.
Examples and evidence
  • The cession of 500 war elephants by Chandragupta to Seleucus Nicator in exchange for matrimonial alliance and territorial concessions.
  • The establishment of regional administrative hubs like Taxila and Ujjain to consolidate newly conquered territories.
Important distinctions
  • Chandragupta laid the territorial foundation through aggressive annexation and strategic treaties.
  • His grandson Ashoka later consolidated and pacified the empire rather than undertaking massive new conquests, save for Kalinga.
Common misconceptions
  • Confusing Chandragupta's southern limits with Ashoka's later conquests.
  • Assuming Seleucus defeated Chandragupta rather than the historical reality of Seleucus ceding territory.
Exam focus
  • Provinces acquired through the treaty with Seleucus Nicator.
  • Geographical boundaries of the early Mauryan state.
  • Strategic significance of the matrimonial alliance with the Seleucid Empire.
Concept 3

Administration and Economy

The highly centralized administrative apparatus and agrarian economy structured during Chandragupta's reign.

Detailed explanation

  • Chandragupta's administration was heavily centralized, functioning according to the principles outlined in Kautilya's Arthashastra.
  • The empire was divided into provinces ruled by royal princes or members of the royal family, functioning as viceroys.
  • Municipal administration in Pataliputra was managed by six committees of five members each, overseeing industries, foreigners, births, deaths, trade, and tax collection.
  • The economy was predominantly agrarian, with the state maintaining strict control over land revenue, irrigation projects, and mining operations.
  • A vast network of espionage and a standing army maintained internal security and protected trade routes.

Key points

  • Primary administrative text is Kautilya's Arthashastra.
  • Capital city Pataliputra was governed by six specialized boards.
  • State revenue relied heavily on bhaga, the agricultural land tax.
  • Megasthenes' Indica provides firsthand accounts of city administration and social stratification.
Examples and evidence
  • The construction of the Sudarsana lake in Saurashtra by his provincial governor Pushyagupta Vaisya for irrigation purposes.
  • The employment of a complex spy network consisting of poisons-givers, ascetics, and householders as detailed in the Arthashastra.
Important distinctions
  • Chandragupta's administration relied heavily on direct bureaucratic control and extensive state monopolies.
  • Later feudal polities delegated authority to local intermediaries, contrasting with the direct Mauryan bureaucratic reach.
Common misconceptions
  • Believing the Mauryan economy was entirely driven by maritime trade rather than internal agriculture and state-controlled commerce.
  • Assuming local self-government existed without central bureaucratic oversight.
Exam focus
  • Functions of the six municipal boards of Pataliputra.
  • Role of Pushyagupta Vaisya and the Sudarsana Lake inscription.
  • Key administrative terminology from the Arthashastra like Tirthas and Adhyakshas.
Concept 4

Foreign Accounts and Megasthenes

Greco-Roman accounts of Chandragupta's court, particularly Megasthenes' Indica and diplomatic exchanges.

Detailed explanation

  • Megasthenes was a Greek ambassador sent by Seleucus I Nicator to the court of Chandragupta Maurya at Pataliputra.
  • His lost work, Indica, survives only in fragments quoted by later classical writers like Strabo, Arrian, Diodorus, and Pliny.
  • Megasthenes described Pataliputra as Palibothra, noting it as a magnificent city fortified with wooden walls and surrounded by a deep moat.
  • Greek accounts noted the absence of slavery in India in the classical Western sense, alongside observations of the Indian caste system divided into seven categories.
  • Descriptions of the king's lifestyle highlight his tight security, including female bodyguards and living in heavily guarded wooden palaces.

Key points

  • Ambassador was Megasthenes.
  • Surviving text is known as Indica.
  • Greek name for Pataliputra was Palibothra.
  • Identified seven distinct social classes in India rather than the traditional four varnas.
Examples and evidence
  • Megasthenes' observation that Indian farmers were exempted from military service and fought wars away from cultivated fields.
  • Accounts of Chandragupta hunting wild beasts in enclosed parks and tasting his food for poison before consumption.
Important distinctions
  • Greek observers misinterpreted the Indian caste system into seven occupational groups rather than four traditional varnas.
  • Native texts like Arthashastra focus on normative statecraft, whereas foreign accounts record external perceptions of daily life.
Common misconceptions
  • Treating Megasthenes' Indica as an entirely infallible text without recognizing its occasional exaggerations.
  • Confusing the original complete text of Indica with the extant fragments compiled from secondary classical writers.
Exam focus
  • Identification of Sandrokottos as Chandragupta Maurya by William Jones in 1793.
  • Seven-caste division recorded by Megasthenes.
  • Architectural description of Pataliputra by classical writers.
Concept 5

Jain Tradition, Abdication, and Death

Chandragupta's renunciation of worldly life, conversion to Jainism, and final days at Shravanabelagola.

Detailed explanation

  • According to Jain traditions recorded in texts like Hemachandra's Parishishtaparvan, Chandragupta abdicated his throne in favor of his son Bindusara.
  • He converted to Jainism and became a disciple of the Jain ascetic Bhadrabahu.
  • He migrated to southern India, specifically to Chandragiri hill at Shravanabelagola in modern Karnataka.
  • He spent his final days engaging in Sallekhana, the Jain ritual of fasting to death for spiritual purification.
  • The Basadi complex on Chandragiri hill commemorates his ascetic penance and association with Bhadrabahu.

Key points

  • Successor to the throne was his son Bindusara.
  • Jain preceptor who guided his retirement was Bhadrabahu.
  • Location of his death is Shravanabelagola in Karnataka.
  • Mode of death was Sallekhana (Santhara).
Examples and evidence
  • The naming of Chandragiri hill in Shravanabelagola after Chandragupta Maurya.
  • Epigraphical references in later medieval inscriptions at Shravanabelagola linking Bhadrabahu and Chandragupta.
Important distinctions
  • Jain sources emphasize his religious renunciation and peaceful ascetic death.
  • Brahmanical and Buddhist sources provide alternative narratives or focus primarily on his political achievements without detailing his religious end.
Common misconceptions
  • Assuming Chandragupta died a violent death or remained a lifelong follower of Brahmanical or Vedic rituals.
  • Confusing his religious affiliation with Ashoka's later conversion to Buddhism.
Exam focus
  • Role of Bhadrabahu in Chandragupta's conversion to Jainism.
  • Association with Shravanabelagola and the practice of Sallekhana.
  • Source text Parishishtaparvan detailing his abdication.

  • Established the Mauryan Empire in 321 BCE.
  • Overthrew Dhanananda of the Nanda dynasty.
  • Guided by Chanakya (Kautilya).
  • Called Sandrokottos in Greek literature and Vrishala in Sanskrit texts.

Mnemonic

  • MCC: Maurya, Chanakya, Chandragupta

Tips

  • Remember the distinction between Buddhist (Kshatriya) and Brahmanical (Shudra) accounts of his birth.

Exam tricks

  • Whenever you see Sandrokottos in a question, immediately link it to William Jones and Chandragupta Maurya.

Common traps

  • Confusing the Mudrarakshasa author Vishakhadatta with Kautilya.
Concept 2

Extent of Empire and Military Conquests

Point-wise rapid recall

  • Conquered northern and northwestern India after Alexander's departure.
  • Fought and signed a treaty with Seleucus I Nicator in 305 BCE.
  • Acquired four satrapies: Aria, Arachosia, Gedrosia, and Paropamisadae.
  • Extended control into southern India as indicated by Jain sources.

Mnemonic

  • GAPA: Gedrosia, Arachosia, Paropamisadae, Aria

Tips

  • Note that Seleucus ceded territory; Chandragupta did not lose to him.

Exam tricks

  • Associate the 500 war elephants given by Chandragupta with the acquisition of the trans-Indus provinces.

Common traps

  • Assuming Chandragupta conquered Kalinga (that was Ashoka).

Administration and Economy

  • Centralized bureaucracy based on Kautilya's Arthashastra.
  • Pataliputra administered by six committees of five members each.
  • Agrarian economy with land tax (bhaga) as primary revenue.
  • Irrigation managed via state projects like Sudarsana Lake.

Mnemonic

Tips

  • Memorize Pushyagupta Vaisya as Chandragupta's provincial governor in Saurashtra.

Exam tricks

  • Link Arthashastra terminology directly to central taxation and espionage.

Common traps

  • Confusing provincial governors of Chandragupta with those of Ashoka or Skandagupta.

Foreign Accounts and Megasthenes

Point-wise rapid recall

  • Megasthenes sent as ambassador by Seleucus Nicator.
  • Wrote Indica, surviving only in fragments.
  • Described Pataliputra as Palibothra.
  • Recorded a unique seven-fold division of Indian society.

Common traps

Concept 5

Jain Tradition, Abdication, and Death

Point-wise rapid recall

  • Abdicated throne in favor of son Bindusara.
  • Adopted Jainism under preceptor Bhadrabahu.
  • Migrated to Shravanabelagola in Karnataka.
  • Died via Sallekhana (fasting to death) on Chandragiri hill.

Mnemonic

  • CBS: Chandragupta, Bhadrabahu, Shravanabelagola

Tips

  • Link Parishishtaparvan by Hemachandra with Chandragupta's Jain retirement.

Exam tricks

  • Sallekhana is the Jain term; do not confuse it with Buddhist practices.

Common traps

  • Assuming Chandragupta converted to Buddhism like Ashoka.

Quick facts

  • Ascension Year: 321 BCE
  • Chief Advisor: Kautilya / Chanakya
  • Greek Name: Sandrokottos
  • Treaty Year: 305 BCE with Seleucus Nicator
  • Ambassador to Court: Megasthenes
  • Capital City: Pataliputra (Palibothra)
  • Religious Retirement: Jainism under Bhadrabahu
  • Place of Death: Shravanabelagola, Karnataka

Topic mnemonics

  • Mauryan Founders Call Chanakya: Maurya, Chandragupta, Chanakya
  • Greek Satrapies Acquired: GAPA (Gedrosia, Arachosia, Paropamisadae, Aria)
  • Jain Retirement Trail: CBS (Chandragupta, Bhadrabahu, Shravanabelagola)

Revision tips

  • Always connect Chandragupta with statecraft (Arthashastra) and Kautilya.
  • Keep foreign accounts separate: Megasthenes for Chandragupta, Deimachus for Bindusara.
  • Distinguish between Chandragupta's Jain leanings and Ashoka's Buddhist path.
  • Verify dates and treaties carefully for chronology-based questions in UPSC and State PCS.

Exam tricks

  • Sandrokottos = William Jones discovery = Chandragupta Maurya.
  • Palibothra = Megasthenes description of Pataliputra.
  • Sudarsana Lake = Pushyagupta Vaisya (Chandragupta) + Tushaspa (Ashoka) + Suvisakha (Rudradaman).
  • Sallekhana at Shravanabelagola = Final historical act of Chandragupta Maurya.

Important comparisons

Chandragupta MauryavsAshoka the Great
  • Chandragupta founded the empire and pursued aggressive territorial unification, whereas Ashoka consolidated and promoted dhamma after the Kalinga war.
  • Chandragupta embraced Jainism in his final years, while Ashoka actively propagated Buddhism across Asia.
  • Chandragupta's administration was heavily militarized and centralized under Kautilya's guidance, while Ashoka introduced paternalistic governance through his Rock Edicts.

Exam traps and cautions

  • Confusing Chandragupta Maurya with Chandragupta I of the Gupta dynasty.
  • Attributing the conquest of Kalinga to Chandragupta instead of Ashoka.
  • Assuming Megasthenes' Indica survived in its original complete form.
  • Believing Chandragupta died as a Hindu following Vedic rituals instead of practicing Jain Sallekhana.
  • Mistaking the Greek ambassador Deimachus (who visited Bindusara) for Megasthenes (who visited Chandragupta).
