Mauryan Empire

Religion under the Mauryas

Core study module, theoretical overview, and exam revision checklist for Religion under the Mauryas under the History curriculum.

Appears in: UP PGT History Tracker → 35 quiz questions10-question mini test

Topic overview

  • Religion under the Mauryas was characterized by remarkable religious pluralism, royal patronage of diverse sects, and a fundamental shift from Vedic ritualism to ethical governance under Ashoka.
  • Buddhism received extensive state patronage from Ashoka, transforming from a regional ascetic movement into a pan-Asian world religion through systematic missionary activities.
  • Jainism held deep royal connections, famously evidenced by Chandragupta Maurya's abdication and ritual starvation at Shravanabelagola according to Digambara tradition.
  • Brahmanism and popular folk cults such as Yakshas, Nagas, and Vedic deities coexisted harmoniously alongside heterodox movements without state persecution.
  • Epigraphic evidence, particularly the Major and Minor Rock Edicts and Pillar Edicts of Ashoka, serves as the primary historical source for reconstructing Mauryan religious policy.
Concept 1

Ashokan Dhamma and Ethical Governance

Ashoka formulated Dhamma as a universal moral code independent of specific sectarian dogmas.

Detailed explanation

  • Ashoka defined Dhamma in Pillar Edict II and Minor Rock Edict I as consisting of minimum sin, many good deeds, compassion, liberality, truthfulness, and purity.
  • Dhamma was not a new religion or a sectarian creed of Buddhism, but a socio-ethical code designed to promote social harmony and moral order.
  • The policy of Dhamma prohibited animal sacrifices, violent rituals, and cruel treatment of prisoners and animals.
  • Ashoka appointed special officers called Dhamma-mahamatras in his 14th regnal year to propagate and monitor Dhamma across all social strata.
  • Religious tolerance was a core tenet of Dhamma, explicitly mandated in Rock Edict XII, which commands respect for all religious sects.

Key points

  • First propagated actively after the Kalinga War in 261 BCE.
  • Exemplified in Major Rock Edict XII advocating concord among all sects.
  • Enforced through a specialized bureaucratic cadre of Dhamma-mahamatras.
  • Emphasized non-injury (ahimsa) to living creatures and filial piety.
Examples and evidence
  • Pillar Edict II questioning 'What is Dhamma?' and answering it with minimal evil and maximum good.
  • Barabar Cave inscriptions dedicating shelters to the Ajivika sect, proving royal religious tolerance.
Important distinctions
  • Dhamma was an ethical framework for societal harmony, whereas Buddhism was a structured monastic and soteriological religion.
  • Vedic religion prioritized ritual sacrifices and caste duties, whereas Dhamma prioritized moral action and social welfare regardless of caste.
Common misconceptions
  • Ashoka converted to Buddhism and made it the official state religion, compelling subjects to convert.
  • Dhamma was merely a political propaganda tool with no genuine moral conviction.
Exam focus
  • Identify Dhamma-mahamatras and their specific functions.
  • Recall the contents of Rock Edict XII regarding religious harmony.
  • Differentiate between Ashoka's personal Buddhism and public Dhamma.
Concept 2

State Patronage and Institutionalization of Buddhism

The Mauryan state under Ashoka elevated Buddhism to imperial prominence and institutionalized its canon.

Detailed explanation

  • Chandragupta Maurya and Bindusara patronized heterodox sects, but Ashoka became an ardent lay follower (Upasaka) after the Kalinga War.
  • Ashoka undertook pilgrimage tours (Dhammayatras) to sacred Buddhist sites like Lumbini, Bodh Gaya, Sarnath, and Kushinagar.
  • The Third Buddhist Council was convened at Pataliputra under the patronage of Ashoka and the presidentship of Moggaliputta Tissa around 250 BCE.
  • Missionaries were dispatched to foreign lands and regions including Gandhara, Kashmir, Suvarnabhumi, and Sri Lanka (led by Mahendra and Sanghamitra).
  • Ashokan edicts at Lumbini (Rummindei Pillar Inscription) exempted the birthplace of the Buddha from the tax of bali and reduced bhaga to an eighth.

Key points

  • Third Buddhist Council purged the Sangha of heretical elements and finalized the Abhidhamma Pitaka.
  • Rummindei Inscription provides direct epigraphic proof of Ashoka's Buddhist pilgrimage and economic concessions.
  • Missionary network transformed Buddhism into a pan-Asian phenomenon.
  • Stupas and monasteries were constructed across the empire, including the Sanchi and Bharhut core structures.
Examples and evidence
  • Dispatch of Buddhist monks Mahendra and Sanghamitra to Sri Lanka during Tissa's reign.
  • Construction of the original brick stupa at Sanchi by Ashoka.
Important distinctions
  • Pre-Ashokan Buddhism was a regional North Indian ascetic order, whereas Mauryan Buddhism became an international missionary religion.
  • Early Buddhism lacked monumental architectural patronage, which the Mauryan state lavishly provided through stupas and monolithic pillars.
Common misconceptions
  • Ashoka established Buddhism as the compulsory state religion of the Mauryan Empire.
  • All Mauryan emperors were devout Buddhists.
Exam focus
  • Recall the patron, location, and president of the Third Buddhist Council.
  • Identify the significance of the Rummindei and Nigali Sagar inscriptions.
  • Trace the missionary routes and leaders mentioned in Ceylonese chronicles and epigraphy.
Concept 3

Jainism and the Early Mauryan Rulers

Jainism enjoyed significant early imperial patronage, particularly through Chandragupta Maurya.

Detailed explanation

  • According to Digambara literary traditions such as Hemachandra's Parishishtaparvan, Chandragupta Maurya abdicated the throne in favor of Bindusara.
  • Chandragupta allegedly converted to Jainism and migrated to Shravanabelagola in Karnataka with the Jain ascetic Bhadrabahu.
  • Chandragupta ended his life through the Jain ritual of Sallekhana (voluntary fasting to death) at Chandragiri hill.
  • The early Mauryan administrative core in Magadha maintained strong ties with Jain ascetics and communities.
  • Jainism retained a strong mercantile following in western and southern India during the Mauryan epoch.

Key points

  • Primary literary source: Parishishtaparvan by Hemachandra and Rajavali-kathe.
  • Key historical site: Shravanabelagola (Chandragiri hill), Karnataka.
  • Ritual practice: Sallekhana or Santhara.
  • Secular support: Early Mauryan rulers provided patronage to Digambara and early Jain monastic orders.
Examples and evidence
  • Chandragupta Maurya's migration to Shravanabelagola with Bhadrabahu.
  • Naming of Chandragiri hill after Chandragupta Maurya.
Important distinctions
  • Jainism received its peak royal patronage during the early Mauryan period under Chandragupta, whereas Buddhism peaked later under Ashoka.
  • Jain monastic discipline required strict asceticism and Sallekhana, whereas Buddhist monasticism practiced the Middle Path within organized sanghas.
Common misconceptions
  • Ashoka promoted Jainism alongside Buddhism in his later edicts.
  • Jainism was completely eclipsed by Buddhism throughout the entire Mauryan period.
Exam focus
  • Identify the association between Chandragupta Maurya, Bhadrabahu, and Shravanabelagola.
  • Recognize Parishishtaparvan as a key literary text for Mauryan-Jain connections.
  • Understand the distinction between early Mauryan Jain patronage and later Ashokan Buddhist patronage.
Concept 4

The Ajivikas and Other Heterodox Sects

The Ajivikas, founded by Gosala Maskariputra, received generous patronage from Mauryan emperors including Bindusara and Ashoka.

Detailed explanation

  • The Ajivikas were a prominent heterodox fatalistic sect (Niyati doctrine) that denied the efficacy of human free will and karma.
  • Emperor Bindusara and his queen Subhadrangi were ardent followers and patrons of the Ajivika ascetic community.
  • Ashoka demonstrated absolute religious impartiality by dedicating rock-cut caves in the Barabar Hills to the Ajivika ascetics.
  • The Barabar Cave inscriptions (Sudama, Karna Chaupar, and Lomas Rishi) explicitly record royal donations to the Ajivikas during Ashoka's 12th and 19th regnal years.
  • Dasharatha Maurya, the grandson of Ashoka, also patronized the Ajivikas, as evidenced by the Nagarjuni Cave inscriptions.

Key points

  • Core philosophical doctrine: Niyati (Absolute Determinism).
  • Key epigraphic evidence: Barabar and Nagarjuni Cave inscriptions in Jehanabad district, Bihar.
  • Royal patrons: Bindusara, Ashoka, and Dasharatha Maurya.
  • Architectural legacy: Earliest examples of rock-cut architecture in India.
Examples and evidence
  • Sudama Cave inscription recording its dedication to the Ajivikas by Ashoka.
  • Nagarjuni Cave inscriptions recording donations by Dasharatha Maurya.
Important distinctions
  • Ajivikas preached complete fatalism (Niyati), whereas Buddhists and Jains emphasized human effort and karma.
  • Ajivika patronage spanned multiple generations of Mauryan rulers from Bindusara to Dasharatha, defying the myth of exclusive Buddhist monopoly.
Common misconceptions
  • Ashoka persecuted all non-Buddhist sects after his conversion.
  • The Ajivikas disappeared immediately after the rise of the Mauryas.
Exam focus
  • Locate and identify the Barabar and Nagarjuni cave inscriptions.
  • Match Bindusara and Dasharatha with Ajivika patronage.
  • Understand the philosophical tenets of the Ajivika sect.
Concept 5

Brahmanism and Popular Folk Religious Cults

Brahmanism and indigenous folk cults retained a vibrant presence among the populace during the Mauryan period.

Detailed explanation

  • Brahmanical priests (Brahmans) continued to receive royal land grants, stipends, and high status in administrative advisory roles.
  • Megasthenes notes the presence of philosophers divided into Brachmanes (Brahmans) and Sarmanes (Sramanas), acknowledging Brahmanical prominence.
  • Popular worship of nature spirits, Yakshas, Nagas, trees, and fertility deities flourished among the rural and urban masses.
  • Monumental stone sculptures of Yakshas and Yakshinis (such as the Parkham Yaksha and Didarganj Yakshini) date to the Mauryan era.
  • Bhagavatism or early Vaishnavism began gaining traction, evidenced by the Besnagar pillar inscription of Heliodorus from the immediate post-Mauryan Sunga period, reflecting roots in this era.

Key points

  • Primary classical source: Indica by Megasthenes describing Brachmanes.
  • Artistic evidence: Monumental polished sandstone Yaksha/Yakshini statues with Mauryan polish.
  • Coexistence: Brahmanism operated alongside Buddhism, Jainism, and Ajivikism without systemic state oppression.
  • Sacrifice: Animal sacrifice in Vedic rituals was curtailed by Ashokan edicts, but Brahmanical rituals continued in private.
Examples and evidence
  • Didarganj Yakshini statue showcasing characteristic Mauryan polish.
  • Parkham Yaksha statue from Mathura dating to the Mauryan period.
Important distinctions
  • Heterodox sects received royal missionary backing, whereas Brahmanism maintained traditional hereditary roles and localized patronage.
  • Folk cults focused on local protective deities (Yakshas), whereas Buddhism and Jainism offered universal soteriological paths.
Common misconceptions
  • Brahmanism was suppressed by Ashoka due to his promotion of Buddhism.
  • Mauryan art was exclusively Buddhist in theme and purpose.
Exam focus
  • Identify Megasthenes' classification of Brachmanes and Sarmanes.
  • Recognize the iconography and provenance of the Didarganj Yakshini.
  • Evaluate the status of Brahmanism under Ashoka's edicts restricting animal slaughter.
Concept 6

Mauryan Religious Architecture and Art

Mauryan religious art witnessed the transition from perishable wood and thatch to monumental polished stone structures.

Detailed explanation

  • Ashokan pillars (Stambhas or Lats) carved from single blocks of Chunar sandstone displayed a mirror-like polish and served as religious-political markers.
  • Stupas, originally mound-like burial tumuli for holy relics, were massively expanded and institutionalized as Buddhist religious centers.
  • Rock-cut architecture commenced with the excavation of monastic shelters in the Barabar and Nagarjuni hills for ascetics.
  • The Mauryan palace at Kumrahar (Pataliputra), described by Megasthenes, possessed religious and ceremonial hypostyle hall characteristics.
  • Animal capitals on pillars (such as lions, bulls, and elephants) carried deep religious and cosmological symbolism rooted in Indian and Achaemenid traditions.

Key points

  • Material: Chunar sandstone with distinctive high gloss (Mauryan polish).
  • Key sites: Sanchi, Sarnath, Barabar Hills, Rampurva, and Basarah-Bakhira.
  • Architectural types: Monolithic pillars, rock-cut caves, stupas, and wooden-stone palatial halls.
  • Symbolism: Sarnath Lion Capital adopted as the National Emblem of India.
Examples and evidence
  • Sarnath Lion Capital featuring the Dharma Chakra and four guardian lions.
  • Lomas Rishi Cave in Barabar Hills featuring an ocha-shaped facade imitating wooden architecture.
Important distinctions
  • Mauryan religious architecture utilized durable stone on a monumental scale for the first time since the Indus Valley Civilization.
  • Subsequent periods developed rock-cut chaityas and viharas, whereas the Mauryas pioneered the initial rock-cut cave excavations.
Common misconceptions
  • Mauryan pillar architecture was entirely copied from Persian Achaemenid models without indigenous elements.
  • All Mauryan religious monuments were Buddhist.
Exam focus
  • Identify the source of Chunar sandstone used in Mauryan pillars.
  • Analyze the features of the Lomas Rishi Cave facade.
  • Distinguish between court art (pillars, capitals) and popular art (Yakshas).

Rapid revision

Concept 1

Ashokan Dhamma and Ethical Governance

Point-wise rapid recall

  • Formulated as a universal moral code independent of specific sectarian dogmas.
  • Emphasized non-violence (ahimsa), respect for elders, kindness to slaves and servants, and liberality.
  • Promoted through Rock Edicts, Pillar Edicts, and royal officials known as Dhamma-mahamatas.
  • Aimed at social harmony and ethical cohesion across a diverse empire rather than propagating theological salvation.

Mnemonic

  • D-H-A-M-M-A: Duty, Harmony, Ahimsa, Morality, Maintenance of Authority.

Tips

  • Remember that Dhamma is not Buddhism; it is a secular ethical code created by Ashoka for governance.

Exam tricks

  • If a question says Dhamma mandated the worship of Buddha, mark it false immediately.

Common traps

  • Confusing Ashokan Dhamma with the Eightfold Path of Buddhism.
Concept 2

State Patronage and Institutionalization of Buddhism

Point-wise rapid recall

  • The Mauryan state under Ashoka elevated Buddhism to imperial prominence.
  • Led to the institutionalization of the Buddhist canon, notably via the Third Buddhist Council.
  • Ashoka dispatched missions to Sri Lanka, Central Asia, and Southeast Asia to spread Buddhism.
  • Stupas and rock-cut sanctuaries were heavily promoted to house relics and support monastic communities.

Mnemonic

  • B-U-D-D-H-A: Buddhism Under Dynamic Dhamma Helps Administration.

Tips

  • Associate Ashoka directly with the Third Buddhist Council held at Pataliputra.

Exam tricks

  • Note that while Ashoka patronized Buddhism, early Mauryans like Chandragupta were linked to Jainism.

Common traps

  • Assuming Buddhism was established as the official state religion; it was heavily patronized, but other sects retained rights.
Concept 3

Jainism and the Early Mauryan Rulers

Point-wise rapid recall

  • Jainism enjoyed significant early imperial patronage, particularly through Chandragupta Maurya.
  • Chandragupta abdicated his throne and converted to Jainism, spending his final days at Shravanabelagola.
  • Traditional accounts link the great famine in Magadha to the migration of Jain monks led by Bhadrabahu accompanied by Chandragupta.
  • The sect retained mercantile and administrative support during the early phase of the empire.

Mnemonic

  • C-J-S: Chandragupta Jain Shravanabelagola.

Tips

  • Link early Mauryan rule with Jainism and later Mauryan rule (Ashoka) with Buddhism.

Exam tricks

  • Check whether the question refers to 'early' or 'late' Mauryan period when assessing Jain influence.

Common traps

  • Believing Ashoka was the sole patron of all religious sects throughout the entire empire's history.
Concept 4

The Ajivikas and Other Heterodox Sects

Point-wise rapid recall

  • The Ajivikas, founded by Gosala Maskariputra, believed in absolute determinism (niyati).
  • Received generous patronage from Mauryan emperors including Bindusara and Ashoka.
  • Ashoka dedicated the Barabar Caves in Gaya to the Ajivika ascetics.
  • Demonstrates the religious pluralism and tolerance practiced by the Mauryan state.

Mnemonic

  • A-B-B: Ajivikas, Bindusara, Barabar.

Tips

  • Memorize that Bindusara specifically patronized the Ajivika sect.

Exam tricks

  • If an exam question claims Ashoka only favored Buddhists, use the Barabar caves to disprove it.

Common traps

  • Thinking Ashoka persecuted non-Buddhist sects; he actually donated cave shelters to Ajivikas.
Concept 5

Brahmanism and Popular Folk Religious Cults

Point-wise rapid recall

  • Brahmanism and indigenous folk cults retained a vibrant presence among the populace during the Mauryan period.
  • Sacrificial rituals, worship of nature spirits (Yakshas and Yakshis), and local deities continued uninterrupted.
  • Brahmans maintained high social status as ministers, advisors, and scholars despite royal patronage shifting to heterodox sects.
  • Popular religious syncretism laid the foundation for later Puranic Hinduism.

Mnemonic

  • B-F-P: Brahmanism, Folk cults, Popular presence.

Tips

  • Do not equate royal patronage of Buddhism with the complete eradication of Brahmanism.

Exam tricks

  • Look for Yaksha statues (like the Parkham Yaksha) as material evidence of popular cults.

Common traps

  • Assuming Brahmanism was suppressed during Ashoka's reign.
Concept 6

Mauryan Religious Architecture and Art

Point-wise rapid recall

  • Witnessed the transition from perishable wood and thatch to monumental polished stone structures.
  • Included monolithic pillars with animal capitals, stupas, and rock-cut cave sanctuaries.
  • Exhibited the distinctive high mirror-like polish characteristic of Mauryan stone masonry.
  • Served as a visual medium for imperial propaganda and religious devotion.

Mnemonic

  • M-S-P: Monolithic, Stupas, Polish.

Tips

  • Associate the 'Mauryan polish' immediately with pillars and cave interiors.

Exam tricks

  • Differentiate between Mauryan rock-cut architecture (Barabar) and later developed structural temples.

Common traps

  • Attributing all Indian rock-cut architecture exclusively to the Gupta period.

Quick facts

  • Ashoka formulated Dhamma as a universal moral code independent of sectarian dogmas.
  • Chandragupta Maurya patronized Jainism and retired to Shravanabelagola.
  • Bindusara patronized the Ajivika sect founded by Gosala Maskariputra.
  • Ashoka donated the Barabar Caves in Gaya to Ajivika ascetics.
  • The Third Buddhist Council was convened under Ashoka's patronage at Pataliputra.
  • Dhamma-mahamatas were special officials appointed by Ashoka to propagate ethical conduct.
  • Mauryan religious art is famous for its distinct mirror-like stone polish.
  • Brahmanism and indigenous folk cults like Yaksha worship remained vibrant among the masses.

Topic mnemonics

  • C-J-A: Chandragupta (Jain), Bindusara (Ajivika), Ashoka (Buddhist/Pluralist).
  • D-M-B: Dhamma, Mahamatas, Barabar caves.
  • S-P-Y: Stupas, Pillars, Yakshas.

Revision tips

  • Always distinguish between Ashoka's personal faith in Buddhism and his administrative policy of Dhamma.
  • Keep track of which ruler patronized which heterodox sect.
  • Remember that architectural transition from wood to stone marks the Mauryan epoch.
  • Note that religious pluralism was a hallmark of Mauryan statecraft.

Exam tricks

  • When questions contrast Dhamma with Buddhism, remember Dhamma is ethical and secular; Buddhism is soteriological and religious.
  • Use the Barabar caves as a trump card in answers regarding Ashoka's religious tolerance.
  • Tie Chandragupta to southern migration and Jain fasting rituals.
  • Identify high polish as a definitive tag for Mauryan stonework.

Important comparisons

Ashokan DhammavsBuddhism
  • Dhamma is an administrative moral code; Buddhism is a formal religious path to Nirvana.
  • Dhamma avoids theological dogmas like rebirth or Nirvana; Buddhism explicitly teaches them.
  • Dhamma was enforced by royal officials (Dhamma-mahamatas); Buddhism relied on the Sangha and monks.
AjivikasvsJainism
  • Ajivikas were founded by Gosala Maskariputra and preached absolute determinism (niyati).
  • Jainism was systematized by Mahavira and emphasized rigorous asceticism and karmic purification.
  • Ajivikas received prominent patronage from Bindusara; early Jainism was closely tied to Chandragupta.

Exam traps and cautions

  • Treating Ashokan Dhamma as synonymous with Buddhism.
  • Assuming Ashoka persecuted non-Buddhists despite his donations to the Ajivikas.
  • Forgetting that Brahmanism and folk cults thrived simultaneously alongside state-patronized heterodox sects.
  • Confusing the patronage of Chandragupta (Jainism) with that of Bindusara (Ajivikas).
  • Believing Mauryan architecture was purely structural without recognizing its reliance on rock-cut traditions.
