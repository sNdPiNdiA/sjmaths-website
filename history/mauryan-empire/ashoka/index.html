Mauryan Empire

Emperor Ashoka

Appears in: UP PGT History Tracker → UP TGT Social Science Tracker →

Topic overview

  • Emperor Ashoka was the third ruler of the Mauryan Empire, succeeding his father Bindusara.
  • He reigned approximately from 268 BCE to 232 BCE, marking the zenith of imperial territorial expansion and moral administration in ancient India.
  • His early reign was characterized by aggressive military campaigns, most notably the destructive Kalinga War in 261 BCE.
  • The profound suffering witnessed during the Kalinga War served as a turning point, leading to his conversion to Buddhism and the adoption of Dhamma.
  • He is historically unique for utilizing rock edicts, pillar edicts, and cave inscriptions spread across the subcontinent to propagate ethical governance and social welfare.
Concept 1

Early Life and Accession to the Throne

Ashoka's turbulent rise to power amidst succession struggles and his early militaristic administration.

Details

  • Ashoka was the son of Mauryan Emperor Bindusara and Subhadrangi (or Dharma).
  • Buddhist texts like Mahavamsa and Dipavamsa mention a brutal war of succession involving the elimination of his brothers, though contemporary epigraphy remains silent on fratricide.
  • He served successfully as the Viceroy of Ujjain and Taxila during his father's reign, suppressing local revolts.
  • His coronation was delayed by four years, taking place around 268 BCE, suggesting internal political instability after Bindusara's death.
  • His early administrative policies followed Kautilyan statecraft, emphasizing centralized control, punitive justice, and military readiness.

Remember

  • Father: Bindusara; Grandfather: Chandragupta Maurya.
  • Pre-accession roles: Viceroy of Avanti (Ujjain) and Taxila.
  • Delay in formal coronation: Four-year gap between succession and consecration (abhisheka).
  • Primary literary sources for succession: Sri Lankan chronicles (Mahavamsa, Dipavamsa) and Divyavadana.
Examples & sources
  • Suppression of the Taxila revolt during Bindusara's reign as royal viceroy.
  • Marriage to Devi, the daughter of a merchant of Vidisha, during his stay in Central India.
Compare
  • Unlike Chandragupta Maurya who established the empire through conquest and Chanakya's radical statecraft, Ashoka inherited a consolidated empire.
  • Unlike Bindusara who maintained status quo and diplomatic ties with Hellenistic kings, Ashoka actively restructured imperial ideology.
Common confusions
  • Ashoka killed all ninety-nine of his brothers; Buddhist texts exaggerate this number to dramatize his transformation.
  • Ashoka was a devout Buddhist from birth; he was actually a follower of Brahmanism/Ajivikas before converting to Buddhism.
Exam points
  • Chronology of the Mauryan succession (Chandragupta -> Bindusara -> Ashoka).
  • Literary sources describing the war of succession.
  • Ashoka's role as viceroy of Ujjain.
Concept 2

The Kalinga War and Ideological Transformation

The conquest of Kalinga in 261 BCE that catalyzed Ashoka's spiritual awakening and rejection of physical conquest.

Details

  • Kalinga was an independent coastal kingdom controlling crucial trade routes to South-East Asia and Sri Lanka.
  • Ashoka invaded Kalinga in the eighth year of his consecration, approximately 261 BCE.
  • Rock Edict XIII provides vivid statistics of the war: 100,000 killed, 150,000 taken prisoner, and many more dying from disease and famine.
  • The immense human carnage deeply remorsefulized Ashoka, prompting him to renounce bherigosha (sound of war drums) in favor of dhammagosha (sound of Dhamma).
  • His spiritual mentor was initially Nigrodha, followed by his interaction with Upagupta, who deepened his understanding of Buddhist teachings.

Remember

  • Date of Kalinga War: 261 BCE (8th regnal year).
  • Primary epigraphic source: Rock Edict XIII at Shahbazgarhi, Mansehra, and Girnar.
  • Core policy shift: Bherigosha replaced by Dhammagosha.
  • Immediate aftermath: Pilgrimages to Buddhist holy sites like Bodh Gaya and Lumbini.
Examples & sources
  • Visit to Lumbini, the birthplace of Buddha, where he exempted the village from land revenue (bali) and reduced its tax (bhaga) to one-eighth.
  • Dedication of the Barabar Hill caves (Sudama and Chaupar) to the ascetic Ajivika sect.
Compare
  • Compared to traditional conquerors (digvijayi) like Samudragupta, Ashoka stands out as a ruler who permanently abandoned aggressive territorial annexation after victory.
  • Unlike contemporary monarchs who used religion exclusively for political legitimation, Ashoka internalized ethical norms into state policy.
Common confusions
  • Ashoka disbanded the Mauryan army after the Kalinga War; he retained his military force to maintain internal security and deter external threats.
  • Ashoka became a Buddhist monk; he remained a lay follower (upasaka) while governing the empire.
Exam points
  • Exact figures and implications mentioned in Rock Edict XIII.
  • Regnal year calculation of the Kalinga War.
  • Transition from military imperialism to moral imperialism.
Concept 3

Ashoka's Dhamma and Moral Code

A universal ethical code designed to foster social harmony, tolerance, and civic duty across a pluralistic empire.

Details

  • Dhamma is the Prakrit form of the Sanskrit word Dharma, though Ashoka's Dhamma was not a religious creed or a Buddhist sect doctrine.
  • It represented a practical code of social conduct, moral ethics, and mutual respect applicable to all religious communities.
  • Core tenets included obedience to parents and elders, respect for teachers, non-injury (ahimsa) to living creatures, and liberality towards ascetics and Brahmans.
  • It emphasized truthfulness, purity of heart, kindness, self-control, and gratitude.
  • Pillar Edict II and VII explicitly pose the rhetorical question 'Kim chu dhamme?' (What is Dhamma?) and enumerate its virtues.

Remember

  • Language used: Prakrit (in Brahmi, Kharosthi, Aramaic, and Greek scripts).
  • Target audience: Multi-ethnic and multi-religious subjects of the vast empire.
  • Key administrative officers appointed: Dhamma-mahamattas (created in the 14th regnal year).
  • Religious tolerance: Explicitly mandated in Rock Edict XII, which demands respect for all sects.
Examples & sources
  • Prohibition of animal sacrifices in the capital and strict regulation of animal slaughter for royal kitchens.
  • Establishment of welfare measures like planting shade trees, digging wells, and setting up medical dispensaries for humans and animals.
Compare
  • Unlike Buddhism which focuses on personal salvation (nirvana), Ashoka's Dhamma focuses on social cohesion and righteous living in this world and the next.
  • Unlike Akbar's Din-i Ilahi which was an elite syncretic order, Ashoka's Dhamma was a mass-oriented public ethics program.
Common confusions
  • Ashoka forced Buddhism upon his subjects; he actively promoted religious tolerance and supported Brahmins and Ajivikas alongside Buddhists.
  • Dhamma is identical to the Eightfold Path of Buddhism; it is a simplified, non-sectarian distillation of general ethical values.
Exam points
  • Definition and sources of Ashoka's Dhamma.
  • Role and functions of Dhamma-mahamattas.
  • Distinction between Buddhism and Ashoka's Dhamma.
Concept 4

Epigraphy: Edicts, Pillars, and Cave Inscriptions

The pioneering stone inscriptions that serve as the primary authentic historical record of Ashoka's reign.

Details

  • Ashoka was the first Indian ruler to issue royal proclamations directly inscribed on rocks and stone pillars.
  • James Prinsep deciphered the Brahmi script in 1837, reading inscriptions that referred to 'Devanampiya Piyadassi' (Beloved of the Gods).
  • The inscriptions are classified into Major Rock Edicts, Minor Rock Edicts, Separate Kalinga Edicts, Pillar Edicts, and Cave Inscriptions.
  • Scripts utilized include Brahmi (majority of the empire), Kharosthi (North-West), Aramaic and Greek (Afghanistan region for foreign populations).
  • Geography of edicts spans from Kandahar in Afghanistan and Shahbazgarhi in Pakistan to Maski, Brahmagiri, and Jaugada in South India.

Remember

  • First decipherment: James Prinsep (1837).
  • Identification with Ashoka: Maski Minor Rock Edict explicitly mentions 'Devanampiya Ashoka'.
  • Major Rock Edict XIII: Details the Kalinga War and lists contemporary Hellenistic kings.
  • Rummindei (Lumbini) Pillar Inscription: Commemorates Ashoka's pilgrimage and tax reduction.
Examples & sources
  • Delhi-Topra and Delhi-Meerut pillar inscriptions, later shifted to Delhi by Firoz Shah Tughlaq.
  • Kandahar Bilingual Inscription written in Greek and Aramaic reflecting administrative reach in the borderlands.
Compare
  • Unlike later Gupta inscriptions (e.g., Allahabad Prasasti of Samudragupta) written in ornate classical Sanskrit on private or royal initiative, Ashoka's edicts are in vernacular Prakrit meant for public instruction.
  • Unlike punch-marked coins carrying only symbols, Ashoka's epigraphs convey explicit royal philosophy and policy.
Common confusions
  • All Ashoka edicts are written in Sanskrit; they are written in regional Prakrit dialects.
  • Ashoka's name appears in all his inscriptions; most use only his title 'Devanampiya Piyadassi', with only a few like Maski, Gujarra, Nettur, and Udegolam naming 'Ashoka'.
Exam points
  • Decipherment details by James Prinsep.
  • Identification of sites where Ashoka's name is explicitly mentioned.
  • Geographical distribution and script variations (Kharosthi vs Brahmi).
Concept 5

Administration, Welfare, and Foreign Relations

Administrative restructuring under Dhamma, extensive public welfare initiatives, and peaceful foreign diplomacy.

Details

  • Ashoka retained the centralized bureaucratic apparatus inherited from Chandragupta Maurya and Kautilya's Arthashastra.
  • Provincial administration was managed by royal princes (kumaras or aryaputras) stationed in capitals like Taxila, Ujjain, Suvarnagiri, and Tosali.
  • He introduced special supervisory officers called Rajukas (judicial and revenue authority), Yuktas (subordinate officials), and Pradesikas (district heads).
  • Foreign policy shifted from military conquest to cultural and diplomatic missions, sending Buddhist missions to Sri Lanka, West Asia, Egypt, and Macedonia.
  • His son Mahendra and daughter Sanghamitra were dispatched to Sri Lanka to propagate Buddhism, establishing deep cultural ties.

Remember

  • Provinces: Takshasila (North), Ujjain (West), Suvarnagiri (South), Tosali (East), with the capital at Pataliputra.
  • Judicial reforms: Rajukas were empowered to reward and punish citizens independently to ensure fair justice.
  • Foreign missions: Mentioned in Rock Edict XIII involving Hellenistic rulers like Antiochus II Theos, Ptolemy II Philadelphos, and Alexander of Epirus.
  • Public welfare: Hospitals, botanical gardens for medicinal herbs, rest houses, and drinking water wells.
Examples & sources
  • Sending missionary delegations to King Tissa of Sri Lanka.
  • Establishment of separate Kalinga Edicts emphasizing impartial paternal administration ('All men are my children').
Compare
  • Unlike the aggressive expansionist diplomacy of the early Mauryas, Ashoka practiced cultural imperialism through Dhamma-vijaya (conquest by righteousness).
  • Unlike Greek satrapies that imposed foreign culture, Mauryan provincial administration integrated local traditions under central oversight.
Common confusions
  • Ashoka dismantled the standing army and intelligence network; imperial espionage and military readiness were maintained to safeguard borders.
  • Ashoka's foreign policy led to the immediate military collapse of the empire; financial and administrative crises after his death caused the decline.
Exam points
  • Roles of Rajukas, Yuktas, and Dhamma-mahamattas.
  • Names of Hellenistic kings mentioned in Rock Edict XIII.
  • Mauryan provincial capitals and their respective regions.
Concept 6

Religious Patronage, Third Buddhist Council, and Historiographical Legacy

Ashoka's institutional support to Buddhism, convening of councils, and his enduring impact on Indian history.

Details

  • Ashoka convened the Third Buddhist Council at Pataliputra around 250 BCE under the presidentship of Moggaliputta Tissa to purge heretical views.
  • The proceedings of the Third Council led to the compilation of the Abhidhamma Pitaka and the dispatch of monks to distant regions.
  • He is credited with building 84,000 stupas across the Indian subcontinent, including foundational structures at Sanchi and Sarnath.
  • His lion capital from Sarnath has been adopted as the National Emblem of India, symbolizing power, courage, and confidence.
  • Historiographically, scholars like D.D. Kosambi and Romila Thapar debate whether his pacifist policies contributed to the economic and political decline of the Mauryan Empire.

Remember

  • Third Buddhist Council: Pataliputra, c. 250 BCE, presided over by Moggaliputta Tissa.
  • Architectural legacy: Sanchi Stupa enlargement, Sarnath Pillar, Barabar Caves.
  • National Emblem: Sarnath Lion Capital featuring the Dharma Chakra.
  • Historiographical debate: Romila Thapar's structural analysis vs H.C. Raychaudhuri's view blaming Ashoka's pacifism for military decline.
Examples & sources
  • Construction of the original brick stupa at Sanchi, later enlarged during the Shunga period.
  • Sarnath Pillar featuring four lions back-to-back and the wheel of law.
Compare
  • Unlike Kanishka who patronized Mahayana Buddhism, Ashoka patronized early Theravada/Sthaviravada traditions.
  • Unlike modern secular states, Ashoka's state actively promoted moral and religious welfare while maintaining strict impartiality among sects.
Common confusions
  • Ashoka built the entire Sanchi Stupa in stone; the original brick structure was commissioned by Ashoka, and stone casing was added later.
  • Ashoka's policies instantly ruined the Mauryan economy; modern economic historians argue that his welfare measures were sustainable within a robust agrarian taxation system.
Exam points
  • President and venue of the Third Buddhist Council.
  • Details of the Sarnath Lion Capital and its adoption.
  • Key historiographical arguments regarding the fall of the Mauryan Empire.

  • Served successfully as Viceroy of Ujjain and Taxila.
  • Fought a disputed war of succession against brothers, delayed coronation by four years.
  • Ascended the throne in approximately 268 BCE.

Concept 6

Religious Patronage, Third Buddhist Council, and Historiographical Legacy

Quick recall

  • Convened Third Buddhist Council at Pataliputra under Moggaliputta Tissa.
  • Commissioned stupas, pillars, and cave shelters for ascetics.
  • Sarnath Lion Capital serves as India's National Emblem.
  • Scholars debate whether his pacifism contributed to imperial decline.

Memory aid

  • 3PC-P: Third Buddhist Council at Pataliputra.

Review tips

  • Associate Moggaliputta Tissa with the Third Council.

Quick checks

  • Keep distinct the First (Rajagriha), Second (Vaisali), and Third (Pataliputra) Buddhist Councils.

Watch for

  • Confusing the Fourth Buddhist Council (Kanishka) with Ashoka's Third Council.

Quick facts

  • Regnal period: c. 268 BCE – 232 BCE.
  • Kalinga War year: 261 BCE (8th regnal year).
  • Decipherer of Brahmi script: James Prinsep (1837).
  • Inscriptions naming Ashoka: Maski, Gujarra, Nettur, Udegolam.
  • Third Buddhist Council venue: Pataliputra (c. 250 BCE).
  • President of Third Buddhist Council: Moggaliputta Tissa.
  • Title used in edicts: Devanampiya Piyadassi.
  • National Emblem origin: Sarnath Lion Capital.

Memory aids

  • Mauryan Kings Sequence: C-B-A (Chandragupta, Bindusara, Ashoka).
  • Scripts of Edicts: BKAG (Brahmi, Kharosthi, Aramaic, Greek).
  • Buddhist Councils: R-V-P-K (Rajagriha, Vaisali, Pataliputra, Kashmir/Kundalavana).

Review tips

  • Always verify whether a question refers to Major Rock Edicts, Pillar Edicts, or Minor Rock Edicts.
  • Focus heavily on the matching of edict numbers with specific contents (e.g., Rock Edict XIII for Kalinga War).
  • Read regional script distributions carefully (Kharosthi in North-West, Brahmi elsewhere).
  • Connect administrative terms (Rajuka, Dhamma-mahamatta) directly to their functions.

Question cues

  • If an exam question claims Ashoka converted to Buddhism immediately at birth or accession, eliminate it instantly.
  • Look for 'Devanampiya' as a giveaway for Ashokan inscriptions.
  • Remember that Kalinga is modern coastal Odisha.
  • Distinguish between internal provincial viceroys (kumaras) and foreign diplomatic envoys.

Compare

Ashoka's DhammavsEarly Buddhism
  • Dhamma is a code of universal social ethics and civic morality; Buddhism is a spiritual path aimed at Nirvana.
  • Dhamma was meant for public governance across all religious sects; Buddhism is a specific monastic and lay spiritual religion.
  • Dhamma does not emphasize Four Noble Truths or Eightfold Path explicitly; Buddhism centers entirely upon them.
Chandragupta MauryavsEmperor Ashoka
  • Chandragupta founded the empire through military conquest and Kautilyan realpolitik; Ashoka consolidated and moralized it through Dhamma.
  • Chandragupta patronized Jainism in his later years; Ashoka patronized Buddhism.
  • Chandragupta left no stone edicts or inscriptions; Ashoka pioneered epigraphy in ancient India.

Watch for

  • Assuming Ashoka was a pacifist who dismantled the Mauryan military apparatus.
  • Confusing the script of North-Western edicts (Kharosthi, written right-to-left) with Brahmi.
  • Believing Ashoka's name appears in all edicts when it only appears in four minor rock edicts.
  • Confusing the Fourth Buddhist Council under Kanishka with the Third Council convened by Ashoka.
  • Assuming Dhamma-mahamattas were religious missionaries only, ignoring their administrative and social welfare functions.
