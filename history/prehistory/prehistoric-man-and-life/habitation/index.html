Prehistory

Habitation of Prehistoric Man

Appears in: UP PGT History Tracker →

Topic overview

  • Prehistoric habitation refers to the ways in which early human groups adapted to their environment for shelter, survival, and social organization before the advent of writing.
  • The study of prehistoric dwelling patterns relies heavily on archaeological evidence, including stone tools, animal bones, hearths, postholes, and cave art.
  • Habitation strategies evolved from open-air river valley sites and natural rock shelters during the Palaeolithic to semi-permanent pit-dwellings and settled village communities in the Neolithic.
  • Indian prehistoric habitation spans multiple geographical zones, including the Soan Valley, Belan Valley, Narmada Valley, and the rock-cut shelters of Central India.
Concept 1

Natural Rock Shelters and Caves

Natural geological formations used by Lower and Upper Palaeolithic humans for primary protection against harsh climates and predators.

Details

  • Natural caves and rock shelters offered immediate, ready-made protection from extreme weather, cold, and predatory carnivores.
  • Limestone and sandstone formations in central and southern India were most frequently chosen for prolonged habitation.
  • Evidence of hearths, ash layers, and carbonized bone fragments inside caves indicates controlled use of fire for warmth and cooking.
  • Stratified deposits within cave floors provide continuous chronological sequences of human occupation over millennia.
  • Rock shelters also served as canvases for prehistoric rock art, linking habitation sites with ritual and symbolic expression.

Remember

  • Bhimbetka in Madhya Pradesh is the most prominent Indian rock shelter site spanning Lower Palaeolithic to historical periods.
  • Adamgarh in Madhya Pradesh provides crucial evidence of prehistoric habitation alongside microlithic industries.
  • Kurnool Caves in Andhra Pradesh have yielded important Upper Palaeolithic bone tools and ash deposits.
  • The stratigraphy of these sites helps archaeologists reconstruct climatic and environmental shifts.
Examples & sources
  • Bhimbetka rock shelters near Bhopal, featuring over 700 shelters with extensive paintings.
  • Billasurgam caves in Andhra Pradesh, excavated by Robert Bruce Foote.
Compare
  • Rock shelters provided permanent or semi-permanent structural stability compared to temporary open-air camps.
  • Caves preserve organic remains and artifacts better than open-air sites due to protection from weathering and erosion.
Common confusions
  • Prehistoric humans lived permanently deep inside dark caves away from sunlight.
  • All caves with rock art were used exclusively as ritual temples rather than daily living quarters.
Exam points
  • Identification of Bhimbetka as a UNESCO World Heritage site and its chronological range.
  • Association of Kurnool caves with early ash deposits and bone tool industries.
  • Distinction between temporary hunting stations and long-term base camps in caves.
Concept 2

Open-Air River Valley Sites

Strategic encampments established by early hominins along river terraces and water sources for easy access to water, game, and lithic raw materials.

Details

  • River valleys offered abundant flora and fauna, making them prime locations for hunter-gatherer subsistence economies.
  • Alluvial terraces provided elevated, dry platforms safe from seasonal flooding during monsoons.
  • River beds served as natural quarries where pebble tools and core materials like quartzite, chert, and jasper were readily collected.
  • Open-air sites generally exhibit scattered artifact scatters, indicating seasonal mobility and temporary campsites.
  • Postholes and structural remains are rare in Lower Palaeolithic open-air sites due to continuous fluvial action and soil erosion.

Remember

  • The Soan Valley in Punjab (now Pakistan) is a classic Lower Palaeolithic terrace site complex studied by the Yale-Cambridge Expedition.
  • The Belan Valley in Uttar Pradesh preserves a continuous sequence from Lower Palaeolithic to Neolithic cultures.
  • Hunsgi and Baichbal valleys in Karnataka represent major Acheulian open-air factory and habitation sites.
  • Narmada Valley sites have yielded both Acheulian tools and the famous human fossil fragment at Hathnora.
Examples & sources
  • Didwana in the Thar Desert region of Rajasthan.
  • Paisra in the Bihar region, showing evidence of Acheulian structural activity and postholes.
Compare
  • Open-air sites are subject to post-depositional fluvial disturbance, unlike protected cave deposits.
  • River valley sites usually display extensive stone-working factory floors alongside living areas.
Common confusions
  • Open-air sites were permanent year-round settlements with advanced architecture.
  • Lower Palaeolithic hominins inhabited dense tropical jungles rather than open woodland-grassland mosaics near water.
Exam points
  • Matching river valleys with their respective archaeological cultures (e.g., Soan Valley with Soanian pebble tools).
  • Significance of Hunsgi valley as an Acheulian base camp with granite raw material sources.
  • Role of the Belan Valley in establishing Indian prehistory sequences.
Concept 3

Pit-Dwellings and Subterranean Habitations

Subsurface excavation structures designed by Neolithic communities in cold regions for thermal insulation and structural protection.

Details

  • Pit-dwellings involved digging circular or oval chambers into the earth and roofing them with perishable timber and thatch.
  • Subterranean or semi-subterranean construction provided natural insulation against harsh winter temperatures and high-altitude winds.
  • Access to the interior was typically managed through steps, ladders, or ramps cut into the earthen walls.
  • Postholes surrounding the pits indicate the use of wooden uprights to support superstructure roofs.
  • Interior hearths and storage pits for grain or tools are frequently found associated with these dwelling floors.

Remember

  • Burzahom and Gufkral in Kashmir are the most famous Neolithic sites featuring subterranean pit-dwellings.
  • Burzahom pits were often mud-plastered on the inner walls and accompanied by stone hearths.
  • The presence of domestic dog burials alongside human graves is a unique feature of the Burzahom pit-dwelling culture.
  • These dwellings reflect specific climatic adaptations to the cold Himalayan environment.
Examples & sources
  • Burzahom site excavations led by T.N. Khazanchi.
  • Gufkral site in the Pulwama district of Jammu and Kashmir.
Compare
  • Pit-dwellings are unique to northern cold-region Neolithic sites and absent in peninsular Indian Neolithic settlements.
  • Subterranean dwellings required more cooperative labor and planning compared to simple surface tents.
Common confusions
  • Pit-dwellings were used exclusively as underground garbage dumps rather than living spaces.
  • All Neolithic cultures across the Indian subcontinent utilized pit-dwellings.
Exam points
  • Identification of Burzahom and Gufkral with pit-dwelling Neolithic traditions.
  • Climatic and environmental causes leading to subterranean architecture.
  • Associated material culture, including polished stone axes and bone tools.
Concept 4

Megalithic Burials and Associated Habitation

Iron Age mortuary monuments and settlement complexes that reflect complex social stratification, ancestor worship, and territorial tenure.

Details

  • Megalithic sites comprise both habitation areas and distinct monumental stone burial grounds located nearby.
  • Burial typologies include dolmens, cists, menhirs, urn burials, and rock-cut chambers constructed with massive stone blocks.
  • Habitation mounds associated with Megalithic builders yield iron implements, black-and-red ware pottery, and evidence of agro-pastoral economies.
  • The labor investment required for erecting megaliths indicates chiefdom-level social organization and communal coordination.
  • The spatial separation of burial sites from living sites highlights early ideological demarcations between the living and the dead.

Remember

  • Adichanallur in Tamil Nadu is a premier Iron Age Megalithic urn burial and habitation site.
  • Brahmagiri in Karnataka provides a stratigraphic sequence linking the Neolithic phase to the Iron Age Megalithic culture.
  • Maski in Karnataka yielded iron slag and evidence of smelting alongside megalithic remains.
  • Junapani in Vidarbha, Maharashtra, is notable for its stone circles and grave goods.
Examples & sources
  • Brahmagiri excavations conducted by Sir Mortimer Wheeler in 1947.
  • Porkalam in Kerala, featuring urn burials covered with terracotta sarcophagi.
Compare
  • Megalithic sites display extensive use of iron technology and specialized craft production compared to Chalcolithic settlements.
  • Burial architecture shifted from simple earthen pits to elaborate monumental stone structures.
Common confusions
  • Megaliths served exclusively as dwelling houses for the living.
  • All stone circles and monuments in India belong to the same historical century.
Exam points
  • Morthemet Wheeler's chronological framework using Brahmagiri excavations.
  • Geographical distribution of Megalithic sites in Peninsular India.
  • Association of Black and Red Ware (BRW) pottery with Iron Age Megalithic habitations.
Concept 5

Chalcolithic Village Communities

Copper-stone age sedentary farming settlements characterized by mud-brick houses, specialized craft production, and regional pottery traditions.

Details

  • Chalcolithic habitation sites marked the transition from nomadic pastoralism to permanent sedentary village farming communities.
  • Houses were typically rectangular or circular structures made of wattle-and-daub, mud bricks, and thatched roofs.
  • Settlements often featured specialized craft areas for bead-making, copper smelting, and pottery kilns.
  • Storage jars sunk into house floors indicate surplus agricultural production and domestic organization.
  • Regional cultural complexes developed distinct ceramic styles, such as Painted Grey Ware, Malwa Ware, and Jorwe Ware.

Remember

  • Inamgaon in Maharashtra provides an exceptionally well-excavated plan of a Chalcolithic village with fortification walls and granaries.
  • Ahar (Banas culture) in Rajasthan is known for stone-walled houses and copper metallurgy.
  • Navdatoli on the Narmada River in Madhya Pradesh reveals diverse crop cultivation including wheat, rice, and lentils.
  • Kayatha in Madhya Pradesh represents an early Chalcolithic culture with sturdy mud-floored houses.
Examples & sources
  • Inamgaon multi-room houses indicating nuclear and extended family structures.
  • Daimabad in Maharashtra, famous for its hoard of bronze artifacts and multi-period habitation.
Compare
  • Chalcolithic houses show advanced architectural planning compared to temporary Palaeolithic shelters.
  • Unlike Harappan urban centers, Chalcolithic settlements were rural village communities with limited metal use.
Common confusions
  • Chalcolithic people possessed advanced iron weapons and large urban administrative centers.
  • All Chalcolithic settlements had defensive fortifications and complex public drainage systems.
Exam points
  • Identification of Jorwe and Malwa cultural assemblages.
  • Significance of Inamgaon settlement layout and social hierarchy evidence.
  • Differentiating Chalcolithic rural village economies from Harappan urbanism.

Rapid revision

Concept 1

Natural Rock Shelters and Caves

Quick recall

  • Used during Lower and Upper Palaeolithic periods for primary shelter.
  • Provided natural defense against extreme weather and predators.
  • Often decorated with rock art reflecting cognitive and cultural developments.
  • Acted as long-term campsites and hubs for social interaction.

Memory aid

  • NRC: Nature Provides Caves for Lower/Upper Palaeolithic folks.

Review tips

  • Associate caves directly with Palaeolithic hunter-gatherer permanence.

Quick checks

  • If the question mentions primary protection against predators and harsh climates in early phases, think rock-shelters.

Watch for

  • Do not mistake natural caves for Neolithic subterranean pit-dwellings.
Concept 2

Open-Air River Valley Sites

Quick recall

  • Strategic encampments established by early hominins along river terraces.
  • Ensured continuous access to fresh water supplies.
  • Attracted diverse game animals for hunting subsistence.
  • Facilitated easy gathering of lithic raw materials for stone tools.

Memory aid

  • ORV: Open Rivers Vital for early hominins.

Review tips

  • Link river terraces with tool-making workshops and water access.

Quick checks

  • Look for 'river terraces' and 'lithic raw materials' keywords in settlement questions.

Watch for

  • Do not confuse river valley campsites with permanent sedentary Chalcolithic farming villages.
Concept 3

Pit-Dwellings and Subterranean Habitations

Quick recall

  • Subsurface excavation structures engineered by Neolithic communities.
  • Built primarily in cold geographical regions.
  • Provided enhanced thermal insulation during harsh winters.
  • Offered structural stability and protection against wind.

Memory aid

  • PDS: Pit Dwellings Save from Snow.

Review tips

  • Associate subterranean pits specifically with the Neolithic in colder zones like Kashmir.

Quick checks

  • Subterranean excavation + cold region = Neolithic pit-dwelling.

Watch for

  • Do not assign pit-dwellings to the Palaeolithic or Chalcolithic periods.
Concept 4

Megalithic Burials and Associated Habitation

Quick recall

  • Iron Age mortuary monuments combined with adjacent settlement complexes.
  • Reflected growing social stratification and status hierarchies.
  • Highlighted organized ancestor worship practices.
  • Indicated established territorial tenure and land ownership.

Memory aid

  • MBA: Megaliths Bring Ancestors, social stratification, and Iron Age tenure.

Review tips

  • Always connect megaliths with the Iron Age and territorial marking.

Quick checks

  • If the question discusses social stratification and large stone monuments together, check for Megalithic culture.

Watch for

  • Do not treat megaliths merely as tombs; they are closely tied to associated habitation and society.
Concept 5

Chalcolithic Village Communities

Quick recall

  • Copper-stone age sedentary farming settlements.
  • Characterized by structured mud-brick houses.
  • Engaged in specialized craft production and metallurgy.
  • Exhibited distinct regional pottery traditions (e.g., Painted Grey or Black-and-Red ware).

Memory aid

  • CVC: Copper-stone Village Communities craft ceramics.

Review tips

  • Remember that Chalcolithic marks the transition to sedentary village farming with copper use.

Quick checks

  • Mud-brick houses plus copper tools equals Chalcolithic.

Watch for

  • Do not mistake Chalcolithic villages for Harappan urban city-planning.

Quick facts

  • Natural rock-shelters were heavily utilized in the Lower and Upper Palaeolithic.
  • Open-air river valley sites provided easy access to lithic raw materials.
  • Pit-dwellings are a hallmark of Neolithic adaptations to cold climates.
  • Megalithic burials reflect Iron Age social stratification.
  • Chalcolithic communities relied on mud-brick architecture.
  • River terraces enabled early hominins to ambush game animals.
  • Megalithic sites signify early concepts of territorial tenure.
  • Chalcolithic village communities practiced specialized craft production.

Memory aids

  • N-O-P-M-C: Natural, Open, Pit, Megalithic, Chalcolithic (Evolution of Habitation).
  • Palaeolithic = Caves and Rivers; Neolithic = Pits; Iron Age = Megaliths; Copper Age = Mud-brick.
  • Thermal Pits Keep Neolithic Warm.

Review tips

  • Chronologically align habitation types from mobile shelters to settled mud-brick towns.
  • Pay close attention to environmental adaptations like pit-dwellings in cold zones.
  • Note the shift from temporary river camps to permanent sedentary villages.
  • Keep track of material culture markers like lithics, copper, and iron.

Question cues

  • Match climate/geography cues (cold region -> pit dwellings; river terrace -> open-air site).
  • Match social indicators (stratification/ancestor worship -> megaliths).
  • Match construction material (mud-brick -> chalcolithic village).
  • Eliminate options that attribute advanced metal use to Palaeolithic cave dwellers.

Compare

natural-rock-shelters-and-cavesvspit-dwellings-and-subterranean-habitations
  • Natural caves are geological formations; pit-dwellings are human-excavated subsurface structures.
  • Caves span Lower and Upper Palaeolithic; pit-dwellings are characteristic of Neolithic cold regions.
  • Caves offer surface protection; pit-dwellings offer enhanced thermal insulation.

Watch for

  • Confusing Palaeolithic cave art with Chalcolithic painted pottery traditions.
  • Assuming Megalithic monuments existed independently of human habitation sites.
  • Attributing mud-brick sedentary architecture to Lower Palaeolithic hominins.
  • Forgetting that open-air river valley sites served as lithic workshop raw material sources.
  • Misidentifying the primary survival purpose of Neolithic pit-dwellings as mere garbage pits rather than thermal insulation.
