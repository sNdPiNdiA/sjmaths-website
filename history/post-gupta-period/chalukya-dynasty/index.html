Post-Gupta Period

Chalukya Dynasty

Appears in: UP PGT History Tracker → UP TGT Social Science Tracker →

Topic overview

  • The Chalukya dynasty ruled large parts of southern and central India between the 6th and 12th centuries.
  • The dynasty is divided into three distinct branches: the Chalukyas of Badami, the Eastern Chalukyas of Vengi, and the Western Chalukyas of Kalyani.
  • Their reign marked a golden period in the history of the Deccan, witnessing remarkable developments in architecture, art, literature, and polity.
  • They successfully checked the northern expansion of Emperor Harsha of Kannauj and engaged in protracted power struggles with the Pallavas of Kanchi.
Concept 1

Origins and Chronology of the Badami Chalukyas

The foundation and early chronology of the imperial Chalukyas of Badami under Pulakeshin I.

Details

  • The dynasty was established by Pulakeshin I in 543 CE when he fortified Vatapi (modern Badami in Karnataka) and made it his capital.
  • Epigraphical records like the Aihole inscription of Pulakeshin II trace their lineage back to the Manu, Moon, or Harithiputras, though indigenous Kannada roots are historically accepted.
  • Pulakeshin I performed the Ashvamedha and other Vedic sacrifices to legitimize his newly acquired sovereign status.
  • The early rulers expanded their control from a small principality in northern Karnataka into a major imperial power dominating the Deccan plateau.
  • The rule of the Badami branch lasted for nearly two centuries until the Rashtrakutas overthrew them in the mid-8th century.

Remember

  • Founder: Pulakeshin I (543 CE)
  • Capital: Vatapi (Badami)
  • Major Source: Aihole Inscription (634 CE)
  • Terminal Year: 753 CE (overthrown by Dantidurga)
Examples & sources
  • Fortification of Vatapi by Pulakeshin I
  • Assumption of titles like Satyashraya by early monarchs
Compare
  • Unlike the Kadambas whom they initially served or displaced, the Chalukyas built a pan-Deccan empire.
  • Unlike the Guptas who patronized Sanskrit predominantly, the Chalukyas also fostered early Kannada language inscriptions.
Common confusions
  • Many students confuse the Badami Chalukyas with the later Kalyani Chalukyas, ignoring the intervening Rashtrakuta period.
  • It is incorrect to assume they were northern invaders; they were native Deccan chieftains.
Exam points
  • Date of establishment of Badami branch
  • Significance of Pulakeshin I
  • Geographical location of Vatapi
Concept 2

Pulakeshin II and Imperial Expansion

The greatest ruler of the Badami Chalukyas and his military triumphs across India.

Details

  • Pulakeshin II (reigned 609-642 CE) was the most prominent monarch of the Chalukya dynasty.
  • His exploits and military campaigns are meticulously detailed in the famous Aihole Inscription composed by his court poet Ravikirti.
  • He successfully halted the northward march of Harshavardhana of Kannauj on the banks of the river Narmada around 630 CE.
  • He defeated the Pallava king Mahendravarman I and pushed deep into the Tamil country, besieging Kanchi itself.
  • He established a collateral branch of the dynasty in Vengi for his brother Kubja Vishnuvardhana around 624 CE.
  • His reign marked the zenith of Chalukyan power, attracting foreign visitors like the Chinese pilgrim Xuanzang (Hiuen Tsang).

Remember

  • Reign Period: 609 - 642 CE
  • Court Poet: Ravikirti (composer of Aihole Inscription)
  • Key Victory: Defeat of Harsha on the Narmada banks
  • Foreign Visitor: Xuanzang
Examples & sources
  • Battle of Narmada against Harshavardhana
  • Creation of the Eastern Chalukya branch at Vengi
Compare
  • Unlike Harsha who unified the north, Pulakeshin II successfully unified and defended the Deccan.
  • Unlike his conflict with Mahendravarman I, Pulakeshin faced severe setbacks later against Narasimhavarman I Pallava.
Common confusions
  • Pulakeshin II did not permanently annex Kannauj; he only defeated Harsha and established the Narmada river as the boundary.
  • Xuanzang visited the Chalukya kingdom and praised the valor of the king and the honesty of the people, not the Gupta empire.
Exam points
  • Author and language of Aihole Inscription
  • Outcome of the Narmada expedition
  • Relations with Pallava rulers Mahendravarman I and Narasimhavarman I
Concept 3

The Chalukya-Pallava Conflict

The prolonged dynastic struggle for supremacy in peninsular India between Badami and Kanchi.

Details

  • The political landscape of 7th and 8th century peninsular India was dominated by the bitter rivalry between the Chalukyas of Badami and the Pallavas of Kanchi.
  • The conflict was driven by geopolitical ambitions, control over fertile river valleys, and lucrative international trade routes.
  • Pulakeshin II initially triumphed over Mahendravarman I, capturing northern Pallava territories up to the Kaveri river.
  • The Pallava king Narasimhavarman I (Mamalla) severely avenged his father's defeat, invaded Vatapi, and killed Pulakeshin II around 642 CE.
  • Narasimhavarman I assumed the title Vatapikonda (conqueror of Vatapi) after sacking the Chalukyan capital.
  • Vikramaditya I of the Chalukyas later revived dynastic fortunes, recaptured Vatapi, and temporarily occupied Kanchi.

Remember

  • Key Arena: Northern Tamil Nadu and Southern Karnataka borderlands
  • Key Pallava Rulers: Mahendravarman I, Narasimhavarman I, Paramesvaravarman I
  • Key Chalukya Rulers: Pulakeshin II, Vikramaditya I, Vinaditya
  • Turning Point: Sacking of Vatapi in 642 CE by Narasimhavarman I
Examples & sources
  • Battle of Manimangalam
  • Capture of Vatapi by Narasimhavarman I
Compare
  • Chalukya military campaigns relied heavily on fierce cavalry and infantry maneuvers across plateau terrain.
  • Pallava military strategy utilized strong naval capabilities alongside infantry and elephant corps.
Common confusions
  • The conflict was not purely religious (Saivism vs Vaishnavism); both dynasties patronized multiple sects harmoniously.
  • The fall of Vatapi in 642 CE did not permanently end the Chalukya dynasty; it experienced a robust revival under Vikramaditya I.
Exam points
  • Title of Vatapikonda and its bearer
  • Circumstances surrounding the death of Pulakeshin II
  • Role of Vikramaditya I in restoring Chalukya power
Concept 4

Chalukyan Architecture and Art (Vesara Style)

The pioneering structural temple architecture and rock-cut traditions of the Badami Chalukyas.

Details

  • The Chalukyas are credited with laying the foundation of the Vesara style (Hybrid style) of temple architecture, combining Nagara and Dravida elements.
  • Their architectural evolution transitioned from rock-cut cave temples to fully evolved structural stone temples.
  • Badami features four remarkable rock-cut cave temples: three Hindu (Brahmanical) and one Jain, dated to the 6th century CE.
  • Aihole, known as the cradle of Indian temple architecture, contains over 70 temples including the Ladh Khan temple and Meguti Jain temple.
  • Pattadakal, a UNESCO World Heritage site, showcases the culmination of Early Chalukyan architecture with temples built by both Chalukya and Rashtrakuta patrons.
  • Notable temples at Pattadakal include the Virupaksha Temple (built by Queen Lokamahadevi) and the Mallikarjuna Temple, emulating the Kailashnath temple of Kanchi.

Remember

  • Architectural Style: Vesara (Deccan style)
  • Key Sites: Badami, Aihole, Pattadakal
  • UNESCO Status: Pattadakal designated in 1987
  • Prominent Temple: Virupaksha Temple at Pattadakal
Examples & sources
  • Cave Temple No. 3 at Badami dedicated to Vishnu
  • Durga Temple at Aihole featuring a apsidal-absidal layout
Compare
  • Unlike Gupta brick architecture, Chalukyas extensively utilized red sandstone for monolithic and structural masonry.
  • Unlike Pallava monolithic rathas at Mahabalipuram, Chalukyas focused on structural stone masonry early on, as seen in Pattadakal.
Common confusions
  • Aihole is not a single temple but a vast complex containing experimental temple designs of the early period.
  • The Virupaksha temple at Pattadakal was built by a Chalukya queen, not by the later Vijayanagara emperors who built a temple of the same name.
Exam points
  • Identification of Aihole as the 'Cradle of Temple Architecture'
  • Architectural features of the Vesara style
  • Patronage of Virupaksha Temple at Pattadakal
Concept 5

Administration, Society, and Economy

The decentralized administrative machinery, social structure, and thriving agrarian-commercial economy.

Details

  • The Chalukyan administrative system was monarchical and highly centralized at the core, but heavily dependent on feudal chiefs and local assemblies at the periphery.
  • The empire was divided into provinces (Rashtra or Mandalam), districts (Vishaya), sub-districts (Bhukti), and villages (Grama).
  • Village autonomy was a prominent feature, with village elders (Mahajanas or Gramamahattaras) managing local affairs, land revenue, and justice.
  • Epigraphic records indicate high status for women in administration; royal women like Vijayabhattarika acted as provincial governors.
  • The economy was robust, supported by intensive agriculture, thriving craft guilds (Manigramam), and extensive maritime and inland trade.
  • The Chalukyas maintained a powerful navy that dominated the Arabian Sea and controlled important ports along the western coast.

Remember

  • Administrative Units: Rashtra -> Vishaya -> Bhukti -> Grama
  • Key Local Body: Mahajanas (assembly of learned Brahmins in agraharas)
  • Trade Guilds: Prominent presence of merchant guilds handling domestic and foreign commerce
  • Naval Power: Control of western coastal ports enabling overseas trade with Southeast Asia and West Asia
Examples & sources
  • Queen Vijayabhattarika issuing copper-plate grants as a regional administrator
  • Agrarian grants made to Brahmins in fertile river valleys like the Tungabhadra doab
Compare
  • Unlike the purely land-locked Gupta administration, the Chalukyas maintained a formidable naval fleet to safeguard oceanic trade.
  • Unlike northern feudal models, southern village assemblies (Mahajanas) possessed greater autonomy in land management.
Common confusions
  • The feudal system under the Chalukyas did not mean absolute lawlessness; central oversight remained strong under energetic monarchs.
  • Society was not exclusively orthodox; Jainism and Buddhism enjoyed considerable royal patronage and merchant support alongside Hinduism.
Exam points
  • Hierarchy of administrative divisions
  • Role of Mahajanas and local assemblies
  • Significance of Chalukyan naval supremacy
Concept 6

Western Chalukyas of Kalyani

The revival of Chalukyan power in the 10th century under Tailapa II and their cultural contributions.

Details

  • The imperial line was revived in 973 CE by Tailapa II, a feudatory of the Rashtrakutas, establishing the Western Chalukya dynasty of Kalyani.
  • They are frequently referred to in historiography as the Later Chalukyas of Kalyani (modern Basavakalyan in Karnataka).
  • Vikramaditya VI (reigned 1076-1126 CE) was their greatest ruler, whose accession marked the beginning of the Chalukya-Vikrama era.
  • His court was adorned by illustrious scholars like Bilhana (author of Vikramankadevacharita) and Vijnaneshwara (author of Mitakshara).
  • They engaged in intense, continuous warfare with the Cholas of Thanjavur and the Hoysalas of Halebidu.
  • The dynasty finally disintegrated towards the end of the 12th century, paving the way for the rise of the Hoysalas, Yadavas, and Kakatiyas.

Remember

  • Founder of Kalyani Branch: Tailapa II (973 CE)
  • Capital: Kalyani
  • Greatest Monarch: Vikramaditya VI (Chalukya-Vikrama Era)
  • Notable Literary Figures: Bilhana and Vijnaneshwara
Examples & sources
  • Composition of Mitakshara, the authoritative legal treatise on Hindu law of inheritance
  • Construction of elaborate temples at Lakkundi, Itagi, and Gadag
Compare
  • Unlike the Badami Chalukyas who fought primarily against the Pallavas, the Kalyani Chalukyas were locked in a century-long struggle against the imperial Cholas.
  • The Kalyani branch witnessed the rise of the Lingayat movement (Virasaivism), which was absent during the early Badami phase.
Common confusions
  • The Kalyani Chalukyas are not a direct uninterrupted continuation of the Badami branch; there is a gap of over two centuries filled by the Rashtrakutas.
  • Mitakshara was composed during this era, not during the Gupta period.
Exam points
  • Founder Tailapa II and the overthrow of Rashtrakutas
  • Works of Bilhana and Vijnaneshwara
  • Key rivals: Imperial Cholas under Rajaraja I and Rajendra I

Rapid revision

Concept 1

Origins and Chronology of the Badami Chalukyas

Quick recall

  • Founded by Pulakeshin I in the 6th century CE, establishing Vatapi (Badami) as the capital.
  • Pulakeshin I performed the Ashvamedha sacrifice, marking sovereign imperial status.
  • Kirtivarman I expanded territories, subduing the Mauryas of Konkan and Kadambas of Banavasi.
  • Mangalesha consolidated rule further, constructing the magnificent Cave Temple III at Badami.

Memory aid

  • PKM: Pulakeshin, Kirtivarman, Mangalesha (First Three Builders of Vatapi)

Review tips

  • Associate Pulakeshin I directly with the fortifying of Vatapi and sovereign sacrifices.

Quick checks

  • Remember that Mangalesha came after Kirtivarman I, acting as regent before his nephew Pulakeshin II took over.

Watch for

  • Do not confuse Pulakeshin I (founder) with Pulakeshin II (the greatest imperial expander).
Concept 2

Pulakeshin II and Imperial Expansion

Quick recall

  • Reigned from 610 to 642 CE; undisputed greatest ruler of the Badami Chalukyas.
  • Defeated Harshavardhana on the banks of the Narmada River, halting his southern expansion.
  • Defeated Pallava king Mahendravarman I and pushed Pallavas up to the Kaveri River.
  • Extensively documented in the Aihole Inscription, composed by his court poet Ravikirti.

Memory aid

  • NHH: Narmada Halt Harsha

Review tips

  • Focus on Aihole Inscription verses for specific Sanskrit literary clues and dates.

Quick checks

  • Whenever you see Aihole Inscription, instantly link it to Ravikirti praising Pulakeshin II.

Watch for

  • Pulakeshin II defeated Harsha, but he was ultimately defeated and killed by Narasimhavarman I Pallava.
Concept 3

The Chalukya-Pallava Conflict

Quick recall

  • Centuries-long dynastic struggle for supremacy in peninsular India between Badami and Kanchi.
  • Pulakeshin II initially defeated Mahendravarman I and besieged Kanchi.
  • Narasimhavarman I (Pallava) retaliated, invaded Vatapi, and killed Pulakeshin II around 642 CE.
  • Narasimhavarman I assumed the title 'Vatapikonda' (Conqueror of Vatapi) after sacking the capital.

Memory aid

  • NVP: Narasimhavarman Victors Pulakeshin

Review tips

  • Keep track of the tit-for-tat retaliation between Badami and Kanchi kings.

Quick checks

  • Match suffix '-konda' with Pallava victories (Vatapikonda = conquered Vatapi).

Watch for

  • Do not assume Pulakeshin II won all battles against the Pallavas; his reign ended with a disastrous defeat at Vatapi.
Concept 4

Chalukyan Architecture and Art (Vesara Style)

Quick recall

  • Pioneered the Vesara architectural style, a synthesis of Nagara and Dravida styles.
  • Famous rock-cut cave temples at Badami feature exquisite sculpture (Vishnu, Shiva, Jain Tirthankaras).
  • Structural temples were pioneered at Aihole (known as the cradle of Indian temple architecture, e.g., Lad Khan temple).
  • Magnificent temples at Pattadakal (UNESCO World Heritage Site), combining both northern and southern styles.

Memory aid

  • APP: Aihole, Pattadakal, Badami (Architecture Triad)

Review tips

  • Remember Aihole = Cradle of temple architecture; Pattadakal = Climax of Vesara experimentation.

Quick checks

  • Vesara = Vertical blend of Nagara base/shikhara elements with Dravidian layout hallmarks.

Watch for

  • Do not attribute Vesara style exclusively to later dynasties like the Hoysalas; it originated with the Chalukyas of Badami.
Concept 5

Administration, Society, and Economy

Quick recall

  • Decentralized administrative machinery with considerable autonomy given to village assemblies and local bodies.
  • Monarchy was hereditary, but succession disputes were common; royal queens actively participated in governance.
  • Thriving agrarian economy supported by extensive irrigation works and diverse crop cultivation.
  • Vibrant commercial network with active maritime trade and powerful merchant guilds operating across regions.

Memory aid

  • DAG: Decentralized Administration, Guilds, Agriculture

Review tips

  • Note the high status of women in administrative roles compared to other contemporary North Indian dynasties.

Quick checks

  • Look for references to local village autonomy when questions mention southern decentralized polities.

Watch for

  • Do not mistake Chalukyan administration for a purely centralized autocracy like the Mauryan state.
Concept 6

Western Chalukyas of Kalyani

Quick recall

  • Revival of Chalukyan power in the late 10th century CE, founded by Tailapa II in 973 CE.
  • Capital shifted to Kalyani (modern Basavakalyan in Karnataka).
  • Engaged in perpetual tripartite-style conflicts with the Cholas of Thanjavur and Paramaras of Malwa.
  • Fostered immense literary growth, patronizing Kannada and Sanskrit scholars like Bilhana and Vijnaneshwara.

Memory aid

  • TBV: Tailapa, Bilhana, Vijnaneshwara

Review tips

  • Always associate Vijnaneshwara (author of Mitakshara) with the Western Chalukyas of Kalyani.

Quick checks

  • Kalyani = Later Chalukyas; Badami = Early Chalukyas. Never mix up Tailapa II with Pulakeshin I.

Watch for

  • Do not confuse the Western Chalukyas of Kalyani with the Eastern Chalukyas of Vengi (who were a collateral branch).

Quick facts

  • Pulakeshin I founded the Badami Chalukya dynasty in the 6th century CE.
  • Vatapi (modern Badami) served as the primary capital of the early Chalukyas.
  • Pulakeshin II defeated Harshavardhana on the banks of the Narmada River.
  • The Aihole Inscription was written by the Sanskrit poet Ravikirti.
  • Hiuen Tsang visited the court of Pulakeshin II and praised his administration.
  • Narasimhavarman I captured Vatapi and earned the title Vatapikonda.
  • Pattadakal features a group of temples built in both Nagara and Dravida styles representing the Vesara tradition.
  • Tailapa II overthrew the Rashtrakutas in 973 CE to establish the Western Chalukyas of Kalyani.

Memory aids

  • VAP: Vatapi, Aihole, Pattadakal (Key Chalukyan Sites)
  • PNH: Pulakeshin defeats Narmada Harsha
  • KVP: Kirtivarman, Vikramaditya, Pulakeshin

Review tips

  • Create a chronological timeline separating Badami Chalukyas from Kalyani Chalukyas.
  • Memorize architectural terms like Vesara, Nagara, and Dravida with corresponding structural examples.
  • Link royal titles like Vatapikonda directly to Pallava-Chalukya military conflicts.
  • Pay close attention to literary works like Mitakshara and Aihole Inscriptions in match-the-following questions.

Question cues

  • If a question mentions Narmada river and Harsha defeat, the answer is always Pulakeshin II.
  • If a question asks about the Mitakshara legal text, look for the Kalyani Chalukya ruler Vikramaditya VI.
  • Aihole is universally known as the laboratory of Indian stone temple architecture.
  • Capital shifts: Vatapi (Badami) for early branch, Kalyani for later branch.

Compare

Badami ChalukyasvsWestern Chalukyas of Kalyani
  • Ruled from the 6th to 8th century CE, whereas Kalyani Chalukyas ruled from the late 10th to 12th century CE.
  • Capital was Vatapi (Badami) for the early branch and Kalyani for the later branch.
  • Major conflict was with the Pallavas of Kanchi for the early branch, and the Cholas of Thanjavur for the later branch.

Watch for

  • Confusing Pulakeshin I (founder) with Pulakeshin II (imperial conqueror).
  • Attributing the Aihole Inscription to Pulakeshin II himself instead of his court poet Ravikirti.
  • Believing the Chalukyas were completely destroyed forever after Narasimhavarman I sacked Vatapi.
  • Mixing up the Eastern Chalukyas of Vengi with the Western Chalukyas of Kalyani.
  • Misidentifying the architectural style of Pattadakal temples as purely Dravidian.
