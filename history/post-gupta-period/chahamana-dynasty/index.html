Post-Gupta Period

Chahamana Dynasty

Appears in: UP PGT History Tracker → UP TGT Social Science Tracker →

Topic overview

  • The Chahamanas, commonly known as the Chauhans, were a prominent Rajput ruling clan in Northern and Western India during the post-Gupta and early medieval periods.
  • Their original territorial base was Sapadalaksha, corresponding to the region around modern Sambhar, Shakambhari, and Ajmer in Rajasthan.
  • They initially served as feudatories of the Gurjara-Pratiharas before establishing an independent and expansive imperial power in the 11th and 12th centuries.
  • The dynasty reached its political zenith under Vigraharaja IV and Prithviraja III (Prithviraj Chauhan), playing a pivotal role in the resistance against early Islamic invasions.
Concept 1

Origins and Early Rulers of Shakambhari

The foundation of the Chahamana dynasty and their early political evolution as feudatories.

Details

  • According to the Prithvirajaraso and certain epigraphical sources, the dynasty originated from the Agnikunda myth at Mount Abu.
  • Epigraphical evidence, such as the Bijolia rock inscription, traces their lineage to the solar race and names Vasudeva as the founder in the 6th century CE.
  • The early Chahamanas ruled as local chieftains and feudatories of the imperial Gurjara-Pratiharas of Kannauj.
  • Simharaja was the first notable ruler to assume the imperial title of Maharajadhiraja, marking the assertion of autonomy.
  • Ajayaraja II shifted the political center of gravity by founding the city of Ajayameru, modern Ajmer, and establishing a mint.

Remember

  • Founder: Vasudeva Chahamana (6th Century CE)
  • Early Capital: Shakambhari (modern Sambhar near Sambhar Salt Lake)
  • Key Inscription: Bijolia Rock Inscription (1170 CE) detailing genealogy
  • Key Shift: Foundation of Ajayameru (Ajmer) by Ajayaraja II
Examples & sources
  • Vasudeva establishing the Shakambhari branch
  • Ajayaraja II minting silver coins bearing his queen's name, Somaladevi
Compare
  • Early Chahamanas started as subordinate feudatories unlike the independent Paramaras of Malwa in their initial phases.
  • Unlike Sambhar, Ajmer was developed as a fortified hill capital to secure trade routes.
Common confusions
  • Prithviraj Chauhan was not the founder of the dynasty.
  • The Agnikunda origin is a later bardic myth and not supported by contemporary stone inscriptions.
Exam points
  • Identify the founder as per the Bijolia inscription.
  • Name of the ruler who founded Ajmer.
  • Feudatory status under the Gurjara-Pratiharas.
Concept 2

Imperial Expansion under Vigraharaja IV

The transformation of the Chahamanas into a major imperial power through aggressive conquests.

Details

  • Vigraharaja IV, also known as Visaladeva, ruled from 1150 to 1164 CE and was the most powerful Chauhan ruler before Prithviraja III.
  • He wrested control of Delhi and the Siwalik hills from the Tomaras, greatly expanding the northern boundaries of the kingdom.
  • He successfully raided Ghaznavid territories and checked the advance of the Muslim governors of Punjab.
  • He was a patron of literature and authored the Sanskrit play Harakeli Drama, which was inscribed on stone slabs.
  • His court poet Palkirti composed the Prasasti inscribed on the Ashokan pillar relocated to Delhi.

Remember

  • Reign Period: c. 1150–1164 CE
  • Major Conquest: Annexation of Delhi from the Tomaras
  • Literary Work: Harakeli Nataka (inscribed at Ajmer)
  • Architectural Contribution: Construction of the Visalapura town and reservoir
Examples & sources
  • Inscribing the Harakeli drama on the walls of the Sarasvati Mandira in Ajmer
  • Capturing Delhi and erecting a victory pillar in the Siwalik region
Compare
  • Vigraharaja IV pursued aggressive imperialism unlike his immediate predecessors who focused on regional consolidation.
  • His cultural patronage matched that of Paramara Bhoja in scale and sophistication.
Common confusions
  • Delhi was not first captured by Prithviraj III; it was integrated into the Chauhan realm by Vigraharaja IV.
  • The Harakeli drama is distinct from the later Sanskrit inscriptions of the Adhai Din Ka Jhonpra.
Exam points
  • Author of Harakeli Nataka.
  • Conqueror of Delhi from the Tomara Rajputs.
  • Extent of the empire during the mid-12th century.
Concept 3

Prithviraja III and Military Campaigns

The reign of Rai Pithora, marked by dramatic conflicts with regional rivals and the Ghurids.

Details

  • Prithviraja III ascended the throne around 1177 CE at a very young age and inherited a vast empire.
  • He engaged in a prolonged military conflict with Paramardin, the Chandela ruler of Jejakabhukti, resulting in the Chauhan raid of Mahoba in 1182 CE.
  • His elopement with Samyukta, the daughter of Jayachandra of Kannauj, created a deep political rift among northern Rajput powers.
  • He maintained an aggressive expansionist policy against the Chaulukyas of Gujarat, though initial campaigns ended inconclusively.
  • He faced the invading forces of Muizz al-Din Muhammad Ghuri in the concluding phase of his reign.

Remember

  • Alternative Title: Rai Pithora
  • Major Conflict (1182 CE): Battle of Mahoba against the Chandelas
  • Rivalry: Jayachandra of Kannauj (Gahadavala dynasty)
  • Primary Adversary: Muizz al-Din Muhammad Ghuri
Examples & sources
  • The defeat of Alha and Udal, the legendary Chandela commanders, during the Mahoba campaign
  • The legendary accounts of the Swayamvara of Samyukta recorded in Chand Bardai's Prithvirajaraso
Compare
  • Prithviraja III relied heavily on charismatic leadership and swift cavalry warfare unlike the bureaucratic centralization of the Guptas.
  • His foreign policy alienated key contemporary powers like the Gahadavalas, preventing a unified Hindu front against Ghuri.
Common confusions
  • Jayachandra of Kannauj did not invite Muhammad Ghuri to invade India, as modern historiography disputes this late medieval bardic claim.
  • Prithviraja III did not rule over an empire spanning the entire Indian subcontinent.
Exam points
  • The year and outcome of the Chandela-Chauhan conflict at Mahoba.
  • Relationship with the Gahadavala dynasty of Kannauj.
  • Chronology of accession and territorial reach.
Concept 4

The Battles of Tarain and Their Aftermath

The watershed military encounters between Prithviraja III and Muhammad Ghuri that altered Indian history.

Details

  • The First Battle of Tarain was fought in 1191 CE, where Prithviraja III decisively defeated Muhammad Ghuri.
  • Following the first battle, Prithviraja failed to pursue the retreating Ghurid army or fortify the Punjab frontier effectively.
  • The Second Battle of Tarain took place in 1192 CE, featuring superior Ghurid cavalry tactics, archery, and strategic planning.
  • Prithviraja III was captured and executed following his defeat in the second battle, marking the collapse of Chauhan central authority.
  • The defeat at Tarain opened the Gangetic plain to Turkish invasions and led to the rapid establishment of the Delhi Sultanate.

Remember

  • First Tarain (1191 CE): Chauhan victory; Ghuri wounded
  • Second Tarain (1192 CE): Ghurid victory; decisive turning point
  • Tactical Advantage: Ghurid use of mobile archers and swift cavalry units
  • Immediate Consequence: Annexation of Ajmer and establishment of Turkish suzerainty
Examples & sources
  • Govindaraja, the son of Prithviraj, being installed as a nominal vassal at Ajmer under Ghurid overlordship
  • Qutb-ud-din Aibak subsequently consolidating control over northern India
Compare
  • Tarain II differed from earlier Indian defeats by foreign invaders due to its permanent institutional and political fallout.
  • Unlike the Hun invasions successfully repelled by Skandagupta, the Ghurid incursions succeeded due to fragmented regional resistance.
Common confusions
  • Prithviraj was not blinded prior to the battle as claimed in the romanticized epic poetry, but captured and killed.
  • The defeat was not solely due to lack of bravery, but due to outdated tactical deployment against Turko-Persian composite bowmen.
Exam points
  • Exact dates of the First and Second Battles of Tarain (1191 and 1192 CE).
  • Strategic reasons for the Chauhan defeat in the second battle.
  • Historical impact of Tarain on the political map of India.
Concept 5

Society, Economy, and Cultural Patronage

The socio-cultural fabric, temple architecture, and literary advancements under Chauhan rule.

Details

  • Society was structured along rigid caste lines, with the dominance of the Brahmanical order and the rise of the Rajput warrior aristocracy.
  • Feudalism was deeply entrenched, with military commanders and nobles holding landed assignments (jagirs/bhugas) in lieu of service.
  • Trade and commerce flourished, facilitated by stable coinage systems and trade routes connecting Gujarat ports with northern markets.
  • Religious pluralism prevailed, with patronage extended to both Shaivism, Vaishnavism, and Jainism by the royal court.
  • Ajmer and Delhi emerged as prominent centers of Sanskrit learning, drama, and monumental temple architecture.

Remember

  • Key Religious Patronage: Jain scholars like Jinadatta Suri received royal support.
  • Literary Figures: Chand Bardai (author of Prithvirajaraso), Somadeva (author of Lalitavigraharaja)
  • Architectural Monuments: Sarasvati Mandira in Ajmer, later converted into Adhai Din Ka Jhonpra
  • Economic Basis: Agriculture supplemented by lucrative inland trade routes
Examples & sources
  • Construction of the Sanskrit college at Ajmer by Vigraharaja IV
  • Jain temples built at Ranthambore and Nadol under Chauhan patronage
Compare
  • Chauhan temple architecture shared stylistic similarities with contemporary Solanki (Chaulukya) architecture in Gujarat.
  • Literary output under the Chauhans leaned heavily toward secular court poetry and historical epics compared to purely theological texts.
Common confusions
  • The Adhai Din Ka Jhonpra was originally a Hindu/Jain educational institution, not a mosque built from scratch.
  • Rajput society was not entirely uniform; clan alliances and rivalries dictated political survival.
Exam points
  • Original purpose of the building converted into Adhai Din Ka Jhonpra.
  • Authors and literary texts associated with the Chauhan court.
  • Economic features of Rajasthan under early medieval feudalism.
Concept 6

The Chahamanas of Ranthambore

The survival and resistance of the Chauhan cadet branch in Ranthambore against the Delhi Sultanate.

Details

  • Following the defeat of Prithviraja III, his descendants established a cadet branch of the dynasty in the impregnable fort of Ranthambore.
  • Govindaraja, a son of Prithviraj, accepted nominal Ghurid suzerainty to save his clan before breaking away.
  • The branch reached its peak under Hammiradeva Chauhan, who ruled from 1283 to 1301 CE.
  • Hammiradeva pursued an aggressive policy of digvijaya, defeating neighboring rulers and offering asylum to rebellious Mongol nobles (New Muslims).
  • His defiance led to the historic Siege of Ranthambore by Alauddin Khalji in 1301 CE.

Remember

  • Founder of Branch: Govindaraja (c. 1194 CE)
  • Most Famous Ruler: Hammiradeva (Hammir Chauhan)
  • Key Conflict: Siege of Ranthambore (1301 CE) against Alauddin Khalji
  • Primary Source: Hammiramahakavya by Nayachandra Suri
Examples & sources
  • Hammiradeva granting asylum to Muhammad Shah, a fleeing Mongol commander
  • The performance of Jauhar by the women of the fort during the 1301 siege
Compare
  • The Ranthambore Chauhans maintained a fierce tradition of independence against the Khalji imperialism unlike contemporary northern vassals.
  • Unlike the main Shakambhari branch, the Ranthambore branch operated in a geopolitical landscape dominated by the centralized Delhi Sultanate.
Common confusions
  • The Ranthambore branch was not extinguished in 1192 CE; it flourished independently for over a century.
  • Hammiradeva's conflict with Alauddin Khalji was driven by geopolitical sanctuary principles rather than purely religious disputes.
Exam points
  • Name of the author of Hammiramahakavya.
  • Reasons for the 1301 Siege of Ranthambore by Alauddin Khalji.
  • Founder of the Ranthambore Chauhan branch.

Rapid revision

Concept 1

Origins and Early Rulers of Shakambhari

Quick recall

  • Founded by Vasudeva in the 6th century CE in the Sapadalaksha region near Shakambhari (Sambhar).
  • Initially ruled as feudatories of regional imperial powers like the Gurjara-Pratiharas.
  • Gradually asserted sovereignty and expanded territorial influence in Rajputana.
  • Established strategic alliances and matrimonial ties to secure their early borders.

Memory aid

  • Vasu Shakambhar: Vasudeva founded Shakambhari.

Review tips

  • Remember that their initial political status was subordinate (feudatory) before becoming imperial.

Quick checks

  • If a question asks for the original home base of the Chauhans, immediately look for Shakambhari or Sapadalaksha.

Watch for

  • Confusing their early feudatory status with immediate imperial sovereignty from the 6th century.
Concept 2

Imperial Expansion under Vigraharaja IV

Quick recall

  • Vigraharaja IV transformed the Chahamanas from local feudatories into a major imperial power.
  • Captured Delhi from the Tomaras, extending frontiers up to the Siwalik hills.
  • A great patron of letters, he authored the drama Harikeli Nataka.
  • Constructed the Saraswati Temple in Ajmer, later converted into Adhai Din Ka Jhonpra by Qutb-ud-din Aibak.

Memory aid

  • Vigraharaja = Victorious, Valiant, Versatile (conqueror, builder, author).

Review tips

  • Associate Vigraharaja IV specifically with the cultural golden age of Ajmer and the capture of Delhi.

Quick checks

  • Link 'Harikeli Nataka' instantly to Vigraharaja IV in matching columns.

Watch for

  • Attributing the construction of Adhai Din Ka Jhonpra as an original Islamic monument rather than a converted temple.
Concept 3

Prithviraja III and Military Campaigns

Quick recall

  • Popularly known as Rai Pithora, he ascended the throne of Ajmer and Delhi.
  • Engaged in intense conflicts with regional rivals like Paramardin of Jejakabhukti (Chandelas).
  • Fought Jaichand of Kannauj, popularized by romantic and political legends involving Samyogita.
  • Faced the invading forces of Shihab-ud-din Muhammad Ghuri on the northwestern frontier.

Memory aid

  • PRP: Prithviraja Rai Pithora.

Review tips

  • Differentiate between his domestic/regional rivalries (Chandelas, Gahadavalas) and his foreign threat (Ghurids).

Quick checks

  • Remember that Chandela generals Alha and Udal fought against Prithviraja III in the battle of Mahoba.

Watch for

  • Assuming Prithviraja III maintained a united front of all Rajput rulers against Muhammad Ghuri.
Concept 4

The Battles of Tarain and Their Aftermath

Quick recall

  • First Battle of Tarain (1191 CE): Prithviraja III decisively defeated Muhammad Ghuri.
  • Second Battle of Tarain (1192 CE): Muhammad Ghuri returned with better tactics and defeated Prithviraja III.
  • Marked a watershed moment in Indian history, opening the gateway for Turkish-Islamic rule in North India.
  • Led to the rapid collapse of Chauhan power in Ajmer and Delhi following the execution/defeat of Prithviraja.

Memory aid

  • Tarain One: Chauhan won; Tarain Two: Ghuri through (victory).

Review tips

  • Memorize the exact years (1191 and 1192 CE) as they are standard chronological anchors in medieval Indian history.

Quick checks

  • Always attribute the strategic turning point of 1192 CE to superior Turkish cavalry mobility and archery tactics.

Watch for

  • Believing that Prithviraja won both battles or that the Ghurid threat vanished after 1191.
Concept 5

Society, Economy, and Cultural Patronage

Quick recall

  • Socio-cultural fabric characterized by feudal hierarchies and prominent military aristocracy.
  • Thriving temple architecture, seen in the monuments of Ajmer and surrounding Rajputana.
  • Literary patronage flourished, housing scholars and dramatists in royal courts.
  • Economic life sustained by agrarian production, internal trade networks, and regional commerce.

Memory aid

  • S-E-C-T: Society, Economy, Culture, Temples.

Review tips

  • Note that Chauhan patronage extended to both Sanskrit literature and architectural marvels.

Quick checks

  • Link Sanskrit dramas like Harikeli Nataka and Somadeva's work directly to the intellectual climate of the Chauhan court.

Watch for

  • Overlooking the robust mercantile and urban economy that funded extensive temple construction.
Concept 6

The Chahamanas of Ranthambore

Quick recall

  • Cadet branch of the Chauhan dynasty established in Ranthambore after the fall of Shakambhari/Ajmer.
  • Maintained fierce resistance against the expansionist drives of the Delhi Sultanate.
  • Most famous ruler was Hammiradeva, known for his valor and adherence to royal prestige (Sharanaagat Vatsala).
  • Eventually subjugated by Ala-ud-din Khilji after a protracted and legendary siege.

Memory aid

  • Ranthambore Resistance: RR.

Review tips

  • Keep Ranthambore distinct from the main Ajmer branch; it represents the later medieval phase of Chauhan struggle against the Sultanate.

Quick checks

  • If a question mentions Hammiradeva and Ala-ud-din Khilji's siege, the correct branch is Ranthambore.

Watch for

  • Confusing Hammiradeva of Ranthambore with Prithviraja III of Ajmer.

Quick facts

  • The Chanamana dynasty was founded by Vasudeva in the 6th century CE.
  • Their original capital region was Shakambhari near Sambhar Lake.
  • Vigraharaja IV captured Delhi from the Tomaras.
  • Vigraharaja IV authored the Sanskrit play Harikeli Nataka.
  • Prithviraja III is popularly known in folklore as Rai Pithora.
  • The First Battle of Tarain took place in 1191 CE.
  • The Second Battle of Tarain took place in 1192 CE.
  • The Ranthambore branch of Chahamanas was famously led by Hammiradeva against Ala-ud-din Khilji.

Memory aids

  • Vasu-Vigraj-Prithvi: The chronological progression of key Chauhan stalwarts (Vasudeva -> Vigraharaja IV -> Prithviraja III).
  • Tarain One-Two: 91 won, 92 undone.
  • Ajmer to Ranthambore: Main branch fell, cadet branch rebelled.

Review tips

  • Always trace the geopolitical shift of the Chauhans from regional feudatories to imperial contenders.
  • Differentiate the cultural achievements of Vigraharaja IV from the military dramatics of Prithviraja III.
  • Keep clear boundaries between the main Ajmer line and the Ranthambore cadet line.
  • Connect architectural transitions such as temples converted into mosques directly to the Sultanate conquest.

Question cues

  • Match Delhi's capture from Tomaras exclusively with Vigraharaja IV.
  • Match the title 'Rai Pithora' instantly with Prithviraja III.
  • Match the defense of Ranthambore against Khilji with Hammiradeva.
  • Identify 1192 CE as the definitive watershed that ended Rajput pre-eminence in North India.

Compare

Main Ajmer Branch (Prithviraja III)vsRanthambore Branch (Hammiradeva)
  • Faced the initial Ghurid invasion (Muhammad Ghuri), whereas Ranthambore faced the later Delhi Sultanate (Ala-ud-din Khilji).
  • Ajmer represented the imperial peak and sudden fall of the central Chauhan power, while Ranthambore was a resilient cadet branch.
  • Ajmer's downfall led to the loss of Delhi and Ajmer, whereas Ranthambore's siege became a legendary symbol of Rajput fortress resistance.

Watch for

  • Assuming all Chauhan rulers fought Muhammad Ghuri; only Prithviraja III did.
  • Confusing the victor of the First Battle of Tarain with the victor of the Second Battle of Tarain.
  • Attributing the Adhai Din Ka Jhonpra entirely to original Islamic construction instead of temple conversion.
  • Mistaking Hammiradeva of Ranthambore for a ruler of the 12th century contemporary with Muhammad Ghuri.
  • Forgetting that the Chauhans started as feudatories before achieving imperial status under Vigraharaja IV.
