The Age of Industrialisation

Understand how production changed in Britain, how colonial rule reshaped Indian textiles, and why factories were only one part of industrialisation.

Complete Concept Learning

Each concept is explained in short, connected blocks — enough to learn, revise and answer questions.

1. Industrialisation ≠ Factories Alone

Industrialisation means a broad transformation in production: new markets, new organisation of labour, technology, investment, factories and changes in traditional production.

Common misconception

“Industrialisation started when factories appeared.”

NCERT idea

Large-scale production for international markets existed before factories. This earlier phase is called proto-industrialisation.

Exam line: Industrialisation was a gradual process; factory production grew alongside older forms of hand and household production.

2. Proto-industrialisation

Merchants moved production into the countryside because urban guilds controlled entry, prices and production.
  • Town merchants supplied raw material/money and collected finished goods.
  • Peasants accepted work because tiny plots and loss of common lands made extra income necessary.
  • Families often combined farming with industrial work.
“Merchant → Village → Market”

3. Why Did Merchants Move to the Countryside?

Guild restrictions

Urban craft guilds controlled entry, production, competition and prices.

Growing demand

World trade and colonies increased demand for goods.

Cheap labour

Poor peasants and artisans needed supplementary income.

Advances

Merchants gave advances and secured production before goods were made.

4. Coming of Factories in Britain

Richard Arkwright's cotton mill brought costly machines and several processes under one roof, making supervision and labour regulation easier.

Important: Factories became highly visible, but they did not immediately replace workshops and domestic production.

5. Why Cotton Became the Leading Industry

First phase

Cotton was the leading industrial sector up to the 1840s.

Machines

Spinning and weaving technologies increased output and strengthened yarn production.

Markets

British manufacturers supplied growing domestic and overseas markets.

Next phase

After the 1840s, iron and steel grew rapidly, especially with railway expansion.

“Cotton first → Iron & Steel next”

6. Industrialisation Was Slower and More Uneven Than It Looks

Factories looked revolutionary, but the entire economy did not become mechanised at once.

  • Even at the end of the nineteenth century, less than 20% of the workforce was in technologically advanced industrial sectors.
  • Much textile output continued in domestic units.
  • Small innovations improved food processing, pottery, glass, tanning, furniture and other industries.
  • Technology was expensive, sometimes unreliable, and often adopted cautiously.
  • Even Watt's improved steam engine spread slowly.
Core understanding: Industrialisation was a mixed economy of factories, workshops, household production and hand skills.

7. Why Did Some Industrialists Prefer Hand Labour?

Hand labour preferred when…

  • Demand was seasonal or uncertain.
  • Products needed variety or fine finishing.
  • Machines were expensive.
  • Labour was easily available and cheap.

Machines preferred when…

  • Production was standardised.
  • Large quantities were needed.
  • Labour was scarce or expensive.
  • Mechanical power reduced labour needs.
Key example: Victorian elites often preferred handmade goods because they symbolised refinement and class.

8. Spinning Jenny and Workers' Fear

The Jenny allowed one worker to operate several spindles, so hand spinners feared loss of employment.
“More spindles → more output → fewer spinners needed → resistance.”

9. Life of Industrial Workers

Migration

Workers travelled from villages to cities seeking jobs.

Job insecurity

Getting a job often depended on friends, relatives and hiring networks.

Seasonal work

Some industries offered work only during busy periods.

Real wages

Nominal wages could rise while high prices reduced purchasing power.

Unemployment

Economic slumps could produce extremely high unemployment in some regions.

Technology fear

Workers often resisted machines when they believed machines threatened employment.

Answer structure: Migration + insecure jobs + seasonal work + real-wage problems + unemployment = difficult industrial working life.

10. Indian Textiles Before British Industrialisation

Before colonial dominance, Indian textiles were deeply connected to international trade through Indian merchants, bankers, supply merchants and ports.
Remember the ports: Surat → Gulf/Red Sea; Masulipatam → Southeast Asia; Hoogly → Southeast Asian trade.

11. Decline of Old Ports and Rise of Colonial Ports

From the 1750s, European companies gained concessions and monopoly rights. This weakened the old merchant-controlled network.

European companies gain powerconcessions + monopoly
Surat + Hoogly declineold trade shrinks
Credit dries upbankers weaken
Bombay + Calcutta growcolonial ports
“Monopoly → Old ports ↓ → Colonial ports ↑”

12. East India Company and the Gomasthas

Gomasthas were Company agents appointed to supervise weavers and secure regular supplies, replacing much of the earlier merchant network.
Why resistance? Weavers lost bargaining power, faced advances tied to contracts, and were often prevented from selling to other buyers.

13. Deindustrialisation of Indian Textiles

Indian piece-goods were 33% of exports in 1811–12 but only 3% by 1850–51. British cotton goods simultaneously became a major part of Indian imports.

Problem 1

British industrialists protected their home market with import duties.

Problem 2

British manufactures were pushed into Indian markets.

Problem 3

Machine-made cloth was cheaper and harder for handloom producers to match.

Problem 4

During the American Civil War, raw cotton shortages made Indian cotton expensive for weavers.

“Export ↓ + Import ↑ + Raw cotton costly = Weaver crisis.”

14. Why Did Handloom Survive?

Industrialisation did not destroy every handloom. Handloom producers adapted and specialised.

  • Fly shuttle increased productivity and allowed wider cloth to be woven.
  • Specialised designs were difficult for mills to copy.
  • Fine saris and distinctive regional products retained demand.
  • Demand for fine cloth bought by richer consumers was more stable than demand for coarse cloth.
  • Entire households, including women and children, often participated in production.
“Specialisation + Flexibility + Skill = Handloom survival.”

15. Rise of Factories in India

1854first cotton mill in Bombay
1855first jute mill in Bengal
1861Elgin Mill, Kanpur
1874first spinning & weaving mill in Madras

European Managing Agencies

Invested in tea, coffee, mining, indigo and jute, mainly for export markets.

Indian entrepreneurs

Started investing in cotton, yarn and other industries despite colonial constraints.

Early strategy

Indian mills often produced coarse yarn rather than directly competing with British cloth.

16. Indian Industrialists and the Swadeshi Shift

Swadeshi linked industrial growth with nationalism: buying Indian goods became both an economic and political statement.
Important: Cotton piece-goods production in India doubled between 1900 and 1912.

17. First World War and Indian Industrial Boom

British millsbusy with war production
Manchester imports ↓Indian market opens
Indian mills ↑cloth + jute + leather
Shifts ↑longer hours + more workers

Indian factories supplied jute bags, army uniforms, tents, leather boots, saddles and other war requirements. New factories were established and existing mills ran multiple shifts.

“War → British supply diverted → Indian demand ↑ → Factory boom.”

18. Industrial Workers in India

Recruitment

Industrial workers often came from villages. Jobbers helped recruit and manage labour.

Jobber

A jobber could recruit workers, maintain discipline and become an important link between workers and management.

Working conditions

Long shifts, low wages, insecurity and unhealthy conditions were common.

Family economy

Workers often maintained village links and moved between rural and urban work.

Key idea: The “industrial worker” was not completely detached from rural society; migration and village ties remained important.

19. Peculiarities of Industrial Growth in India

Export-oriented sectors

  • Tea
  • Coffee
  • Indigo
  • Jute
  • Mining

Indian industrial response

  • Coarse yarn first
  • Later cloth production
  • Swadeshi support
  • Tariff protection demanded
  • War expanded production

Industrial growth was therefore not simply “British factories versus Indian factories”. It involved colonial agencies, Indian entrepreneurs, handlooms, domestic units, workers and changing markets.

20. Market for Goods: Why Advertisements Matter

Advertising did more than announce a product: it created familiarity, trust, desire and consumer culture.
“Label → Trust → Desire → Demand.”

21. Why Did Manchester Labels Use Indian Gods and Kings?

  • Foreign-made goods could seem unfamiliar or distant.
  • Religious images gave the product a sense of familiarity and approval.
  • Historical figures could associate the product with prestige and respect.
  • Indian manufacturers later used similar visual strategies to promote swadeshi.
Important distinction: Advertising was used by both colonial manufacturers and Indian manufacturers, but Indian advertising could also carry a nationalist message.

22. Complete Chapter Causal Map

The chapter is not a simple “machines replaced handwork” story. It is a story of coexistence, competition, colonial control, adaptation and changing markets.

NCERT Exercises + Answer Frameworks

Answers are structured so students can learn the logic rather than memorise paragraphs.

1(a). Why did women workers in Britain attack the Spinning Jenny?
Answer
  • The Jenny allowed one worker to operate several spindles.
  • It increased output per worker.
  • It reduced the demand for hand spinners.
  • Women who depended on hand spinning feared loss of livelihood.
  • Therefore they attacked machines as a defence of employment.
1(b). Why did merchants employ peasants and artisans in villages in the seventeenth century?
Answer
  • World trade increased demand.
  • Urban guilds controlled production and restricted entry.
  • Merchants therefore moved to the countryside.
  • Poor peasants needed supplementary income.
  • Merchants supplied advances and organised production for the market.
1(c). Why did Surat decline by the end of the eighteenth century?
Answer

European companies gained concessions and monopoly rights, weakening Indian merchants. Trade shifted toward ports such as Bombay and Calcutta controlled by European companies and ships.

1(d). Why did the East India Company appoint gomasthas?
Answer

To supervise weavers, secure regular supplies, inspect production, collect cloth and strengthen Company control over textile procurement.

2(a). True or False: At the end of the nineteenth century, 80% of Europe's workforce was in technologically advanced industries.
Answer

False. Less than 20% was employed in technologically advanced industrial sectors.

2(b). Fine textiles were internationally dominated by India until the eighteenth century.
Answer

True.

2(c). The American Civil War reduced India's cotton exports.
Answer

False. Britain's shortage of American cotton increased demand for Indian raw cotton and pushed its price up.

2(d). Fly shuttle improved handloom productivity.
Answer

True.

3. What is proto-industrialisation?
Answer

It was the phase before factory production in which merchants organised large-scale production for international markets, often using peasants and artisans working in their homes in the countryside.

4. Why did some industrialists prefer hand labour over machines?
Answer
  • Machines were expensive.
  • Demand could be seasonal or uncertain.
  • Handwork was better for specialised and varied products.
  • In Britain, labour was abundant and relatively cheap.
  • Elite consumers valued handmade goods.
5. How did the East India Company procure regular supplies of cotton and silk textiles?
Answer
  • It appointed gomasthas.
  • They supervised weavers and inspected production.
  • Weavers received advances tied to contracts.
  • The Company controlled procurement and reduced weavers' ability to bargain or sell elsewhere.
6. Why did industrial production in India increase during the First World War?
Answer
  • British mills were occupied with war production.
  • Manchester imports into India declined.
  • Indian mills gained a large home market.
  • Factories supplied uniforms, tents, jute bags, leather goods and other war materials.
  • New factories opened and existing mills increased shifts.

Chapter Test

Answer first; reveal the model answer afterwards.

1. Define proto-industrialisation.
Answer

Large-scale market-oriented production before factories, often organised by merchants using rural peasants and artisans.

2. Why did merchants move production to villages?
Answer

Urban guilds restricted entry and controlled production, while rural households offered labour and needed supplementary income.

3. Why did workers oppose the Spinning Jenny?
Answer

It allowed one worker to operate several spindles and therefore threatened the jobs of hand spinners.

4. Why did factories not immediately replace domestic production?
Answer

Machines were expensive, demand could be uncertain, hand production remained flexible, and many traditional sectors continued to use small innovations.

5. Name three important pre-colonial Indian textile ports.
Answer

Surat, Masulipatam and Hoogly.

6. What did the gomastha do?
Answer

He supervised weavers and helped the East India Company procure regular supplies of textiles.

7. What is deindustrialisation in the Indian context?
Answer

The decline of traditional Indian textile production and exports under the pressure of colonial policies and cheaper British machine-made imports.

8. Why did handloom survive?
Answer

Weavers used innovations such as the fly shuttle and specialised in designs that mills could not easily reproduce.

9. Why did Indian industrial production increase during WWI?
Answer

British mills focused on war supplies, reducing Manchester imports and creating a large Indian market; Indian factories also supplied war materials.

10. How did advertisements create a market?
Answer

They created familiarity, trust and desire through labels, images, calendars and messages about quality, religion, prestige or nationalism.

Assertion–Reason

A. Assertion: Industrialisation did not immediately eliminate hand production. Reason: New technology was expensive and many products required flexible or specialised handwork.
Answer

Both are correct and the reason explains the assertion.

B. Assertion: Surat declined as Bombay and Calcutta grew. Reason: European companies increasingly controlled colonial trade and shifted trade toward their own ports and shipping networks.
Answer

Both are correct and the reason explains the assertion.

C. Assertion: Indian weavers benefited immediately from EIC political control. Reason: Gomasthas gave them complete freedom to sell to any buyer.
Answer

Both are incorrect.

5-Mark Challenge

“Industrialisation was a process of coexistence and competition rather than simple machine replacement.” Explain.
Model framework
  • Proto-industrial production existed before factories.
  • Factories expanded rapidly in cotton and later iron/steel.
  • Traditional industries continued alongside factories.
  • Technology was expensive and adoption was gradual.
  • Handloom producers used skill and small innovations to survive.
  • In India, colonial rule created both factory growth and severe pressure on traditional textiles.
  • Therefore industrialisation transformed production without eliminating older forms immediately.
Master answer formula: For a 3–5 mark History answer use CONTEXT → CAUSE → PROCESS → EFFECT → EXAMPLE. For “Why” questions, never write effects alone.
