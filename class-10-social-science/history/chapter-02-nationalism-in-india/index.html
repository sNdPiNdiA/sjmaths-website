Nationalism in India

From Satyagraha and Non-Cooperation to Civil Disobedience, Salt March, social participation and the making of collective belonging.

02of 5 History Chapters

Chapter at a Glance

First understand the story. Then learn the details through visual memory maps.

Master story: colonial hardship → Gandhian Satyagraha → mass movements → different groups join with different hopes → unity and limits.
1919 = Rowlatt + Jallianwala 1920 = Khilafat + Non-Cooperation 1922 = Chauri Chaura 1929 = Purna Swaraj 1930 = Dandi + Civil Disobedience 1932 = Poona Pact
Core idea: Indian nationalism grew through the anti-colonial struggle, but different social groups joined for different reasons. National unity was powerful — and never completely conflict-free.

Taxes ↑ • prices doubled • recruitment • crop failure • influenza.

Truth + non-violence + active resistance against injustice.

Concepts — Learn Visually

Short blocks + diagrams + mnemonics + exam traps. No long textbook paragraphs.

1. Why Did Nationalism Grow in India?

Nationalism grew from both political struggle and cultural processes.
Remember: “SAME” → Shared oppression • Anti-colonial action • Mass participation • Emotional symbols.

2. First World War: Why Did It Accelerate Nationalism?

War created economic hardship and political anger. The hoped-for post-war relief did not arrive.
Exam trick: If asked “How did WWI help nationalism?”, write both economic hardship and political anger — not only “people suffered”.

3. Gandhi Returns: The Idea of Satyagraha

Satyagraha was active resistance based on truth and non-violence — not passive surrender.
“T-F-N-C” → Truth • Firmness • Non-violence • Conscience.

1917 · Champaran

Peasants against oppressive plantation system.

1918 · Ahmedabad

Cotton mill workers' satyagraha.

1918 · Kheda

Revenue relief demanded after crop failure and plague.

4. Rowlatt Act → Satyagraha → Jallianwala Bagh

The Rowlatt Act created repression; the April 1919 protests were followed by the Jallianwala Bagh massacre and wider anger.
Rowlatt Act: allowed detention of political prisoners without trial for up to two years.
Jallianwala Bagh: 13 April 1919; General Dyer blocked exits and ordered firing on the crowd.

5. Khilafat + Non-Cooperation: Why Combine Them?

Khilafat leaders Muhammad Ali and Shaukat Ali worked with Gandhi; Congress adopted the Non-Cooperation programme at Nagpur in December 1920.
“K + S = Unity” → Khilafat issue + Swaraj demand = wider Hindu-Muslim mass mobilisation.

6. Non-Cooperation Movement — What Was Boycotted?

The movement began with withdrawal from colonial institutions and boycott of foreign goods.
“T-S-C-C-F” → Titles • Schools • Courts • Councils • Foreign goods.

7. Different Groups, Different Meanings of Swaraj

The same national slogan could carry very different economic and social meanings.
High-scoring point: Do not write “all Indians joined for the same reason.” The chapter explicitly stresses different aspirations.

8. Towns: Middle-Class Participation

Students

Left government schools and colleges.

Teachers

Some resigned.

Lawyers

Gave up legal practice.

Merchants

Refused foreign trade.

Liquor

Shops were picketed.

Foreign Cloth

Boycotted and burnt; imports fell sharply.

“S-T-L-M-L-F” → Students • Teachers • Lawyers • Merchants • Liquor shops • Foreign cloth.
Why did the urban movement slow? Khadi was expensive; alternative Indian institutions were slow to develop.

9. Awadh Peasants — What Did They Want?

Baba Ramchandra led the Awadh peasant struggle; the Oudh Kisan Sabha was established in 1920.
“R-B-T-B” → Reduce revenue • Banish begar • Tenure security • Boycott oppressive landlords.

10. Tribal Swaraj — Alluri Sitaram Raju

Gudem rebels interpreted swaraj through forest rights and resistance to forced labour; their methods differed sharply from Gandhian non-violence.
Exam distinction: Raju admired Gandhi but believed freedom could require force. Therefore, do not describe the Gudem movement as a purely Gandhian non-violent movement.

11. Plantation Workers — Their Swaraj

Plantation workers connected swaraj with freedom of movement and returning to their villages.

12. Why Was Non-Cooperation Withdrawn?

Gandhi believed satyagrahis needed training in non-violence before another mass struggle.
“C = Cancel” → Chauri Chaura → violence → Gandhi calls off Non-Cooperation.

“S-S-L-P-I” → Swaraj Party → Simon → Lahore → Purna Swaraj → Independence Day.

14. Simon Commission — Why “Go Back Simon”?

The Simon Commission had no Indian member. Demonstrations included Congress and Muslim League participants.
Lala Lajpat Rai: assaulted during a peaceful anti-Simon demonstration and later died from injuries.

15. Salt March — Why Was Salt So Powerful?

Gandhi reached Dandi on 6 April and broke the salt law by making salt from seawater.
“S-78-24-240-D” → Sabarmati → 78 volunteers → 24 days → 240+ miles → Dandi.
Why salt? Everyone consumed it — rich and poor. The salt tax therefore gave a simple everyday symbol to the wider anti-colonial struggle.

16. Non-Cooperation vs Civil Disobedience

Point Non-Cooperation Civil Disobedience
Period 1921–22 1930–34
Main idea Withdraw cooperation Break unjust colonial laws + refuse cooperation
Symbolic action Boycott foreign goods/institutions Salt law breaking
Major turning point Chauri Chaura Dandi March
End/relaunch Withdrawn Feb 1922 Pact 1931; relaunched; faded by 1934
“CO = Cooperate NO; CD = Colonial laws DEFIED.”

17. Civil Disobedience — What Did People Actually Do?

Civil disobedience moved beyond non-participation: people deliberately violated selected colonial laws.

18. Rich Peasants vs Poor Peasants

Rich Peasants

  • Patidars of Gujarat
  • Jats of Uttar Pradesh
  • Commercial crop producers
  • Hit by falling prices
  • Wanted lower revenue
  • Strong supporters initially

Poor Peasants

  • Small tenants
  • Rented land from landlords
  • Cash incomes fell
  • Wanted rent remission
  • Radical movements influenced them
  • Congress often avoided no-rent campaigns
“RICH = Revenue; POOR = Rent.”

19. Business Classes

Business groups supported the movement when it aligned with protection from colonial economic restrictions.
Exam nuance: Their support was not unlimited. Prolonged disruption and militant activity made many business groups cautious.

20. Women in Civil Disobedience

Participation

Protest marches, salt making, picketing, jail.

Urban

Many participants came from high-caste families.

Rural

Many came from rich peasant households.

New Public Role

Many women entered public political life for the first time.

Limitation

Congress was reluctant to give women positions of authority.

Gandhian View

National service was encouraged alongside traditional domestic roles.

Balanced answer: “Participation expanded” does not mean “gender equality was achieved.” Both points are required.

21. Dalits, Ambedkar and the Poona Pact

The Poona Pact provided reserved seats for the Depressed Classes in provincial and central councils, elected by the general electorate.
“P-P-R-G” → Political demand → Pact → Reserved seats → General electorate.

22. Why Was Muslim Participation Limited in Civil Disobedience?

Political representation and minority safeguards became central points of disagreement.
Do not oversimplify: The chapter explains political distrust, representation concerns and communal tensions — not a single cause.

23. Collective Belonging — How Did People Imagine “India”?

Nationalism entered popular imagination through images, songs, flags, folklore and new interpretations of history.

24. Bharat Mata — Key Facts

Bankim Chandra

Wrote “Vande Mataram” in the 1870s.

Anandamath

The hymn was later included in the novel.

Abanindranath Tagore

Painted the famous 1905 Bharat Mata image.

Ascetic Form

Calm, composed, divine and spiritual.

Popular Prints

The image later took multiple forms.

Limitation

Hindu iconography could make some communities feel excluded.

“B-A-A” → Bankim = Vande Mataram • Anandamath = novel • Abanindranath = 1905 painting.

25. Folklore + History = National Pride

Inspirepride + national struggle

Rabindranath Tagore

Collected Bengali ballads, nursery rhymes and myths.

Published a four-volume collection of Tamil folk tales.

Nationalist History

Highlighted ancient achievements in art, science, mathematics, trade and culture.

26. The Big Limitation: One Nation, Many Voices

The chapter's conclusion: a nation was emerging with many voices, expectations and conflicts.
Best conclusion line: Nationalism united diverse groups in a common anti-colonial struggle, but the unity was incomplete because different groups had different aspirations.

NCERT Exercise + Exam-Ready Answers

Answers are deliberately structured in short points for fast revision and writing practice.

1. Why is the growth of nationalism in the colonies linked to an anti-colonial movement?3 marks
View Answer

Colonial rule created a shared experience of oppression. Different groups discovered common interests while resisting colonialism. The anti-colonial struggle therefore helped create a broader sense of national unity.

2. How did the First World War help in the growth of the National Movement in India?3 marks
View Answer
  • Defence expenditure caused higher taxes.
  • Prices doubled between 1913 and 1918.
  • Forced recruitment created rural anger.
  • Crop failures caused food shortages.
  • The influenza epidemic added severe hardship.
3. Why were Indians outraged by the Rowlatt Act?3 marks
View Answer

The Act gave the government sweeping powers to repress political activity and allowed political prisoners to be detained without trial for up to two years. It was passed despite united Indian opposition.

4. Why did Gandhiji decide to withdraw the Non-Cooperation Movement?3 marks
View Answer

Gandhi withdrew it in February 1922 after the Chauri Chaura incident because the movement was becoming violent. He believed satyagrahis needed proper training in non-violence before another mass struggle.

5. What is meant by the idea of Satyagraha?3 marks
View Answer

Satyagraha is a method of active resistance based on truth and non-violence. The satyagrahi does not physically harm the opponent but seeks to awaken the opponent's conscience and establish the truth.

6. Write a newspaper report on the Jallianwala Bagh massacre.5 marks
Model Report

Amritsar, 13 April 1919: A large crowd gathered at Jallianwala Bagh. General Dyer entered the enclosed ground, blocked the exits and ordered troops to fire. Hundreds were killed. The incident caused widespread anger and intensified opposition to British rule.

7. Write a newspaper report on the Simon Commission.5 marks
Model Report

India, 1928: The Simon Commission arrived to review constitutional arrangements. Its complete absence of Indian members provoked widespread protests. Demonstrators raised the slogan “Go Back Simon”. Congress and Muslim League groups participated in the protests.

8. Compare Bharat Mata with Germania.5 marks
View Answer
Bharat Mata Germania
Personification of India. Personification of Germany.
Associated with motherland and spirituality. Associated with heroism, strength and national defence.
Abanindranath Tagore's 1905 image became famous. German national allegory used symbols such as oak leaves and sword.
9. Explain why the Salt March was an effective symbol of resistance.5 marks
View Answer
  • Salt was used by both rich and poor.
  • The salt tax represented colonial economic exploitation.
  • Breaking the law was simple and visible.
  • The march attracted thousands of people.
  • It converted the abstract idea of freedom into an everyday issue.
10. Why did political leaders differ sharply over separate electorates?5 marks
View Answer

Ambedkar and many dalit leaders wanted separate electorates to secure political representation and empowerment. Gandhi opposed separate electorates because he feared they would weaken social integration. The disagreement ultimately produced the Poona Pact of 1932.

11. Why did different social groups participate in the Civil Disobedience Movement?5 marks
View Answer
  • Rich peasants wanted reduction of revenue.
  • Poor peasants wanted remission of unpaid rent.
  • Business groups wanted fewer colonial restrictions.
  • Some workers adopted boycott ideas alongside labour demands.
  • Women participated in marches, salt making and picketing.
12. Why did the unity of the national movement repeatedly break down?5 marks
View Answer

Different classes and communities had different economic and political interests. Congress had to balance competing demands. Communal tensions, representation disputes and conflicts between rich and poor peasants also limited unity.

5-Mark Challenge

“Nationalism in India was a process of both unity and conflict.” Explain.
Model Answer Framework
  • Unity: common opposition to colonial rule.
  • Mass mobilisation: NCM and CDM brought different groups together.
  • Symbols: Bharat Mata, flags, songs, folklore and history.
  • Conflict: different class interests.
  • Community politics: dalit and Muslim representation disputes.
  • Conclusion: the movement created a national consciousness but contained many competing aspirations.
