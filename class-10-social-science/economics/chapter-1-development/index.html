Chapter 1 · Economics

Development

Understanding Economic Development · NCERT 2026–27

1 / 5 Learning sections

Complete Concept Notes

All major ideas, indicators, comparisons, public facilities and sustainability concepts from the chapter.

1Meaning of Development

Development or progress is about aspirations, desires and the ways people and societies work towards better lives.

  • People ask what they need, how they should live together and whether life can be better for all.
  • Development involves goals such as greater equality and improved quality of life.
  • Development is not only an economic idea; it also connects with history and political science.
Core idea: Development is a broad process of improving people's lives and achieving desired goals.

2Different People, Different Goals

People have different aspirations because their life situations, needs and priorities differ.

  • A landless rural labourer may want more days of work, better wages, education and freedom from discrimination.
  • A prosperous farmer may seek assured income, better crop prices and opportunities for children.
  • A rural woman may seek equal freedom, education and decision-making opportunities.
  • An unemployed urban youth may prioritise regular employment and security.
  • People from different social and economic groups can therefore define development differently.
Important distinction: Different goals can also be conflicting goals.

3Conflicting Developmental Goals

What is development for one person or group may not be development for another and may even be destructive for them.

  • Industrialists may favour large dams for electricity.
  • People displaced by dams, including tribals, may prefer smaller check dams or tanks.
  • A girl's desire for equal freedom may conflict with expectations within a household.
Exam point: Development must be evaluated from the perspectives of different affected groups.

4Income and Other Goals

People want more income, but income alone does not capture everything important in life.

  • Material goals: regular work, better wages, decent prices, higher consumption.
  • Non-material goals: equal treatment, freedom, security, respect and dignity.
  • Some non-material goals can be more important than additional income.
  • Job choice may depend on family facilities, work atmosphere, learning opportunities, regular employment and time for family.
Remember: A good development outcome is a mix of material and non-material goals.

5National Development

When thinking about national development, different people can have different and conflicting ideas about what India should achieve.

  • We must ask which goals are important and how conflicts between goals should be resolved.
  • A fair and just path should be considered.
  • We should ask whether a proposed development benefits a large number of people or only a small group.
National development = thinking about collective goals, fairness, conflicts and wider benefits.

6How Countries or States Are Compared

The criterion used for comparison depends on the purpose. For development, income is an important starting point, but it is not sufficient.

  • Total income is not suitable for comparing countries with different populations.
  • Average income gives a more meaningful comparison of what an average person earns.
Per Capita Income = Total income of the country ÷ Total population

7Per Capita Income

Average income is also called per capita income. It is widely used to compare countries and states.

  • The World Bank uses per capita income as a major criterion for classifying countries.
  • The chapter's 2024 figures identify high-income/rich countries at US$66,500 or above and low-income countries at about US$2,300 or less.
  • The chapter states India's 2024 per capita income at about US$11,000, placing it in the low-middle-income category.
Do not confuse total income with per capita income.

8Average Hides Inequality

Average income is useful but does not show how income is distributed among people.

  • Two countries can have the same average income but very different distributions.
  • A society with relatively equal incomes may be preferred over one where most people are poor and one person is extremely rich.
Exam phrase: Average income hides disparities in the distribution of income.

9Income Is Not a Complete Indicator

Higher income does not automatically guarantee better health, education, safety or environmental quality.

  • Money cannot by itself buy a pollution-free environment.
  • It cannot guarantee unadulterated medicines.
  • Protection from infectious diseases often requires community-level preventive action.
  • Public services can strongly influence quality of life.

10Development Indicators

The chapter uses several indicators to compare quality of life and human development.

Per Capita Income Infant Mortality Rate Literacy Rate Net Attendance Ratio Life Expectancy Mean Years of Schooling GNI Per Capita HDI
  • IMR: number of children dying before age one per 1,000 live births.
  • Literacy Rate: proportion of literate population in the 7-and-above age group.
  • Net Attendance Ratio: children aged 15–17 attending school as a percentage of all children in that age group.
  • Life Expectancy at birth: average expected length of life at the time of birth.

12Public Facilities

Many important goods and services are best provided collectively because collective provision can be cheaper and more effective.

  • Examples include security, schools, healthcare and other basic facilities.
  • Government and society have a role in providing facilities so that people can access essential services.
  • Girls' access to secondary education can depend on adequate public and social facilities.
  • The Public Distribution System can influence health and nutritional outcomes.
Key idea: Development depends not only on private income but also on access to public facilities.

13Human Development

Once income is recognised as inadequate by itself, development is assessed through a broader human-centred approach.

  • Health and education indicators are used along with income.
  • The UNDP's Human Development Report compares countries using educational levels, health status and per capita income.
  • HDI stands for Human Development Index.
  • The chapter emphasises that what matters in development is what is happening to citizens—their health and well-being.

15Purchasing Power Parity (PPP)

PPP is used in international income comparisons so that a dollar represents comparable purchasing power across countries.

  • The chapter uses GNI per capita in PPP dollars in its international comparison table.
  • The purpose is to make cross-country comparisons of income more meaningful.

16Nutrition and BMI

The chapter includes BMI as an activity for thinking about nutritional status.

BMI = Weight in kg ÷ (Height in metres)²
  • Students measure weight and height, convert height to metres and calculate BMI.
  • The chapter directs students to BMI-for-age tables to interpret nutritional status.
  • The activity explicitly asks students to discuss food and exercise without body shaming.

17Sustainability of Development

Development should not destroy the resources and environmental conditions required by future generations.

  • The chapter warns that present types and levels of development may not be sustainable.
  • Environmental degradation is not confined by state or national boundaries.
  • Our future is linked together.
Memory line: Development must be desirable today and capable of being maintained for future generations.

18Renewable Resources

Renewable resources can be replenished by nature, but they can still be overused.

  • Groundwater is an example of a renewable resource.
  • If groundwater extraction exceeds replenishment through rain, the resource is being overused.
  • Thus, “renewable” does not mean “unlimited”.

19Groundwater Overuse

The chapter gives groundwater depletion as an example of unsustainable development.

  • About 300 districts had reported a water-level decline of over 4 metres during the previous 20 years cited by the chapter.
  • Nearly one-third of the country was overusing groundwater reserves.
  • If the trend continued, the chapter projected that 60% of the country could be doing the same in another 25 years.
  • Overuse is particularly noted in agriculturally prosperous Punjab and western Uttar Pradesh, hard-rock plateau areas, some coastal areas and rapidly growing urban settlements.

20Non-Renewable Resources

Non-renewable resources exist as a fixed stock and get exhausted after continued use because they cannot be replenished by nature on the relevant human timescale.

  • Crude oil is the chapter's main example.
  • New discoveries can add to known reserves, but these too can eventually be exhausted.
  • Countries dependent on imports can face problems when international oil prices rise.

22Big Picture of the Chapter

The chapter moves from individual aspirations to national development, from income to human development, and finally to sustainability.

  • People: different goals and aspirations.
  • Comparison: income and per capita income.
  • Limitations: averages hide inequality and income misses many quality-of-life dimensions.
  • Human development: health, education and income together.
  • Public facilities: collective provision supports quality of life.
  • Sustainability: development must protect future generations and natural resources.

Development of a country can generally be determined by

  2. its average literacy level

Which neighbouring country has better performance in terms of human development than India?

  1. Bangladesh
  2. Sri Lanka
  3. Nepal
  4. Pakistan
Answer: (ii) Sri Lanka.
3

Four families have an average per capita income of ₹5,000. Three families earn ₹4,000, ₹7,000 and ₹3,000 respectively. Find the fourth family's income.

  1. ₹7,500
  2. ₹3,000
  3. ₹2,000
  4. ₹6,000
Answer: (iv) ₹6,000. Total income = 4 × ₹5,000 = ₹20,000; fourth income = ₹20,000 − ₹14,000 = ₹6,000.

What is the main criterion used by the World Bank in classifying different countries? What are the limitations of this criterion, if any?

Long Answer Questions (5 Marks)
2021C, 2020C, 2015

LA-1. What is development? Why do different people have different development goals? Explain with four examples.

Answer: Development refers to improvement in quality of life, expansion of capabilities, and economic well-being. Different people have different goals because their life situations and immediate needs are different.
Examples:
  1. Landless rural laborer: Seeks more days of work and better wages because their immediate challenge is daily survival and lack of assets.
  2. Prosperous farmer from Punjab: Seeks higher support prices and cheap labor to maximize profit, as they already possess land and assets.
  3. An urban unemployed youth: Prioritizes getting a regular and stable job to achieve financial independence in a competitive urban environment.
  4. A girl from a rich urban family: Seeks freedom and opportunities equal to her brother to study and work, as she is already economically secure.
2016

LA-4. What is national development? What aspects does it cover?

Answer: National development is a comprehensive concept that refers to the capability of a nation to improve the lives of its citizens.
Aspects covered:
  1. Economic aspect: Increase in national income and per capita income, and equitable distribution.
  2. Social aspect: Improvement in health, literacy, gender equality, and access to basic amenities.
  3. Infrastructure aspect: Development of roads, transport, schools, hospitals, and power grids.
  4. Fair and Just Paths: Resolving conflicting interests between different groups so that development benefits the majority rather than a select few.
  5. Environmental Sustainability: Planning development so that it does not deplete resources or degrade the environment.
2014

LA-5. Explain with examples that there are other important development goals besides income.

Answer: Although income is a vital material goal, non-material goals are equally crucial for a high-quality life.
Examples:
  1. Dignity and Work Culture: A job with a very high salary but no job security and no respect may not be preferred over a job with moderate pay but high security, regular hours, and respect.
  2. Dignity of Women: If women are engaged in paid work, their dignity increases, which requires greater sharing of housework and safety in public spaces.
  3. Pollution-Free Environment: A rich family living in a highly polluted city cannot buy clean air or safety from infectious diseases with money alone.
  4. Freedom of Choice: An individual's quality of life depends on the freedom to practice their own religion, speak their language, and associate freely.

LA-3. What is the main criterion used by the World Bank in classifying countries? Describe its limitations.

Answer: The World Bank uses Per Capita Income (Average Income) as the sole criterion for classifying countries into high-income, middle-income, and low-income categories.
Limitations:
  1. Hides Inequalities: A country may have a high per capita income because a few individuals are extremely wealthy, even if the majority of citizens are poor. It fails to show the actual distribution of income.
  2. Ignores Non-Material Aspects: It ignores key parameters like literacy, school attendance, life expectancy, health status, and safety.
  3. Narrow Scope: Economic growth is only a subset of development. The World Bank approach treats income as the sole end rather than a means to a better life.
  4. Purchasing Disparities: Unless strictly measured in PPP dollars, nominal per capita income does not accurately reflect the cost-of-living differences.
2021C, 2017

LA-6. In what respect is the criterion used by UNDP different from World Bank? What are the limitations of the World Bank's criterion?

Answer:
  1. UNDP Criterion (Human Development Index): UNDP uses a composite index combining (i) health (life expectancy), (ii) education (literacy and mean/expected years of schooling), and (iii) income (GNI per capita). This presents a comprehensive view of human capabilities.
  2. World Bank Criterion (Per Capita Income): The World Bank compares countries based strictly on their average per capita income.
Limitations of World Bank's criterion:
  • It fails to reveal disparities in how income is distributed among the population.
  • It completely excludes quality of life parameters such as healthcare access, education availability, safety, and gender equality.
  • It equates economic growth directly with human development, which is incorrect since money alone cannot buy all the elements of a good life.

How to revise this chapter

First remember the framework, then the indicators, then the comparisons, and finally the sustainability examples. Use the mnemonics below to recall the structure quickly.

🧠 Master Map — “G → I → H → S”

  • G — Goals: Different people have different and sometimes conflicting developmental goals.
  • I — Income: Per capita income is useful, but averages can hide inequality.
  • H — Human Development: Health + education + income give a broader picture.
  • S — Sustainability: Development must remain possible for future generations.
One-line recall: Goals → Income → Human Development → Sustainability.

🎯 Different Goals — “I-R-S-F”

  • I — Income: Better wages, regular work, better prices.
  • R — Respect: Dignity and freedom from discrimination.
  • S — Security: Safe work and a secure environment.
  • F — Freedom: Equal opportunities and ability to make choices.
Exam trick: When asked “other than income”, immediately write respect, security, freedom, equality and then give a suitable example.

⚡ Different vs Conflicting Goals

  • Different: Two people simply want different things.
  • Conflicting: Achieving one group's goal may harm or obstruct another group's goal.
  • Classic chapter example: a large dam may provide electricity but can displace people.
Shortcut: Different = “not same”; Conflicting = “one can hurt the other”.

➗ Per Capita Income — “Total ÷ People”

Per Capita Income = Total Income ÷ Total Population
  • It is also called average income.
  • It is more useful than total income for comparing countries with different populations.
  • The World Bank uses per capita income as a major criterion for country classification.
Numerical trick: If average and number of people are given → Total income = Average × Population.

⚠️ Average Income — “Average ≠ Equality”

  • An average tells us the overall average but not the distribution.
  • Two societies can have the same average but very different income distributions.
  • Always mention disparity / distribution when asked for a limitation of averages.
Board tip: Write one mini-example: “Most people poor + one person extremely rich” can produce a high average.

📊 Development Indicators — “I-L-A-L”

  • I — Infant Mortality Rate: infant deaths per 1,000 live births.
  • L — Literacy Rate: proportion of literate population aged 7+.
  • A — Attendance: Net Attendance Ratio for the 15–17 age group.
  • L — Life Expectancy: expected length of life at birth.
Mnemonic: ILAL = Infant → Literacy → Attendance → Life.

🏥 IMR — Remember the Direction

  • Lower IMR generally indicates better health conditions.
  • It counts deaths before one year of age.
  • Unit: per 1,000 live births.
Trap alert: Do not write “percentage” for IMR. The chapter defines it per 1,000 live births.

🎓 Literacy vs Attendance

  • Literacy Rate: looks at the proportion of literate people in the 7-and-above age group.
  • Net Attendance Ratio: looks specifically at children aged 15–17 attending school.
Easy distinction: Literacy = “Can read/write?” · Attendance = “Is the child attending school?”

🌍 World Bank vs UNDP — “W = Wealth, U = Wider Human View”

  • World Bank: per capita income is the major criterion used for classification.
  • UNDP: human development approach combines income with health and education indicators.
Exam shortcut: WB → income classification. UNDP → broader human development.

🧑‍⚕️ Human Development — “H-E-I”

  • H — Health
  • E — Education
  • I — Income
Mnemonic: HEI. If a 3-mark question asks what makes development broader, start with these three.

🏫 Public Facilities — “Collective = Cheaper + Wider”

  • Many essential services are best provided collectively.
  • Examples: schools, healthcare and security.
  • Public facilities can improve access even when individual incomes differ.
  • PDS can influence health and nutritional outcomes.
Answer trick: For “Why public facilities?” write collective provision + wider access + essential services + quality of life.

🔴 Haryana vs Kerala — “Income Can Mislead”

  • Haryana has higher per capita income in the chapter's state table.
  • Kerala has lower IMR and higher literacy and net attendance in the cited data.
  • This proves that income alone cannot fully measure development.
Board trick: Whenever Kerala–Haryana appears, immediately write: income + health + education.

♻️ Sustainability — “Future First”

  • Development should not destroy the resource base needed by future generations.
  • Environmental degradation can affect areas beyond national or state boundaries.
  • Present development must be capable of being maintained.
One-line answer: Sustainable development meets present needs without undermining the ability of future generations to develop.

💧 Renewable ≠ Unlimited

  • Groundwater is renewable because nature can replenish it.
  • If extraction exceeds replenishment, groundwater is overused.
  • Therefore a renewable resource can still become scarce.
Logic chain: Extraction > Replenishment → Overuse → Falling resource availability.

🛢️ Non-Renewable Resources — “Fixed Stock”

  • Crude oil is the chapter's main example.
  • It exists in a finite stock and gets exhausted through continued use.
  • New discoveries can add to known reserves, but the resource remains exhaustible.
Memory: Renewable = replenished by nature; Non-renewable = finite/exhaustible stock.

🧮 Data Question Strategy — “C-U-C”

  • C — Compare: identify the highest and lowest.
  • U — Understand: explain what the numbers mean.
  • C — Conclude: connect the data to development.
Example: Don't merely state Kerala's IMR is lower; conclude that the cited data indicate better health outcomes.

✍️ 3-Mark Answer Formula — “D-E-C”

  • D — Define: give the key meaning/term.
  • E — Explain: explain the reason or mechanism.
  • C — Connect: connect it to the chapter's example.
Example: Define per capita income → explain why averages are used → connect to country comparison.

📝 5-Mark Answer Formula — “P-E-E-C”

  • P — Point: directly answer the question.
  • E — Explain: give the reasoning.
  • E — Example: use a chapter example/data point.
  • C — Conclusion: link back to development.
Presentation tip: Use short paragraphs or numbered points instead of one large block of text.

🚨 Common Exam Traps

  • Do not say per capita income is the only measure of development.
  • Do not say a higher average means every person is better off.
  • Do not confuse literacy rate with school attendance.
  • Do not treat renewable resources as unlimited.
  • Do not forget public facilities when discussing quality of life.
  • Do not discuss sustainability only in terms of money; include natural resources and future generations.

⏱️ 60-Second Final Revision

  • Different people → different/conflicting goals.
  • Income matters → use per capita income.
  • Average can hide inequality.
  • Income alone is insufficient.

1. What is another name for average income?

Correct Answer: B. Per capita income.
1 mark

2. Which indicator measures infant deaths before the age of one year per 1,000 live births?

Correct Answer: B. Infant Mortality Rate (IMR).

3. State one reason why people may have different developmental goals.

Suggested Answer: People live in different situations and have different needs, priorities, and aspirations.
1 mark

4. Is groundwater a renewable or non-renewable resource according to the chapter?

Correct Answer: Groundwater is a renewable resource, but it can still be overused and depleted if extraction exceeds natural replenishment.

5. Write the formula for per capita income.

Correct Answer: Per Capita Income = Total Income of Country ÷ Total Population of Country.
2 marks

1. Two countries have the same average income. Why might one still be preferred over the other?

Suggested Answer: Average income doesn't show how wealth is distributed. One country might have an equitable income distribution where most citizens live well, while the other might have extreme disparity (most people poor and one person extremely rich).
3 marks

2. Haryana has higher per capita income than Kerala in the chapter's data, yet Kerala performs better on IMR, literacy and net attendance. What does this demonstrate?

Suggested Answer: It demonstrates that income alone is not an adequate measure of development. Health, education, access to basic public infrastructure, and collective welfare are crucial parts of real development.
3 marks

3. Distinguish between different developmental goals and conflicting developmental goals.

Suggested Answer: Different goals mean different groups seek different targets based on their unique situations (e.g., landless labourers seeking wage employment vs rich urban youth seeking study abroad). Conflicting goals mean that achieving development for one group directly harms another group (e.g., dams providing power for industrialists while submerging lands and displacing tribals).
3 marks

4. Why are public facilities important even for people who have income?

Suggested Answer: Individual purchasing power cannot buy collective benefits. Money cannot buy a pollution-free environment, ensure protection from infectious diseases, or build public safety networks. Provisioning these facilities collectively is also cheaper and more efficient.
3 marks

5. Explain why “renewable” does not mean “unlimited”.

Suggested Answer: A resource is renewable because nature replenishes it (like rain replenishing groundwater). However, if our rate of extraction exceeds the natural replenishment rate, the stock declines. Hence, it can still face exhaustion if overused.
4 marks

1. “Per capita income is useful, but it cannot be the sole measure of development.” Justify using the chapter's examples.

Suggested Answer: Per capita income (average income) is a single, measurable value that facilitates quick comparison, but it conceals major disparities and distributions of wealth. Furthermore, development is multi-dimensional and requires aspects like educational attainment (literacy, school attendance) and health indicators (IMR, life expectancy) which money cannot buy directly. The comparison of Haryana's high income vs Kerala's superior health and educational parameters is a prime chapter example of this limitation.
4 marks

2. Explain the relationship among income, public facilities and human development.

Suggested Answer: High income provides individual families with purchasing power for private goods. However, public facilities (like schools, hospitals, public distribution systems) represent collective provisions that make vital services affordable and accessible to all. The availability of these public facilities directly improves health, literacy, and capabilities, which are the core parameters of human development. Therefore, income and public facilities are dual pillars required to achieve high human development.
4 marks

3. A region increases groundwater extraction every year to raise agricultural output. Explain why this can create a development problem.

Suggested Answer: Increasing groundwater extraction to push agricultural yields leads to overuse if extraction exceeds natural recharge from rainfall. Over time, water tables fall dramatically, raising extraction costs, depleting the resource, and causing land subsidence or salinization. This creates a severe developmental problem by compromising water security, risking future agricultural failure, and destroying the resource base needed for future generations (unsustainable growth).
5 marks

4. Compare the World Bank and UNDP approaches to development. Why is the UNDP approach broader?

Suggested Answer: The World Bank uses a single economic criterion, Per Capita Income (Average Income), to classify countries into development stages. In contrast, the UNDP uses the Human Development Index (HDI), which integrates economic income with life expectancy at birth (health indicator) and mean/expected years of schooling (education indicator). The UNDP approach is broader because development ultimately concerns expanding human capabilities, health, and literacy, rather than just raising monetary output. Income is viewed as a means to live a decent life, not the sole end.
5 marks

5. “The future of development is linked to sustainability.” Explain with both renewable and non-renewable resource examples from the chapter.

Suggested Answer: Sustainability means development that meets present needs without compromising the ability of future generations to meet theirs.
1. Renewable Resources (Groundwater): These are replenished by nature. However, if overused (extraction exceeds recharge), water tables collapse, creating scarcity. Thus, renewable resources require sustainable limits.
2. Non-Renewable Resources (Crude Oil): These exist in a finite stock and cannot be replaced once exhausted. If we consume crude oil rapidly without moving to renewable energy, future generations will face severe energy crises.
Therefore, to keep development going, we must manage resources efficiently, shift to renewable options, and minimize ecological damage.
