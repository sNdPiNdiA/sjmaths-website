Part A — Multiple Choice Questions (1 Mark)
2023, 2022

Q1. A consumer is one who:

Answer: (B) Buys goods/services for personal use
Explanation: A consumer is any individual or household that purchases goods or avails services for personal consumption, not for resale or production purposes.
2022, 2020

Q2. Which of the following is an example of consumer exploitation?

Answer: (C) Selling adulterated food products
Explanation: Adulteration — mixing inferior or harmful substances into food — is a classic form of consumer exploitation. It harms health and deceives buyers about what they are paying for.
2022, 2021

Q3. Consumers are exploited in markets because:

Answer: (B) Producers are few and powerful; consumers are many but dispersed
Explanation: This power imbalance is the root cause of consumer exploitation. A small number of organised producers can collectively influence prices, quality, and supply, while millions of individual, unorganised consumers have no countervailing power.
OEB

Q4. Which is NOT a form of consumer exploitation?

Answer: (D) ISI mark on products
Explanation: The ISI mark is a quality certification that protects consumers — it is NOT a form of exploitation. Underweighting, adulteration, and false ads are all exploitative practices that harm consumers.

Part D — Very Short Answer (2 Marks)
2022, 2021, 2020, 2019

VSA Q1. Why do consumers need protection in the marketplace? Give any two reasons.

Answer:
  1. Power imbalance: Producers are few and organised; consumers are many but scattered and individually weak — making consumers vulnerable to exploitation.
  2. Unfair practices: Without protection, sellers resort to adulteration, false advertising, short-weighing, and overcharging, harming consumers with no legal recourse.
2022, 2020, 2019

VSA Q2. What is meant by unfair trade practices? Give two examples.

Answer:
Unfair trade practices are deceptive or exploitative actions by sellers that harm consumers.

Examples:
  1. False advertising: Claiming a product cures a disease it cannot (e.g., spurious medical claims).
  2. Short-weighing: Selling a 500g packet that actually weighs only 450g.
Other examples: charging above MRP, selling adulterated/substandard goods, withholding bills.

Part D — Very Short Answer (2 Marks)
2023, 2022, 2021, 2020, 2019

VSA Q1. What is COPRA? When was it enacted?

Answer:
COPRA stands for the Consumer Protection Act. It is a law that protects consumers from unfair trade practices and provides them with a legal mechanism to seek redressal.

  • First enacted in 1986 as a result of the consumer movement.
  • Amended comprehensively in 2019 to expand coverage (e-commerce, product liability), revise claim limits, and establish the Central Consumer Protection Authority (CCPA).
  • Established a three-tier quasi-judicial redressal system: District Commission, State Commission, National Commission.
2022, 2021, 2020, 2019, 2016

VSA Q2. When did the consumer movement begin in India and why?

Answer:
The consumer movement gained momentum in India in the 1970s.

Reasons:
  1. Widespread food shortages, hoarding, and black marketing during this period led to public anger.
  2. Rampant adulteration of food products endangered health.
  3. Consumers were short-weighted, overcharged, and misled by false advertisements with no legal recourse.
  4. Inspired by the US consumer movement (Ralph Nader), Indian activists began forming consumer organisations to fight exploitation.
This pressure eventually led to the enactment of COPRA in 1986.
Part E — Short Answer (3 Marks)
2022, 2021, 2020, 2019, 2016

SA Q1. What is a consumer movement? How did it lead to the enactment of COPRA?

Answer:
The consumer movement is a social force that arose from dissatisfaction of consumers with unfair marketplace practices. It is a collective effort by consumers and organisations to demand their rights and legal protections.

How it led to COPRA:
  1. 1970s activism: Consumer organisations emerged across India to expose and fight adulteration, overcharging, short-weighing, and false advertising.
  2. Awareness campaigns: Groups published books and pamphlets exposing unsafe products and unfair practices.
  3. International influence: India was inspired by the US consumer movement and UN Guidelines for Consumer Protection (1985).
  4. Legal victory (1986): Decades of activism culminated in the Consumer Protection Act (COPRA) in 1986, giving consumers legal rights and a formal redressal mechanism.
  5. 2019 amendment: The movement continued pushing for stronger protections, resulting in the comprehensive 2019 amendment covering e-commerce and product liability.
2022, 2021, 2019

SA Q2. What are the responsibilities of a consumer under COPRA?

Answer: Rights come with responsibilities. Consumers must:
  1. Ask for a cash memo/bill: Proof of purchase is required to file a consumer complaint; always insist on a receipt.
  2. Check quality certification logos: Look for ISI, Agmark, or Hallmark before buying to ensure quality and safety.
  3. File complaints: Don't ignore exploitation — reporting unfair practices protects other consumers too.
  4. Read labels carefully: Check expiry dates, ingredients, MRP, and manufacturing details before purchase.
  5. Join or form consumer groups: Collective action is more effective than individual complaints in influencing policy and business practices.
Part F — Long Answer (5 Marks)
2022, 2021, 2019, 2016

LA Q1. Describe the consumer movement in India — its origin, growth, and role of COPRA.

Answer:
Origin:
  1. Consumer movement began as a social force in the 1970s due to rampant consumer exploitation — food adulteration, hoarding, short-weighing, and false advertising.
  2. Inspired by Ralph Nader's US consumer movement and the UN Guidelines for Consumer Protection (1985).
Growth:
  1. Consumer organisations multiplied across India, forming the consumer movement as a powerful social force.
  2. They published books exposing unsafe products, lodged complaints, and launched awareness campaigns.
  3. World Consumer Rights Day (March 15) became a platform for awareness and advocacy.
  4. The “Jago Grahak Jago” campaign by the government spread consumer awareness nationwide.
COPRA (1986) — Key Provisions:
  1. Gave consumers six legal rights: safety, information, choice, be heard, redressal, consumer education.
  2. Established three-tier quasi-judicial redressal machinery (District, State, National commissions).
  3. Covered both goods AND services (banking, insurance, healthcare, transport).
  4. 2019 Amendment: Higher claim limits, e-commerce coverage, product liability, CCPA established.
Impact:
  • Millions of consumers became aware of their rights.
  • Consumer court complaints increased dramatically.
  • Quality logos (ISI, Agmark, Hallmark) gained prominence.
  • RTI Act (2005) further empowered citizens against public authorities.
Limitation: Still low awareness in rural areas; more NGOs and grassroots consumer education are needed.

Q1. A consumer finds that the pressure cooker they bought has no safety valve — which right is violated?

Answer: (B) Right to safety
Explanation: The Right to Safety protects consumers from goods and services hazardous to life. A pressure cooker without a safety valve is a life-threatening product defect — a clear violation of this right.
2023, 2022, 2021

Q2. A shopkeeper refuses to give a bill after purchase. Which right is being denied?

Answer: (C) Right to information
Explanation: The Right to Information includes the right to receive a bill/receipt, which documents the transaction details (price, product, date). Without a bill, consumers also lose their ability to file complaints under COPRA.
2022, 2021, 2020

Q3. Displaying price, ingredients, batch number, and expiry date on a product package relates to:

Answer: (B) Right to information
Explanation: The Right to Information entitles consumers to accurate details about the goods they buy — including price, quantity, quality, ingredients, batch number, manufacturing date, and expiry date. This is why all packaged goods must display these details by law.
2022, 2020

Q4. Having a wide variety of products at competitive prices relates to the consumer's:

Answer: (C) Right to choose
Explanation: The Right to Choose ensures that consumers have access to a variety of goods and services at competitive prices without being forced or pressured to buy a particular brand. No shopkeeper can compel a consumer to buy a specific product.
2022, 2021, 2019

Q5. A patient who suffered medical negligence and received compensation exercised:

Answer: (C) Right to seek redressal
Explanation: The Right to Seek Redressal entitles consumers to compensation for defective goods or deficient services. This includes services by professionals like doctors — medical negligence is covered under COPRA, and victims can approach consumer courts for compensation.
2023, 2022, 2021

Q6. RTI (Right to Information Act 2005) empowers citizens by giving them the:

Answer: (D) Right to information from government offices
Explanation: The RTI Act 2005 is an extension of the consumer right to information into the public domain — it gives every citizen the right to request information from any government office, helping expose corruption and holding authorities accountable.
2022, 2021

Q7. The right that ensures consumer grievances are heard and addressed is:

Answer: (B) Right to be heard
Explanation: The Right to be Heard ensures that consumer interests and grievances receive due consideration from manufacturers, service providers, and government bodies. It is the right to have a voice — consumer courts and feedback mechanisms embody this right.

Part F — Long Answer (5 Marks)
2023, 2022, 2021, 2020, 2019, 2016

LA Q1. Explain any five consumer rights with examples. Why are these rights necessary?

Answer:
Consumers face exploitation — adulteration, overcharging, false ads, unsafe products. COPRA guarantees six rights to protect them:

  1. Right to Safety: Protects from goods/services hazardous to life.
    E.g., LPG cylinders must have safety valves; medicines tested before sale.
  2. Right to Information: Consumers must get correct details — price, quantity, ingredients, expiry, manufacturer.
    E.g., food packet must show all details; MRP must be marked on packaging.
  3. Right to Choose: Access to a variety of goods at competitive prices without pressure.
    E.g., no shopkeeper can force you to buy a particular brand of medicine or any product.
  4. Right to be Heard: Consumer grievances must be given due consideration.
    E.g., consumer courts under COPRA are specifically created to hear complaints.
  5. Right to Seek Redressal: Compensation for defective goods or deficient services.
    E.g., Reji Mathew received compensation for medical negligence under COPRA.
  6. Right to Consumer Education: Awareness of rights and remedies.
    E.g., “Jago Grahak Jago” campaign; RTI Act 2005 extends information rights to government.
Why necessary: Without these rights, producers — who are few but powerful — can exploit consumers freely. Laws without awareness are ineffective; these rights empower consumers to demand accountability and seek justice.
All six rights together create a comprehensive consumer protection framework covering safety, information, choice, voice, compensation, and education.

Explanation: The National Consumer Commission is the highest consumer court in India. Further appeal beyond it lies only with the Supreme Court of India, the apex court.

Answer: (B) Three-tier
Explanation: COPRA established a three-tier quasi-judicial consumer dispute redressal system with three levels: District Commission, State Commission, and National Commission.

Answer: (C) Quasi-judicial bodies
Explanation: Consumer commissions are quasi-judicial bodies — they have powers similar to civil courts (summon witnesses, examine documents, award compensation) but are designed to be simpler, faster, and cheaper than regular courts. Consumers can represent themselves without a lawyer.

Part E — Short Answer (3 Marks)

SA Q1. How does the three-tier quasi-judicial machinery under COPRA work?

Answer:
COPRA created three levels of consumer dispute redressal:
  1. District Consumer Commission: Handles claims up to ₹1 crore. The first point of complaint for most consumers. Located in every district to ensure accessibility.
  2. State Consumer Commission: Handles claims of ₹1–10 crore; also hears appeals against District Commission orders. Located at the state capital.
  3. National Consumer Commission: Handles claims above ₹10 crore; hears appeals against State Commission orders. Based in New Delhi. Further appeal lies with the Supreme Court.
Key features of this system:
  • All three are quasi-judicial bodies — faster, cheaper, and simpler than civil courts.
  • Consumers can represent themselves without a lawyer.
  • Covers both goods and services (banking, insurance, healthcare, transport).
  • Can award compensation, order replacement, or direct refunds.

Explanation: The ISI mark is granted by the Bureau of Indian Standards (BIS) — India's national standards body — only after testing that the product meets the relevant Indian Standards.

CBQ 4 — Q4. Why should consumers look for quality logos before buying?

Answer:
Quality logos certify that products have been tested and meet established safety and quality standards set by official agencies. They protect consumers because:
  1. They guarantee the product is safe to use (e.g., an ISI-marked electrical appliance won't cause electrocution).
  2. They ensure authenticity and prevent fraud (e.g., Hallmark on gold prevents buying impure metal).
  3. They certify the product is free from adulteration (e.g., Agmark on honey ensures purity).
Without such marks, consumers have no independent assurance of product quality or safety.

2026 · VSA

VSA. What is the significance of the RTI Act 2005 for consumers? Give two points.

Answer:
The Right to Information (RTI) Act 2005 gives every citizen the right to request information from any government office. Its significance for consumers:
  1. Transparency and accountability: RTI empowers citizens to expose corruption and malpractice in public services — helping consumers access information about decisions that affect their lives (e.g., ration distribution, public utility pricing).
  2. Extension of consumer rights: RTI effectively gives citizens the right to information from government — extending the COPRA's Right to Information beyond markets to the broader public administration domain.

2026 · LA

LA. Explain the various rights of consumers under the Consumer Protection Act. How do quality logos help in protecting consumers?

Answer:
Six Consumer Rights under COPRA:
  1. Right to Safety: Protection from goods/services hazardous to life. E.g., pressure cooker must have safety valve; LPG cylinder must meet BIS norms.
  2. Right to Information: Consumers must receive complete and accurate details — price, quantity, quality, ingredients, expiry. E.g., all packaged foods must display manufacturing and expiry dates.
  3. Right to Choose: Access to variety of goods at competitive prices without coercion. E.g., no shopkeeper can force a consumer to buy a specific brand.
  4. Right to be Heard: Consumer grievances must receive due consideration. E.g., consumer commissions exist to hear and adjudicate complaints.
  5. Right to Seek Redressal: Compensation for defective goods or deficient services. E.g., Reji Mathew received compensation for medical negligence.
  6. Right to Consumer Education: Awareness of rights and how to assert them. E.g., “Jago Grahak Jago” campaign; RTI Act 2005.
Role of Quality Logos in Consumer Protection:
  1. ISI (Bureau of Indian Standards): Certifies manufactured goods meet Indian safety standards (electrical appliances, helmets, LPG cylinders). Prevents unsafe products from reaching consumers.
  2. Agmark: Certifies agricultural commodities (honey, ghee, pulses) are pure and unadulterated. Protects consumers from food fraud.
  3. Hallmark: Certifies gold/silver jewellery purity, preventing jewellers from selling impure metal at pure-metal prices.
Without these logos, consumers must trust sellers blindly. These certifications shift quality assurance from individual judgment to official standards — making markets safer and more trustworthy.
Always look for ISI, Agmark, or Hallmark before buying — they are your quality guarantee in the marketplace.
