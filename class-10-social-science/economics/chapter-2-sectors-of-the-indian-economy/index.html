Complete Concept Notes

Chapter 2 organised into the full set of ideas needed for understanding, application and board-style questions.

1What is a Sector?

Economic activities can be grouped into sectors using a suitable criterion. This classification helps us study the structure and functioning of an economy.

  • People engage in activities that produce goods as well as services.
  • The chapter uses three major bases of classification: nature of activity; conditions of employment; and ownership of assets and delivery of services.
Classification is a tool for analysing economic activities in a useful way.

2Primary Sector

The primary sector includes activities that use natural resources directly to produce natural products.

  • Agriculture, dairy, fishing, forestry, mining and extraction of minerals are examples.
  • It is called primary because it forms the base for many other products.
  • It is also called the agriculture and related sector.
Natural resource → primary sector.

3Secondary Sector

The secondary sector changes natural products into other forms through manufacturing or industrial activity.

  • Examples include spinning yarn and weaving cloth from cotton, making sugar or gur from sugarcane, and making bricks and buildings.
  • Manufacturing may take place in a factory, workshop or even at home.
  • It is also called the industrial sector.
Natural product + manufacturing → secondary sector.

4Tertiary Sector

The tertiary sector provides services that support production and trade rather than producing a physical good itself.

  • Transport, storage, communication, banking and trade support the primary and secondary sectors.
  • Teachers, doctors, lawyers, administrative workers and personal-service providers are also part of the service sector.
  • IT-based services such as internet cafés, ATMs, call centres and software companies have become important.
Service/support activity → tertiary sector.

5Interdependence of the Three Sectors

The three sectors are highly interdependent. A change in one sector can affect the others.

  • Farmers need fertilisers, pumpsets, electricity, transport and markets.
  • Industries need agricultural raw materials and consumers need the goods produced.
  • Transport disruptions can prevent rural products from reaching urban consumers.
No sector functions in complete isolation.

6Value of Production

Because thousands of different goods and services cannot simply be added by counting physical quantities, economists use their monetary values.

  • For example, the value of wheat or coconuts is calculated from quantity × price.
  • The values of goods and services produced in the sectors can then be added.
Production is aggregated through value, not physical units.

7Final Goods and Intermediate Goods

Only final goods and services are counted in total production to avoid counting the same value more than once.

  • Intermediate goods are used up in producing final goods.
  • In the textbook example, wheat becomes flour and flour becomes biscuits.
  • The value of the final biscuits already contains the value of the intermediate flour and wheat.
Final good = reaches the consumer; intermediate good = used to make another good.

8GDP

Gross Domestic Product (GDP) is the value of all final goods and services produced within a country during a particular year.

  • GDP indicates the size of an economy.
  • Adding the production values of the three sectors gives the country's GDP.
  • The chapter explains that the government estimates GDP using information on production volumes and prices.
GDP = value of final goods + services produced within the country in a year.

9GVA

Gross Value Added (GVA) measures the contribution of sectors after adjusting for taxes and subsidies.

  • The chapter presents sectoral contribution through GVA to align with global practices.
  • The detailed difference between GDP and GVA is left for higher classes in the chapter.
For this chapter: use GVA to discuss sectoral contribution.

10Historical Shift in Developed Countries

The chapter describes a broad historical movement from primary to secondary and later from secondary to tertiary activities in developed countries.

  • Initially the primary sector dominated production and employment.
  • Industrialisation increased the importance of the secondary sector.
  • Later, the tertiary sector became dominant in production and employment in developed countries.
Primary → Secondary → Tertiary is the broad historical pattern.

11Changing Importance of Sectors in India

India has also experienced a major rise in the tertiary sector. Between 1977–78 and 2017–18, tertiary production increased the most and it became the largest producing sector.

  • All three sectors increased their production over the period.
  • The tertiary sector replaced the primary sector as the largest producing sector in 2017–18.
  • The chapter uses comparable data for these two years.
In production: tertiary becomes the largest by 2017–18.

12Why the Tertiary Sector is Growing

Several forces have increased the importance of services in India.

  • Basic services such as hospitals, schools, police, courts, transport and banking are required.
  • Agriculture and industry create demand for transport, trade, storage and related services.
  • Rising incomes increase demand for tourism, eating out, private hospitals, schools and professional training.
  • New ICT-based services have expanded rapidly.
Government needs + sectoral support + rising incomes + ICT → service growth.

13Production vs Employment in India

The shift in production has not been matched by a similar shift in employment.

  • The primary sector continues to employ the largest share of workers.
  • Industry and services have increased production much faster than their employment.
  • This mismatch is a major feature of India's sectoral structure discussed in the chapter.
Largest producing sector ≠ largest employer.

14Underemployment

Underemployment occurs when people are working but their labour is not fully utilised.

  • In agriculture, more people may work on a small plot than are actually needed.
  • Removing some workers may not reduce production.
  • The chapter calls this hidden underemployment disguised unemployment.
Working ≠ fully employed.

15Disguised Unemployment

Disguised unemployment is a form of underemployment in which people appear to be working, but some can leave without reducing output.

  • Laxmi's family example shows five members working on a small rain-fed plot even though fewer people could maintain production.
  • The people who move to other productive work can increase family income without reducing farm output.
If some workers leave and output stays the same → disguised unemployment.

16Employment Generation through Irrigation

Irrigation can create additional employment in agriculture by enabling more crops to be grown.

  • A well, canal or other irrigation facility can allow a second crop.
  • More cultivation creates work in sowing, watering, fertilising and harvesting.
  • Government spending or bank credit can help create such facilities.
Irrigation → more cropping → more work.

17Employment through Infrastructure and Markets

Employment can expand when better roads, transport, storage and marketing facilities connect farmers to markets.

  • Higher farm output creates demand for transport and trade.
  • Cold storage lets farmers store crops and sell when prices are favourable.
  • Agro-based processing units such as dal mills can create local employment.
Produce + connectivity + processing → productive employment.

18Other Employment Opportunities

Employment can also be expanded through education, healthcare, tourism, regional crafts and new services.

  • More schools require teachers and other staff.
  • Better healthcare requires doctors, nurses and health workers.
  • Tourism, IT and regional craft industries can create region-specific opportunities.
  • Government planning and support may be required.
Look for local potential + infrastructure + skills + support.

19Employment Guarantee

The chapter discusses the rural employment guarantee framework, originally MGNREGA 2005 and notes its replacement in 2025 by Viksit Bharat–G RAM G 2025.

  • MGNREGA 2005 guaranteed 100 days of employment in a year to eligible rural households in need of work.
  • The text notes an unemployment allowance if the government failed to provide employment.
  • The chapter says the 2025 Act replaced MGNREGA.
Remember the chapter's sequence: MGNREGA 2005 → Viksit Bharat–G RAM G 2025.

20Organised Sector

The organised sector consists of enterprises or workplaces where employment terms are regular and government rules and regulations are followed.

  • Workers generally have assured work and fixed working hours.
  • Benefits may include paid leave, provident fund, gratuity, medical benefits and pension.
  • Workers may receive overtime for work beyond fixed hours.
  • Enterprises are registered and covered by relevant laws.
Regular terms + registration + legal protection → organised sector.

21Unorganised Sector

The unorganised sector consists largely of small and scattered units where rules and regulations are often not followed.

  • Jobs are often low-paid and irregular.
  • There may be no paid leave, overtime, holidays or secure employment.
  • Workers can include street vendors, repair workers, casual labourers and self-employed workers.
  • The sector contains a large share of India's workers and needs protection.
Irregular work + weak protection → unorganised sector.

22Protection of Unorganised Workers

Workers in the unorganised sector need protection because employment is often insecure and earnings are low.

  • Rural workers include landless labourers, small and marginal farmers, sharecroppers and artisans.
  • Urban workers include casual construction workers, street vendors, head-load workers, garment makers and rag pickers.
  • Support can include timely seeds and inputs, affordable credit, storage, marketing, raw materials and labour protection.
  • Protection supports both economic and social development.
Protection = wages + safety/health + security + support.

23Public Sector and Private Sector

The public/private classification is based on ownership of assets and responsibility for delivering services.

  • In the public sector, the government owns most assets and provides services.
  • In the private sector, assets and services are owned or delivered by private individuals or companies.
  • Private-sector activities are generally guided by profit.
  • Public-sector activity is not driven only by profit.
Ownership is the criterion.

24Why Government Provides Public Services

Some activities require large investment or are difficult to provide privately at an affordable cost, so government undertakes them.

  • Examples include roads, bridges, railways, harbours, electricity generation and irrigation.
  • The government also has primary responsibility for health, education, safe drinking water, housing and nutrition.
  • Government can support industries and consumers through measures such as electricity support and procurement of food grains.
Public need + high cost + universal access → government role.

25Why Classifications Matter

Classification helps us analyse economic activities according to the question we want to answer.

  • Nature of activity → primary, secondary, tertiary.
  • Employment conditions → organised, unorganised.
  • Ownership and service delivery → public, private.
  • Each classification highlights different problems and possible solutions.
Choose the classification according to the criterion being studied.

Rapid Revision · Mnemonics · Tips & Tricks

Quick recall tools for definitions, comparisons, data questions and board answers.

Revision order

First recall the three classification systems, then GDP/GVA, sectoral change, employment, worker conditions and the public/private sector.

Sector Mnemonic — “P-S-T”

  • Primary = natural resources; Secondary = manufacturing; Tertiary = services.
Memory hook: P → S → T = Produce naturally → Shape/manufacture → Support with services.

Three Classifications — “N-E-O”

  • Nature of activity → Primary/Secondary/Tertiary. Employment conditions → Organised/Unorganised. Ownership → Public/Private.
Memory hook: N-E-O keeps all three classification bases in order.

Final vs Intermediate — “F reaches, I feeds”

  • Final goods reach consumers. Intermediate goods are used to make another final good.
Memory hook: Count the final value to avoid double counting.

GDP

  • GDP is the value of all final goods and services produced within a country during a particular year.
Memory hook: GDP question → remember “final + within country + one year”.

GVA

  • GVA measures sectoral contribution after adjusting for taxes and subsidies.
Memory hook: For this chapter, use GVA when comparing sector shares.

Historical Shift — “Farm → Factory → Services”

  • Developed countries generally moved from primary dominance to secondary dominance and later tertiary dominance.
Memory hook: The direction is the key idea, not merely the names.

India's Production Pattern

  • Tertiary became the largest producing sector in 2017–18, replacing primary.
Memory hook: Production leader = tertiary.

India's Employment Pattern

  • Primary sector continued to be the largest employer even while tertiary became the largest producer.
Memory hook: Remember: “largest producer ≠ largest employer”.

Disguised Unemployment

  • People appear employed, but some can leave without reducing output.
Memory hook: If output stays the same after removing workers → disguised unemployment.

Open vs Disguised

  • Open unemployment is visible because a person has no job. Disguised unemployment is hidden within apparent employment.
Memory hook: Open = no job; Disguised = too many people for the work.

Employment Creation

  • Irrigation, roads, storage, affordable credit, agro-processing, education, healthcare, tourism and regional industries can create employment.
Memory hook: Think “water + roads + credit + processing + services”.

Organised Sector

  • Regular employment, registration, fixed hours and legal/employee benefits characterise the organised sector.
Memory hook: Organised = rules + regularity + protection.

Unorganised Sector

  • Small/scattered units, irregular work, low wages and weak enforcement of protections characterise the unorganised sector.
Memory hook: Unorganised = uncertainty + weak protection.

Worker Protection

  • Unorganised workers need protection regarding wages, safety, health, security and access to support.
Memory hook: Write both rural and urban examples in long answers.

Public vs Private

  • Public = government ownership/responsibility. Private = private ownership/service delivery, generally with profit motive.
Memory hook: Criterion = ownership, not number of workers.

Why Public Sector Matters

  • Large investments, universal access and essential services make government involvement necessary.
Memory hook: Roads, railways, electricity, irrigation, health and education are key examples.

Exam Answer Tip

  • For comparison questions, make a two-column distinction and then add one example.
Memory hook: Definition → 2–3 differences → example → conclusion.

Data Question Tip

  • When a graph/table is given, first identify the largest/smallest, then compare change over time, then state the conclusion.
Memory hook: Do not only copy numbers; explain what the change means.
