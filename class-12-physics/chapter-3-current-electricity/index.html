Electric Current & Steady Flows

Fundamental Definition

3.2.1 Electric Current ($I$) & Current Density ($\mathbf{j}$)

  • Electric Current ($I$): Rate of flow of net electric charge across a cross-section:
$$I = \lim_{\Delta t \to 0} \frac{\Delta Q}{\Delta t} = \frac{dQ}{dt}$$
  • SI Unit: Ampere ($\text{A} \equiv \text{C s}^{-1}$). It is an SI Base Unit. Current is a scalar quantity (follows algebraic addition, not vector laws).
  • Conventional Direction: Direction of flow of positive charge (opposite to the drift of electrons).
  • Current Density ($\mathbf{j}$): Current per unit area normal to flow. It is a vector quantity:
$$\mathbf{j} = \frac{I}{A}\,\hat{\mathbf{n}}, \qquad I = \int \mathbf{j} \cdot d\mathbf{S} = j A \cos\theta$$

Ohm's Law & Electrical Resistance

Ohm's Law & Resistivity

3.4.1 Scalar & Vector Form of Ohm's Law

Ohm's law states that current $I$ flowing through a conductor is directly proportional to potential difference $V$ across its ends, provided temperature and other physical conditions remain constant.

$$V = I R, \qquad R = \rho \frac{l}{A} = \frac{l}{\sigma A}$$
  • Resistance ($R$): Opposition offered by conductor to current flow. SI Unit: Ohm ($\Omega \equiv \text{V A}^{-1}$). Dimensions: $[M L^2 T^{-3} A^{-2}]$.
  • Resistivity ($\rho$): Intrinsic material property independent of dimensions ($\rho = R A / l$). SI Unit: $\Omega\text{ m}$.
  • Conductivity ($\sigma$): Reciprocal of resistivity ($\sigma = 1/\rho$). SI Unit: $\text{S m}^{-1}$ or $\Omega^{-1}\text{ m}^{-1}$.
  • Vector (Microscopic) Form of Ohm's Law:
$$\mathbf{j} = \sigma \mathbf{E} = \frac{\mathbf{E}}{\rho}$$

Drift of Electrons & Origin of Resistivity

Microscopic Derivations

3.5.1 Drift Velocity ($v_d$), Mobility ($\mu$) & Relaxation Time ($\tau$)

  • Thermal Velocity: In absence of field, electrons move randomly with speeds $\sim 10^5\text{ m s}^{-1}$, but average thermal velocity $\langle\mathbf{u}\rangle = \mathbf{0}$.
  • Acceleration under Field $\mathbf{E}$: $\mathbf{a} = -\frac{e\mathbf{E}}{m}$.
  • Drift Velocity ($\mathbf{v}_d$): Average velocity acquired by electrons opposite to field:
$$\mathbf{v}_d = - \frac{e\mathbf{E}}{m}\tau, \qquad v_d = \frac{e E \tau}{m}$$
  • Relaxation Time ($\tau$): Average time interval between two successive collisions ($\sim 10^{-14}\text{ s}$).
  • Current in terms of $v_d$: Total charge passing area $A$ in time $\Delta t$ is $\Delta Q = n e A v_d \Delta t$:
$$I = n e A v_d, \qquad j = n e v_d = \frac{n e^2 \tau}{m} E$$
  • Microscopic Formula for Resistivity ($\rho$) & Resistance ($R$):
$$\rho = \frac{m}{n e^2 \tau}, \qquad R = \left(\frac{m}{n e^2 \tau}\right)\frac{l}{A}$$
  • Mobility ($\mu$): Magnitude of drift velocity per unit electric field:
$$\mu = \frac{v_d}{E} = \frac{e\tau}{m} \quad (\text{SI Unit: }\text{m}^2\text{ V}^{-1}\text{ s}^{-1})$$
Example 3.1

Drift Speed of Conduction Electrons in Copper Wire

Estimate the average drift speed of conduction electrons in a copper wire of cross-sectional area $1.0 \times 10^{-7}\text{ m}^2$ carrying a current of $1.5\text{ A}$. Density of copper is $9.0 \times 10^3\text{ kg m}^{-3}$, atomic mass is $63.5\text{ u}$.

Number density of electrons ($n$):
$n = \frac{6.02 \times 10^{23} \times (9.0 \times 10^3\text{ kg m}^{-3})}{63.5 \times 10^{-3}\text{ kg}} \approx 8.5 \times 10^{28}\text{ m}^{-3}$.
Drift speed ($v_d$):
$v_d = \frac{I}{n e A} = \frac{1.5}{(8.5 \times 10^{28}) \times (1.6 \times 10^{-19}) \times (1.0 \times 10^{-7})} \approx 1.1 \times 10^{-3}\text{ m s}^{-1} = 1.1\text{ mm s}^{-1}$.
Answer: $v_d \approx 1.1\text{ mm s}^{-1}$. (Extremely small compared to thermal speed $10^5\text{ m s}^{-1}$ and EM wave speed $3 \times 10^8\text{ m s}^{-1}$).

Non-Ohmic Devices & Temperature Dependence

Temperature Coefficients & Limits

3.6.1 Limitations of Ohm's Law & Temperature Coefficient ($\alpha$)

  • Limitations of Ohm's Law (Non-Ohmic Behavior):
    1. $V$ ceases to be proportional to $I$ (e.g. good conductor at high currents heating up).
    2. Relation between $V$ and $I$ depends on sign of $V$ (e.g. p-n junction diode forward vs reverse bias).
    3. Relation between $V$ and $I$ is not unique — negative resistance region (e.g. GaAs thyristor).
  • Temperature Dependence of Resistivity ($\rho(T)$):
$$\rho(T) = \rho_0 [1 + \alpha(T - T_0)], \qquad R(T) = R_0 [1 + \alpha(T - T_0)]$$
  • Temperature Coefficient ($\alpha$): $\alpha = \frac{1}{\rho_0}\frac{d\rho}{dT}$ (Unit: $\text{K}^{-1}$ or $^\circ\text{C}^{-1}$).
    • Metals (Conductors): $\alpha > 0$ (Positive). As temperature increases, lattice vibrations increase, relaxation time $\tau$ decreases $\implies$ resistivity increases ($\rho \uparrow$).
    • Alloys (Nichrome, Manganin, Constantan): Very high resistivity and almost zero $\alpha$. Ideal for standard resistance coils.
    • Semiconductors (Carbon, Silicon, Germanium): $\alpha < 0$ (Negative). As temperature increases, carrier concentration $n$ increases exponentially ($n \propto e^{-E_g/k_B T}$), dominating over the slight decrease in $\tau \implies$ resistivity decreases ($\rho \downarrow$).

    • 3.7.1 Resistors in Series and Parallel Derivations

    • Series Combination (Current $I$ same through all):
      Total potential drop: $V = V_1 + V_2 + \dots + V_n = I R_1 + I R_2 + \dots + I R_n = I(R_1 + R_2 + \dots + R_n)$.
    $$R_{\text{eq}} = R_1 + R_2 + \dots + R_n$$
    • Parallel Combination (Potential difference $V$ same across all):
      Total current: $I = I_1 + I_2 + \dots + I_n = \frac{V}{R_1} + \frac{V}{R_2} + \dots + \frac{V}{R_n} = V\left(\frac{1}{R_1} + \frac{1}{R_2} + \dots + \frac{1}{R_n}\right)$.
    $$\frac{1}{R_{\text{eq}}} = \frac{1}{R_1} + \frac{1}{R_2} + \dots + \frac{1}{R_n}, \qquad R_{\text{eq}} = \frac{R_1 R_2}{R_1 + R_2} \quad (\text{for 2 resistors})$$
Example 3.2

Temperature of Heating Element of Electric Toaster

An electric toaster uses nichrome for heating element. When negligible current flows through it at room temperature ($27.0^\circ\text{C}$), resistance is $75.3\,\Omega$. When connected to $230\text{ V}$ supply, current settles to $2.68\text{ A}$. What is steady temperature of element? ($\alpha = 1.70 \times 10^{-4}\;^\circ\text{C}^{-1}$).

Steady Resistance ($R_T$): $R_T = \frac{V}{I} = \frac{230\text{ V}}{2.68\text{ A}} = 85.8\,\Omega$.
Temperature Rise ($\Delta T$):
$R_T = R_0[1 + \alpha(T - T_0)] \implies T - T_0 = \frac{R_T - R_0}{\alpha R_0} = \frac{85.8 - 75.3}{(1.70 \times 10^{-4}) \times 75.3} = \frac{10.5}{0.0128} \approx 820^\circ\text{C}$.
Steady Temperature ($T$): $T = T_0 + 820^\circ\text{C} = 27.0 + 820 = 847^\circ\text{C}$.
Answer: $T \approx 847^\circ\text{C}$ (or $847.2^\circ\text{C}$).

Electrical Energy and Power

Joule's Heating & Power Transmission

3.8.1 Electric Power ($P$) & High-Voltage Transmission

$$P = V I = I^2 R = \frac{V^2}{R}, \qquad \Delta W = I^2 R \Delta t$$
  • Joule Heating: Energy dissipated as heat due to collisions of drifting electrons with metal lattice ions.
  • High-Voltage Transmission Principle: For transmitting power $P$ at voltage $V$ over cable of resistance $R_c$: $I = \frac{P}{V} \implies P_{\text{loss}} = I^2 R_c = \frac{P^2 R_c}{V^2}$ Hence, transmitting electric power at very high voltages minimizes line dissipation loss ($P_{\text{loss}} \propto 1/V^2$).

Cells, EMF, Internal Resistance & Combinations

Cell Physics & Grouping Derivations

3.9.1 Electromotive Force ($\mathcal{E}$) & Cell Combination Derivations

  • EMF ($\mathcal{E}$): Potential difference between the positive and negative electrodes in open circuit ($I = 0$). $\mathcal{E} = V_+ - (-V_-) = V_+ + V_- > 0$.
  • Terminal Voltage ($V$) & Internal Resistance ($r$): When current $I$ is drawn from cell: $$V = V_A - V_B = \mathcal{E} - I r \implies I = \frac{\mathcal{E}}{R + r}, \qquad r = \left(\frac{\mathcal{E}}{V} - 1\right)R$$
  • Series Combination Derivation ($n$ cells in series):
    For two cells: $V_{AC} = V_{AB} + V_{BC} = (\mathcal{E}_1 - I r_1) + (\mathcal{E}_2 - I r_2) = (\mathcal{E}_1 + \mathcal{E}_2) - I(r_1 + r_2)$.
    Comparing with $V = \mathcal{E}_{\text{eq}} - I r_{\text{eq}}$:
$$\mathcal{E}_{\text{eq}} = \mathcal{E}_1 + \mathcal{E}_2 + \dots + \mathcal{E}_n, \qquad r_{\text{eq}} = r_1 + r_2 + \dots + r_n$$
  • If one cell of EMF $\mathcal{E}_2$ is connected in opposition: $\mathcal{E}_{\text{eq}} = \mathcal{E}_1 - \mathcal{E}_2$ (assuming $\mathcal{E}_1 > \mathcal{E}_2$), but internal resistances still add: $r_{\text{eq}} = r_1 + r_2$.
  • Parallel Combination Derivation (Two cells $\mathcal{E}_1, r_1$ and $\mathcal{E}_2, r_2$):
    Let total current $I = I_1 + I_2$.
    Terminal potential across both cells is identical: $V = \mathcal{E}_1 - I_1 r_1 = \mathcal{E}_2 - I_2 r_2 \implies I_1 = \frac{\mathcal{E}_1 - V}{r_1}, \; I_2 = \frac{\mathcal{E}_2 - V}{r_2}$.
    $I = \left(\frac{\mathcal{E}_1 - V}{r_1}\right) + \left(\frac{\mathcal{E}_2 - V}{r_2}\right) = \left(\frac{\mathcal{E}_1}{r_1} + \frac{\mathcal{E}_2}{r_2}\right) - V\left(\frac{1}{r_1} + \frac{1}{r_2}\right)$.
    Rearranging in the standard form $V = \mathcal{E}_{\text{eq}} - I r_{\text{eq}}$ gives:
$$\mathcal{E}_{\text{eq}} = \frac{\mathcal{E}_1 r_2 + \mathcal{E}_2 r_1}{r_1 + r_2} = \frac{\frac{\mathcal{E}_1}{r_1} + \frac{\mathcal{E}_2}{r_2}}{\frac{1}{r_1} + \frac{1}{r_2}}, \qquad \frac{1}{r_{\text{eq}}} = \frac{1}{r_1} + \frac{1}{r_2}$$
Example 3.4

EMF and Internal Resistance of Battery

A battery of EMF $10\text{ V}$ and internal resistance $3\,\Omega$ is connected to a resistor. If current in circuit is $0.5\text{ A}$, what is resistance of resistor and terminal voltage of battery?

Circuit Resistance ($R$): $I = \frac{\mathcal{E}}{R + r} \implies 0.5 = \frac{10}{R + 3} \implies R + 3 = 20 \implies R = 17\,\Omega$.
Terminal Voltage ($V$): $V = I R = 0.5\text{ A} \times 17\,\Omega = 8.5\text{ V}$ (or $V = \mathcal{E} - I r = 10 - 0.5 \times 3 = 8.5\text{ V}$).
Answers: $R = 17\,\Omega$, $V = 8.5\text{ V}$.

Kirchhoff's Rules & Wheatstone Bridge

Circuit Analysis Laws & Derivations

3.10.1 Junction Rule, Loop Rule & Wheatstone Bridge Derivation

  • Kirchhoff's First Rule (Junction Rule): At any junction of circuit paths, $\sum I = 0$ (Current entering $=$ Current leaving). Based on Conservation of Electric Charge.
  • Kirchhoff's Second Rule (Loop Rule): Around any closed mesh, $\sum \Delta V = 0$ or $\sum \mathcal{E} = \sum I R$. Based on Conservation of Energy.
  • Wheatstone Bridge Derivation: Consider 4 resistors $R_1, R_2, R_3, R_4$ in closed bridge ABCD with galvanometer $G$ across BD and battery across AC.
    Applying loop rules in balanced state ($I_g = 0 \implies I_1 = I_2$ and $I_3 = I_4$):
    • Loop ABDA: $I_1 R_1 + 0 - I_3 R_3 = 0 \implies I_1 R_1 = I_3 R_3 \quad \text{--- (1)}$
    • Loop BCDB: $I_2 R_2 - I_4 R_4 - 0 = 0 \implies I_1 R_2 = I_3 R_4 \quad \text{--- (2)}$
    • Dividing Eq. (1) by Eq. (2):
$$\text{Balanced Condition: } \qquad \frac{R_1}{R_2} = \frac{R_3}{R_4} \qquad \text{or} \qquad R_4 = R_3 \left(\frac{R_2}{R_1}\right)$$
  • Conjugate Arms Principle: If battery and galvanometer positions are interchanged, balance condition remains completely unaffected.
  • Metre Bridge: Practical application of Wheatstone bridge using a uniform wire of $100\text{ cm}$: $$R = S \left(\frac{l_1}{100 - l_1}\right)$$
Example 3.6

Equivalent Resistance of 12-Resistor Cube Network

A battery of $10\text{ V}$ and negligible internal resistance is connected across diagonally opposite corners of a cubical network of 12 identical resistors, each of resistance $r = 1\,\Omega$. Determine equivalent resistance $R_{\text{eq}}$ and current along each edge.

Symmetry Current Distribution: Total current $3I$ enters corner A, splitting into $I, I, I$ along three edges AB, AD, AA'. Each $I$ splits into $I/2, I/2$ at next vertices, recombining to $I$ and exiting at opposite corner C' as $3I$.
Loop Equation: $\mathcal{E} = I r + \frac{I}{2} r + I r = \frac{5}{2} I r$.
Also $\mathcal{E} = (3I) R_{\text{eq}} \implies 3I R_{\text{eq}} = \frac{5}{2} I r \implies R_{\text{eq}} = \frac{5}{6} r$.
Numerical Values ($r = 1\,\Omega, \mathcal{E} = 10\text{ V}$):
$R_{\text{eq}} = \frac{5}{6}\,\Omega \approx 0.83\,\Omega$. Total current $I_{\text{tot}} = \frac{10\text{ V}}{5/6\,\Omega} = 12\text{ A}$. Edge currents are $4\text{ A}$ (outer edges) and $2\text{ A}$ (middle edges).
Answer: $R_{\text{eq}} = \frac{5}{6} r = \frac{5}{6}\,\Omega$, Total current $= 12\text{ A}$.

Q1 UNANSWERED

When a steady current flows through a metallic conductor of non-uniform cross-section, the quantity that remains constant throughout is:

Option A is correct. By conservation of charge in steady state, the rate of flow of charge across any cross-section is identical. Current density $j$, electric field $E$, and drift velocity $v_d$ vary inversely with area $A$.
Option B is incorrect. $j = I/A$, which increases where the conductor narrows.
Option C is incorrect. $v_d = I / (n e A) \propto 1/A$.
Option D is incorrect. $E = \rho j = \rho I / A \propto 1/A$.
Core Rule
Current $I$ is constant along a single non-uniform conductor; $j, E, v_d \propto 1/A$.
Q2 UNANSWERED

If the length of a wire of resistance $R$ is stretched uniformly to $n$ times its original length, its new resistance will be:

Option A is correct. Volume $V = A l$ remains constant. When stretched to $l' = n l$, area becomes $A' = A/n$. Thus $R' = \rho \frac{l'}{A'} = \rho \frac{n l}{A/n} = n^2 \rho \frac{l}{A} = n^2 R$.
Option B is incorrect. This neglects the simultaneous reduction in cross-sectional area.
Option C is incorrect. Resistance increases upon stretching.
Option D is incorrect. Stretching increases length and decreases area.
Core Rule
For uniform stretching with constant mass/volume: $R' = n^2 R$.
Q3 UNANSWERED

For a semiconductor, the temperature coefficient of resistivity ($\alpha$) is:

Option A is correct. As temperature increases, covalent bonds break and free charge carrier density $n$ increases exponentially ($n \propto e^{-E_g/k_B T}$), causing resistivity to decrease ($\rho \downarrow \implies \alpha < 0$).
Option B is incorrect. Positive $\alpha$ is a characteristic of metals (conductors).
Option C is incorrect. Standard alloys have near-zero $\alpha$, not semiconductors.
Option D is incorrect. $\alpha$ is finite and negative.
Core Rule
Semiconductors: $\alpha < 0$ (resistivity decreases with temperature). Metals: $\alpha > 0$.
Q4 UNANSWERED

Kirchhoff's First Rule ($\sum I = 0$) and Second Rule ($\sum \Delta V = 0$) are based respectively on conservation of:

Option A is correct. Junction rule represents conservation of charge (no accumulation at nodes). Loop rule represents conservation of energy (electrostatic field is conservative).
Option B is incorrect. Order is reversed.
Option C is incorrect. Kirchhoff's laws stem from charge and energy conservation.
Option D is incorrect. Loop rule is based on energy conservation.
Core Rule
Junction Rule $\leftrightarrow$ Conservation of Charge; Loop Rule $\leftrightarrow$ Conservation of Energy.
Q5 UNANSWERED

In a balanced Wheatstone bridge, if the positions of the galvanometer and cell are interchanged, the balance condition:

Option A is correct. Conjugate arms principle: Interchanging galvanometer and battery arms does not alter the balance condition ($\frac{R_1}{R_2} = \frac{R_3}{R_4}$).
Option B is incorrect. The bridge remains in balance.
Option C is incorrect. Galvanometer current remains zero.
Option D is incorrect. Null point is preserved.
Core Rule
Conjugate arms: Balance condition of Wheatstone bridge is independent of interchanging battery and galvanometer.

Ex 3.1 Maximum Car Battery Current

3.1 The storage battery of a car has an EMF of $12\text{ V}$. If the internal resistance of the battery is $0.4\,\Omega$, what is the maximum current that can be drawn from the battery?

$I_{\text{max}} = 30\text{ A}$.
Formula: Maximum current is drawn when external load resistance $R = 0$ (short circuit): $I_{\text{max}} = \frac{\mathcal{E}}{r}$
Calculation: $I_{\text{max}} = \frac{12\text{ V}}{0.4\,\Omega} = 30\text{ A}$.
Ex 3.2 Internal Resistance from Load

3.2 A battery of EMF $10\text{ V}$ and internal resistance $3\,\Omega$ is connected to a resistor. If the current in the circuit is $0.5\text{ A}$, what is the resistance of the resistor? What is the terminal voltage of the battery when the circuit is closed?

Resistance $R = 17\,\Omega$ • Terminal Voltage $V = 8.5\text{ V}$.
Circuit Resistance ($R$): $I = \frac{\mathcal{E}}{R + r} \implies 0.5 = \frac{10}{R + 3} \implies R + 3 = 20 \implies R = 17\,\Omega$.
Terminal Voltage ($V$): $V = I R = 0.5\text{ A} \times 17\,\Omega = 8.5\text{ V}$ (or $V = \mathcal{E} - I r = 10 - 0.5 \times 3 = 8.5\text{ V}$).
Ex 3.3 Resistors in Series

3.3 (a) Three resistors $1\,\Omega, 2\,\Omega$, and $3\,\Omega$ are combined in series. What is the total resistance of the combination? (b) If the combination is connected to a battery of EMF $12\text{ V}$ and negligible internal resistance, obtain the potential drop across each resistor.

(a) $R_{\text{eq}} = 6\,\Omega$ • (b) $V_1 = 2\text{ V}, V_2 = 4\text{ V}, V_3 = 6\text{ V}$.
(a) Total Resistance: $R_{\text{eq}} = R_1 + R_2 + R_3 = 1 + 2 + 3 = 6\,\Omega$.
(b) Circuit Current & Voltage Drops:
$I = \frac{\mathcal{E}}{R_{\text{eq}}} = \frac{12\text{ V}}{6\,\Omega} = 2\text{ A}$.
$V_1 = I R_1 = 2 \times 1 = 2\text{ V}$.
$V_2 = I R_2 = 2 \times 2 = 4\text{ V}$.
$V_3 = I R_3 = 2 \times 3 = 6\text{ V}$.
Ex 3.4 Resistors in Parallel

3.4 (a) Three resistors $2\,\Omega, 4\,\Omega$, and $5\,\Omega$ are combined in parallel. What is the total resistance of the combination? (b) If connected to a $20\text{ V}$ battery of negligible internal resistance, determine current through each resistor and total current drawn.

(a) $R_{\text{eq}} = \frac{20}{19}\,\Omega$ • (b) $I_1 = 10\text{ A}, I_2 = 5\text{ A}, I_3 = 4\text{ A}$, Total $I = 19\text{ A}$.
(a) Equivalent Resistance: $\frac{1}{R_{\text{eq}}} = \frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{4} + \frac{1}{5} = \frac{10 + 5 + 4}{20} = \frac{19}{20} \implies R_{\text{eq}} = \frac{20}{19}\,\Omega \approx 1.05\,\Omega$.
(b) Branch & Total Currents ($I = V/R$):
$I_1 = \frac{20\text{ V}}{2\,\Omega} = 10\text{ A}$.
$I_2 = \frac{20\text{ V}}{4\,\Omega} = 5\text{ A}$.
$I_3 = \frac{20\text{ V}}{5\,\Omega} = 4\text{ A}$.
$I_{\text{total}} = I_1 + I_2 + I_3 = 10 + 5 + 4 = 19\text{ A}$ (or $I = \frac{V}{R_{\text{eq}}} = \frac{20}{20/19} = 19\text{ A}$).
Ex 3.5 Temperature Coefficient of Platinum

3.5 At room temperature ($27.0^\circ\text{C}$) the resistance of a heating element is $100\,\Omega$. What is the temperature of the element if the resistance is found to be $117\,\Omega$, given that the temperature coefficient of the material of the resistor is $1.70 \times 10^{-4}\;^\circ\text{C}^{-1}$?

$T = 1027^\circ\text{C}$.
Formula: $R_T = R_0[1 + \alpha(T - T_0)] \implies T - T_0 = \frac{R_T - R_0}{\alpha R_0}$.
Calculation:
$T - 27.0 = \frac{117 - 100}{(1.70 \times 10^{-4}) \times 100} = \frac{17}{0.017} = 1000^\circ\text{C}$.
$T = 1000 + 27.0 = 1027^\circ\text{C}$.
Ex 3.6 Resistivity Calculation

3.6 A negligibly small current is passed through a wire of length $15\text{ m}$ and uniform cross-section $6.0 \times 10^{-7}\text{ m}^2$, and its resistance is measured to be $5.0\,\Omega$. What is the resistivity of the material at the temperature of the experiment?

$\rho = 2.0 \times 10^{-7}\,\Omega\text{ m}$.
Formula: $\rho = \frac{R A}{l}$.
Calculation: $\rho = \frac{5.0\,\Omega \times (6.0 \times 10^{-7}\text{ m}^2)}{15\text{ m}} = \frac{30.0 \times 10^{-7}}{15} = 2.0 \times 10^{-7}\,\Omega\text{ m}$.
Ex 3.7 Silver Wire Temperature Coefficient

3.7 A silver wire has a resistance of $2.1\,\Omega$ at $27.5^\circ\text{C}$, and a resistance of $2.7\,\Omega$ at $100^\circ\text{C}$. Determine the temperature coefficient of resistivity of silver.

$\alpha = 0.0039\;^\circ\text{C}^{-1} = 3.9 \times 10^{-3}\;^\circ\text{C}^{-1}$.
Formula: $\alpha = \frac{R_2 - R_1}{R_1(T_2 - T_1)}$.
Calculation:
$\Delta R = 2.7 - 2.1 = 0.6\,\Omega$.
$\Delta T = 100 - 27.5 = 72.5^\circ\text{C}$.
$\alpha = \frac{0.6}{2.1 \times 72.5} = \frac{0.6}{152.25} \approx 0.00394\;^\circ\text{C}^{-1} \approx 3.9 \times 10^{-3}\;^\circ\text{C}^{-1}$.
Ex 3.8 Nichrome Heating Element

3.8 A heating element using nichrome connected to a $230\text{ V}$ supply draws an initial current of $3.2\text{ A}$ which settles after a few seconds to a steady value of $2.8\text{ A}$. What is the steady temperature of the heating element if the room temperature is $27.0^\circ\text{C}$? Temperature coefficient of resistance of nichrome averaged over the temperature range involved is $1.70 \times 10^{-4}\;^\circ\text{C}^{-1}$.

Steady Temperature $T \approx 867^\circ\text{C}$.
Initial & Steady Resistance:
$R_1 = \frac{V}{I_1} = \frac{230\text{ V}}{3.2\text{ A}} = 71.875\,\Omega$ (at $T_1 = 27.0^\circ\text{C}$).
$R_2 = \frac{V}{I_2} = \frac{230\text{ V}}{2.8\text{ A}} = 82.143\,\Omega$.
Temperature Rise:
$T_2 - T_1 = \frac{R_2 - R_1}{\alpha R_1} = \frac{82.143 - 71.875}{(1.70 \times 10^{-4}) \times 71.875} = \frac{10.268}{0.012219} \approx 840.3^\circ\text{C}$.
$T_2 = 27.0 + 840.3 = 867.3^\circ\text{C} \approx 867^\circ\text{C}$.
Ex 3.9 Wheatstone Bridge Network Currents

3.9 Determine the current in each branch of the network shown in Fig. 3.30 (Wheatstone bridge with $AB = 10\,\Omega, BC = 5\,\Omega, CD = 10\,\Omega, AD = 5\,\Omega, BD = 15\,\Omega$ and battery $\mathcal{E} = 10\text{ V}$ across AC).

$I_{AB} = \frac{4}{17}\text{ A}, I_{BC} = \frac{6}{17}\text{ A}, I_{AD} = \frac{6}{17}\text{ A}, I_{DC} = \frac{4}{17}\text{ A}, I_{BD} = -\frac{2}{17}\text{ A}$, Total $I = \frac{10}{17}\text{ A}$.
Applying Kirchhoff's Loop Rules:
Let current in AB be $I_1$, in AD be $I_2$, and in galvanometer/diagonal BD be $I_g$.
Loop ABDA: $10 I_1 + 15 I_g - 5 I_2 = 0 \implies 2 I_1 + 3 I_g - I_2 = 0$.
Loop BCDB: $5(I_1 - I_g) - 10(I_2 + I_g) - 15 I_g = 0 \implies I_1 - 2 I_2 - 6 I_g = 0$.
Loop ADCA with $10\text{ V}$ battery: $5 I_2 + 10(I_2 + I_g) = 10 \implies 15 I_2 + 10 I_g = 10$.
Solving System of Equations:
$I_1 = \frac{4}{17}\text{ A} \approx 0.235\text{ A}$, $I_2 = \frac{6}{17}\text{ A} \approx 0.353\text{ A}$, $I_g = -\frac{2}{17}\text{ A} \approx -0.118\text{ A}$ (current flows from D to B).
Total current $I = I_1 + I_2 = \frac{10}{17}\text{ A} \approx 0.588\text{ A}$.
Ex 3.10 Metre Bridge Unknown Resistor

3.10 (a) In a metre bridge, the balance point is found to be at $39.5\text{ cm}$ from the end A, when the resistor $Y$ is of $12.5\,\Omega$. Determine the resistance of $X$. Why are the connections between resistors in a Wheatstone or metre bridge made of thick copper strips? (b) Determine the balance point of the bridge above if $X$ and $Y$ are interchanged. (c) What happens if the galvanometer and cell are interchanged at the balance point of the bridge? Would the galvanometer show any current?

(a) $X \approx 8.16\,\Omega$ • (b) Balance point $= 60.5\text{ cm}$ from A • (c) Galvanometer still shows zero current.
(a) Resistance $X$: $\frac{X}{Y} = \frac{l_1}{100 - l_1} \implies X = 12.5 \times \frac{39.5}{60.5} = \frac{493.75}{60.5} \approx 8.16\,\Omega$.
Thick copper strips: Thick copper strips have large cross-sectional area and very low resistivity, minimizing end-strip contact resistance.
(b) Interchanged $X$ and $Y$: $l_1' = 100 - 39.5 = 60.5\text{ cm}$ from end A.
(c) Interchanging galvanometer and battery: The condition for balance remains unchanged; the galvanometer will continue to show zero deflection.
Ex 3.11 Storage Battery Charging

3.11 A storage battery of EMF $8.0\text{ V}$ and internal resistance $0.5\,\Omega$ is being charged by a $120\text{ V}$ dc supply using a series resistor of $15.5\,\Omega$. What is the terminal voltage of the battery during charging? What is the purpose of having a series resistor in the charging circuit?

Terminal Voltage $V = 11.5\text{ V}$ • Series resistor limits the charging current to a safe value.
Effective Net Voltage in Circuit: $V_{\text{net}} = V_{\text{supply}} - \mathcal{E} = 120 - 8.0 = 112\text{ V}$.
Charging Current ($I$): $I = \frac{V_{\text{net}}}{R + r} = \frac{112}{15.5 + 0.5} = \frac{112}{16} = 7.0\text{ A}$.
Terminal Voltage During Charging: $V = \mathcal{E} + I r = 8.0 + (7.0 \times 0.5) = 8.0 + 3.5 = 11.5\text{ V}$.
Purpose of Series Resistor: Without the series resistor $R$, the current would be dangerously high ($I = \frac{112}{0.5} = 224\text{ A}$), which would destroy the battery and cause overheating/fire.

Points to Ponder

  1. Scalar Nature of Current: Although current has a specified direction along a wire, it is a scalar quantity because it does not obey the parallelogram law of vector addition; currents at a junction add algebraically ($\sum I_{\text{in}} = \sum I_{\text{out}}$).
  2. Speed of Current vs Electron Drift: Electric current and electric field propagate along conductors with the speed of electromagnetic waves ($\approx 3 \times 10^8\text{ m s}^{-1}$), whereas individual electron drift speed is extremely slow ($\sim 1\text{ mm s}^{-1}$).
  3. Origin of Joule Heat: Thermal energy generated in a current-carrying resistor originates from work done by the electric field on electrons, which is transferred to the lattice via repeated inelastic collisions.
  4. EMF is Not a Mechanical Force: Electromotive force (EMF) is not a force in the mechanical sense (unit is Newton), but work done per unit charge (unit is Volt $\equiv\text{J C}^{-1}$).
  5. Internal Resistance Variation: The internal resistance of a secondary cell is not strictly constant; it increases as the cell discharges and electrolyte concentration drops.
  6. Conjugate Arms of Wheatstone Bridge: In a balanced Wheatstone bridge, the battery and galvanometer arms are conjugate. Interchanging their positions leaves the null balance condition completely invariant.
  7. Kirchhoff's Rules Scope: Kirchhoff's loop rule is strictly valid for steady currents where electrostatic fields are conservative ($\oint \mathbf{E}\cdot d\mathbf{l} = 0$). For time-varying magnetic fields, induced non-conservative electric fields modify the loop equation.
