Atomic Masses, Composition of Nucleus & Discovery of Neutron

Nuclear Constituents & Units

13.2.1 Unified Atomic Mass Unit ($u$), Isotopes, Isobars, and Isotones

  • Unified Atomic Mass Unit ($u$): Defined as exactly $1/12th$ of the mass of an unbound neutral Carbon-12 ($12C$) atom at rest: $$1 u = 1.992647 × 10-26 kg12 = 1.660539 × 10-27 kg$$ $$1 u \equiv 931.5 MeV/c^2 \quad (E = 931.5 MeV)$$
  • Constituents of Nucleus (Nucleons):
    • Proton ($p$): Charge $= +e = +1.602 × 10-19 C$, Mass $m_p = 1.00727 u = 1.67262 × 10-27 kg$.
    • Neutron ($n$): Discovered by James Chadwick (1932) by bombarding Beryllium with $α$-particles. Neutral ($q=0$), Mass $m_n = 1.00866 u = 1.6749 × 10-27 kg$. Free neutron is unstable ($τ ≈ 1000 s$, decays to $p + e^- + \bar{ν}_e$), but stable inside bound nuclei.
  • Nuclide Classification ($^A_ZX$):
    • Isotopes: Same atomic number $Z$, different neutron number $N$ and mass number $A$ (e.g. $^1_1H, ^2_1H, ^3_1H$; $3517Cl, 3717Cl$). Same chemical properties.
    • Isobars: Same mass number $A$, different $Z$ and $N$ (e.g. $^3_1H$ and $^3_2He$; $4018Ar, 4020Ca$).
    • Isotones: Same neutron number $N = A - Z$, different $Z$ and $A$ (e.g. $198\ 80Hg$ and $197\ 79Au$ where $N = 118$).

Size and Constant Density of the Nucleus

Nuclear Radius & Density

13.3.1 Radius Formula ($R = R_0 A1/3$) & Universal Nuclear Density

  • Nuclear Radius Law: High-energy electron scattering measurements reveal: $$R = R_0 A1/3 \quad where R_0 ≈ 1.2 fm = 1.2 × 10-15 m$$
  • Nuclear Volume & Mass Proportionality: $$V = 43π R^3 = 43π R_0^3 A \implies V ∝ A$$
  • Universal Nuclear Density ($ρ$): $$ρ = MassVolume = m A43π R_0^3 A = 3 m4π R_0^3 ≈ 2.3 × 1017 kg m-3$$ (Remarkable Fact: Nuclear density is completely constant and independent of mass number $A$ for all elements; comparable to the density of astrophysical neutron stars).
Example 13.1

Nuclear Density Calculation of Iron ($56Fe$)

Given the mass of iron nucleus $mFe = 55.85 u$ and $A = 56$, compute the nuclear density.

Mass of nucleus $M = 55.85 × (1.66 × 10-27 kg) = 9.27 × 10-26 kg$.
Nuclear radius $R = R_0 A1/3 = (1.2 × 10-15 m) × (56)1/3 = 4.59 × 10-15 m$.
$$Density ρ = M43π R^3 = 9.27 × 10-2643π (1.2 × 10-15)^3 × 56 = 2.29 × 1017 kg m-3$$
Nuclear density $ρ = 2.29 × 1017 kg/m^3$ ($2.3 × 1014$ times denser than water).

Mass-Energy, Binding Energy & Nuclear Forces

Einstein Relation & Mass Defect

13.4.1 Mass-Energy Equivalence ($E = Δ M c^2$) & Binding Energy ($E_b$)

  • Einstein's Mass-Energy Relation: $E = m c^2$ ($1 g$ matter converts into $9 × 1013 J$).
  • Mass Defect ($Δ M$): The difference between the sum of masses of free individual nucleons and the rest mass $M$ of the bound nucleus: $$Δ M = [Z m_p + (A - Z) m_n] - M_N$$
  • Total Nuclear Binding Energy ($E_b$): $$E_b = Δ M × c^2 = (Δ M in u) × 931.5 MeV$$
  • Binding Energy Per Nucleon ($Ebn = E_b / A$):
    Measures the stability of a nucleus against disassembly:
    • Practically constant ($≈ 8.0 MeV/nucleon$) across $30 < A < 170$ due to the saturation property of short-range nuclear forces.
    • Peak maximum at $56Fe$ ($Ebn ≈ 8.75 MeV/nucleon$, most stable nuclide).
    • Decreases for heavy nuclei ($Ebn ≈ 7.6 MeV$ for $238U$) due to Coulomb repulsion $\implies$ Fission is exothermic.
    • Low for very light nuclei ($A < 30$, $Ebn ≈ 1.1 MeV$ for $^2H$) $\implies$ Fusion is exothermic.

Fundamental Strong Interaction

13.5 Characteristics of the Nuclear Force

  1. Strongest Fundamental Force: $\sim 100$ times stronger than Coulomb electrostatic force, easily overcoming proton-proton repulsion inside the nucleus.
  2. Extremely Short-Range: Operates only over a few femtometres ($\sim 1 to 3 fm$), dropping rapidly to zero for $r > 3 fm$.
  3. Saturation Property: A nucleon interacts only with its immediate nearest neighbours.
  4. Repulsive Core at Ultra-Short Range: Attractive for $r > r_0 ≈ 0.8 fm$, but becomes strongly repulsive for $r < 0.8 fm$, preventing nuclear collapse.
  5. Charge Independence: Force between $(p-p)$, $(p-n)$, and $(n-n)$ is identically strong. Non-central spin-dependent force.

Example 13.2 & 13.3

Energy of 1 g Matter & Oxygen $16O$ Binding Energy

(a) Calculate energy equivalent of $1 g$ substance.
(b) For $16\ 8O$ ($m = 15.99053 u$), find mass defect and total binding energy.

(a) $1 g$ mass energy: $E = mc^2 = (10-3 kg) × (3 × 10^8 m/s)^2 = 9.0 × 1013 J$.
(b) $16O$ Mass Defect:
Constituents $= 8 m_p + 8 m_n = 8(1.00727) + 8(1.00866) = 16.12744 u$.
$Δ M = 16.12744 - 15.99053 = 0.13691 u$.
$E_b = 0.13691 × 931.5 MeV = 127.5 MeV$.
$Ebn = 127.5 MeV16 = 7.97 MeV/nucleon$.

Radioactivity, Nuclear Fission & Thermonuclear Fusion

Nuclear Fission Mechanics

13.7.1 Nuclear Fission ($Q ≈ 200 MeV$)

  • Fission: A heavy unstable nucleus ($A \sim 240$) splits into two medium-mass fragments ($A \sim 120$) upon absorbing a thermal neutron: $$^1_0n + {235\ 92U} \to {236\ 92U}^* \to {144\ 56Ba} + {8936Kr} + 3\ ^1_0n + Q$$
  • Energy Released: $Δ Ebn = 8.5 MeV - 7.6 MeV = 0.9 MeV/nucleon \implies Q ≈ 240 × 0.9 MeV ≈ 216 MeV ≈ 200 MeV/fission$.
  • Energy Density: $1 kg$ $235U$ yields $\sim 1014 J$ ($\sim 2.5 × 10^6$ times more than burning $1 kg$ coal).
Thermonuclear Fusion & Solar Cycles

13.7.2 Thermonuclear Fusion & Proton-Proton Cycle in Stars

  • Thermonuclear Fusion: Two light nuclei ($A ≤ 10$) combine at extremely high temperatures ($T \sim 10^7 - 10^8 K$) to overcome the Coulomb electrostatic barrier ($\sim 400 keV$).
  • Proton-Proton ($p-p$) Cycle in the Sun:
    $$4\ ^1_1H + 2e^- \to {^4_2He} + 2ν_e + 6γ + 26.7 MeV$$
  • $4$ Hydrogen atoms fuse into one Helium nucleus with release of $26.7 MeV$ ($6.675 MeV/nucleon$).
Example 13.4

Conservation Laws in Nuclear vs Chemical Reactions

(a) How are nuclear reaction equations balanced compared to chemical equations?
(b) If proton and neutron numbers are conserved, where does mass-energy come from?
(c) Does mass-energy interconversion occur in chemical reactions?

(a) Chemical equations balance atoms of each element. Nuclear reactions transmute elements, but strictly conserve total charge ($Z$) and total nucleon/baryon number ($A$).
(b) The rest mass of free nucleons is identical on both sides, but the total nuclear binding energy of reactants differs from products. The difference in binding energies ($Δ E_b = Δ M c^2$) appears as kinetic energy/photons.
(c) Yes, chemical binding energy also creates a mass defect. However, chemical energy releases ($\sim eV$) involve mass changes a million times smaller than nuclear processes ($\sim MeV$), making chemical mass changes undetectable in ordinary measurements.

Ex 13.1Binding Energy of Nitrogen-14

13.1 Obtain the binding energy (in MeV) of a nitrogen nucleus ($14\ 7N$), given $m(14\ 7N) = 14.00307 u$, $m_H = 1.007825 u$, $m_n = 1.008665 u$.

Binding Energy $E_b = 104.66 MeV$ ($Ebn = 7.48 MeV/nucleon$).
Constituent mass: $$7 m_H + 7 m_n = 7(1.007825) + 7(1.008665) = 7.054775 + 7.060655 = 14.115430 u$$ $$Δ M = 14.115430 - 14.003070 = 0.112360 u$$ $$E_b = 0.112360 × 931.5 MeV = 104.66 MeV$$
Ex 13.2Binding Energy of Iron-56 and Bismuth-209

13.2 Obtain the binding energy of $5626Fe$ ($m = 55.934939 u$) and $209\ 83Bi$ ($m = 208.980388 u$).

$56Fe$: $E_b = 492.26 MeV$ ($Ebn = 8.79 MeV/nucleon$)
$209Bi$: $E_b = 1640.3 MeV$ ($Ebn = 7.85 MeV/nucleon$).
For $5626Fe$:
$Δ M = [26(1.007825) + 30(1.008665)] - 55.934939 = 56.463400 - 55.934939 = 0.528461 u$.
$E_b = 0.528461 × 931.5 MeV = 492.26 MeV \implies Ebn = 492.2656 = 8.79 MeV/nucleon$.
For $209\ 83Bi$:
$Δ M = [83(1.007825) + 126(1.008665)] - 208.980388 = 210.741265 - 208.980388 = 1.760877 u$.
$E_b = 1.760877 × 931.5 MeV = 1640.26 MeV \implies Ebn = 1640.26209 = 7.85 MeV/nucleon$.
Ex 13.3Disassembly Energy of a 3.0 g Copper Coin

13.3 A coin has mass $3.0 g$. Calculate nuclear energy required to separate all nucleons from each other, assuming coin is pure $6329Cu$ ($m = 62.92960 u$).

Total Energy Required $Etotal = 1.58 × 1025 MeV = 2.53 × 1012 J$ (equivalent to $\sim 700 MWh$).
Number of Cu atoms in $3.0 g$: $$N = 3.0 g63 g/mol × (6.023 × 1023 mol-1) = 2.868 × 1022 atoms$$
Mass defect per $63Cu$ nucleus: $$Δ M = [29(1.007825) + 34(1.008665)] - 62.92960 = 63.521535 - 62.92960 = 0.591935 u$$ $$E_b = 0.591935 × 931.5 MeV = 551.39 MeV$$
$$Etotal = N × E_b = (2.868 × 1022) × (551.39 MeV) = 1.58 × 1025 MeV = 2.53 × 1012 J$$

13.4 Obtain approximately the ratio of the nuclear radii of the gold isotope $197\ 79Au$ and silver isotope $107\ 47Ag$.

Ex 13.5Q-Values of Nuclear Reactions

13.5 Determine Q-values and state whether reactions are exothermic or endothermic:
(i) $^1_1H + {^3_1H} \to {^2_1H} + {^2_1H}$
(ii) $12\ 6C + {12\ 6C} \to {2010Ne} + {^4_2He}$
Given: $m(^2H) = 2.014102 u, m(^3H) = 3.016049 u, m(12C) = 12.000000 u, m(20Ne) = 19.992439 u, m(^4He) = 4.002603 u$.

(i) $Q = -4.03 MeV$ (Endothermic) • (ii) $Q = +4.61 MeV$ (Exothermic).
(i) $Δ m = [m(^1H) + m(^3H)] - 2m(^2H) = [1.007825 + 3.016049] - 2(2.014102) = 4.023874 - 4.028204 = -0.004330 u$.
$Q = -0.004330 × 931.5 MeV = -4.033 MeV \implies$ Endothermic.
(ii) $Δ m = 2m(12C) - [m(20Ne) + m(^4He)] = 24.000000 - [19.992439 + 4.002603] = 24.000000 - 23.995042 = +0.004958 u$.
$Q = +0.004958 × 931.5 MeV = +4.618 MeV \implies$ Exothermic.
Ex 13.6Fission of Iron-56 into Aluminum-28

13.6 Is the symmetric fission of $5626Fe \to 2\ {2813Al}$ energetically possible? Work out $Q$. ($m(56Fe) = 55.93494 u, m(28Al) = 27.98191 u$).

$Q = -26.90 MeV < 0 \implies$ Fission of Iron-56 is energetically impossible (highly endothermic).
$$Δ m = m(56Fe) - 2 m(28Al) = 55.93494 - 2(27.98191) = 55.93494 - 55.96382 = -0.02888 u$$ $$Q = -0.02888 × 931.5 MeV = -26.90 MeV$$ Since $Q < 0$, $56Fe$ is at the peak of the binding energy curve and cannot spontaneously undergo fission.
Ex 13.7Energy Released in 1 kg Plutonium-239 Fission

13.7 Energy released per fission of $239\ 94Pu$ is $180 MeV$. How much energy (in MeV and Joules) is released if all atoms in $1 kg$ of pure $239Pu$ undergo fission?

Energy Released $E = 4.53 × 1026 MeV = 7.26 × 1013 J$.
Number of $239Pu$ nuclei in $1 kg = 1000 g$: $$N = 1000 g239 g/mol × (6.023 × 1023 mol-1) = 2.520 × 1024 nuclei$$
$$E = N × (180 MeV) = (2.520 × 1024) × 180 = 4.536 × 1026 MeV$$ $$E = (4.536 × 1026) × (1.602 × 10-13 J/MeV) = 7.267 × 1013 J$$
Ex 13.8Glow Time of 100 W Lamp with 2.0 kg Deuterium

13.8 How long can a $100 W$ electric lamp be kept glowing by fusion of $2.0 kg$ deuterium? Reaction: $^2_1H + {^2_1H} \to {^3_2He} + n + 3.27 MeV$.

Time $t = 1.57 × 1012 s ≈ 4.98 × 10^4 years ≈ 50,000 years$.
Number of deuterium nuclei in $2.0 kg = 2000 g$: $$N = 20002 × 6.023 × 1023 = 6.023 × 1026 nuclei$$ Two deuterons release $3.27 MeV \implies$ energy per deuteron $= 3.272 = 1.635 MeV$.
Total Energy: $$E = (6.023 × 1026) × (1.635 MeV) = 9.848 × 1026 MeV = (9.848 × 1026) × (1.602 × 10-13 J) = 1.578 × 1014 J$$
Time to consume at $P = 100 W$: $$t = EP = 1.578 × 1014 J100 J/s = 1.578 × 1012 s$$ $$In years: 1.578 × 1012 s3.154 × 10^7 s/yr ≈ 50,030 years ≈ 5.0 × 10^4 years$$
Ex 13.9Coulomb Potential Barrier for Deuteron-Deuteron Fusion

13.9 Calculate the height of the Coulomb potential barrier for head-on collision of two deuterons (treated as hard spheres of radius $r = 2.0 fm$).

Potential Barrier Height $U = 360 keV = 5.76 × 10-14 J$.
At contact, separation distance $d = 2r = 2(2.0 fm) = 4.0 fm = 4.0 × 10-15 m$. $$U = 14π\varepsilon_0 e^2d = (9.0 × 10^9 N m^2 C-2) × (1.602 × 10-19 C)^24.0 × 10-15 m = 5.77 × 10-14 J$$ $$U = 5.77 × 10-14 J1.602 × 10-16 J/keV = 360 keV$$
Ex 13.10Independence of Nuclear Matter Density from Mass Number A

13.10 From the relation $R = R_0 A1/3$, show that nuclear matter density is nearly constant (independent of $A$).

Let $m$ be the average mass of a nucleon ($m ≈ 1.66 × 10-27 kg$).
Total mass of a nucleus of mass number $A$ is $M = m A$.
Radius of the nucleus is $R = R_0 A1/3 \implies$ Volume $V = 43π R^3 = 43π R_0^3 A$.
$$Nuclear Density ρ = MV = m A43π R_0^3 A = 3 m4π R_0^3$$ Since $m$, $π$, and $R_0$ are fundamental physical constants, the mass number $A$ cancels out completely. Hence, the nuclear matter density $ρ ≈ 2.3 × 1017 kg m-3$ is constant across all chemical elements.

Chapter Summary & Points to Ponder

Core nuclear properties, radius and density relations, binding energy curve characteristics, fission/fusion equations, and official NCERT Points to Ponder for Chapter 13.

100% SYLLABUS

Universal Nuclear Density

R = R_0 A1/3 \implies ρ ≈ 2.3 × 1017 kg/m^3

Nuclear volume is proportional to mass number $A$; density is constant for all nuclei regardless of size.

Mass Defect & 1 u Energy

1 u = 931.5 MeV • Δ M c^2 = E_b

$Δ M = [Z m_p + (A - Z) m_n] - M_N$. Released energy upon formation is binding energy $E_b$.

Binding Energy Curve

Peak at 56Fe (8.75 MeV/A)

Constancy at $≈ 8.0 MeV$ ($30 < A < 170$) due to force saturation. Drops for light ($A < 30$) and heavy ($A > 170$) nuclei.

Nuclear Force Properties

Short Range (\sim 1-3 fm) • Repulsive (<0.8 fm)

Strongest fundamental force, charge-independent, saturating, spin-dependent, non-central.

Nuclear Fission

Q ≈ 200 MeV / fission

$235U + n \to {144Ba} + {89Kr} + 3n + 200 MeV$. $1 kg$ uranium $\sim 1014 J$.

Thermonuclear Fusion

4\ ^1H \to {^4He} + 26.7 MeV

Solar energy source ($p-p$ cycle). Requires $T \sim 10^7 - 10^8 K$ to overcome $≈ 400 keV$ Coulomb barrier.

NCERT Official

Points to Ponder

  1. Universal Density: Nuclear density is completely constant and independent of the size/mass of the nucleus ($ρ ≈ 2.3 × 1017 kg/m^3$). Atomic density does not obey this rule.
  2. Probe Dependency of Nuclear Size: Nuclear radius measured by high-energy electrons senses charge distribution ($Z$), whereas $α$-particles sense the nuclear strong matter distribution.
  3. Unified Mass-Energy Conservation: Due to $E = mc^2$, mass and energy are unified. The $Q$-value is defined directly as $Q = (∑ Mreactants - ∑ Mproducts) c^2$.
  4. Coulomb Barrier in Fusion: Light nuclei require extreme thermal kinetic energy ($T \sim 10^8 K$) to penetrate the mutual electrostatic repulsion barrier ($\sim 400 keV$) before the attractive strong force binds them.
  5. Neutron-to-Proton Ratio and Stability: Stable light nuclei have $N/Z ≈ 1$. For heavy nuclei, repulsive Coulomb force necessitates $N/Z ≈ 1.5$ (3:2) for stability. Only $\sim 10%$ of all known nuclides are stable.
