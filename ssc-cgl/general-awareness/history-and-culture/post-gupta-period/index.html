1. Introduction to Post-Gupta Period & Pushyabhutis1. Introduction to Post-Gupta Period & Pushyabhutis

The decline of the mighty Gupta Empire around the mid-6th century CE led to political fragmentation across India. This period saw the rise of numerous independent regional kingdoms, replacing the centralized imperial structure.

  • **Regional Powers:** Prominent among these were the Pushyabhutis in Thanesar, the Maukhari in Kannauj, the Maitrakas in Valabhi, and the Gaudas in Bengal.
  • **Pushyabhuti Dynasty (Vardhana Dynasty):** This dynasty originated from Thanesar (Sthaneshwar), near Kurukshetra, in present-day Haryana.
  • **Founder:** The dynasty's founder was Pushyabhuti.
  • **Prabhakaravardhana:** He was the first significant ruler of the dynasty, who expanded its influence and adopted imperial titles like 'Maharajadhiraja' and 'Paramabhattaraka'. He was the father of Harshavardhana.
  • **Rajyavardhana:** Prabhakaravardhana's elder son, Rajyavardhana, succeeded him but was tragically assassinated by Shashanka, the Gauda ruler of Bengal.
  • **Harshavardhana:** Following his brother's death, Harshavardhana ascended the throne, marking a new era of consolidation in North India.

2. Harshavardhana (606-647 CE)2. Harshavardhana (606-647 CE)

Harshavardhana is considered one of the most important rulers of ancient India after the Guptas, known for his administrative prowess, military campaigns, and patronage of arts and religion.

  • **Accession:** He ascended the throne of Thanesar in 606 CE.
  • **Capital:** Harsha shifted his capital from Thanesar to **Kannauj** after rescuing his sister Rajyashri and consolidating his position. Kannauj subsequently became the political and cultural hub of North India.
  • **Empire:** Harsha successfully extended his empire across a vast part of North India, encompassing regions like Punjab, Bengal, Odisha, and parts of Central India. He was famously known as **'Sakala Uttara Patha Natha'** (Lord of the entire North).
  • **Conflict with Pulakeshin II:** His ambition to expand southwards was thwarted by the powerful Chalukya ruler **Pulakeshin II** in the decisive **Battle of Narmada** (circa 618-619 CE). This defeat is a significant event recorded in the Aihole inscription.
  • **Administration:** Harsha's administration was relatively centralized but also featured a feudal structure, with land grants (jagirs) given to officers. Revenue was typically divided into four parts: for government expenditure, for scholars, for public servants, and for religious endowments.
  • **Religious Policy:** Initially a worshipper of Surya and Shiva, Harsha later became a devout patron of **Buddhism** (Mahayana sect), heavily influenced by the Chinese pilgrim Xuanzang. He organized a grand assembly at **Kannauj** to propagate Mahayana Buddhism and held a quinquennial religious festival at **Prayag** (Allahabad), distributing wealth to various religious sects.
  • **Literary Works:** Harsha himself was a distinguished scholar and playwright. He is credited with writing three Sanskrit plays:
    • **Priyadarshika**
    • **Ratnavali**
    • **Nagananda**

3. Important Personalities & Sources3. Important Personalities & Sources

The reign of Harshavardhana is well-documented due to the contributions of contemporary scholars and foreign travelers.

  • **Banabhatta:**
    • He was the renowned court poet (Asthana Kavi) of Harshavardhana.
    • His most significant work is **Harshacharita**, a biography of Harsha, which serves as a crucial primary source for understanding Harsha's reign and the political climate of the time.
    • He also authored **Kadambari**, a classic Sanskrit romance novel, considered one of the world's earliest novels.
  • **Xuanzang (Hiuen Tsang):**
    • A famous Chinese Buddhist pilgrim who visited India during Harsha's reign (630-645 CE).
    • He spent many years studying at **Nalanda University**, a prominent Buddhist learning center, and collected Buddhist scriptures.
    • His detailed travelogue, **Si-Yu-Ki** (Records of the Western World), provides invaluable insights into the social, economic, political, and religious conditions of India during the 7th century CE.
    • Xuanzang highly praised Harsha's administration, his patronage of Buddhism, and the general prosperity of the kingdom.

4. The Chalukyas of Badami (Vatapi)4. The Chalukyas of Badami (Vatapi)

The Chalukyas were a prominent dynasty that ruled large parts of Southern and Central India between the 6th and 12th centuries. The Badami Chalukyas were the earliest and most influential branch.

  • **Period:** Mid-6th to mid-8th century CE.
  • **Region:** Deccan plateau, primarily modern Karnataka, with their capital at **Badami** (also known as Vatapi).
  • **Founder:** **Pulakeshin I** (circa 543 CE) established the independent Chalukya kingdom.
  • **Most Famous Ruler:** **Pulakeshin II** (610-642 CE) was the most powerful ruler.
    • He famously defeated Harshavardhana on the banks of the Narmada river, halting Harsha's southern expansion.
    • His military achievements and conquests are extensively documented in the **Aihole inscription**, composed by his court poet **Ravikirti**.
    • He was eventually defeated and killed by the Pallava ruler Narasimhavarman I.
  • **Art and Architecture:** The Chalukyas were pioneers in temple architecture, particularly the **Vesara style** (a hybrid style blending elements of North Indian Nagara and South Indian Dravida styles).
    • **Badami:** Famous for its rock-cut cave temples dedicated to Vishnu, Shiva, and Jain Tirthankaras.
    • **Aihole:** Often referred to as the "cradle of Indian temple architecture," it boasts numerous early structural temples like the Durga Temple, Lad Khan Temple, and Ravana Phadi Cave.
    • **Pattadakal:** A **UNESCO World Heritage Site**, it showcases a magnificent group of temples, including the Virupaksha Temple (built by Lokamahadevi, queen of Vikramaditya II) and the Papanatha Temple, demonstrating both Dravidian and Nagara styles.
  • **Successors:** After the decline of the Badami Chalukyas, two other major branches emerged: the **Eastern Chalukyas of Vengi** (in Andhra Pradesh) and the **Western Chalukyas of Kalyani** (who later revived Chalukya power).

5. The Pallavas of Kanchi5. The Pallavas of Kanchi

The Pallavas were a powerful South Indian dynasty that played a significant role in the political and cultural history of Tamil Nadu.

  • **Period:** 6th to 9th century CE.
  • **Region:** Northern Tamil Nadu, with their capital at **Kanchipuram**, a renowned center of learning and religion.
  • **Founder:** **Simhavishnu** (late 6th century CE) is considered the founder of the imperial Pallava dynasty.
  • **Important Rulers:**
    • **Mahendravarman I** (600-630 CE):
      • A versatile ruler, initially a Jain, he converted to Shaivism under the influence of the Nayanar saint Appar (Tirunavukkarasar).
      • He was a great patron of art and literature, pioneering rock-cut architecture.
      • He authored the Sanskrit satirical play **Mattavilasa Prahasana**.
    • **Narasimhavarman I** (630-668 CE):
      • Known by the title **'Mamalla'** (meaning 'great wrestler').
      • He achieved a significant victory by defeating and killing the Chalukya ruler Pulakeshin II, subsequently sacking the Chalukyan capital Vatapi (earning him the title **'Vatapikonda'**).
      • He founded the city of **Mamallapuram** (Mahabalipuram), which became a hub of Pallava art and architecture.
      • He patronized the construction of the famous **Pancha Rathas** (monolithic rock-cut temples) and the Shore Temple at Mahabalipuram.
  • **Art and Architecture:** The Pallavas were instrumental in developing the distinct **Dravidian style** of temple architecture. Its evolution is typically categorized into four stages:
    • **Mahendra Style:** Characterized by rock-cut mandapas (e.g., Mandagapattu).
    • **Mamalla Style:** Marked by monolithic Rathas and mandapas (e.g., Pancha Rathas, Shore Temple at Mahabalipuram).
    • **Rajasimha Style:** Saw the emergence of structural temples (e.g., Kailasanathar Temple at Kanchipuram, Shore Temple).
    • **Nandivarman Style:** Characterized by smaller structural temples.
  • **UNESCO World Heritage Site:** Mahabalipuram, with its group of monuments, is a UNESCO World Heritage Site.
  • **Literature:** The Pallavas patronized both Sanskrit and Tamil literature. The Bhakti movement, with its Alvars (Vaishnava saints) and Nayanars (Shaiva saints), flourished during their rule.

6. The Rashtrakutas of Manyakheta6. The Rashtrakutas of Manyakheta

The Rashtrakutas were a powerful imperial dynasty that ruled large parts of the Indian subcontinent between the 8th and 10th centuries.

  • **Period:** Mid-8th to late 10th century CE.
  • **Region:** Deccan, with their capital at **Manyakheta** (Malkhed) in modern Karnataka.
  • **Founder:** **Dantidurga** (circa 735 CE) established the Rashtrakuta kingdom, overthrowing the Badami Chalukyas.
  • **Important Rulers:**
    • **Govinda III** (793-814 CE): Expanded the empire significantly, engaging in successful campaigns against the Palas and Pratiharas.
    • **Amoghavarsha I** (814-878 CE):
      • One of the longest-reigning Rashtrakuta rulers, known for his peaceful and scholarly rule.
      • He was a patron of Jainism and authored **Kavirajamarga** (a work on poetics in Kannada, making it one of the earliest literary works in the language) and **Prashnottara Ratnamalika** (in Sanskrit).
  • **Tripartite Struggle:** The Rashtrakutas were one of the three major powers (along with the Palas of Bengal and the Pratiharas of Malwa/Rajasthan) involved in a prolonged **Tripartite Struggle** for control over the strategic city of Kannauj in North India.
  • **Art and Architecture:** The Rashtrakutas are renowned for their magnificent rock-cut architecture.
    • **Ellora Caves (Maharashtra):** A **UNESCO World Heritage Site**, famous for its complex of Hindu, Buddhist, and Jain caves. The most spectacular is the monolithic **Kailasa Temple** (Cave 16), dedicated to Shiva. Carved entirely out of a single massive rock, it is considered an architectural marvel.
    • **Elephanta Caves (near Mumbai):** Another **UNESCO World Heritage Site**, known for its colossal sculptures, particularly the Trimurti (three-faced Shiva).

7. Sangam Linkages & Regional Powers (Brief)7. Sangam Linkages & Regional Powers (Brief)

While the Post-Gupta period is distinct from the earlier Sangam Age (c. 300 BCE - 300 CE), the Southern Dynasties built upon and evolved the cultural and political landscape of the South.

  • **Cultural Continuity:** The Pallavas, for instance, emerged in a region that had been part of the Sangam kingdoms. Their patronage of Tamil literature and the flourishing of the Bhakti movement (Alvars and Nayanars) during their rule demonstrate a continuity and evolution of indigenous cultural traditions.
  • **Rise of Regionalism:** This era marked a significant shift from the pan-Indian imperial model to a more decentralized political structure characterized by powerful regional kingdoms. These kingdoms engaged in intense rivalries for territorial expansion and political supremacy.
  • **Tripartite Struggle:** The protracted **Tripartite Struggle** for control over Kannauj between the Palas (Eastern India), Pratiharas (Northern/Western India), and Rashtrakutas (Deccan) is a prime example of this era's regional competition and the strategic importance of key centers.
  • **Architectural and Linguistic Development:** This period witnessed the development of distinct regional architectural styles (Nagara in the North, Dravidian in the South, and Vesara as a hybrid) and the flourishing of regional languages and literatures, laying crucial groundwork for the cultural diversity of medieval India.
