Section 15 • Social Problems

Drug Addiction

Drug addiction is studied in sociology not merely as an individual pathology or biochemical imbalance, but as a complex social problem rooted in social disorganization, anomie, differential association, and structural alienation. For UP PGT Sociology and UGC NET examinations, substance abuse is analyzed through classical paradigms of deviance, functionalist strain theories, and Indian social structural transformations.

UP PGT Sociology • Code 16 Section 15

1. Definitional Framework, Etymology & Classical Foundations

Etymologically, the term 'drug' derives from the Old French 'drogue' (possibly from Middle Dutch 'droge-vate' meaning dry barrels containing medicinal herbs). 'Addiction' stems from the Latin 'addicere', historically referring to a debtor legally bound or enslaved to a creditor. In sociological parlance, drug addiction represents compulsive drug-seeking behavior and physiological/psychological dependence that disrupts normative social roles and institutional functioning.

  • Émile Durkheim and Anomie: In 'Le Suicide' (1897), Durkheim introduced 'anomie' (normlessness), which Robert K. Merton later expanded in 'Social Theory and Social Structure' (1938). Drug addiction and substance abuse are frequently categorized as forms of 'retreatism'—an individual adaptation where both cultural goals and institutionalized means are rejected, leading to withdrawal from conventional societal participation.
  • Robert K. Merton's Strain Theory: Merton classifies retreatism as the rejection of both culturally approved success goals (e.g., wealth, status) and legitimate institutional means (e.g., education, career), resulting in escapist behaviors such as chronic alcoholism and narcotic addiction.
  • Edwin Sutherland's Differential Association Theory: Formulated in 'Principles of Criminology' (1939), Sutherland posits that deviant behavior, including drug use, is learned through social interaction within intimate personal groups. Individuals learn techniques of drug consumption and the rationalizations, drives, and attitudes favorable to violation of legal and moral norms.
  • Howard Becker's Labeling Theory: In 'Outsiders' (1963), Becker argues that social groups create deviance by making rules whose infraction constitutes deviance, and by applying those labels to particular people and labeling them as outsiders. Drug addiction is thus heavily mediated by social reaction and stigmatization.

2. Structural Concepts, Empirical Processes & Indian Sociological Context

In the framework of Indian society, drug addiction must be evaluated against the backdrop of rapid industrialization, urbanization, the breakdown of the joint family system, and the erosion of traditional social controls (such as caste panchayats and religious sanctions). Radhakamal Mukerjee, in his pioneering works on social ecology and social values, emphasized how urban disorganization and slum environments breed psychological anomie and deviant escapes.

  • Traditional vs. Modern Substance Use: Indian sociology distinguishes between traditional, ritually sanctioned, or communally regulated use of substances (e.g., bhang during Holi or sadhus using cannabis as part of ascetic traditions) and modern chemical/synthetic drug abuse (heroin, cocaine, pharmaceutical opioids) driven by commercial markets, urban anonymity, and westernization.
  • M.N. Srinivas and Social Change: The destabilization of traditional rural structures through Sanskritization and Westernization has intensified cultural lag, where material culture and modern stressors outpace normative regulatory mechanisms, leading to heightened vulnerability among urban youth.
  • Yogendra Singh's Modernization Paradigm: Singh's 'Modernization of Indian Tradition' (1973) highlights how structural discontinuities generate structural strain, identity crises, and generational conflict, which frequently manifest in escapist subcultures and substance abuse.
  • Ecosystem of Addiction: Sociological field studies in India highlight the intersection of poverty, unemployment, peer pressure, migration, and homelessness with high rates of substance abuse, transforming a psychological issue into a structural malaise.

3. Contemporary Dynamics, Social Policy & Applied Dimensions

Modern sociological analysis of drug addiction incorporates policy frameworks, legislative mechanisms, and institutional rehabilitation structures. The state views addiction simultaneously as a law-and-order problem and a public health crisis.

  • Legislative Framework in India: The primary legal instrument is the Narcotic Drugs and Psychotropic Substances (NDPS) Act, 1985, which repealed earlier archaic laws and instituted stringent penalties for cultivation, production, possession, sale, purchase, transport, and consumption of narcotic drugs.
  • Constitutional Mandate: Article 47 of the Directive Principles of State Policy (DPSP) in the Constitution of India directs the State to bring about prohibition of the consumption except for medicinal purposes of intoxicating drinks and of drugs which are injurious to health.
  • Institutional Interventions: The Ministry of Social Justice and Empowerment implements the National Action Plan for Drug Demand Reduction (NAPDDR), establishing Integrated Rehabilitation Centres for Addicts (IRCAs) and community-based peer counseling programs.
  • Sociological Critique of Prohibition: Sociologists often critique pure punitive models (carceral approaches) for marginalizing addicts further, driving the underground economy, and exacerbating stigmatization, advocating instead for harm reduction, decriminalization of minor possession, and sociological rehabilitation.

Memory Shortcut / Mnemonic
Merton's Modes of Individual Adaptation
CO-IRR (Conformity, Innovation, Ritualism, Retreatism, Rebellion)

To remember Merton's five modes of adaptation to cultural goals and institutional means in strain theory, recall CO-IRR. Drug addiction and alcoholism fall squarely under 'Retreatism' (the fourth mode).

High-Yield Exam Points for UP PGT Sociology

  • Émile Durkheim introduced the concept of 'Anomie' in 'The Division of Labour in Society' (1893) and applied it directly to social deregulation in 'Suicide' (1897).
  • Robert K. Merton classified drug addiction as 'Retreatism' in his seminal essay 'Social Structure and Anomie' (1938).
  • Edwin Sutherland formulated the Theory of Differential Association in 'Principles of Criminology' (1939), emphasizing that deviant behavior is learned.
  • Howard Becker propounded Labeling Theory in his classic sociological study 'Outsiders: Studies in the Sociology of Deviance' (1963).
  • The Narcotic Drugs and Psychotropic Substances (NDPS) Act was enacted in India in the year 1985.
  • Article 47 of the Indian Constitution (under Directive Principles of State Policy) directs the state to endeavor to bring about prohibition of intoxicating drinks and injurious drugs.

Common Misconceptions & Exam Traps

  • Misconception 1: Students often treat drug addiction purely as a biological, medical, or psychological disease, ignoring the core sociological dimensions of power, structural strain, peer socialization, and social labeling.
  • Misconception 2: Confusing Merton's 'Retreatism' with 'Ritualism'. Ritualism involves abandoning goals while strictly adhering to institutional means (bureaucratic red tape), whereas Retreatism involves rejecting both goals and means entirely (e.g., vagrancy, drug addiction).

Q1 Which sociological theorist classified drug addiction and alcoholism as 'retreatism' in his typology of modes of individual adaptation?
  • A Émile Durkheim
  • B Robert K. Merton
  • C Talcott Parsons
  • D Edwin Sutherland
Correct Option: B (Robert K. Merton)

Robert K. Merton, in his structural strain theory ('Social Structure and Anomie', 1938), categorized retreatism as the rejection of both cultural goals and institutionalized means, with drug addicts and vagrants exemplifying this adaptive mode. Option 0 is incorrect because Durkheim coined anomie but Merton formulated the specific typology. Option 2 is structural functionalism macro-theory, and Option 3 formulated differential association theory.

Q2 Who propounded the 'Differential Association Theory' to explain how deviant behavior, including drug use, is learned through social interaction?
  • A Howard Becker
  • B Cesare Lombroso
  • C Edwin Sutherland
  • D Albert Cohen
Correct Option: C (Edwin Sutherland)

Edwin Sutherland propounded the Differential Association Theory in 1939, arguing that criminal and deviant behavior is learned within intimate personal groups. Option 0 formulated labeling theory; Option 1 represents early biological positivism; Option 4 studied delinquent subcultures.

Q3 In which landmark book did Howard Becker advance 'Labeling Theory' regarding deviance and outsiders?
  • A Suicide (1897)
  • B Outsiders (1963)
  • C Social Theory and Social Structure (1938)
  • D Principles of Criminology (1939)
Correct Option: B (Outsiders (1963))

Howard Becker published 'Outsiders: Studies in the Sociology of Deviance' in 1963, establishing the sociological foundation of labeling theory. Option 0 is by Durkheim; Option 2 is by Merton; Option 3 is by Sutherland.

Q4 In the context of Indian legislation, the Narcotic Drugs and Psychotropic Substances (NDPS) Act was enacted in which year?
  • A 1976
  • B 1981
  • C 1985
  • D 1992
Correct Option: C (1985)

The NDPS Act was enacted in 1985 to consolidate and amend laws relating to narcotic drugs, strengthen enforcement, and fulfill India's international treaty obligations.

Q5 Which Article of the Constitution of India contains the Directive Principle regarding the prohibition of intoxicating drinks and injurious drugs?
  • A Article 39
  • B Article 47
  • C Article 48
  • D Article 50
Correct Option: B (Article 47)

Article 47 of the Directive Principles of State Policy mandates that the State shall regard the raising of the level of nutrition and the improvement of public health as its primary duties and endeavor to bring about prohibition of intoxicating drugs injurious to health.

Q6 According to Émile Durkheim, a state of normlessness that leads to social deviance and deregulated desires is known as:
  • A Alienation
  • B Anomie
  • C Altruism
  • D Anomie-Solidarity
Correct Option: B (Anomie)

Anomie is Durkheim's term for normlessness or the breakdown of social rules governing human conduct, which he linked to suicide and social deviance. Option 0 is a Marxian concept.

Q7 Which Indian sociologist extensively analyzed social stratification, tradition, and modernity in India, highlighting structural changes and generational shifts?
  • A G.S. Ghurye
  • B M.N. Srinivas
  • C Yogendra Singh
  • D D.P. Mukerji
Correct Option: C (Yogendra Singh)

Yogendra Singh authored 'Modernization of Indian Tradition' (1973), providing a comprehensive sociological analysis of cultural and structural transformations in India.

Q8 Which sociological approach views drug addiction as a consequence of societal reaction, stigma, and the imposition of deviant categories by powerful social actors?
  • A Functionalist Approach
  • B Labeling Approach
  • C Exchange Theory
  • D Evolutionary Approach
Correct Option: B (Labeling Approach)

Labeling theory (Interactionist perspective) emphasizes that deviance is not an intrinsic quality of an act, but a consequence of the application of rules and labels to an offender.

Q9 Which of the following is a key characteristic of Merton's 'Retreatism' adaptation mode?
  • A Acceptance of goals and acceptance of means
  • B Rejection of goals and acceptance of means
  • C Rejection of goals and rejection of means
  • D Substitution of new goals and new means
Correct Option: C (Rejection of goals and rejection of means)

Retreatism involves the rejection of both culturally approved goals and institutionalized means, leading individuals to drop out of conventional society, often manifesting as drug addiction or vagrancy.

Q10 What is the primary sociological critique of punitive-only models of tackling drug addiction?
  • A They completely eliminate the drug supply
  • B They exacerbate social stigmatization, drive users underground, and ignore structural root causes
  • C They violate international human rights exclusively in European nations
  • D They replace medical treatment with free pharmaceuticals
Correct Option: B (They exacerbate social stigmatization, drive users underground, and ignore structural root causes)

Sociologists argue that strictly punitive carceral approaches increase marginalization, criminalize public health issues, and fail to address underlying structural strains like poverty and alienation.

Frequently Asked Questions

How does sociology differentiate drug addiction from individual psychology?

While psychology and psychiatry focus on individual cognitive processes, neurochemistry, and behavioral therapy, sociology examines the broader structural conditions—such as anomie, differential association, socioeconomic deprivation, urban disorganization, and cultural labeling—that produce and sustain substance abuse across social groups.

What is Robert K. Merton's contribution to understanding drug addiction?

Merton classified drug addiction as 'Retreatism' in his structural strain theory. He argued that when individuals experience a disjunction between culturally prescribed success goals and institutionalized means to achieve them, and they abandon both, they often withdraw into escapist behaviors like chronic drug use.

How is traditional drug use in India viewed differently from modern synthetic drug abuse?

Traditional drug use (e.g., bhang during festivals or traditional cannabis use among ascetics) was historically regulated by ritual, custom, and community norms. Modern synthetic drug abuse operates within global illicit markets, urban anonymity, consumer capitalism, and lacks traditional regulatory frameworks, often resulting in severe social disorganization.

What are the key legal and constitutional provisions regarding drugs in India?

Constitutionally, Article 47 under the Directive Principles of State Policy directs the State to prohibit intoxicating drinks and drugs injurious to health. Statutorily, the Narcotic Drugs and Psychotropic Substances (NDPS) Act, 1985, provides the legal framework governing drug offenses, penalties, and regulatory control in India.
