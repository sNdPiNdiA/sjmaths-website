Section 1 • Introduction to Sociology

Origin and Development of Sociology

The emergence of sociology as a distinct academic discipline in the 19th-century West was a direct intellectual and structural response to the monumental transformations brought about by the Industrial Revolution and the French Revolution. In the context of the UP PGT Sociology and UGC NET examinations, understanding this genealogy involves mastering Auguste Comte's positivism and Law of the Three Stages, alongside the pioneering intellectual trajectories introduced by Herbert Spencer, Émile Durkheim, Max Weber, and Karl Marx, bridging classical European theories with the evolution of Indian sociological traditions.

1. Etymological Roots, Historical Context & Classical Foundations

Sociology is a hybrid discipline etymologically derived from the Latin word 'socius' (meaning companion, associate, or society) and the Greek word 'logos' (meaning study, reason, or discourse). Coined originally by Emmanuel-Joseph Sieyès and later formally established by Auguste Comte in 1838 in his seminal work 'Cours de Philosophie Positive' (Course in Positive Philosophy), the term literally translates to the 'science of society'.

  • The Dual Revolutions: The intellectual birth of sociology was catalyzed by the French Revolution (1789), which dismantled the feudal-monarchical order and highlighted the problem of social order and normative disintegration, and the Industrial Revolution of 18th-century Britain, which triggered massive urbanization, class polarization, alienation, and rapid technological disruption.
  • Auguste Comte and Positivism: Comte envisioned sociology as the 'queen of the sciences' and propounded the Law of the Three Stages—the Theological (fictitious), the Metaphysical (abstract), and the Positive (scientific/empirical). He advocated for Positivism, asserting that social phenomena must be studied using the rigorous objective methodologies of the natural sciences.
  • Herbert Spencer and Social Darwinism: Spencer applied evolutionary theory to human societies in books like 'First Principles' (1862) and 'The Study of Sociology' (1873). He formulated the Organic Analogy (comparing society to a biological organism) and coined the phrase 'survival of the fittest', advocating for laissez-faire non-interference in social evolution.
  • Émile Durkheim: As the institutionalizer of academic sociology in France, Durkheim established sociology as an independent discipline with distinct subject matter: Social Facts (ways of acting, thinking, and feeling external to the individual and endowed with a coercive power). In 'The Division of Labour in Society' (1893), he contrasted Mechanical Solidarity (based on likeness and repressive law) with Organic Solidarity (based on functional interdependence and restitutive law).
  • Karl Marx and Historical Materialism: Marx viewed society through the prism of conflict and material conditions. In works like 'The Communist Manifesto' (1848) and 'Capital' (Das Kapital, 1867), he argued that the history of all hitherto existing society is the history of class struggles, driven by the contradictions between the forces and relations of production (Infrastructure) and legal-political-ideological institutions (Superstructure).
  • Max Weber and Verstehen Sociology: Diverging from positivism, Weber defined sociology in 'Economy and Society' (Wirtschaft und Gesellschaft, 1922) as a science concerning itself with the interpretative understanding of Social Action (Verstehen). He introduced methodological tools such as Ideal Types and argued in 'The Protestant Ethic and the Spirit of Capitalism' (1904-05) that religious ethics played a decisive role in the rise of modern Western capitalism.

2. Institutionalization, Theoretical Schools & Indian Sociological Pioneers

The institutionalization of sociology outside Western Europe occurred through distinct trajectories, most notably in India, where the discipline developed as an interface between Western theoretical frameworks and indigenous civilizational realities.

  • G.S. Ghurye and the Indological Approach: Known as the 'Father of Indian Sociology', Ghurye established the Department of Sociology at the University of Bombay. In his classic work 'Caste and Race in India' (1932), he provided a historical-indological analysis utilizing scriptural texts, historical records, and structural features of caste such as endogamy, hierarchy, pollution-purity, and civic disabilities.
  • M.N. Srinivas and Fieldwork Traditions: Srinivas brought structural-functional fieldwork to India, heavily influenced by Radcliffe-Brown. His pathbreaking village study of Rampura led to epochal concepts including Sanskritization (lower castes imitating upper caste lifestyles to elevate status), Westernization (impact of British rule on Indian institutions), and Dominant Caste (castes wielding numerical strength, economic power, and political clout, as analyzed in 'Religion and Society Among the Coorgs of South India', 1952).
  • D.P. Mukerji (D.P.): A prominent Marxist theorist with a deep grounding in Indian classical music, aesthetics, and history, D.P. Mukerji advocated that Indian sociologists must study Indian traditions (Tradition and Modernity, 'Modern Indian Culture', 1942). He posited that social change in India must be understood through the dialectic of tradition and the impact of colonial capitalism.
  • Radhakamal Mukerjee and Social Ecology: Founder of the Lucknow School of Sociology, Mukerjee integrated sociology with economics, ecology, and social psychology. In 'The Social Structure of Values' (1949), he pioneered the theory of social values, institutional economics, and regional ecological balance.
  • Irawati Karve and Kinship Studies: A pioneer in Indian anthropology and sociology, Karve's monumental work 'Kinship Organization in India' (1953) mapped kinship structures across four major cultural zones (Northern, Central, Southern, and Eastern) using linguistic, textual, and empirical data.
  • B.R. Ambedkar: A radical sociological thinker whose writings like 'Annihilation of Caste' (1936) and 'Castes in India: Their Mechanism, Genesis and Development' (1916) offered a scathing structural critique of Hindu social order, Brahmanical hegemony, and untouchability, framing caste as an enclosed class.

3. Methodological Debates, Structural Formulations & Modern Scope

The evolution of sociology is marked by continuous epistemological debates regarding methodology, objectivity, and the scope of social inquiry.

  • Positivism vs. Anti-Positivism (Interpretivism): While Comte and Durkheim championed quantitative, natural science methods for uncovering social laws, Weber, Dilthey, and the phenomenological/interpretative schools argued that human action is meaningful and requires subjective understanding (Verstehen) and qualitative hermeneutics.
  • Functionalism vs. Conflict Theory: Structural functionalism (Parsons, Merton, Durkheim) views society as a stable, integrated system maintaining equilibrium through consensus and shared values. Conversely, conflict theory (Marx, Dahrendorf, Coser) views society as fractured by power differentials, coercion, and competing interests among strata.
  • Micro vs. Macro Sociology: Classical sociology predominantly focused on macro-structures (state, class, religion, division of labour), whereas contemporary sociological developments encompass micro-level interactions (Symbolic Interactionism, Ethnomethodology, Goffmanian dramaturgy).
  • Application to Contemporary Social Problems: Modern sociological development extends into applied fields—addressing issues of globalization, demographic shifts, structural inequalities, environmental sustainability, gender justice, and the socio-economic transitions of post-colonial societies.

High-Yield Exam Points for UP PGT Sociology

  • Auguste Comte officially coined the term 'Sociology' in 1838, replacing his earlier term 'Social Physics', in his monumental treatise 'Cours de Philosophie Positive'.
  • Émile Durkheim defined 'Social Facts' in 'The Rules of Sociological Method' (1895) as ways of acting, thinking, and feeling that are external to the individual and endowed with coercive power.
  • Max Weber introduced the methodological concept of 'Verstehen' (interpretative understanding) in 'Economy and Society' to study subjective meanings attached to social action.
  • G.S. Ghurye authored 'Caste and Race in India' (1932), providing the definitive indological structural analysis of the six core features of the caste system.
  • M.N. Srinivas introduced the concepts of 'Sanskritization' and 'Dominant Caste' in his classic empirical monograph 'Religion and Society Among the Coorgs of South India' (1952).
  • Karl Marx and Friedrich Engels published 'The Communist Manifesto' in 1848, establishing the foundational tenets of historical materialism and class conflict theory.

Common Misconceptions & Exam Traps

  • Misconception 1: Students often confuse Comte's 'Social Physics' with Quetelet's statistical studies. Clarification: Comte originally named the new science 'Social Physics', but due to intellectual disputes with Adolphe Quételet, he coined 'Sociology' in 1838.
  • Misconception 2: Many aspirants believe Sanskritization results in structural transformation of the entire social hierarchy. Clarification: As M.N. Srinivas explicitly stated, Sanskritization leads only to 'positional change' within the existing caste framework, not 'structural change' of the system itself.

Practice Examination Questions (10 MCQs)

Test your conceptual understanding. Select an answer and check the detailed pedagogical rationale.

Q1 Who among the following officially coined the term 'Sociology' in the year 1838?
  • A Herbert Spencer
  • B Auguste Comte
  • C Émile Durkheim
  • D Max Weber
Correct Option: B (Auguste Comte)

Auguste Comte coined the term 'Sociology' in 1838 in Volume IV of his work 'Cours de Philosophie Positive'. He initially called it 'Social Physics', but changed it due to its prior use by Adolphe Quetelet. Option 0 is incorrect because Spencer applied evolutionary theory later; Option 2 and 3 established sociology academically later in the 19th and 20th centuries.

Q2 Which sociologist is associated with the formulation of the 'Law of the Three Stages'?
  • A Karl Marx
  • B Vilfredo Pareto
  • C Auguste Comte
  • D Pitirim Sorokin
Correct Option: C (Auguste Comte)

Auguste Comte propounded the Law of the Three Stages, asserting that human thought and society pass through Theological, Metaphysical, and Positive stages. Option 0 formulated Historical Materialism; Option 1 studied elite circulation; Option 4 studied cultural dynamics.

Q3 In which book did Émile Durkheim formally introduce and elaborate upon the concept of 'Social Facts'?
  • A The Division of Labour in Society
  • B Suicide
  • C The Rules of Sociological Method
  • D The Elementary Forms of the Religious Life
Correct Option: C (The Rules of Sociological Method)

Émile Durkheim explicitly defined and methodologically defended 'Social Facts' in 'The Rules of Sociological Method' (1895). While his earlier study 'The Division of Labour' (1893) applied this empirically, the methodological manual outlines their definition and characteristics.

Q4 Who authored the seminal work 'Caste and Race in India' (1932) and is regarded as the pioneer of Indian sociology?
  • A M.N. Srinivas
  • B G.S. Ghurye
  • C D.P. Mukerji
  • D Radhakamal Mukerjee
Correct Option: B (G.S. Ghurye)

G.S. Ghurye authored 'Caste and Race in India' (1932) and established academic sociology at Bombay University. Option 0 is famous for Sanskritization and Coorg studies; Option 2 championed Marxist-Indological synthesis; Option 3 founded the Lucknow school and ecological sociology.

Q5 The concept of 'Dominant Caste' was introduced by M.N. Srinivas based on his empirical study of which village?
  • A Shamirpet
  • B Kishan Garhi
  • C Rampura
  • D Sripuram
Correct Option: C (Rampura)

M.N. Srinivas formulated the concept of 'Dominant Caste' during his fieldwork in Rampura village, Mysore (Karnataka), published in his essay 'The Dominant Caste in Rampura' (1959) and his earlier Coorg monograph. Option 0 was studied by S.C. Dube; Option 1 by McKim Marriott; Option 3 by Andre Beteille.

Q6 What did Max Weber mean by the term 'Verstehen' in sociological methodology?
  • A Statistical measurement
  • B Interpretative understanding of social action
  • C Biological organic analogy
  • D Class conflict dynamics
Correct Option: B (Interpretative understanding of social action)

Weber used 'Verstehen' (German for 'understanding') to signify the methodological approach of interpretative understanding of subjective meanings, motives, and intentions behind social actions. Option 0 pertains to quantitative positivism; Option 2 to Spencer; Option 3 to Marx.

Q7 According to Émile Durkheim, societies characterized by mechanical solidarity are bound together primarily by:
  • A Functional interdependence
  • B Division of labour
  • C Collective consciousness and likeness
  • D Contractual legal systems
Correct Option: C (Collective consciousness and likeness)

Mechanical solidarity, typical of traditional societies, relies on a strong 'collective consciousness' and high degree of likeness among members. Organic solidarity relies on functional interdependence and division of labour.

Q8 Which classical theorist applied the 'Organic Analogy' to human society, comparing institutions to biological organs?
  • A Auguste Comte
  • B Herbert Spencer
  • C Karl Marx
  • D Max Weber
Correct Option: B (Herbert Spencer)

Herbert Spencer rigorously elaborated the Organic Analogy in works like 'The Principles of Sociology', viewing society as a biological organism exhibiting growth, structure, and functional differentiation.

Q9 Who among the following Indian sociologists emphasized the dialectic of 'Tradition and Modernity' and advocated for a Marxist-Indological synthesis?
  • A D.P. Mukerji
  • B G.S. Ghurye
  • C Irawati Karve
  • D B.R. Ambedkar
Correct Option: A (D.P. Mukerji)

D.P. Mukerji (D.P.) stressed that Indian sociology must reckon with Indian traditions and history through a dialectical framework, famously stating that Indians acquire their personality through traditions.

Q10 Which of the following processes, according to M.N. Srinivas, refers to the imitation of lifestyle, customs, and rituals of higher castes by lower castes?
  • A Westernization
  • B Industrialization
  • C Sanskritization
  • D Secularization
Correct Option: C (Sanskritization)

Sanskritization is defined by M.N. Srinivas as the process by which lower castes or tribes elevate their social status by adopting the linguistic, ritual, and dietary practices of upper 'twice-born' castes.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why is Auguste Comte considered the 'Father of Sociology'?

Auguste Comte is designated as the father of sociology because he formally coined the term 'Sociology' in 1838 and systematically advocated for establishing a distinct, positive science dedicated to the study of social order and social progress.

What is the difference between Varna and Jati in the Indian sociological context?

Varna refers to the macro-level, four-fold pan-Indian scriptural classification (Brahmin, Kshatriya, Vaishya, Shudra) based on the division of labor in Vedic texts. Jati refers to the empirical, micro-level endogamous local groups rooted in hereditary occupations, pollution-purity norms, and specific regional hierarchies.

How did Max Weber's approach differ from Auguste Comte's Positivism?

While Comte's positivism argued that social phenomena should be studied using quantitative, objective laws identical to natural sciences (nomothetic approach), Weber's interpretive sociology (Verstehen) emphasized understanding the subjective meanings, values, and motives actors attach to their social actions (ideographic approach).

What is the significance of M.N. Srinivas's concept of 'Dominant Caste' in Indian politics and sociology?

The 'Dominant Caste' concept highlights that ritual status (Varna/Jati rank) does not always coincide with political and economic power. A caste is dominant when it wields numerical strength, economic power (land ownership), and political clout in a region, thereby steering local power dynamics.
