Section 6 • Social Processes

Conflict

Conflict is a fundamental social process characterized by a conscious struggle between individuals or groups for scarce resources, power, status, or incompatible values. In classical and modern sociological theory, conflict is viewed not merely as a disruptive pathology, but as an essential engine of social change, group solidarity, and structural transformation.

1. Definitional Framework, Etymology & Classical Foundations

The term conflict is derived from the Latin root conflictus, meaning 'a striking together' or 'clash'. In sociology, conflict is defined as a competitive social interaction in which the parties involved are aware of the incompatibility of potential choices and in which each party wishes to occupy a position of dominance or secure mutually exclusive goals.

  • Karl Marx (1818–1883): In Das Kapital (1867) and The Communist Manifesto (1848, co-authored with Friedrich Engels), Marx posited that all recorded human history is the history of class struggles. Conflict between the bourgeoisie (owners of the means of production) and the proletariat (wage laborers) is structural and inevitable under capitalism, serving as the prime mover of historical materialism.
  • Georg Simmel (1858–1918): In his seminal work Soziologie (1908), specifically the section translated as Conflict (1955), Simmel treated conflict as a form of socialization (Vergesellschaftung). He argued that conflict is a vitalizing integrative force that establishes group boundaries, reduces internal tensions, and promotes coalitions.
  • Max Weber (1864–1920): In Economy and Society (1922), Weber analyzed conflict through the multidimensional stratification model involving class, status, and party. Unlike Marx, Weber viewed conflict as operating across multiple autonomous spheres rather than being solely reducible to economic property relations.
  • Lewis A. Coser (1913–2003): In The Functions of Social Conflict (1956), Coser synthesized Simmel's insights into structural-functionalism. He argued that conflict is not invariably dysfunctional; rather, 'in-group conflict' can strengthen group cohesion, while external conflict unifies fragmented internal factions against a common adversary.
  • Ralf Dahrendorf (1929–2009): In Class and Class Conflict in Industrial Society (1959), Dahrendorf introduced the 'conflict model of society', arguing that authority is the central locus of conflict. Authority is imperatively coordinated associations; those in authority dominate those in subjection, generating structural cleavages between ruling and ruling-subject groups.

2. Structural Concepts, Empirical Processes & Indian Sociological Context

In the context of Indian society, conflict manifests through complex structural hierarchies such as caste, class, gender, religion, and regionalism. Indian sociology has systematically analyzed how traditional hierarchies intersect with modern democratic politics to generate new idioms of social conflict.

  • M.N. Srinivas & Dominant Caste Conflict: Srinivas observed in his Rampura village studies that dominant castes (holding land, numerical strength, and ritual status) frequently engage in intense factional and inter-caste conflicts over political power and economic resources, which intensified following the introduction of adult franchise and Panchayati Raj.
  • B.R. Ambedkar & Annihilation of Caste: In Annihilation of Caste (1936), Ambedkar diagnosed the Hindu social order as a graded system of inequalities. He argued that organic social solidarity is impossible without destroying the scriptural foundations of caste, positing that revolutionary structural conflict against Brahmanical hegemony is essential for modern civic emancipation.
  • Andre Beteille's Caste, Class, and Power (1965): Beteille demonstrated how traditional overlapping hierarchies (where caste, class, and power coincided in Sripuram) became increasingly disjoined in modern India, leading to polycentric arenas of conflict where economic class and political power diverge from ritual caste hierarchy.
  • Radhakamal Mukerjee & Social Values: Mukerjee analyzed social conflict as a maladjustment of institutionalized social values and economic arrangements, emphasizing the need for institutional ethics and welfare economics to resolve systemic structural disharmonies.

3. Contemporary Dynamics, Social Policy & Applied Dimensions

Modern Indian society experiences multi-dimensional conflicts ranging from agrarian distress, displacement due to industrialization and mega-dams (studied extensively by sociologists like Walter Fernandes and Nandini Sundar), to identity politics, communalism, and Naxalism.

  • Agrarian and Peasant Conflicts: Rooted in unequal land tenure systems (Zamindari, Ryotwari), post-independence agrarian conflicts transformed from tenant-landlord struggles (e.g., Tebhaga, Telangana armed struggle) into 'new farmers' movements' championed by organizations like Bharatiya Kisan Union (BKU) focusing on terms of trade, electricity tariffs, and crop pricing.
  • Constitutional and Legal Mechanisms: The Indian State utilizes constitutional instruments—such as Articles 14, 15, 16, 17, and the Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes (Prevention of Atrocities) Act, 1989—to institutionalize dispute resolution and protect marginalized groups from structural violence and caste-based discrimination.
  • Ethnic and Sub-Nationalist Conflicts: Especially prominent in Northeast India and regions experiencing rapid demographic shifts, these conflicts highlight the friction between indigenous rights, linguistic identity, and centralizing state apparatuses.

High-Yield Exam Points for UP PGT Sociology

  • Karl Marx and Friedrich Engels co-authored 'The Communist Manifesto' in 1848, establishing historical materialism and class struggle as the driving force of history.
  • Georg Simmel's 1908 work 'Soziologie' introduced the concept that conflict is a form of socialization and can act as an integrative, boundary-maintaining mechanism.
  • Lewis A. Coser published 'The Functions of Social Conflict' in 1956, arguing that conflict can increase internal group cohesion and establish group identities.
  • Ralf Dahrendorf's 'Class and Class Conflict in Industrial Society' (1959) shifted the basis of conflict from economic property ownership to authority within imperatively coordinated associations.
  • B.R. Ambedkar's radical text 'Annihilation of Caste' (1936) critiqued the Varna system and called for the systematic dismantling of graded inequality in Hindu social structure.
  • Andre Beteille's empirical monograph 'Caste, Class, and Power' (1965) studied Sripuram village to analyze the disjunction between ritual caste hierarchy, economic class, and political power.

Common Misconceptions & Exam Traps

  • Misconception 1: Students often assume conflict is always destructive and anti-social. Sociologically (per Simmel and Coser), conflict can be highly functional in strengthening in-group solidarity and clearing structural bottlenecks.
  • Misconception 2: Conflating Marxian conflict theory with Weberian conflict theory. Marx reduced all conflict strictly to economic ownership of the means of production, whereas Weber introduced a multidimensional model encompassing economic class, social status, and political party.

Practice Examination Questions (10 MCQs)

Test your conceptual understanding. Select an answer and check the detailed pedagogical rationale.

Q1 Who authored the seminal work 'The Functions of Social Conflict' (1956) which integrated conflict theory with structural-functionalism?
  • A Karl Marx
  • B Lewis A. Coser
  • C Ralf Dahrendorf
  • D Max Weber
Correct Option: B (Lewis A. Coser)

Lewis A. Coser authored 'The Functions of Social Conflict' (1956), drawing heavily on Georg Simmel's earlier insights to demonstrate how conflict can perform positive, integrative functions within social structures. Marx focused on historical materialism; Dahrendorf focused on authority relations; Weber focused on multi-dimensional stratification.

Q2 According to Ralf Dahrendorf, what is the primary basis of social conflict in modern industrial society?
  • A Control over religious rituals
  • B Ownership of agricultural land
  • C Differential distribution of authority in associations
  • D Biological differences and race
Correct Option: C (Differential distribution of authority in associations)

Ralf Dahrendorf argued in 'Class and Class Conflict in Industrial Society' (1959) that conflict arises from the authority structure of imperatively coordinated associations, dividing society into those who hold authority and those who are subjected to it.

Q3 Who among the following classical sociologists conceptualized conflict itself as a specific 'form of socialization'?
  • A Auguste Comte
  • B Georg Simmel
  • C Herbert Spencer
  • D Emile Durkheim
Correct Option: B (Georg Simmel)

Georg Simmel, in his essay 'Conflict' (part of his 1908 work 'Soziologie'), famously treated conflict as a form of socialization (Vergesellschaftung), emphasizing that opposition is a way of making and maintaining social ties.

Q4 In which book did B.R. Ambedkar deliver a scathing critique of the Hindu social order and call for the destruction of the caste system?
  • A Who Were the Shudras?
  • B The Untouchables
  • C Annihilation of Caste
  • D Buddha and His Dhamma
Correct Option: C (Annihilation of Caste)

'Annihilation of Caste' (1936) was written by B.R. Ambedkar as a prepared address for the Jat-Pat-Todak Mandal of Lahore, which was cancelled because of its radical critique of the Vedas and Shastras.

Q5 Andre Beteille's classic community study 'Caste, Class, and Power' (1965) was conducted in which village?
  • A Rampura
  • B Shamirpet
  • C Sripuram
  • D Kishengarh
Correct Option: C (Sripuram)

Andre Beteille conducted his sociological fieldwork in Sripuram, a village in Thanjavur district of Tamil Nadu, examining how traditional overlapping hierarchies of caste, class, and power became differentiated.

Q6 Which sociological perspective views society as being held together by coercion, power, and the dominance of specific groups over others?
  • A Structural-Functionalism
  • B Symbolic Interactionism
  • C Conflict Perspective
  • D Ethnomethodology
Correct Option: C (Conflict Perspective)

The Conflict Perspective (associated with Marx, Weber, and modern conflict theorists) posits that social order is maintained not by consensus, but by power, coercion, and domination.

Q7 M.N. Srinivas utilized the concept of 'Dominant Caste' based primarily on empirical studies conducted in which village?
  • A Sripuram
  • B Rampura
  • C Wangala
  • D Mohana
Correct Option: B (Rampura)

M.N. Srinivas developed his famous concept of the 'dominant caste' based on his intensive ethnographic fieldwork in Rampura village, Mysore (Karnataka).

Q8 According to Karl Marx, what constitutes the foundation (base) upon which the legal, political, and ideological superstructure rests?
  • A Religious institutions
  • B Mode of production / Economic relations
  • C State bureaucratic apparatus
  • D Cultural values and morals
Correct Option: B (Mode of production / Economic relations)

In Marxian historical materialism, the economic base (forces and relations of production) determines the legal, political, and ideological superstructure of society.

Q9 Which theorist expanded social stratification beyond Marx's economic class by adding 'status' and 'party' as distinct dimensions of conflict and power?
  • A Emile Durkheim
  • B Max Weber
  • C Vilfredo Pareto
  • D Talcott Parsons
Correct Option: B (Max Weber)

Max Weber formulated a three-component theory of stratification: class (economic order), status (social/prestige order), and party (political order), creating a multidimensional approach to social conflict.

Q10 What is the primary sociological distinction between competition and conflict?
  • A Competition is always conscious, while conflict is always unconscious.
  • B Competition is goal-oriented and regulated by rules, whereas conflict involves direct struggle against opponents and attempts to neutralize them.
  • C Competition involves violence, while conflict is peaceful.
  • D There is no theoretical distinction between the two processes.
Correct Option: B (Competition is goal-oriented and regulated by rules, whereas conflict involves direct struggle against opponents and attempts to neutralize them.)

Sociologically, competition is an impersonal struggle for scarce goods where attention is focused on the goal rather than the rival. Conflict is direct, conscious, and personal, where opponents attempt to neutralize, injure, or eliminate each other.

Frequently Asked Questions

How is conflict distinguished from competition in sociological theory?

While both are struggle-based social processes, competition is an impersonal, indirect contest for scarce resources regulated by established norms where attention is focused on the objective (e.g., market competition). Conflict is a direct, conscious, and often personalized struggle where opponents are aware of each other and seek to neutralize, overcome, or eliminate the rival.

Is conflict always viewed as a negative or destructive force in sociology?

No. While early theorists like Marx viewed conflict primarily through revolutionary polarization, thinkers like Georg Simmel and Lewis Coser demonstrated that conflict can be functional. It establishes group boundaries, prevents stagnation, promotes internal solidarity, and acts as a safety valve for releasing accumulated social tensions.

How do Indian sociologists apply the theory of conflict to caste and class structures?

Indian sociologists such as M.N. Srinivas, B.R. Ambedkar, and Andre Beteille show that caste in India is not merely a harmonious hierarchy (as functionalists suggest), but a site of intense structural inequality, exploitation, and conflict. With democratization and land reforms, caste identity has increasingly merged with political mobilization and class interests.

What is the difference between Marxian and Dahrendorf's conflict theories?

Karl Marx located conflict exclusively in the economic base regarding the ownership of the means of production (bourgeoisie vs. proletariat). Ralf Dahrendorf argued that society has evolved beyond simple property ownership; therefore, modern conflict is institutionalized around authority and power relations within associations.
