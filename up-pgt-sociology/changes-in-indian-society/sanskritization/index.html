Section 13 • Changes in Indian Society

Sanskritization

Sanskritization is a foundational sociological concept introduced by M.N. Srinivas to describe the process by which lower castes or tribes adopt the customs, rituals, beliefs, and lifestyle of upper castes to raise their social status in the local hierarchy. It is a vital dynamic of social change in Indian society, heavily tested in the UP PGT Sociology and UGC NET examinations.

1. Definitional Framework, Etymology & Classical Foundations

The term Sanskritization was originally coined by Professor Mysore Narasimhachar Srinivas (M.N. Srinivas) in his monumental doctoral study, 'Religion and Society Among the Coorgs of South India' (1952), later revised and published globally by Oxford University Press (1952). Originally, Srinivas used the term 'Brahmanization' in his early work to denote the upward mobility of lower castes imitating Brahmanical customs. However, recognizing that lower castes often imitated Kshatriyas, Vaishyas, or dominant non-Brahmin clean castes rather than exclusively Brahmins, he broadened and renamed the concept to 'Sanskritization' in his later sociological writings.

  • Etymological Root: Derived from 'Sanskrit', the classical sacred language of Hindu scriptures, philosophy, and ritual texts, symbolizing high ritual purity and prestige in the Hindu Varna system.
  • Definition by Srinivas: Sanskritization is defined as "the process by which a 'low' Hindu caste, or tribal or other group, changes its customs, ritual, ideology, and way of life in the direction of a high, and frequently, 'twice-born' (dwija) caste."
  • Theoretical Roots in Classical & Indian Thought: While Srinivas utilized an empirical, structural-functionalist field-view approach heavily inspired by A.R. Radcliffe-Brown, the underlying mechanisms relate to Weberian concepts of status group mobility and status symbols, as well as classical Indian views of hierarchical ritual purity and pollution (G.S. Ghurye's indological formulations).

2. Structural Concepts, Empirical Processes & Indian Sociological Context

Sanskritization is not merely an isolated cultural borrowing; it is an active mechanism of social mobility within a rigid hierarchical stratification system. Because the caste system prohibits structural changes in one's ascribed birth status (jati), lower castes utilize cultural emulation as a vehicle for collective upward mobility.

  • Key Characteristics & Mechanisms:
    • It involves adopting vegetarianism, teetotalism, wearing the sacred thread (Yajnopavita), performing sanskaras (life-cycle rituals), and shifting from polytheistic blood sacrifices to puritanical Puranic Hinduism.
    • Sanskritization does not automatically lead to structural redistribution of economic or political power initially; however, economic or political ascendancy (often tied to the concept of the Dominant Caste, also formulated by Srinivas in his Rampura study) frequently precedes or accelerates Sanskritization.
  • Relation to Other Concepts: Yogendra Singh in 'Modernization of Indian Tradition' (1973) contextualized Sanskritization alongside Westernization and modernization, noting that Sanskritization is an intrinsic, indigenous tradition-oriented process of mobility, whereas Westernization represents exogenous, rational-secular institutional changes brought by British colonial impact.
  • Critical Scholarly Critiques: Sociologists like D.N. Majumdar (who studied the Khasa of U.P. and used the term 'Deculturation' or cultural adaptation), Harold Gould, and Marxist scholars point out that Sanskritization validates rather than challenges the Brahminical hegemony and caste hierarchy. It promotes positional change rather than structural transformation.

3. Contemporary Dynamics, Social Policy & Applied Dimensions

In contemporary post-independence India, Sanskritization has undergone complex transformations under the influence of democratic politics, universal adult franchise, constitutional reservation policies, and secularization.

  • De-Sanskritization & Dalit Assertions: Modern lower-caste and Dalit movements frequently reject Sanskritization in favor of anti-caste, secular, and Ambedkarite ideologies that contest Brahmanical supremacy rather than imitating it (B.R. Ambedkar's 'Annihilation of Caste', 1936).
  • Popular Culture & Mass Media: Regional vernacular mass media, Dalit literature, and political empowerment have led to reverse cultural assertions where folk and tribal cultures take pride in their distinct identities rather than assimilating into upper-caste norms.
  • Policy Relevance: For UP PGT aspirants, understanding Sanskritization provides the foundational theoretical lens to analyze rural social transformation, peasant upward mobility, and the interplay between tradition and modernity in contemporary Indian social structure.

High-Yield Exam Points for UP PGT Sociology

  • M.N. Srinivas first coined 'Brahmanization' in his 1952 study 'Religion and Society Among the Coorgs', later replacing it with 'Sanskritization'.
  • Sanskritization denotes a process of cultural emulation leading to positional change in the caste hierarchy, not structural change.
  • Yogendra Singh analyzed the interplay between Sanskritization and Modernization in his seminal work 'Modernization of Indian Tradition' (1973).
  • Economic and political dominance (Dominant Caste concept) often precedes or accompanies the process of Sanskritization.
  • D.N. Majumdar studied the Khasa of Uttar Pradesh and noted that lower-caste mobility involves complex cultural adaptations.
  • Unlike Westernization, which introduces secular and scientific values, Sanskritization reinforces traditional religious and ritual paradigms.

Common Misconceptions & Exam Traps

  • Misconception 1: Students often believe Sanskritization results in a structural revolution that abolishes the caste system. Fact: It only allows 'positional change' within the existing caste framework; the caste system itself remains intact.
  • Misconception 2: Confusing 'Brahmanization' with Sanskritization as distinct independent processes. Fact: Brahmanization was the original narrower term that Srinivas discarded in favor of the broader term Sanskritization.

Practice Examination Questions (10 MCQs)

Q1 Who among the following sociologists originally introduced the concept of 'Sanskritization' in Indian sociology?
  • A G.S. Ghurye
  • B M.N. Srinivas
  • C Yogendra Singh
  • D S.C. Dube
Correct Option: B (M.N. Srinivas)

M.N. Srinivas coined the term Sanskritization in his study of the Coorgs of South India (1952) to describe how lower castes emulate upper-caste lifestyles for social mobility. Thus, option 1 is correct while others are incorrect.

Q2 In which seminal book did M.N. Srinivas first introduce the concept of Sanskritization?
  • A Social Change in Modern India
  • B The Remembered Village
  • C Religion and Society Among the Coorgs
  • D India's Villages
Correct Option: C (Religion and Society Among the Coorgs)

The term was first formulated in 'Religion and Society Among the Coorgs of South India' (1952). 'Social Change in Modern India' (1966) expanded the concept further, but the origin lies in the Coorg study.

Q3 Which of the following best describes the outcome of Sanskritization according to M.N. Srinivas?
  • A Structural change of the caste system
  • B Positional change within the caste system
  • C Complete abolition of social stratification
  • D Transition from feudalism to industrial capitalism
Correct Option: B (Positional change within the caste system)

Sanskritization leads to positional mobility (a caste moves up in the local hierarchy) without altering the structural framework of the caste system itself.

Q4 Who authored the book 'Modernization of Indian Tradition' (1973) which systematically evaluates Sanskritization alongside Westernization?
  • A Andre Beteille
  • B D.P. Mukerji
  • C Yogendra Singh
  • D A.R. Desai
Correct Option: C (Yogendra Singh)

Yogendra Singh authored 'Modernization of Indian Tradition' (1973), providing a comprehensive theoretical synthesis of Sanskritization, Westernization, and Little/Great traditions.

Q5 Which term was initially used by M.N. Srinivas before he generalized it to 'Sanskritization'?
  • A Aryanization
  • B Brahmanization
  • C Hinduization
  • D Tradition-alization
Correct Option: B (Brahmanization)

Srinivas initially used 'Brahmanization' in his early papers, but later broadened it to 'Sanskritization' because lower castes often imitated non-Brahmin upper castes like Kshatriyas.

Q6 According to M.N. Srinivas, what is a crucial prerequisite that frequently accompanies or facilitates the process of Sanskritization for a lower caste?
  • A Acquisition of political or economic power (Dominant Caste)
  • B Immediate constitutional amendment
  • C Mass migration to metropolitan cities
  • D Complete rejection of all religious rituals
Correct Option: A (Acquisition of political or economic power (Dominant Caste))

Economic betterment and political power (often conceptualized under the 'Dominant Caste' framework) generally precede or accelerate the capacity of a lower caste to successfully Sanskritize its lifestyle.

Q7 Which sociologist studied the Khasa of Uttar Pradesh and examined cultural adaptation processes comparable to Sanskritization?
  • A G.S. Ghurye
  • B D.N. Majumdar
  • C M.N. Srinivas
  • D Irawati Karve
Correct Option: B (D.N. Majumdar)

D.N. Majumdar conducted extensive ethnographic studies on the Khasa of U.P. and analyzed cultural changes, utilizing terms like 'deculturation' and acculturation.

Q8 What is the primary cultural marker typically adopted by a lower caste undergoing Sanskritization?
  • A Adoption of Western formal attire
  • B Adoption of vegetarianism and puritanical rituals
  • C Abandonment of all caste symbols
  • D Adoption of industrial labor practices
Correct Option: B (Adoption of vegetarianism and puritanical rituals)

Sanskritization prominently features the adoption of upper-caste ritual practices, such as strict vegetarianism, teetotalism, and pure ritual habits associated with 'dwija' castes.

Q9 Which theoretical approach is primarily reflected in M.N. Srinivas's formulation of Sanskritization?
  • A Marxian Historical Materialism
  • B Structural-Functionalism and Empirical Field-View
  • C Radical Conflict Theory
  • D Exchange Theory
Correct Option: B (Structural-Functionalism and Empirical Field-View)

M.N. Srinivas utilized a structural-functionalist framework combined with intensive micro-empirical field studies (such as Rampura and Coorg), emphasizing cultural integration and social equilibrium.

Q10 How do Dalit and anti-caste social movements often view Sanskritization?
  • A As the ultimate path to complete social equality
  • B As an imitation of Brahmanical hegemony that validates the caste hierarchy
  • C As an economic policy enforced by the state
  • D As identical to Western modernization
Correct Option: B (As an imitation of Brahmanical hegemony that validates the caste hierarchy)

Critical and Dalit sociological perspectives (such as those inspired by B.R. Ambedkar) critique Sanskritization because lower castes end up internalizing and validating the oppressive ritual superiority of upper castes instead of dismantling caste.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the exact definition of Sanskritization according to M.N. Srinivas?

Sanskritization is the process by which a 'low' Hindu caste, tribal, or other group changes its customs, ritual, ideology, and way of life in the direction of a high, and frequently, 'twice-born' (dwija) caste.

Did M.N. Srinivas believe Sanskritization changes the structure of the caste system?

No. Srinivas explicitly maintained that Sanskritization leads only to 'positional change'—allowing specific jatis to move up within the hierarchy—while leaving the overall structural framework of the caste system completely intact.

What is the difference between Sanskritization and Westernization?

Sanskritization is an indigenous, tradition-bound process focusing on ritual purity, religious lifestyle, and imitation of upper castes. Westernization is an exogenous process driven by British rule, introducing secularism, rationalism, science, and modern legal-political institutions.

Why did Srinivas shift from 'Brahmanization' to 'Sanskritization'?

He shifted terminology because empirical field studies revealed that lower castes did not always imitate Brahmins; they frequently emulated local dominant upper castes such as Kshatriyas or Vaishyas, making 'Sanskritization' a more inclusive analytical category.
