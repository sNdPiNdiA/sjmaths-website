Section 17 • Women in Indian Society

Problems of Indian Women

The study of the problems of Indian women within sociology examines the systemic inequalities, patriarchal structures, and institutionalized oppressions faced by women in India. Grounded in classical and Indian sociological paradigms, it analyzes how historical institutions like caste, class, and family perpetuate gender stratification and modern social movements seek emancipation.

1. Definitional Framework, Etymology & Classical Foundations

The sociological analysis of the problems of Indian women requires unpacking the etymological and conceptual foundations of gender subordination. The term gender, derived from the Latin root genus (meaning kind or type), was popularized by feminist sociologists to distinguish socially constructed roles from biological sex. In Indian society, women's problems are deeply intertwined with structural institutions such as the family, marriage, and kinship, theorized extensively by classical and modern thinkers.

  • Patriarchy and Power: Defined by theorists like Sylvia Walby and classical sociologists as a system of social structures and practices in which men dominate, oppress, and exploit women. In India, patriarchy operates through institutionalized mechanisms such as endogamy, patrilocality, and patrilineal descent.
  • Classical Sociological Perspectives: Karl Marx and Friedrich Engels (in The Origin of the Family, Private Property and the State, 1884) linked the subjugation of women to the rise of private property and class stratification, viewing monogamous marriage as a tool for ensuring the inheritance of wealth through male lines. Émile Durkheim examined how division of labour within the family assigns expressive roles to women and instrumental roles to men, reinforcing organic solidarity with traditional gendered constraints.

2. Structural Concepts, Empirical Processes & Indian Sociological Context

Indian sociology has provided profound empirical insights into the condition of women, moving beyond Western theoretical frameworks to address indigenous complexities. Pioneers like M.N. Srinivas, Irawati Karve, and Leela Dube have demonstrated how caste and kinship shape women's lives.

  • Caste-Gender Nexus: G.S. Ghurye and B.R. Ambedkar highlighted how the caste system maintains its purity through the strict control of women's sexuality. Ambedkar, in Annihilation of Caste (1936) and his seminal essay Castes in India: Their Mechanism, Genesis and Development (1916), argued that hypergamy (anuloma) and hypogamy (pratiloma) are regulated by endogamy, making the subjugation of women central to caste preservation.
  • Kinship and Regional Variations: Irawati Karve, in Kinship Organization in India (1953), demonstrated how kinship structures vary between North and South India, impacting women's autonomy, property rights, and social mobility. North Indian kinship systems are characterized by patrilocality and strict spatial segregation (purdah), whereas South Indian kinship systems often allow cross-cousin marriages, conferring greater familial support and relative autonomy to women.
  • Sanskritization and Women's Status: M.N. Srinivas observed that lower castes often adopt upper-caste customs (such as bans on widow remarriage, dowry, and seclusion of women) during Sanskritization, paradoxically leading to a decline in women's economic independence and social freedom as they emulate patriarchal upper-caste models.

3. Contemporary Dynamics, Social Policy & Applied Dimensions

Modern Indian society grapples with structural problems such as female foeticide, domestic violence, dowry harassment, and workplace discrimination, mediated by rapid social change, globalization, and legislative interventions.

  • Demographic Imbalances: The skewed child sex ratio (CSR) in India, highlighted by census data, reflects deep-seated son preference driven by patriarchal inheritance patterns and ritual obligations, exacerbated by the misuse of reproductive technologies (Pre-Conception and Pre-Natal Diagnostic Techniques Act, 1994).
  • Legislative and Constitutional Frameworks: The Indian state has enacted progressive legislation to safeguard women's rights, including the Dowry Prohibition Act (1961), the Protection of Women from Domestic Violence Act (PWDVA, 2005), and the Sexual Harassment of Women at Workplace (Prevention, Prohibition and Redressal) Act (2013). The 73rd and 74th Constitutional Amendments (1992) revolutionized local governance by mandating a one-third reservation for women in Panchayats and Municipalities, fostering grassroots political leadership.

  • The Protection of Women from Domestic Violence Act (PWDVA) was enacted in India in the year 2005.

Common Misconceptions & Exam Traps

  • Misconception 1: Students often assume that Sanskritization invariably improves the social status of women across all castes. Sociological reality: Lower-caste women often lose economic autonomy and face stricter behavioral controls when their communities adopt upper-caste patriarchal practices.
  • Misconception 2: Confusing matrilineal societies (such as the Nayars of Kerala or Khasis of Meghalaya) with matriarchal societies. Sociological reality: Matriliny denotes descent and property inheritance through the female line, but authority and political power frequently remain wielded by maternal uncles or males (matrilocality/avunculate), not women.

Practice Examination Questions (10 MCQs)

Test your conceptual understanding. Select an answer and check the detailed pedagogical rationale.

Q1 Who among the following classical theorists linked the subjugation of women directly to the emergence of private property and the patriarchal monogamous family?
  • A Émile Durkheim
  • B Friedrich Engels
  • C Max Weber
  • D Auguste Comte
Correct Option: B (Friedrich Engels)

Friedrich Engels, in his seminal work 'The Origin of the Family, Private Property and the State' (1884), argued that the historic defeat of the female sex occurred with the accumulation of private property and the need for men to ensure patrilineal inheritance. Thus, option 1 is correct. Durkheim focused on division of labour and solidarity; Weber on rationalization and stratification; Comte on positivism.

Q2 According to Dr. B.R. Ambedkar, what is the primary mechanism used to maintain the purity of the caste system?
  • A Class struggle
  • B Control of women's sexuality through endogamy
  • C Religious rituals and sacrifices
  • D Division of agricultural labour
Correct Option: B (Control of women's sexuality through endogamy)

In 'Castes in India: Their Mechanism, Genesis and Development' (1916) and 'Annihilation of Caste' (1936), Ambedkar asserted that caste is essentially enclosed class maintained by endogamy, which necessitates rigorous control over women's marital choices and sexuality.

Q3 Which sociologist famously highlighted the North-South dichotomy in Indian kinship systems and its implications for women's autonomy?
  • A M.N. Srinivas
  • B G.S. Ghurye
  • C Irawati Karve
  • D D.P. Mukerji
Correct Option: C (Irawati Karve)

Irawati Karve published 'Kinship Organization in India' in 1953, providing a comprehensive regional analysis showing that North Indian kinship is patrilocal and strictly exogamous regarding villages, whereas South Indian kinship allows cousin marriages and offers greater mobility to women.

Q4 The process of 'Sanskritization' as defined by M.N. Srinivas frequently affects women by:
  • A Granting them absolute financial independence
  • B Imposing stricter upper-caste patriarchal norms such as seclusion and dowry
  • C Eliminating all forms of gender stratification
  • D Promoting matriarchal family structures
Correct Option: B (Imposing stricter upper-caste patriarchal norms such as seclusion and dowry)

M.N. Srinivas observed that when lower castes emulate upper-caste customs, they often adopt restrictive practices like dowry, purdah, and bans on widow remarriage, leading to a decline in women's relative freedom.

Q5 In which year was the Protection of Women from Domestic Violence Act (PWDVA) enacted in India?
  • A 1994
  • B 2005
  • C 2013
  • D 1961
Correct Option: B (2005)

The Protection of Women from Domestic Violence Act was passed in 2005 to provide a civil remedy for protection against physical, emotional, sexual, verbal, and economic abuse within the household.

Q6 The 73rd and 74th Constitutional Amendment Acts (1992) ensure what proportion of reservation for women in local self-government institutions?
  • A 25 percent
  • B 33 percent (one-third)
  • C 50 percent in all states uniformly by constitution
  • D 10 percent
Correct Option: B (33 percent (one-third))

The 73rd and 74th Constitutional Amendments mandated not less than one-third (33%) of total seats to be reserved for women in Panchayati Raj institutions and municipalities, transforming women's political participation.

Q7 What does the term 'Matriliny' specifically denote in sociological literature?
  • A Rule and authority exercised exclusively by women
  • B Descent and inheritance traced through the mother's line
  • C A society devoid of any patriarchal influence
  • D Polyandrous family structures
Correct Option: B (Descent and inheritance traced through the mother's line)

Matriliny refers strictly to descent and inheritance passing through the female line (mother to daughter/niece). It does not automatically imply matriarchy (rule by women), as authority is often held by male members (avunculate).

Q8 Which sociological perspective views gender inequality as functional for maintaining social integration and social order?
  • A Conflict Perspective
  • B Functionalist Perspective
  • C Feminist Standpoint Theory
  • D Marxist Sociology
Correct Option: B (Functionalist Perspective)

Classical functionalism (such as Talcott Parsons' sex-role differentiation theory) argued that the traditional division of labour—where men take instrumental roles and women take expressive roles—serves to integrate society and maintain equilibrium.

Q9 The Pre-Conception and Pre-Natal Diagnostic Techniques (PCPNDT) Act was enacted in India primarily to combat which issue?
  • A Dowry harassment
  • B Female foeticide and skewed sex ratio
  • C Domestic violence
  • D Lack of maternity benefits
Correct Option: B (Female foeticide and skewed sex ratio)

The PCPNDT Act was enacted in 1994 (amended in 2003) to ban and penalize prenatal sex determination and selective abortion, addressing the declining child sex ratio caused by rampant son preference.

Q10 Who wrote 'The Origin of the Family, Private Property and the State'?
  • A Karl Marx
  • B Friedrich Engels
  • C Max Weber
  • D Auguste Comte
Correct Option: B (Friedrich Engels)

Friedrich Engels wrote 'The Origin of the Family, Private Property and the State' in 1884, drawing heavily on Lewis H. Morgan's ethnographic research to connect gender oppression with economic systems.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do Indian sociologists distinguish between sex and gender?

In Indian sociology, 'sex' refers to the biological and anatomical differences between males and females, whereas 'gender' is a socially and culturally constructed set of roles, expectations, behaviors, and power dynamics assigned to men and women by patriarchal social institutions.

Why is the study of kinship important for understanding women's problems in India?

Kinship systems dictate rules of residence, marriage, inheritance, and authority. Sociologists like Irawati Karve demonstrated that North Indian patrilocal kinship enforces strict spatial and social seclusion on women, whereas regional variations impact women's autonomy, mobility, and support systems.

What is the relationship between caste endogamy and women's subordination?

As analyzed by B.R. Ambedkar and G.S. Ghurye, the caste system maintains its distinct boundaries through strict endogamy (marriage within the caste). Because caste status is traditionally patrilineal, controlling women's sexuality and marriage choices becomes essential to preventing caste mixing (inter-caste unions) and preserving caste purity.

How have constitutional amendments empowered women in rural India?

The 73rd and 74th Constitutional Amendment Acts (1992) mandated a minimum one-third reservation of seats for women in Panchayati Raj institutions (village councils) and urban local bodies, bringing millions of women into formal political decision-making processes at the grassroots level.
