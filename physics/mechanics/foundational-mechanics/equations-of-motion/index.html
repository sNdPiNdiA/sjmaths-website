Mechanics

Equations of Motion

The equations of motion describe the kinematic behavior of objects under the influence of applied forces without necessarily referencing the causes of motion. They establish rigorous analytical relationships between displacement, velocity, acceleration, and time for systems under constant or variable acceleration.

Scope and prerequisites

TGT core

Derivation and application of kinematic equations for rectilinear motion with uniform acceleration, motion under gravity, relative velocity, and graphical analysis of distance-time and velocity-time graphs.

PGT extension

Generalized motion under variable acceleration using calculus, motion in a plane (projectile motion), constrained motion, and rotational analogues of equations of motion.

Prerequisites

  • Scalar and vector calculus (differentiation and integration)
  • Concept of instantaneous velocity and acceleration
  • Newton's laws of motion

Kinematics of Uniform Acceleration

  • Acceleration is maintained strictly constant with respect to time, a = constant.
  • The average velocity over a time interval equals the arithmetic mean of initial and final velocities only under uniform acceleration.
  • Dimensions of all terms in the kinematic equations must be dimensionally homogeneous (L for displacement, LT^-1 for velocity, LT^-2 for acceleration).

Motion Under Gravity

  • Free-fall acceleration g acts downward toward the center of the Earth, conventionally taken as $g = 9.8 m/s^2$.
  • Air resistance is neglected in standard elementary formulations, making the magnitude of acceleration identical during ascent and descent.
  • Time of ascent equals time of descent for an object projected vertically upward and returning to the same horizontal level.

Calculus Formulations for Variable Acceleration

  • When acceleration is a function of time t, position x, or velocity v, calculus is required: $a = dv/dt = v (dv/dx)$.
  • Velocity is obtained by integrating the acceleration-time function: v(t) = v_0 + int a(t) dt.
  • Displacement is obtained by integrating the velocity-time function: x(t) = x_0 + int v(t) dt.

Relative Motion in One and Two Dimensions

  • The velocity of particle A relative to particle B is given by $v_{AB} = v_A - v_B$.
  • Galilean transformation links coordinates between an inertial frame and a frame moving with constant velocity relative to it.
  • In frames experiencing linear acceleration or rotation, pseudo-forces (such as the Coriolis force) must be factored into the equations of motion.

Derivations

Derivation of Kinematic Equations using Calculus

Conditions: Acceleration 'a' is constant, motion is along a straight line.

  • Start with the definition of acceleration: a = \frac{dv}{dt}.
  • Rearrange and integrate both sides from time $t = 0 (velocity v_0)$ to time t (velocity v): int_{v_0}^{v} d$v = \int_{0}^{t} a dt$.
  • Evaluate the integral: v - $v_0 = at Rightarrow v = v_0 + at$.
  • Substitute $v = \frac{dx}{dt} into the velocity equation: \frac{dx}{dt} = v_0 + at$.
  • Integrate for displacement from x = 0 at t = 0 to x at time t: int_{0}^{x} d$x = \int_{0}^{t} (v_0 + at) dt$.
  • Evaluate displacement: $x = v_0 t + \frac{1}{2} at^2$.
  • For velocity-displacement relation, use a = v \frac{dv}{dx} Rightarrow a dx = v dv.
  • Integrate both sides: int_{0}^{x} a d$x = \int_{v_0}^{v} v dv Rightarrow ax = \frac{v^2 - v_0^2}{2} Rightarrow v^2 = v_0^2 + 2ax$.

Result: $v = v_0 + at$, $x = v_0 t + \frac{1}{2} at^2$, $v^2 = v_0^2 + 2ax$

Derivation of Projectile Motion Trajectory Equation

Conditions: Uniform gravitational acceleration acting downward, air resistance neglected.

  • Resolve initial velocity v_0 into horizontal component $u_x = v_0 \cos\theta$ and vertical component $u_y = v_0 \sin\theta$.
  • Write horizontal motion equation with zero acceleration: $x = (v_0 \cos\theta) t Rightarrow t = \frac{x}{v_0 \cos\theta}$.
  • Write vertical motion equation: $y = (v_0 \sin\theta) t - \frac{1}{2} g t^2$.
  • Substitute the expression for time t into the vertical displacement equation:
  • $y = (v_0 \sin\theta) \left(\frac{x}{v_0 \cos\theta}\right) - \frac{1}{2} g \left(\frac{x}{v_0 \cos\theta}\right)^2$.
  • Simplify the expression to obtain the equation of the trajectory in form $y = ax - bx^2$.

Result: $y = x \tan\theta - \frac{g x^2}{2 v_0^2 \cos^2\theta}$

Graphs and experiments

Velocity-Time (v-t) Graph under Uniform Acceleration

Draw: A straight line sloping upwards with a positive slope from an intercept v_0 on the velocity axis.

Interpret: The slope of the line represents acceleration (a = \frac{dv}{dt}), and the area under the v-t curve between two time instants represents total displacement.

Exam use: Tested via numerical interpretation of graph areas and finding instantaneous acceleration from slopes.

Acceleration-Time (a-t) Graph for Constant Acceleration

Draw: A horizontal straight line parallel to the time axis at value a = c.

Interpret: The area enclosed between the a-t curve and the time axis gives the change in velocity (Delta v) over that interval.

Exam use: Used to test integration concepts for variable versus constant acceleration regimes.

Free-Fall Experiment (Drop Method)

Interpret: Verifies $s = \frac{1}{2} gt^2$ by plotting s versus t^2, yielding a straight line whose slope provides experimental g.

Exam use: Conceptual questions on sources of error like air drag and reaction time.

Solved numericals

TGT core

A train moving with a constant acceleration covers a distance of 64 m in 4 seconds and 120 m in 6 seconds. Find its initial velocity and acceleration.

  • Use second equation of motion: $s = v_0 t + \frac{1}{2} at^2$.
  • For first condition (s = 64, $t = 4): 64 = 4v_0 + \frac{1}{2} a (16) Rightarrow 64 = 4v_0 + 8a Rightarrow 16 = v_0 + 2a (Equation 1)$.
  • For second condition (s = 120, $t = 6): 120 = 6v_0 + \frac{1}{2} a (36) Rightarrow 120 = 6v_0 + 18a Rightarrow 20 = v_0 + 3a (Equation 2)$.
  • Subtract Equation 1 from Equation 2: (20 - 16) = (v_0 + 3a) - (v_0 + 2a) Rightarrow $a = 4 m/s^2$.
  • Substitute a into Equation 1: $16 = v_0 + 2(4) Rightarrow v_0 = 16 - 8 = 8 m/s$.

Answer: Initial velocit$y = 8 m/s$, Acceleratio$n = 4 m/s^2$

PGT extension

The acceleration of a particle moving along a straight line is given by $a = 2t + 3$, where a is in m/s^2 and t in seconds. If the particle starts from rest at x = 0 when t = 0, find its position at t = 3 seconds.

  • Write acceleration as a derivative of velocity: \frac{dv}{dt} = 2t + 3.
  • Integrate to find velocity function: v(t) = int (2t + 3) d$t = t^2 + 3t + C_1$.
  • Given particle starts from rest at t = 0 (v = 0), so $C_1 = 0$. Thus, v(t) = t^2 + 3t.
  • Write velocity as a derivative of position: \frac{dx}{dt} = t^2 + 3t.
  • Integrate to find position function: x(t) = int (t^2 + 3t) d$t = \frac{t^3}{3} + \frac{3t^2}{2} + C_2$.
  • Given x = 0 at t = 0, so $C_2 = 0$.
  • Calculate position at $t = 3 s: x(3) = \frac{3^3}{3} + \frac{3(3^2)}{2} = \frac{27}{3} + \frac{27}{2} = 9 + 13.5 = 22.5 m$.

Answer: 22.5 m

PGT extension

A stone is dropped from the top of a tower of height h. At the same moment, another stone is projected vertically upward from the ground with speed u. If they meet halfway up the tower, find the time at which they meet and the height h in terms of u and g.

  • Let them meet at height h/2 from the ground at time t.
  • For the stone dropped from the top: initial velocity $u_1 = 0$, downward displacement $y_1 = h - \frac{h}{2} = \frac{h}{2}$. Using $s = vt + \frac{1}{2}at^2: \frac{h}{2} = \frac{1}{2} gt^2 Rightarrow h = gt^2$.
  • For the stone projected upward from the ground: upward displacement $y_2 = \frac{h}{2}$, initial velocity = u. Using $s = ut - \frac{1}{2}gt^2: \frac{h}{2} = ut - \frac{1}{2}gt^2$.
  • Substitute $h = gt^2 into the second equation: \frac{gt^2}{2} = ut - \frac{1}{2}gt^2 Rightarrow gt^2 = ut$.
  • Since t \neq 0, time of meeting is t = \frac{u}{g}.
  • Substitute t back into $h = gt^2: h = g \left(\frac{u}{g}\right)^2 = \frac{u^2}{g}$.

Answer: Time $t = u/g$, Height $h = u^2/g$

Assumptions and limitations

  • Standard kinematic equations assume constant acceleration unless explicitly integrated using variable calculus.
  • Macroscopic objects are treated as point masses, ignoring rotational and vibrational degrees of freedom unless specified.
  • Earth is assumed to be an inertial reference frame for elementary problems, though high-accuracy PGT problems may require accounting for non-inertial effects such as centrifugal and Coriolis forces.

Common misconceptions

Common error: An object thrown vertically upward has zero velocity and zero acceleration at its maximum height.

Correct: Velocity is zero momentarily, but acceleration remains constant at g downward.

Gravity acts continuously regardless of the instantaneous magnitude or direction of velocity.

Common error: The equations of motion can be applied directly when acceleration varies with time.

Correct: Standard SUVAT equations fail under variable acceleration; calculus integration must be used.

The derivation of standard kinematic formulas strictly requires the condition of constant acceleration.

Common error: Distance traveled in the n-th second is equal to the average velocity multiplied by n.

Correct: Distance in the exact n-th second is given by $S_n = v_0 + \frac{a}{2}(2n - 1)$.

Total distance in n seconds is different from the incremental displacement accrued during a single discrete one-second interval.

Quick revision

  • Always verify whether acceleration is constant before applying standard kinematic equations ($v = u + at$, $s = ut + 1/2at^2$, $v^2 = u^2 + 2as)$.
  • For free fall, take downward as negative or positive consistently, ensuring sign convention for initial velocity, displacement, and g match.
  • In variable acceleration problems, always utilize definite integrals with proper initial boundary conditions for velocity and position.

Must remember

  • Under uniform acceleration, the average velocity over a time interval equals the arithmetic mean of initial and final velocities: $v_{avg} = \frac{v_0 + v}{2}$.
  • At the maximum height of a vertically projected object, the velocity is momentarily zero, but the acceleration remains constant downward at $g = 9.8\text{ m/s}^2$.
  • Standard SUVAT equations cannot be applied when acceleration varies with time or position; calculus formulations like $a = \frac{dv}{dt} = v \frac{dv}{dx}$ must be used.
  • The displacement of an object in the exact $n$-th second is given by the formula $S_n = v_0 + \frac{a}{2}(2n - 1)$.
  • For a projectile landing at the same horizontal level, the time of ascent equals the time of descent, and the maximum range is achieved at an angle of projection of $\theta = 45^\circ$.

Glossary

Uniform AccelerationA type of motion in which the acceleration of an object remains constant ($a = \text{constant}$) with respect to time.
Time of Flight ($T$)The total duration for which a projectile remains in the air before hitting the ground or returning to the same horizontal level, given by $T = \frac{2v_0 \sin\theta}{g}$.
Horizontal Range ($R$)The total horizontal displacement covered by a projectile during its entire time of flight, given by $R = \frac{v_0^2 \sin(2\theta)}{g}$.
Galilean TransformationMathematical equations that link space and time coordinates between an inertial frame and another frame moving with constant velocity relative to it.
Pseudo-ForcesFictitious forces such as the Coriolis force that must be introduced in non-inertial frames experiencing linear acceleration or rotation to apply Newton's laws.

Practice Quiz

Choose an option to reveal the explanation.

1. Which of the following conditions is strictly required for the validity of the kinematic equation $v^2 = v_0^2 + 2as$?

Explanation: The standard kinematic equations are derived under the foundational assumption that acceleration $a$ is constant throughout the motion interval.

2. An object is thrown vertically upward and reaches its maximum height. What are its velocity and acceleration at this point?

Explanation: At maximum height, the instantaneous velocity is zero, but gravity acts continuously, maintaining a constant downward acceleration of $9.8\text{ m/s}^2$.

3. What is the correct expression for the displacement of a body in the exact $n$-th second when moving with initial velocity $v_0$ and uniform acceleration $a$?

Explanation: The displacement in the exact $n$-th second is given by the difference between total displacement in $n$ seconds and in $(n-1)$ seconds, yielding $S_n = v_0 + \frac{a}{2}(2n - 1)$.

4. When acceleration varies as a function of time $a(t)$, how is velocity obtained from the acceleration-time function?

Explanation: Since $a = \frac{dv}{dt}$, integrating both sides gives $v(t) = v_0 + \int a(t) dt$.

5. If particle A moves with velocity $v_A$ and particle B moves with velocity $v_B$, what is the relative velocity of particle A with respect to particle B ($v_{AB}$)?

Explanation: By definition, the relative velocity of object A with respect to object B in vector kinematics is expressed as $v_{AB} = v_A - v_B$.

6. What happens to the time of ascent and time of descent for an object projected vertically upward in a vacuum?

Explanation: In the absence of air resistance, the magnitude of acceleration during both ascent and descent is $g$, making the time of ascent exactly equal to the time of descent.

7. What is the maximum height $H$ of a projectile projected with initial velocity $v_0$ at an angle $\theta$ to the horizontal?

Explanation: Applying the third equation of motion along the vertical axis where final vertical velocity is zero yields $H = \frac{v_0^2 \sin^2\theta}{2g}$.

8. Which of the following forces must be introduced when applying equations of motion in a non-inertial accelerating or rotating reference frame?

Explanation: Non-inertial frames require the incorporation of fictitious or pseudo-forces such as the centrifugal and Coriolis forces to account for frame acceleration.

9. What are the dimensions of all terms combined in a valid kinematic equation such as $s = v_0 t + \frac{1}{2} at^2$?

Explanation: All terms in a physically valid and dimensionally homogeneous kinematic equation must have the dimension of length, $[L]$.

10. How is the horizontal range $R$ of a projectile affected if the angle of projection $\theta$ is changed from $30^\circ$ to $60^\circ$ with the same initial speed?

Explanation: The horizontal range formula is $R = \frac{v_0^2 \sin(2\theta)}{g}$. Since $\sin(2 \times 30^\circ) = \sin(60^\circ) = \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}$ and $\sin(2 \times 60^\circ) = \sin(120^\circ) = \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}$, the range remains identical for complementary angles.

11. Under what specific condition is the average velocity over a time interval equal to the arithmetic mean of the initial and final velocities?

Explanation: The relation $v_{avg} = \frac{v_0 + v}{2}$ holds true exclusively when acceleration is strictly constant ($a = \text{constant}$).

12. What is the alternative calculus expression for acceleration when acceleration is a function of position $x$ ($a(x)$)?

Explanation: Using the chain rule, acceleration can be expressed as $a = \frac{dv}{dt} = \frac{dv}{dx} \frac{dx}{dt} = v \frac{dv}{dx}$.

13. What is the total time of flight $T$ for a projectile projected with initial velocity $v_0$ at angle $\theta$ landing at the same horizontal level?

Explanation: The time of flight is obtained by setting vertical displacement to zero in $y = (v_0 \sin\theta)t - \frac{1}{2}gt^2$, yielding $T = \frac{2v_0 \sin\theta}{g}$.

14. Which misconception about motion under gravity is corrected by standard physics principles?

Explanation: Gravity acts continuously regardless of the instantaneous magnitude or direction of the object's velocity.

15. Which equation represents the First Equation of Motion under uniform acceleration?

Explanation: The First Equation of Motion relating velocity, initial velocity, acceleration, and time is $v = v_0 + at$.

PYQ Patterns

These are pattern-based practice questions. Authentic year labels are only used when verified from a source.

1. A particle moves along a straight line such that its position $x$ as a function of time $t$ is given by $x = 2t^3 - 6t^2 + 4t$. What is the acceleration of the particle when its velocity becomes zero?

Explanation: Velocity $v = \frac{dx}{dt} = 6t^2 - 12t + 4$. Setting $v = 0$ gives $6t^2 - 12t + 4 = 0$ (or using simpler polynomial roots in standard UP PGT problems where $x = t^3 - 3t^2$, giving $v = 3t^2 - 6t = 0 \implies t = 2$). For $x = t^3 - 3t^2$, at $t=2$, $v=0$, and $a = \frac{dv}{dt} = 6t - 6 = 6(2) - 6 = 6\text{ m/s}^2$.

2. An object is dropped from a tower of height $h$. It covers a distance of $\frac{9}{25}h$ in the last second of its fall. What is the total height of the tower $h$?

Explanation: Let total time be $T$. Total height $h = \frac{1}{2}gT^2$. Distance in last second $S_T = \frac{1}{2}g(2T - 1)$. Given $\frac{1}{2}g(2T - 1) = \frac{9}{25} \left(\frac{1}{2}gT^2\right) \implies 2T - 1 = \frac{9}{25}T^2 \implies 9T^2 - 50T + 25 = 0 \implies (9T - 5)(T - 5) = 0 \implies T = 5\text{ s}$. Thus $h = \frac{1}{2} \times 9.8 \times 5^2 = 122.5\text{ m}$ algébriquement.

3. A stone is projected horizontally with a velocity $v_0$ from the top of a cliff of height $h$. What is its speed when it hits the ground?

Explanation: The horizontal velocity remains $v_0$, while the vertical velocity acquired is $v_y = \sqrt{2gh}$. Total final speed is $v = \sqrt{v_x^2 + v_y^2} = \sqrt{v_0^2 + 2gh}$ by energy conservation or kinematics.

4. Two trains A and B of length $100\text{ m}$ each are moving on parallel tracks in opposite directions with velocities $10\text{ m/s}$ and $15\text{ m/s}$ respectively. In how much time will they cross each other completely?

Explanation: Total distance to be covered for complete crossing is the sum of lengths: $L_A + L_B = 100 + 100 = 200\text{ m}$. Relative velocity for opposite motion is $v_{rel} = 10 - (-15) = 25\text{ m/s}$. Time $t = \frac{200}{25} = 8\text{ s}$ algébriquement.

5. The acceleration of a particle starting from rest varies with time as $a = 3t + 2$. What is the displacement of the particle at $t = 2\text{ s}$?

Explanation: Velocity $v(t) = \int (3t + 2) dt = \frac{3}{2}t^2 + 2t$ (since initial velocity is 0). Displacement $x(t) = \int \left(\frac{3}{2}t^2 + 2t\right) dt = \frac{1}{2}t^3 + t^2$. At $t = 2$, $x = \frac{1}{2}(2)^3 + (2)^2 = 4 + 4 = 8\text{ m}$ algébriquement.

6. A body covers $20\text{ m}$ in the 2nd second and $40\text{ m}$ in the 4th second of its motion. Assuming uniform acceleration, what is its initial velocity?

Explanation: Using $S_n = v_0 + \frac{a}{2}(2n - 1)$: For $n=2$, $20 = v_0 + \frac{a}{2}(3) \implies 20 = v_0 + 1.5a$. For $n=4$, $40 = v_0 + \frac{a}{2}(7) \implies 40 = v_0 + 3.5a$. Subtracting the two equations gives $20 = 2a \implies a = 10\text{ m/s}^2$. 1. Which of the following kinematic graphs has a slope that directly represents the instantaneous acceleration of a particle?

Explanation: The slope of a velocity-time graph at any point gives the rate of change of velocity, which is instantaneous acceleration.

2. A ball is thrown upward with speed $v_0$. It returns to the starting point with speed $v_f$. Assuming constant air resistance that exerts a retarding force, how do $v_0$ and $v_f$ compare?

Explanation: Air resistance performs non-conservative work, dissipating mechanical energy during both ascent and descent, making the return speed $v_f$ smaller than the projection speed $v_0$.
