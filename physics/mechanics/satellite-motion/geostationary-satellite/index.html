Mechanics

Geostationary Satellite

A geostationary satellite is an earth-orbiting satellite that has an orbital period equal to the earth's rotational period of 23 hours 56 minutes and 4 seconds. It appears stationary relative to a specific point on the earth's equator due to its synchronous prograde equatorial orbit.

Scope and prerequisites

TGT core

Definition, time period, orbital radius from the earth's surface, equatorial orbit condition, and basic applications in communication.

PGT extension

Derivation of orbital radius and velocity, energy considerations, stability criteria, perturbation effects due to solar-lunar gravitational forces, and parking orbit injection mechanics.

Prerequisites

  • Newton's Law of Gravitation
  • Centripetal Force and Central Force Motion
  • Kepler's Third Law of Planetary Motion

Orbital Synchronicity and Period

  • The time period T of the satellite must exactly match the sidereal rotation period of the Earth, which is T = 86164 s.
  • The satellite must move in the same direction as the Earth's rotation (prograde motion).
  • The angular velocity of the satellite in its orbit must equal the angular velocity of the Earth about its spin axis, ω = 7.292 × 10<sup>-5</sup> rad/s.

Equatorial Plane Requirement

  • The orbit must lie strictly in the plane of the Earth's equator (inclination i = 0<deg>).
  • If the orbit is inclined, the satellite will trace a figure-eight path in the sky daily, failing to remain stationary over a single geographic location.
  • Equatorial placement ensures that the line of sight from the ground station to the satellite maintains a fixed azimuth and elevation.

Orbital Radius and Altitude

  • The orbital radius r is calculated from the center of the Earth, approximately equal to 42,164 km.
  • The height h above the Earth's surface is approximately 35,786 km, accounting for the mean equatorial radius of the Earth (R_e ≈ 6378 km).
  • This high altitude allows a single satellite to cover roughly 40% of the Earth's surface for continuous telecommunication.

Perturbations and Station-Keeping

  • Non-uniform gravitational fields of the Earth (J_2 term), gravitational pulls from the Sun and Moon, and solar radiation pressure perturb the orbit.
  • Station-keeping thrusters must be fired periodically to counteract longitudinal and latitudinal drift caused by these perturbations.
  • Without station-keeping, inclination drifts by about 0.85<deg> per year.

Derivations

Derivation of Geostationary Orbital Radius

Conditions: Circular, equatorial, synchronous orbit around a spherically symmetric Earth.

  • Equate the gravitational force providing the necessary centripetal force: \frac{GMm}{r^2} = m\omega^2 r
  • Express angular velocity in terms of the time period T: \omega = \frac{2\pi}{T}
  • Substitute \omega into the force equation: \frac{GM}{r^2} = \left(\frac{2\pi}{T}\right)^2 r
  • Rearrange to solve for the orbital radius r: r^3 = \frac{GMT^2}{4\pi^2}

Result: r = \left(\frac{GMT^2}{4\pi^2}\right)^{1/3}

Derivation of Orbital Velocity for Geostationary Orbit

Conditions: Circular equatorial orbit at radius r.

  • Start with the balance of gravitational and centripetal acceleration: \frac{v^2}{r} = \frac{GM}{r^2}
  • Express orbital velocity v in terms of gravitational constant G, Earth mass M, and radius r: v = \sqrt{\frac{GM}{r}}
  • Alternatively, relate v to the angular velocity \omega and radius r: v = \omega r
  • Substitute numerical values for \omega and r to find the linear speed.

Result: v = \omega r = \sqrt{\frac{GM}{r}}

  • Time period T = 24 \times 3600 = 86400 s.
  • Using formula r^3 = \frac{GMT^2}{4\pi^2}, substitute the values: r^3 = \frac{6.67 \times 10^{-11} \times 6 \times 10^{24} \times (86400)^2}{4 \times (3.1416)^2}.

  • Height h = r - R_e = 42200 km - 6400 km = 35800 km.

PGT extension

Find the orbital velocity and the binding energy of a geostationary satellite of mass 500 kg. Take orbital radius r = 4.2 \times 10^7 m, M = 6 \times 10^{24} kg, and G = 6.67 \times 10^{-11} N m^2 kg^{-2}.

  • Orbital velocity v = \sqrt{\frac{GM}{r}} = \sqrt{\frac{6.67 \times 10^{-11} \times 6 \times 10^{24}}{4.2 \times 10^7}}.
  • v = \sqrt{\frac{4.002 \times 10^{14}}{4.2 \times 10^7}} = \sqrt{9.528 \times 10^6} \approx 3086 m/s.
  • Binding energy E_b = \frac{GMm}{2r} = \frac{6.67 \times 10^{-11} \times 6 \times 10^{24} \times 500}{2 \times 4.2 \times 10^7}.
  • E_b = \frac{2.001 \times 10^{17}}{8.4 \times 10^7} \approx 2.38 \times 10^9 J.

Answer: v = 3086 m/s, Binding Energy = 2.38 \times 10^9 J

PGT extension

What is the minimum extra velocity (impulse delta-v) that must be imparted to a satellite in a circular low earth orbit (radius R_e) to transfer it to a geostationary transfer orbit whose apogee is at the geostationary radius r_g = 7 R_e?

  • Low Earth Orbit (LEO) velocity v_1 = \sqrt{\frac{GM}{R_e}}.
  • Semi-major axis of transfer ellipse a = \frac{R_e + r_g}{2} = \frac{R_e + 7R_e}{2} = 4R_e.
  • Velocity at perigee of transfer ellipse v_p = \sqrt{GM \left(\frac{2}{R_e} - \frac{1}{a}\right)} = \sqrt{GM \left(\frac{2}{R_e} - \frac{1}{4R_e}\right)} = \sqrt{\frac{7GM}{4R_e}} = \sqrt{\frac{7}{4}} v_1.
  • Delta-v = v_p - v_1 = v_1 \left(\sqrt{\frac{7}{4}} - 1\right) = v_1 (1.3229 - 1) = 0.3229 v_1.

Answer: 0.3229 \sqrt{\frac{GM}{R_e}}

Assumptions and limitations

  • The Earth is treated as a uniform sphere with spherically symmetric mass distribution, neglecting equatorial bulge (J_2 perturbation) in basic derivations.
  • Gravitational pull from third bodies (Sun and Moon) is neglected in introductory models but accounted for in long-term orbital station-keeping.

Common misconceptions

Common error: A geostationary satellite can be placed directly above any point on the Earth, such as the poles.

Correct: Geostationary satellites can only remain stationary above points on the Earth's equator.

The gravitational central force must act toward the center of the Earth, constraining the orbit to lie in a plane passing through the Earth's center (the equatorial plane).

Common error: Geostationary and geosynchronous satellites are identical terms.

Correct: All geostationary satellites are geosynchronous, but not all geosynchronous satellites are geostationary.

A geosynchronous satellite has a 24-hour period but may have an inclined or elliptical orbit, whereas a geostationary satellite must be in a circular, zero-inclination equatorial orbit.

Common error: A geostationary satellite experiences zero gravity because it is weightless.

Correct: The satellite experiences normal Earth gravity at its altitude, which acts as the required centripetal force for its orbit.

Weightlessness in orbit is a consequence of free fall, not the absence of gravity.

Quick revision

  • Geostationary orbit is circular, equatorial, prograde, with T = 24 hours and altitude h ≈ 35,786 km.
  • Kepler's Third Law for Satellite: r^3 = \frac{GMT^2}{4\pi^2}
  • Geostationary Radius Expression: r = \left(\frac{GMT^2}{4\pi^2}\right)^{1/3}

Must remember

  • The time period of a geostationary satellite is equal to the sidereal rotation period of the Earth, exactly $T = 86164\text{ s}$.
  • A geostationary orbit must lie strictly in the equatorial plane with an inclination $i = 0^\circ$ and prograde motion.
  • The orbital radius from the center of the Earth is approximately $r \approx 42164\text{ km}$, and the height above the Earth's surface is $h \approx 35786\text{ km}$.
  • All geostationary satellites are geosynchronous, but not all geosynchronous satellites are geostationary due to orbital inclination or eccentricity.
  • Perturbations from non-uniform gravitational fields ($J_2$ term), Sun, Moon, and solar radiation pressure require periodic station-keeping thruster firings.

Glossary

Geostationary SatelliteA satellite in a circular equatorial orbit with a period of $24$ hours ($86164\text{ s}$) that remains stationary relative to a specific point on the Earth's surface.
Sidereal DayThe time taken by the Earth to complete one rotation relative to distant stars, equal to $86164\text{ s}$.
Inclination ($i$)The angle between the orbital plane of the satellite and the Earth's equatorial plane; for geostationary orbits, $i = 0^\circ$.
Station-KeepingThe process of firing onboard thrusters to counteract orbital perturbations and maintain the satellite's designated position.
Binding Energy ($U_b$)The total energy required to remove the satellite from its bound orbit to infinity, given by $U_b = \frac{GMm}{2r}$.

Practice Quiz

Choose an option to reveal the explanation.

1. What is the exact time period $T$ required for a geostationary satellite to match the Earth's rotation?

Explanation: The Earth completes one rotation relative to the stars (sidereal day) in $86164\text{ s}$, which is the precise time period required for a geostationary satellite.

2. What is the orbital inclination requirement for a geostationary satellite?

Explanation: For a satellite to remain stationary over a single point on the equator, its orbit must lie strictly in the equatorial plane, meaning the inclination $i = 0^\circ$.

3. What is the approximate height $h$ of a geostationary satellite above the Earth's surface?

Explanation: The orbital radius from the Earth's center is about $42164\text{ km}$. Subtracting the mean equatorial radius of the Earth ($\approx 6378\text{ km}$), the height above the surface is approximately $35786\text{ km}$.

4. Which of the following statements correctly distinguishes geostationary and geosynchronous satellites?

Explanation: A geosynchronous satellite has a 24-hour period but may be inclined or eccentric, whereas a geostationary satellite must be circular and equatorial ($0^\circ$ inclination).

5. What happens if a geostationary satellite's orbit is inclined at a non-zero angle?

Explanation: An inclined orbit causes the satellite to oscillate north and south of the equator, tracing a figure-eight (analemma) pattern in the sky daily.

6. Using Kepler's Third Law $r^3 = \frac{GMT^2}{4\$\pi$^2}$, what physical parameter primarily determines the orbital radius of a geostationary satellite?

Explanation: The orbital radius depends on the universal gravitational constant $G$, the mass of the Earth $M$, and the time period $T$. The mass of the satellite $m$ cancels out.

7. What is the expression for the total mechanical energy $E$ of a geostationary satellite of mass $m$ in an orbit of radius $r$?

Explanation: The total mechanical energy of a satellite in a circular bound orbit is the sum of kinetic and potential energy, resulting in $E = -\frac{GMm}{2r}$.

8. Why are station-keeping thrusters periodically fired on a geostationary satellite?

Explanation: Non-uniform gravitational fields ($J_2$ term), solar radiation pressure, and third-body pulls from the Sun and Moon cause orbital drift, requiring corrective thruster firings.

9. What is the approximate rate of inclination drift per year for an unmaintained geostationary satellite?

Explanation: Due to gravitational pulls from the Sun and Moon, an unmaintained geostationary satellite experiences an inclination drift of about $0.85^\circ$ per year.

10. What is the binding energy $U_b$ required to remove a geostationary satellite of mass $m$ to infinity?

Explanation: Binding energy is the negative of the total mechanical energy, given by $U_b = -E = \frac{GMm}{2r}$.

11. What fraction of the Earth's surface can be continuously covered by a single geostationary satellite?

Explanation: Due to its high altitude ($35786\text{ km}$), a single geostationary satellite has a line-of-sight coverage of roughly $40\%$ of the Earth's surface.

12. Which direction must a geostationary satellite move relative to the Earth's rotation?

Explanation: To maintain synchronicity with the rotating Earth, the satellite must move in the same direction as the Earth's rotation (prograde motion).

13. What force provides the necessary centripetal force for a geostationary satellite?

Explanation: The gravitational attraction between the Earth and the satellite acts toward the Earth's center, providing the necessary centripetal force for its circular orbit.

14. What is the angular velocity $\$\omega$$ of a geostationary satellite about the Earth's spin axis?

Explanation: The angular velocity matches the Earth's rotation rate, $\$\omega$ = \frac{2\$\pi$}{86164} \approx 7.292 \times 10^{-5}\text{ rad/s}$.

15. Which misconception about geostationary satellites is scientifically incorrect?

Explanation: Satellites in orbit experience normal gravity which supplies the centripetal acceleration; 'weightlessness' is a state of free fall, not the absence of gravity.

PYQ Patterns

These are pattern-based practice questions. Authentic year labels are only used when verified from a source.

1. The orbital radius of a geostationary satellite is approximately:

Explanation: The orbital radius measured from the center of the Earth is approximately $42164\text{ km}$. ($35786\text{ km}$ is the altitude above the surface).

2. If the mass of the Earth is $M$ and radius is $$R_{e}$$, the height $h$ of a geostationary satellite of time period $T$ is given by:

Explanation: From Kepler's law, $r = \left(\frac{GMT^2}{4\$\pi$^2}\right)^{1/3}$, and height $h = r - $R_{e}$ = \left(\frac{GMT^2}{4\$\pi$^2}\right)^{1/3} - $R_{e}$$.

3. What is the ratio of potential energy to kinetic energy for a satellite in a circular geostationary orbit?

Explanation: Kinetic energy $K = \frac{GMm}{2r}$ and Potential energy $U = -\frac{GMm}{r}$, so $\frac{U}{K} = -2$, meaning $U : K = -2 : 1$.

4. A geostationary satellite can be placed directly above:

Explanation: Central gravitational forces constrain geostationary orbits to lie strictly in the equatorial plane, allowing stationary positioning only above the equator.

5. The orbital speed of a geostationary satellite orbiting at radius $r$ is expressed as:

Explanation: Equating gravitational force to centripetal force: $\frac{GMm}{r^2} = \frac{mv^2}{r}$, which gives $v = \sqrt{\frac{GM}{r}}$.

6. Which of the following perturbations is primarily responsible for the inclination drift of a geostationary satellite?

Explanation: Gravitational influences from the Sun and Moon pull the orbital plane away from the equatorial plane, causing an inclination drift of about $0.85^\circ$ per year.

Timed Topic Test

Attempt these questions under a ten-minute limit using a separate timer.

1. What is the angular velocity of a geostationary satellite?

Explanation: Calculated as $\$\omega$ = \frac{2\$\pi$}{86164} \approx 7.292 \times 10^{-5}\text{ rad/s}$.

2. Why must the orbit of a geostationary satellite be circular?

Explanation: An elliptical orbit has varying orbital speed (Kepler's second law), which would cause the satellite to lead or lag the Earth's rotation, failing to stay stationary.

3. If a geostationary satellite has a mass $m$, what is its gravitational acceleration $g'$ at the orbital altitude?

Explanation: Gravitational acceleration at distance $r$ from the center of the Earth is given by $g' = \frac{GM}{r^2}$.

4. What is the primary operational advantage of a geostationary satellite for telecommunications?

Explanation: Because it maintains a fixed azimuth and elevation relative to ground stations, directional antennas do not need tracking mechanisms.

5. Which of the following parameters does NOT affect the orbital radius of a geostationary satellite?

Explanation: From Kepler's Third Law, the mass of the satellite $m$ cancels out of the equation for orbital radius $r$.

6. The $J_2$ term in the Earth's gravitational potential represents:

Explanation: The $J_2$ zonal harmonic coefficient accounts for the Earth's oblateness (equatorial bulge), which perturbs satellite orbits.

7. What is the total mechanical energy of a satellite of mass $m$ in terms of its binding energy $U_b$?

Explanation: Binding energy is defined as $U_b = -E$, hence $E = -U_b$.

8. What happens to a geostationary satellite if station-keeping is completely neglected over several years?

Explanation: Neglecting station-keeping leads to longitudinal drift and inclination drift (about $0.85^\circ$/year), so it no longer remains stationary over a fixed point.

9. How many geostationary satellites spaced evenly can theoretically provide near-global telecommunication coverage (excluding polar caps)?

Explanation: Three geostationary satellites spaced $120^\circ$ apart along the equator can cover almost the entire Earth's surface, excluding extreme polar regions.

10. Which expression represents the orbital speed $v$ in terms of the Earth's radius $$R_{e}$$ and surface gravity $g$?

Explanation: Since $GM = g $R_{e}^{2}$$, substituting into $v = \sqrt{\frac{GM}{r}}$ gives $v = \sqrt{\frac{g $R_{e}^{2}$}{r}}$.
