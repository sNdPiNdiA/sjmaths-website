Stokes Theorem and Applications

Stokes' Theorem relates the surface integral of the curl of a vector field over an open surface to the line integral of the vector field around the boundary curve of that surface. It forms a fundamental bridge between differential vector calculus and integral theorems in classical mechanics and electromagnetism.

Scope and prerequisites

TGT core

Evaluation of simple line integrals and curl calculations in Cartesian coordinates.

PGT extension

Application of Stokes' theorem to verify conservative force fields, central force potentials, and hydrodynamic circulations.

Prerequisites

  • Vector differentiation and concept of gradient, divergence, and curl
  • Line integrals and surface integrals of vector fields
  • Parametrization of curves and surfaces

Statement of Stokes' Theorem

  • Let S be a piecewise smooth, oriented open surface bounded by a simple, closed, piecewise smooth boundary curve C.
  • If vector field F has continuous first-order partial derivatives in a domain containing S, then the line integral of F along C equals the surface integral of the curl of F over S.
  • The orientation of curve C and surface S must follow the right-hand screw rule.

Physical Interpretation of Curl

  • The curl of a vector field represents the infinitesimal rotation or vorticity of the field at a point.
  • If velocity field v of a fluid has non-zero curl, the fluid flow exhibits local rotation.
  • In mechanics, if a force field F satisfies curl F = 0 everywhere, the field is irrotational and conservative.

Path Independence and Conservative Fields

  • For a conservative force field (such as a central gravitational force), the line integral around any closed loop is identically zero.
  • By Stokes' theorem, this implies that curl F = 0 for a conservative force field.
  • Potential energy V can be defined such that $F = -\nabla V$.

Geometrical Conditions and Boundary Orientation

  • The surface S must be two-sided with a defined unit normal vector n^.
  • The direction of integration along the boundary curve C is chosen such that if you walk along C with your head pointing in the direction of n^, the surface S is always to your left.
  • Discontinuities in the vector field or its derivatives across the surface invalidate the theorem.

Derivations

Proof of Stokes' Theorem for Infinitesimal Rectangular Loop

Conditions: Vector field F with continuous partial derivatives in a region containing a differential rectangular element in the xy-plane.

  • Consider a small rectangle in the xy-plane with sides dx and dy, centered at (x, y).
  • Calculate the line integral of $F = F_x \hat{i} + F_y \hat{j} counterclockwise around the four sides of the rectangle$.
  • Along the bottom side: F_x dx; along the right side: F_y dy + (\partial F_y / \partial x) dx dy; along the top side: -[F_x + (\partial F_x / \partial y) dy] dx; along the left side: -F_y dy.
  • Summing these contributions yields (\partial F_y / \partial x - \partial F_x / \partial y) dx dy.
  • Recognize the term in parentheses as the z-component of curl F, and dx dy as the differential surface area ds_z.

Result: \oint_C F \cdot d$r = \int_S (\nabla \times F) \cdot \hat{n} ds$

Equivalency of Line Integral to Circulation in Fluid Dynamics

Conditions: Velocity field v of a fluid moving in a steady-state regime across an arbitrary bounded surface S.

  • Define circulation \Gamma as the line integral of fluid velocity v around a closed material loop C.
  • Apply Stokes' theorem to convert the closed line integral of velocity into an open surface integral.
  • Substitute the curl of velocity, which equals twice the local angular velocity vector \omega (vorticity vector), into the surface integral.
  • Express circulation as the flux of vorticity through the surface bounded by the loop.

Result: $\Gamma = \oint_C v \cdot dr = \int_S (\nabla \times v) \cdot \hat{n} ds = 2 \int_S \omega \cdot \hat{n} ds$

Solved numericals

TGT core

Evaluate the line integral \oint_C (y i^ + x j^) \cdot dr where C is the circle x^2 + $y^2 = 1 in the xy-plane$, oriented counterclockwise.

  • Identify the vector field $F = y \hat{i} + x \hat{j}$ and curve C.
  • Method 1 (Direct integration): Parametrize circle as x = \cos \theta, y = \sin \theta, d$x = -\sin \theta d\theta$, dy = \cos \theta d\theta for \theta from 0 to 2\pi.
  • Substitute into line integral: \int_0^{2\pi} [(\sin \theta)(-\sin \theta) + (\cos \theta)(\cos \theta)] d$\theta = \int_0^{2\pi} (\cos^2 \theta - \sin^2 \theta) d\theta = \int_0^{2\pi} \cos(2\theta) d\theta = 0$.
  • Method 2 (Stokes' theorem): Calculate curl $F = (\partial / \partial x(x) - \partial / \partial y(y)) \hat{k} = (1 - 1) \hat{k} = 0$.
  • Since curl F = 0, the surface integral over S is zero.

Answer: 0

PGT extension

A force field is given by $F = (2x + y^2) \hat{i} + (3y - 4x) \hat{j}$. Find the work done in moving a particle once counterclockwise around the triangle with vertices (0,0), (1,0), and (0,1) using Stokes' theorem.

  • Use Stokes' theorem: $W = \oint_C F \cdot dr = \iint_S (\nabla \times F) \cdot \hat{k} dx dy$.
  • Calculate curl $F_z = \frac{\partial F_y}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial F_x}{\partial y} = \frac{\partial}{\partial x}(3y - 4x) - \frac{\partial}{\partial y}(2x + y^2) = -4 - 2y$.

  • Integrate: \int_0^1 \int_0^{1-x} (-4 - 2y) dy dx.
  • Inner integral: \left[ -4y - y^2 \right]_0^{1-x} = -4(1-x) - (1-x)^2 = -4 + 4x - (1 - 2x + x^2) = -5 + 6x - x^2$.

Assumptions and limitations

  • The vector field must possess continuous first-order partial derivatives throughout the region containing the surface and its boundary.
  • The surface S must be orientable; non-orientable surfaces such as a Möbius strip cannot be analyzed with standard Stokes' theorem.
  • The boundary curve C must be a simple closed curve without self-intersections.

Common misconceptions

Common error: Stokes' theorem requires the surface to be flat (planar).

Correct: Stokes' theorem applies to any smooth or piecewise smooth open 3D surface, provided it shares the same boundary curve C.

The surface integral evaluates the curl flux through any arbitrary open surface spanning the boundary.

Common error: The orientation of the curve C can be chosen arbitrarily without matching the surface normal.

Correct: The direction of curve integration and surface normal must strictly follow the right-hand screw rule.

Failing to respect orientation results in a sign error in the calculated integral.

Common error: If curl F = 0 at a single point, the line integral around any closed loop is zero.

Correct: The curl must be zero everywhere in a simply connected domain for the line integral around any closed loop to vanish.

Zero curl at an isolated point does not guarantee irrotationality across the entire enclosed surface.

Quick revision

  • Stokes' theorem connects an open surface integral of curl F to a closed line integral of F.
  • Formula: \oint_C F \cdot d$r = \int_S (\nabla \times F) \cdot \hat{n} ds$.
  • If \nabla \times F = 0, the force field is conservative and irrotational.
  • Right-hand rule governs the relationship between the boundary curve direction and surface normal.

Must remember

  • Stokes' theorem relates a surface integral of the curl of a vector field over an open surface $S$ to the line integral of the vector field around its boundary curve $C$: $\oint_C F \cdot dr = \int_S (\nabla \times F) \cdot \hat{n} ds$.
  • The orientation of the boundary curve $C$ and the surface normal $n^ must satisfy the right-hand screw rule.
  • A force field is conservative if and only if its curl is identically zero everywhere: $\nabla \times F = 0$.
  • The curl of a vector field represents the infinitesimal rotation or vorticity of the field at a given point.
  • For any conservative field, the line integral around a closed loop is identically zero, making it path-independent.

Practice Quiz

Choose an option to reveal the explanation.

1. What does Stokes' theorem directly relate?

Explanation: Stokes' theorem transforms the surface integral of the curl of a vector field over an open surface $S$ into a line integral around its boundary curve $C$: $\oint_C F \cdot dr = \int_S (\nabla \times F) \cdot \hat{n} ds$.

2. What is the condition for a vector field $F$ to be conservative in a simply connected domain?

Explanation: A vector field is conservative if and only if its curl is identically zero everywhere in the domain, i.e., $\nabla \times F = 0$.

3. Which rule relates the direction of the boundary curve $C$ to the unit normal vector $\hat{n} of surface $S$?

Explanation: The right-hand screw rule dictates that if you curl the fingers of your right hand in the direction of traversal along $C$, your thumb points in the direction of $n^.

4. If the curl of a fluid velocity field $v$ is zero everywhere, the fluid flow is called:

Explanation: When $\nabla \times v = 0$, the fluid flow exhibits no local rotation and is termed irrotational.

5. What is the Cartesian component formula for the $z$-component of $\nabla \times F$?

Explanation: In Cartesian coordinates, the $z$-component of the curl is given by $(\nabla \times F)_z = \frac{\partial F_y}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial F_x}{\partial y}$.

6. Which of the following must be true for Stokes' theorem to apply to a surface $S$ bounded by $C$?

Explanation: Stokes' theorem requires $S$ to be an open, oriented, piecewise smooth surface and $F$ to possess continuous first-order partial derivatives in a domain containing $S$.

7. For a conservative force field $F$, what is the line integral $\oint_C F \cdot dr$ around any closed path $C$?

Explanation: By Stokes' theorem, since $\nabla \times F = 0$ for conservative fields, the line integral around any closed loop is identically zero.

8. What physical quantity does the line integral $\Gamma = \oint_C v \cdot dr$ represent in fluid dynamics?

Explanation: The line integral of the velocity vector around a closed contour $C$ defines the circulation $\Gamma$ of the fluid.

9. If a scalar potential function $V$ exists such that $F = -\nabla V$, what can be said about $\nabla \times F$?

Explanation: The curl of any gradient of a scalar field is identically zero ($\nabla \times (\nabla V) = 0$), so $\nabla \times F = 0$.

10. What is the unit of the curl of a vector field $F$ whose components have units of Newtons per Coulomb (N/C) and coordinates in meters?

Explanation: Curl involves spatial derivatives ($\partial / \partial x$, etc.), dividing the units of $F$ by meters. Thus, the unit is $\text{N} / (\text{C} \cdot \text{m})$.

11. Which surface property invalidates Stokes' theorem if it occurs across $S$?

Explanation: Discontinuities in the vector field or its derivatives across the surface or its interior violate the differentiability requirements of Stokes' theorem.

12. What is evaluated by the surface integral $\int_S (\nabla \times F) \cdot \hat{n} ds$?

Explanation: The integral computes the total flux of the curl of $F$ through the open surface $S$.

13. If surface $S$ lies entirely in the $xy$-plane, which component of $\nabla \times F$ contributes to the $z$-normal Stokes' theorem form?

Explanation: When the surface lies in the $xy$-plane, the unit normal is $\hat{k}, meaning only the $z$-component of the curl contributes: $(\nabla \times F) \cdot k^ = \frac{\partial F_y}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial F_x}{\partial y}$.

14. Can two different open surfaces $S_1$ and $S_2$ sharing the same boundary curve $C$ give the same value when integrating $\int_S (\nabla \times F) \cdot \hat{n} ds$?

Explanation: Since both surface integrals evaluate to the same boundary line integral $\oint_C F \cdot dr$ via Stokes' theorem, any two surfaces bounded by the same $C$ yield identical results.

15. What is the value of $\oint_C (r \times dr)$ for a closed curve $C$ enclosing the origin in the plane?

Explanation: Using Stokes' theorem with $F = r \times a$ or via specific vector identities, $\oint_C (r \times dr) = 2 \int_S \hat{n} ds$, which equals twice the vector area enclosed by $C$.

PYQ Patterns

These are pattern-based practice questions. Authentic year labels are only used when verified from a source.

1. If a vector field is given by $F = y \hat{i} + x \hat{j} + z \hat{k}, what is its curl $\nabla \times F$?

Explanation: Here $F_x = y$, $F_y = x$, $F_z = z$. The curl components are: $(\frac{\partial z}{\partial y} - \frac{\partial x}{\partial z})\hat{i} + (\frac{\partial y}{\partial z} - \frac{\partial z}{\partial x})\hat{j} + (\frac{\partial x}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial y}{\partial y})\hat{k} = (0-0)\hat{i} + (0-0)\hat{j} + (1-1)\hat{k} = 0$.

2. Evaluate the line integral $\oint_C (x^2 dy)$ where $C$ is the unit circle in the $xy$-plane traversed counterclockwise.

Explanation: Using Stokes' theorem in the plane: $\oint_C (F_x dx + F_y dy) = \iint_S (\frac{\partial F_y}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial F_x}{\partial y}) dx dy$. Here $F_x = 0$, $F_y = x^2$. The integrand is $\frac{\partial}{\partial x}(x^2) - 0 = 2x$. Integrating $2x$ over the unit disk $x^2 + y^2 \le 1$ using polar coordinates gives $\int_0^{2\pi} \int_0^1 (2r \cos\theta) r dr d\theta = \pi$.

3. Which of the following force fields is conservative?

Explanation: For $F = y \hat{i} + x \hat{j}, $\nabla \times F = (\frac{\partial x}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial y}{\partial y})k^ = (1-1)k^ = 0$. Thus, it is conservative.

4. What is the physical interpretation of a non-zero curl in a fluid velocity field?

Explanation: A non-zero curl ($\nabla \times v \neq 0$) indicates that fluid elements rotate about their axes, representing vorticity.

5. If $F = \nabla \phi$, where $\phi$ is a scalar function with continuous second partial derivatives, what is $\oint_C F \cdot dr$ around any closed curve $C$?

Explanation: Since $F = \nabla \phi$ is a conservative field, its curl is zero, and by Stokes' theorem or the gradient theorem, the line integral around any closed loop is zero.

6. In applying Stokes' theorem to a hemispherical surface bounded by a circle in the $xy$-plane, what boundary curve orientation is chosen for an upward normal $n^ = k^?

Explanation: By the right-hand screw rule, if the thumb points upward ($+z$), the fingers curl in the counterclockwise direction when viewed from the positive $z$-axis.

Timed Topic Test

Attempt these questions under a ten-minute limit using a separate timer.

1. What does Stokes' theorem equate?

Explanation: Stokes' theorem equates the surface integral of the curl of a vector field over an open surface to the line integral around its boundary.

2. If $\nabla \times F = 0$ throughout a simply connected region, the vector field is:

Explanation: A vector field with zero curl in a simply connected domain is irrotational and conservative.

3. What does the right-hand rule determine in Stokes' theorem?

Explanation: The right-hand rule ensures correct relative orientation between the curve traversal and the surface unit normal vector.

4. In Cartesian coordinates, the $x$-component of $\nabla \times F$ is given by:

Explanation: The $x$-component of the curl operator is $(\frac{\partial F_z}{\partial y} - \frac{\partial F_y}{\partial z})$.

5. The circulation $\Gamma$ of a fluid velocity field $v$ around a closed contour $C$ is expressed as:

Explanation: Fluid circulation is defined mathematically as the line integral of velocity around a closed contour: $\Gamma = \oint_C v \cdot dr$.

6. Why is a central gravitational force field conservative?

Explanation: Central force fields depend only on distance and can be derived from a scalar potential ($F = -\nabla V$), making their curl identically zero.

7. What happens to Stokes' theorem if the vector field $F$ has a discontinuity across the surface $S$?

Explanation: Continuity of first-order partial derivatives across the surface is a fundamental prerequisite for Stokes' theorem.

8. If $\int_S (\nabla \times F) \cdot \hat{n} ds = 0$ for every open surface $S$, what must be true of $F$?

Explanation: If the curl flux is zero for any arbitrary surface, the curl of the field itself must be identically zero throughout the domain.

9. Which mathematical theorem is Stokes' theorem a generalization of in higher dimensions?

Explanation: Stokes' theorem in three dimensions is the direct vector generalization of Green's theorem in the plane.

10. What are the dimensions (units) of the curl of a force field measured in Newtons (N) with spatial coordinates in meters (m)?

Explanation: Since curl involves spatial derivatives (division by meters), the units are Newtons per meter (N/m).
