Volume Integrals in Mechanics

Volume integrals mathematically accumulate scalar or vector fields over a bounded three-dimensional region in space. In mechanics, they are foundational for evaluating continuous mass distributions, gravitational potentials, and inertial properties.

UP TGT ScienceUP PGT PhysicsStudy Notes + Practice

Scope and prerequisites

TGT core

Evaluation of simple volume integrals in Cartesian coordinates for mass, volume, and uniform density bodies.

PGT extension

Transformation of volume integrals to cylindrical and spherical polar coordinates for calculating moments of inertia, center of mass, and gravitational self-potential for non-uniform bodies.

Prerequisites

  • Vector calculus and triple integration techniques
  • Coordinate transformations (Cartesian to cylindrical and spherical)
  • Definition of density, mass, and center of mass

Definition and Physical Meaning

  • A volume integral integrates a continuous scalar function f(x, y, z) over a defined region V in three-dimensional space.
  • Represented mathematically as triply nested integrals with the differential volume element dV = dx dy dz in Cartesian coordinates.
  • Physically, when the integrand represents mass density rho(x, y, z), the volume integral yields the total mass of the continuous body.

Differential Volume Elements in Curvilinear Coordinates

  • In cylindrical coordinates (r, theta, z), the volume element is given by dV = r dr dtheta dz, arising from the Jacobian determinant of the coordinate transformation.
  • In spherical polar coordinates (r, theta, phi), the volume element is d$V = r^{2} \sin(\theta) dr dtheta dphi$, where theta is the polar angle and phi is the azimuth.
  • Choosing the appropriate coordinate system exploits the spatial symmetry of mechanical systems, simplifying calculations for central forces and rotational dynamics.

Application to Center of Mass

  • The position vector of the center of mass R_cm of a continuous body is defined via the volume integral of position weighted by density: $R_cm = (1/M) \int r \rho(r) dV$.
  • Total mass M is evaluated using the zeroth-order volume integral of the density function over the entire volume V.
  • Symmetry arguments simplify these integrals; if a body possesses a plane of symmetry, the center of mass lies directly on that plane.

Application to Moment of Inertia and Gravitational Potential

  • Moments of inertia for continuous rigid bodies are calculated by integrating the square of the perpendicular distance from an axis multiplied by density over the volume.
  • Gravitational potential V(r) at an external point due to a continuous mass distribution is evaluated by integrating over the source volume elements dV.
  • These integrals directly link macroscopic inertial properties to microscopic mass distributions, crucial for analyzing angular momentum and central force motion.

Derivations

Mass of a Solid Sphere of Non-Uniform Density

Conditions: Sphere of radius R with density varying radially as rho(r) = rho_0 (1 - r/R)

  • Write the volume integral for mass in spherical polar coordinates: $M = \int_V \rho(r) dV$.
  • Substitute the volume element d$V = r^{2} \sin(\theta) dr dtheta dphi$ and the given density function.
  • Integrate over the angular variables: int_0^{2pi} dphi = 2pi and int_0^{pi} sin(theta) dtheta = 2.
  • Evaluate the radial integral: $M = 2pi * 2 * \int_0^R \rho_0 (1 - r/R) r^{2} dr$.
  • Perform the integration: $M = 4pi \rho_0 [ R^3/3 - R^3/4 ] = (\pi \rho_0 R^3)/3$.

Result: $M = \frac{\pi \rho_0 R^3}{3}$

Center of Mass of a Hemispherical Shell of Uniform Density

Conditions: Solid hemisphere of uniform density rho and radius R

  • Use cylindrical or spherical coordinates due to axial symmetry; by symmetry, $X_cm = Y_cm = 0$.
  • Set up the integral for the Z-coordinate of the center of mass: $Z_cm = (1/M) \int_V z \rho dV$.
  • In spherical coordinates, z = r cos(theta), and d$V = r^{2} \sin(\theta) dr dtheta dphi$.
  • Integrate over V where r ranges from 0 to R, theta from 0 to pi/2, and phi from 0 to 2pi.
  • Numerator yields rho * 2pi * int_0^R r^3 dr * int_0^{pi/2} cos(theta)sin(theta) dthet$a = \rho * 2pi * (R^4/4) * (1/2) = (\pi \rho R^4)/4$.
  • Since $M = (2/3)\pi R^3 \rho$, divide the numerator by M.

Result: $Z_{cm} = \frac{3}{8} R$

Solved numericals

TGT core

Find the volume of a rectangular box bounded by planes x = 0, x = a, y = 0, y = b, and z = 0, z = c using a volume integral.

  • Set up the volume integral: $V = \int_0^c \int_0^b \int_0^a dx dy dz$.
  • Integrate with respect to x: int_0^a dx = a.
  • Integrate with respect to y: int_0^b dy = b.
  • Integrate with respect to z: int_0^c dz = c.

Answer: abc m^3

PGT extension

Determine the total mass of a solid sphere of radius R where the density varies with the distance r from the center according to rho(r) = rho_0 r.

  • Write the mass volume integral in spherical coordinates: $M = \int_0^{2pi} dphi \int_0^{\pi} \sin(\theta) dtheta \int_0^R (\rho_0 r) r^2 dr$.
  • Evaluate the angular integrals: int_0^{2pi} dphi int_0^{pi} sin(theta) dtheta = 4pi.
  • Evaluate the radial integral: int_0^R rho_0 r^3 d$r = \rho_0 R^4 / 4$.
  • Multiply the results: $M = 4pi * (\rho_0 R^4 / 4) = \pi \rho_0 R^4$.

Answer: \pi \rho_0 R^4

PGT extension

Calculate the moment of inertia about the Z-axis of a uniform cylinder of radius R, height h, and total mass M.

  • Use cylindrical coordinates where density is rh$o = M / (\pi R^2 h)$.
  • Set up the moment of inertia integral: $I_z = \int_0^h dz \int_0^{2pi} dtheta \int_0^R (r^2) \rho r dr$.
  • Perform the radial integration: int_0^R r^3 d$r = R^4 / 4$.
  • Perform the angular and height integrations: int_0^{2pi} dtheta = 2pi and int_0^h dz = h.
  • Combine terms: $I_z = (M / (\pi R^2 h)) * (2pi) * h * (R^4 / 4) = (1/2) M R^2$.

Answer: \frac{1}{2} M R^2

Assumptions and limitations

  • The density function rho(x, y, z) must be continuous or piecewise continuous over the domain of integration.
  • The volume V must be bounded and measurable in R^3 for the Riemann volume integral to converge.
  • Relativistic mass variation is neglected; classical Newtonian mechanics and Euclidean geometry are assumed.

Common misconceptions

Common error: The volume element in spherical coordinates is simply dr dtheta dphi.

Correct: The correct volume element is d$V = r^2 \sin(\theta) dr dtheta dphi$.

Failing to include the Jacobian determinant r^2 sin(theta) ignores the expansion of spherical volume shells with radius.

Common error: The center of mass always coincides with the geometric center of any object.

Correct: The center of mass coincides with the geometric center only if the body is homogenous (uniform density) and possesses spatial symmetry.

Non-uniform density creates a position-dependent weighting in the volume integral, shifting the center of mass.

Quick revision

  • Volume integrals accumulate scalar fields over 3D space using dV.
  • Cartesian dV = dx dy dz, Cylindrical dV = r dr dtheta dz, Spherical d$V = r^2 \sin(\theta) dr dtheta dphi$.
  • Total mass is the volume integral of density: M = int rho dV.
  • Center of mass vector uses the first moment of mass divided by total mass.

Must remember

  • The differential volume element in Cartesian coordinates is $dV = dx dy dz$.
  • In cylindrical coordinates, the volume element includes the scale factor: $dV = r dr d\theta dz$.
  • In spherical polar coordinates, the volume element is $dV = r^2 \sin(\theta) dr d\theta d\phi$.
  • The total mass of a continuous body is given by the zeroth-order volume integral: $M = \iiint_V \rho(x, y, z) dV$.
  • The position vector of the center of mass is $R_{cm} = \frac{1}{M} \iiint_V \mathbf{r} \rho(\mathbf{r}) dV$.
  • The moment of inertia about the Z-axis is calculated using $I_z = \iiint_V (x^2 + y^2) \rho(x, y, z) dV$.
  • Symmetry arguments can significantly simplify volume integrals; if a body possesses a plane of symmetry, the center of mass lies directly on that plane.
  • Non-uniform density creates a position-dependent weighting in the volume integral, shifting the center of mass away from the geometric center.

Practice Quiz

Choose an option to reveal the explanation.

1. What is the differential volume element $dV$ in Cartesian coordinates?

Explanation: In Cartesian coordinates, the volume element is represented as the product of infinitesimal linear displacements: $dV = dx dy dz$.

2. Which of the following correctly represents the differential volume element in cylindrical coordinates $(r, \theta, z)$?

Explanation: In cylindrical coordinates, the Jacobian determinant introduces an extra factor of $r$, yielding $dV = r dr d\theta dz$.

3. What is the correct expression for the differential volume element in spherical polar coordinates $(r, \theta, \phi)$?

Explanation: The spherical polar coordinate volume element is $dV = r^2 \sin(\theta) dr d\theta d\phi$, which accounts for the expansion of spherical volume shells.

4. When evaluating a volume integral where the integrand represents mass density $\rho(x, y, z)$, what physical quantity is obtained?

Explanation: Integrating the mass density $\rho(x, y, z)$ over the defined volume region $V$ yields the total mass $M = \iiint_V \rho(x, y, z) dV$.

5. How is the position vector of the center of mass $R_{cm}$ defined for a continuous mass distribution?

Explanation: The center of mass position vector is defined by the volume integral of the position vector weighted by density, divided by total mass: $R_{cm} = \frac{1}{M} \iiint_V \mathbf{r} \rho(\mathbf{r}) dV$.

6. Which coordinate system is most appropriate for evaluating volume integrals of a body with spherical symmetry?

Explanation: Spherical polar coordinates exploit spherical symmetry, simplifying the limits of integration and evaluation of central force problems.

7. What is the formula for the moment of inertia $I_z$ of a continuous rigid body rotating about the fixed Z-axis?

Explanation: The perpendicular distance squared from the Z-axis is $x^2 + y^2$, making the moment of inertia $I_z = \iiint_V (x^2 + y^2) \rho(x, y, z) dV$.

8. Under what condition does the center of mass of a body coincide with its geometric center?

Explanation: The center of mass coincides with the geometric center only if the body has uniform (homogeneous) density and spatial symmetry.

9. What does the zeroth-order volume integral $\iiint_V dV$ evaluate to?

Explanation: Integrating the differential volume element $dV$ over the region $V$ simply calculates the total geometric volume of the region.

10. In spherical polar coordinates, what does the polar angle $\theta$ typically represent?

Explanation: By convention in physics, $\theta$ is the polar angle measured from the positive z-axis, while $\phi$ is the azimuthal angle.

11. What is the units of the gravitational potential $V_g(\mathbf{r})$ evaluated via volume integration?

Explanation: Gravitational potential is potential energy per unit mass, having units of $\text{J kg}^{-1}$ or $\text{m}^2 \text{s}^{-2}$.

12. Why are curvilinear coordinate systems used in mechanical volume integrals?

Explanation: Curvilinear coordinates (cylindrical and spherical) are chosen to match the natural symmetry of systems like cylinders or spheres, simplifying calculations.

13. If a continuous body possesses a plane of symmetry, where does its center of mass lie?

Explanation: Symmetry arguments dictate that if a body has a plane of symmetry, the contributions from mass elements on either side cancel out perpendicular to the plane, placing the CM on the plane.

14. Which of the following functions must be satisfied for a general volume integral $I = \iiint_V f(x, y, z) dx dy dz$ to be valid?

Explanation: The fundamental mathematical requirement for any volume integral is that the scalar field $f(x, y, z)$ is integrable over the bounded domain $V$.

15. What mathematical operation generates the volume element $dV = r dr d\theta dz$ from Cartesian coordinates?

Explanation: The differential volume element in curvilinear coordinates is derived by taking the absolute value of the Jacobian determinant of the coordinate transformation.

PYQ Patterns

These are pattern-based practice questions. Authentic year labels are only used when verified from a source.

1. What is the volume of a sphere of radius $R$ obtained by evaluating the volume integral $\iiint_V dV$ in spherical coordinates?

Explanation: Evaluating $\int_{0}^{R} \int_{0}^{\pi} \int_{0}^{2\pi} r^2 \sin(\theta) d\phi d\theta dr$ gives the standard volume of a sphere: $\frac{4}{3}\pi R^3$.

2. In calculating the moment of inertia of a uniform solid cylinder of radius $R$ and height $H$ about its longitudinal axis (Z-axis), which coordinate system and volume element are most suitable?

Explanation: A solid cylinder possesses cylindrical symmetry, making cylindrical coordinates ($r, \theta, z$) with $dV = r dr d\theta dz$ the ideal choice.

3. A continuous body has a non-uniform mass density $\rho(x, y, z)$. Which integral expression gives its total mass $M$?

Explanation: The total mass of a continuous body is found by the zeroth-order volume integral of the density function over the entire volume region: $M = \iiint_V \rho(x, y, z) dx dy dz$.

4. What is the Jacobian determinant $J$ used when transforming from Cartesian coordinates $(x, y, z)$ to spherical polar coordinates $(r, \theta, \phi)$?

Explanation: The transformation Jacobian determinant magnitude is $r^2 \sin(\theta)$, which directly forms the differential volume element $dV = r^2 \sin(\theta) dr d\theta d\phi$.

5. When computing the gravitational potential at an external point due to a continuous mass distribution, what term appears in the denominator of the volume integrand?

Explanation: The gravitational potential formula is $V_g(\mathbf{r}) = -G \iiint_V \frac{\rho(\mathbf{r}')}{|\mathbf{r} - \mathbf{r}'|} dV'$, where $|\mathbf{r} - \mathbf{r}'|$ is the distance from the source element to the field point.

6. If a homogeneous solid hemisphere of radius $R$ is placed with its flat base on the xy-plane, where is its center of mass located along the z-axis?

Explanation: Using spherical volume integrals for a hemisphere, the center of mass coordinate along the axis of symmetry is located at $z = \frac{3R}{8}$.

Timed Topic Test

Attempt these questions under a ten-minute limit using a separate timer.

1. Which integral correctly represents the volume of a region $V$ in 3D Cartesian space?

Explanation: The fundamental volume formula in Cartesian coordinates is given by the triple integral of the differential volume element: $V = \iiint_V dx dy dz$.

2. In cylindrical coordinates, what limits of integration are typically used for the azimuthal angle $\theta$ for a full revolution?

Explanation: A complete rotation around the z-axis in cylindrical or spherical coordinates spans an azimuthal angle $\theta$ (or $\phi$) from $0$ to $2\pi$.

3. What is the primary physical reason for transforming integrals from Cartesian to spherical polar coordinates in mechanics?

Explanation: Spherical polar coordinates exploit spatial central symmetry, greatly simplifying the integration boundaries for radial potentials and forces.

4. If the density of a body is given by $\rho(r) = \rho_0 r$ in spherical coordinates, how does the mass integrand appear when evaluated using $dV$?

Explanation: Multiplying density $\rho(r) = \rho_0 r$ by the spherical volume element $dV = r^2 \sin(\theta) dr d\theta d\phi$ yields $\rho_0 r^3 \sin(\theta) dr d\theta d\phi$.

5. Which of the following describes the calculation of the moment of inertia about the X-axis for a continuous mass distribution?

Explanation: The perpendicular distance squared from the X-axis is $y^2 + z^2$, making the moment of inertia $I_x = \iiint_V (y^2 + z^2) \rho(x, y, z) dV$.

6. What error occurs if the Jacobian factor $r^2 \sin(\theta)$ is omitted when evaluating a spherical volume integral?

Explanation: Omitting the Jacobian determinant $r^2 \sin(\theta)$ fails to account for the spatial expansion of spherical shells, leading to an incorrect result.

7. For a uniform body, how does the volume integral for the center of mass simplify?

Explanation: When density is uniform, $\rho$ is constant and cancels out with $M = \rho V$, simplifying the CM expression to the geometric centroid of the region.

8. What are the standard limits for the polar angle $\theta$ in spherical polar coordinates when integrating over a complete sphere?

Explanation: The polar angle $\theta$ in spherical coordinates is measured from the positive z-axis, ranging from $0$ (North Pole) to $\pi$ (South Pole).

9. Which mechanical property links macroscopic rotational inertia to microscopic mass distribution via volume integration?

Explanation: Moments of inertia integrate squared perpendicular distances multiplied by density over volume, linking macroscopic inertia to microscopic mass distribution.

10. In evaluating the volume integral for total mass of a cylinder using cylindrical coordinates, what are the typical integration variables and their differentials?

Explanation: Cylindrical symmetry uses radial distance $r$, azimuth $\theta$, and height $z$, with the differential volume element $dV = r dr d\theta dz$.
