Variation of Length with Velocity (Length Contraction)

Length contraction, also known as Lorentz-FitzGerald contraction, is the phenomenon where the measured length of an object is shorter when it is moving relative to the observer than when it is at rest. It is a fundamental consequence of the constancy of the speed of light in all inertial frames of reference as established by Einstein's special theory of relativity.

Prerequisites

  • Inertial frames of reference
  • Postulates of Special Theory of Relativity
  • Lorentz coordinate transformations

Proper Length vs. Relativistic Length

  • Proper length (L0) is the length of an object measured in the frame of reference in which the object is at rest.
  • Relativistic or contracted length (L) is the length measured by an observer moving at a constant relative velocity v with respect to the object.
  • Length contraction occurs strictly along the direction of relative motion; dimensions perpendicular to the motion remain unaffected.

Physical Interpretation of Lorentz Factor

  • The contraction is governed by the Lorentz factor gamma where $\gamma = (1 - v^{2}/c^{2})^{-1/2}$.
  • As the relative velocity v approaches the speed of light c, the contracted length approaches zero.
  • Length contraction is a reciprocal phenomenon; each observer in uniform relative motion perceives the other's measuring rods as contracted.

Simultaneity and Measurement

  • Measuring the length of a moving object requires determining the positions of both ends simultaneously in the observer's frame.
  • Events that are simultaneous in the object's rest frame are generally not simultaneous in the moving observer's frame.
  • This failure of simultaneity is the physical origin of why length contraction occurs.

Anisotropy of Length Contraction

  • If a rod makes an angle with the direction of motion, only its component parallel to the velocity vector undergoes contraction.
  • The transverse component of length remains invariant, i.e., $y = y_{0}$ and $z = z_{0}$.
  • Consequently, the orientation and shape of moving objects appear distorted to a stationary observer.

Derivations

Derivation of Length Contraction using Lorentz Transformations

Conditions: Rod is at rest in frame S' moving with velocity v along the x-axis relative to frame S.

  • Let the proper length of the rod in S' be L0 = x'_2 - x'_1, where x'_1 and x'_2 are the coordinates of the ends of the rod in S'.
  • Using the Lorentz coordinate transformation, relate the coordinates in S to S': x' = γ(x - vt).
  • An observer in frame S measures the positions of the two ends of the rod simultaneously at a single instant of time t (so $t_1 = t_2 = t)$.
  • Therefore, the length measured in S is $L = x_2 - x_1$.
  • Substitute the transformation equations: x'_$1 = \gamma(x_1 - vt)$ and x'_$2 = \gamma(x_2 - vt)$.
  • Subtract the two equations to get L0 = x'_2 - x'_$1 = \gamma(x_2 - x_1) = \gammaL$.
  • Rearrange to express the contracted length in terms of proper length: $L = L_0 / \gamma$.

Result: $L = L_0 \sqrt{1 - \frac{v^2}{c^2}}$

Transverse Length Invariance

Conditions: Rod or object oriented perpendicular to the direction of motion.

  • Consider an object of length y0 placed along the y-axis in frame S', moving along the x-axis relative to S.
  • The Lorentz transformation for the y-coordinate is simply y = y'.
  • Since there is no relative motion along the y-axis or z-axis, measuring instruments oriented transversely experience no spatial compression.
  • Therefore, y0 measured in S' equals y measured in S.

Result: $y = y_0 \text{}$ and } $z = z_0$

Solved numericals

TGT core

A rod has a proper length of 2 m. At what speed must it move so that its length appears to be 1 m to a stationary observer?

  • Identify given values: Proper length $L_0 = 2 m$, Contracted length L = 1 m.
  • Use the length contraction formula: $L = L_0 * \sqrt(1 - v^2/c^2)$.
  • Substitute values: $1 = 2 * \sqrt(1 - v^2/c^2)$.
  • Simplify: 1/$2 = \sqrt(1 - v^2/c^2)$.
  • Square both sides: 1/$4 = 1 - v^2/c^2$.
  • Rearrange for v^2/c^2: $v^2/c^2 = 1 - 1/4 = 3/4$.
  • Solve for v: $v = (\sqrt(3)/2) * c$.

Answer: 0.866 c (or \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2} c)

PGT extension

A spaceship of rest length 100 m passes an observer at a speed of 0.6c. What is the length of the spaceship as measured by the observer, and what is the time taken by the spaceship to pass a stationary point for that observer?

  • Identify proper length $L_0 = 100 m$ and velocity v = 0.6c.
  • Calculate the contracted length: $L = L_0 * \sqrt(1 - v^2/c^2) = 100 * \sqrt(1 - (0.6)^2) = 100 * \sqrt(1 - 0.36) = 100 * \sqrt(0.64) = 100 * 0.8 = 80 m$.
  • Calculate the time taken to pass a stationary point: $t = L / v$.
  • Substitute L = 80 m and $v = 0.6 * 3 * 10^8 m/s = 1.8 * 10^8 m/s$.
  • $t = 80 / (1.8 * 10^8) = 4.44 * 10^-7 s$.

Answer: Length = 80 m, Tim$e = 4.44 × 10^{-7} s$

PGT extension

A square plate of side 2 m is moving with a velocity of 0.8c along one of its edges parallel to the x-axis. Find the area of the plate as measured by a stationary observer.

  • Identify proper dimensions: $L_x0 = 2 m$, $L_y0 = 2 m$. Proper area $A_0 = L_x0 * L_y0 = 4 m^2$.
  • Motion is along the x-axis, so only the dimension parallel to x contracts: $L_x = L_x0 * \sqrt(1 - v^2/c^2)$.
  • Calculate contraction factor: sqrt(1 - (0.8)^2) = sqrt(1 - 0.64) = sqrt(0.36) = 0.6.
  • Contracted length along x: $L_x = 2 * 0.6 = 1.2 m$.
  • Transverse length remains unchanged: $L_y = L_y0 = 2 m$.
  • Calculate measured area: $A = L_x * L_y = 1.2 * 2 = 2.4 m^2$.

Answer: 2.4 m^2

Assumptions and limitations

  • Assumes uniform relative motion between two inertial frames of reference in empty space.
  • Valid only for speeds comparable to the speed of light (v approaching c); breaks down in classical mechanics limits where v << c.

Common misconceptions

Common error: Length contraction is an optical illusion caused by the time light takes to travel from the object to the eye.

Correct: Length contraction is a real physical kinematic effect of spacetime geometry, independent of visual perception.

It arises directly from the Lorentz transformation of spatial coordinates under simultaneous measurement protocols.

Common error: An object physically shrinks or experiences internal mechanical stress when it moves.

Correct: No internal stress is created simply by moving at constant velocity in an inertial frame.

Motion is relative; in the object's own rest frame, its dimensions are completely normal.

Common error: Contraction occurs in all directions around a moving object.

Correct: Contraction is strictly unidirectional, occurring exclusively along the axis of relative motion.

Components perpendicular to the velocity vector remain identical in all inertial frames.

Quick revision

  • Length contraction states that moving objects appear shortened along the direction of motion: $L = L_0 / \gamma$.
  • Length Contraction Formula: $L = L_0 \sqrt{1 - \frac{v^2}{c^2}}$
  • Lorentz Factor: $\gamma = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 - \frac{v^2}{c^2}}}$

Must remember

  • Proper length ($L_0$) is measured in the rest frame of the object, while relativistic length ($L$) is measured by a moving observer.
  • Length contraction occurs exclusively along the direction of relative motion, governed by the formula $L = L_0 \sqrt{1 - v^2/c^2}$.
  • Transverse dimensions perpendicular to the velocity vector remain invariant ($y = y_0$, $z = z_0$).
  • Length contraction is a reciprocal phenomenon; each inertial observer perceives the other's measuring rods as contracted.
  • Measuring moving length requires simultaneous determination of the positions of both ends in the observer's frame.

Practice Quiz

Choose an option to reveal the explanation.

1. What is defined as the proper length $L_0$ of an object?

Explanation: Proper length ($L_0$) is specifically defined as the length of an object measured in its own rest frame, representing its maximum possible measured length.

2. Which mathematical expression correctly represents the Lorentz factor $\gamma$?

Explanation: The Lorentz factor is defined as $\gamma = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 - v^2/c^2}} = (1 - v^2/c^2)^{-1/2}$.

3. How does length contraction affect dimensions perpendicular to the direction of relative motion?

Explanation: Length contraction occurs strictly along the axis of relative motion; transverse dimensions (such as $y$ and $z$) remain invariant ($y = y_0$).਼

4. If a rod of proper length $L_0$ moves at a velocity $v = 0.6c$ along its length, what is its contracted length $L$?

Explanation: Using $L = L_0 \sqrt{1 - v^2/c^2} = L_0 \sqrt{1 - (0.6c)^2/c^2} = L_0 \sqrt{1 - 0.36} = L_0 \sqrt{0.64} = 0.8 L_0$.

5. What is the physical origin of length contraction according to special relativity?

Explanation: Length contraction arises because events that are simultaneous in one frame are not simultaneous in another, making simultaneous endpoint measurement yield a shorter length.

6. What happens to the contracted length of an object as its velocity $v$ approaches the speed of light $c$?

Explanation: As $v \to c$, the term $\sqrt{1 - v^2/c^2} \to 0$, causing the contracted length $L$ to approach zero.

7. Is length contraction a reciprocal phenomenon between two inertial observers?

Explanation: Due to the principle of relativity, length contraction is symmetric and reciprocal; each observer considers themselves at rest and sees the other's objects contracted.

8. A square plate of proper area $A_0$ moves with velocity $v$ parallel to one of its edges. What is its moving area $A$?

Explanation: The dimension parallel to motion contracts by $\sqrt{1 - v^2/c^2}$, while the transverse dimension remains $L_{y0}$. Thus, $A = (L_0 \sqrt{1 - v^2/c^2}) L_{y0} = A_0 \sqrt{1 - v^2/c^2}$.

9. If the measured length of a moving rod is half its proper length ($L = L_0 / 2$), what is its velocity $v$?

Explanation: Using $v = c \sqrt{1 - (L/L_0)^2} = c \sqrt{1 - (1/2)^2} = c \sqrt{1 - 1/4} = c \sqrt{3/4} = \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}c$.

10. Which of the following statements regarding internal stresses during length contraction is correct?

Explanation: Uniform inertial motion does not create internal mechanical stress; in the object's own rest frame, its dimensions and forces are completely normal.

11. How does an angled rod's orientation appear to a stationary observer when moving at relativistic speeds?

Explanation: Because only the component parallel to motion contracts while the transverse component remains invariant, the orientation and shape appear distorted.

12. What is the relation between the differential length interval $dx$ in frame $S$ and $dx'$ in frame $S'$ (moving at velocity $v$ along $x$) for simultaneous measurements?

Explanation: For simultaneous measurement in $S$, the proper length interval in $S'$ ($dx'$) is related to $dx$ by $dx = dx' / \gamma$ (or $dx' = \gamma dx$ depending on rest frame designation).

13. What is the condition required for the validity of the standard length contraction formula $L = L_0 \sqrt{1 - v^2/c^2}$?

Explanation: The formula requires $v < c$ and is strictly applicable along the line of relative motion.

14. If a rectangular volume $V_0$ moves parallel to one of its principal axes with velocity $v$, what is its relativistic volume $V$?

Explanation: Only the length dimension parallel to the velocity vector contracts by $\gamma^{-1}$, while the two transverse dimensions remain unchanged, leading to $V = V_0 \sqrt{1 - v^2/c^2}$.

15. Which common misconception about length contraction is corrected by spacetime geometry?

Explanation: Length contraction is a real physical kinematic effect of spacetime geometry, completely independent of optical illusions or light travel time.

PYQ Patterns

These are pattern-based practice questions. Authentic year labels are only used when verified from a source.

1. At what fraction of the speed of light $c$ must a rod travel so that its length is contracted to $60\%$ of its proper length?

Explanation: Given $L = 0.6 L_0$. Using $v = c \sqrt{1 - (L/L_0)^2} = c \sqrt{1 - (0.6)^2} = c \sqrt{1 - 0.36} = c \sqrt{0.64} = 0.8c$.

2. A meter stick moves past an observer at a speed of $0.8c$ parallel to its length. What length does the observer measure?

Explanation: $L_0 = 1.0\text{ m}$, $v = 0.8c$. $L = 1.0 \sqrt{1 - (0.8)^2} = 1.0 \sqrt{1 - 0.64} = 1.0 \sqrt{0.36} = 0.6\text{ m}$.

3. If the Lorentz factor for a moving spaceship is $\gamma = 5$, by what factor has its length contracted along the direction of motion?

Explanation: The contracted length is $L = L_0 / \gamma$. Thus, the length becomes $1/5 = 0.2$ times the proper length.

4. A circular disc of proper radius $R_0$ is moving with velocity $v$ along the $x$-axis. What is the shape of the disc as measured by a stationary observer?

Explanation: The dimension along the $x$-axis contracts to $R_0 \sqrt{1 - v^2/c^2}$, while the $y$-axis dimension remains $R_0$. This forms an ellipse.

5. Two observers $S$ and $S'$ are in relative motion. Observer $S$ measures the length of an object at rest in $S'$ to be $L$. What is the proper length of the object?

Explanation: The object is at rest in $S'$, so $L$ is the contracted length measured in $S$. The proper length $L_0$ in $S'$ is given by $L_0 = \gamma L = \frac{L}{\sqrt{1 - v^2/c^2}}$.

6. At what velocity will the length of an object contract to half of its proper length?

Explanation: Setting $L = L_0/2$, $v = c \sqrt{1 - (1/2)^2} = c \sqrt{3/4} = \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}c \approx 0.866c$.

Timed Topic Test

Attempt these questions under a ten-minute limit using a separate timer.

1. What is the primary reason why length contraction is not observed in everyday macroscopic experiences?

Explanation: In daily life, $v \ll c$, making the term $v^2/c^2$ practically zero, so $\gamma \approx 1$ and length contraction is undetectable.

2. A rod is oriented at an angle of $60^\circ$ to the direction of motion in its rest frame. As it moves with relativistic speed $v$, what happens to its transverse component?

Explanation: Transverse length components are perpendicular to the velocity vector and remain strictly invariant under Lorentz transformations.

3. If an object moves at $v = 0.99c$, approximately what is the value of the Lorentz factor $\gamma$?

Explanation: For $v = 0.99c$, $\gamma = 1 / \sqrt{1 - 0.99^2} = 1 / \sqrt{1 - 0.9801} = 1 / \sqrt{0.0199} \approx 7.087 \approx 7.1$.

4. Which of the following describes the transformation of volume $V_0$ of a cube moving along one of its edges at speed $v$?

Explanation: The dimension parallel to motion contracts by $\sqrt{1 - v^2/c^2}$, while the other two dimensions remain $L_0$. Thus, $V = L_0 \sqrt{1 - v^2/c^2} \times L_0 \times L_0 = V_0 \sqrt{1 - v^2/c^2}$.

5. In the context of length contraction, what does the measurement of both ends of a moving object require?

Explanation: Because a moving object changes position continuously, measuring its length requires recording the locations of both ends at the exact same instant (simultaneously) in the observer's frame.

6. A spaceship of proper length $100\text{ m}$ passes an observer at $0.6c$. What length does the observer measure?

Explanation: $L = 100 \sqrt{1 - (0.6)^2} = 100 \sqrt{0.64} = 80\text{ m}$.

7. Why is length contraction considered a kinematic effect rather than a dynamical force effect?

Explanation: Length contraction is a fundamental property of spacetime geometry and coordinate transformations between inertial frames, not the result of physical forces crushing the object.

8. If an observer measures a moving object to be half its proper length, what is the ratio $v/c$?

Explanation: $v/c = \sqrt{1 - (1/2)^2} = \sqrt{3/4} \approx 0.866$.

9. What happens to the measurement of time intervals when discussing the simultaneous measurement protocol of length contraction?

Explanation: The failure of simultaneity between frames is precisely why the moving observer must account for time differences when noting endpoint positions.

10. Which formula correctly relates proper length $L_0$, measured length $L$, and speed $v$ when solving for velocity?

Explanation: Rearranging $L = L_0 \sqrt{1 - v^2/c^2}$ yields $\frac{L}{L_0} = \sqrt{1 - v^2/c^2}$, leading to $v = c \sqrt{1 - (L/L_0)^2}$.
