Time Dilation in Special Relativity

Time dilation is the phenomenon where a clock moving relative to an observer is measured to tick slower than a clock at rest in the observer's own reference frame. It is a direct consequence of the constancy of the speed of light in all inertial frames.

Prerequisites

  • Lorentz coordinate transformations

Proper Time and Coordinate Time

  • Proper time (t_0) is the time interval measured by a clock that is at rest relative to the events being observed, occurring at the same spatial point in that frame.
  • Coordinate time (t) is the time interval measured by an observer in relative motion with respect to the clock.
  • The measured time interval in the moving frame is always greater than the proper time interval.

The Lorentz Factor

  • The magnitude of time dilation is governed by the Lorentz factor gamm$a = (1 - v^2/c^2)^(-1/2)$.
  • As the relative velocity v approaches the speed of light c, the Lorentz factor approaches infinity.
  • For speeds much less than c (v << c), gamma approaches 1, and classical Newtonian mechanics is recovered.

Experimental Verification: Muon Decay

  • Atmospheric muons produced by cosmic rays at high altitudes have a proper mean lifetime of about 2.2 microseconds.
  • Without time dilation, classical physics predicts they would decay before reaching Earth's surface.
  • Due to relativistic time dilation at high speeds (v approx 0.99c), their lifetime in the Earth frame is extended, allowing them to be detected at sea level.

Symmetry and Reciprocity

  • Time dilation is reciprocal between two inertial frames: observer A sees B's clock running slow, and observer B sees A's clock running slow.
  • The apparent paradoxes (such as the Twin Paradox) are resolved by noting that the traveling twin must undergo acceleration and switch inertial frames, breaking the symmetry.

Derivations

Derivation of Time Dilation using Light Clock

Conditions: Observer at rest relative to the clock versus an observer moving with constant velocity v.

  • Consider a light clock consisting of two parallel mirrors separated by a vertical distance L, with a photon bouncing between them.
  • In the rest frame of the clock, the time for a round trip is $t_0 = 2L/c$.
  • In a frame moving horizontally with velocity v, the photon travels a diagonal path of length 2s, where $s = \sqrt(L^2 + (v t / 2)^2)$.
  • Using the constancy of light speed, the total time t in the moving frame satisfies (ct/2)^2 = L^2 + (vt/2)^2$.
  • Substitute $L = c t_0 / 2 into the equation$ and solve for t.

Result: $t = t_0 / \sqrt(1 - v^2/c^2) = \gamma t_0$

Derivation using Lorentz Transformations

Conditions: Two inertial frames S and S' with S' moving at velocity v along the x-axis.

  • Write the inverse Lorentz time transformation: $t = \gamma (t' + v x' / c^2)$.
  • For a single clock kept at a fixed position in frame S', the spatial position does not change, so delta x' = 0.
  • The time interval measured in frame S is therefore delta t = gamma delta t'.
  • Identify delta t' as the proper time t_0 and delta t as the dilated time t.

Result: $t = \gamma t_0$

Graphs and experiments

Time Dilation vs Velocity Graph

Draw: A plot of time ratio t/t_0 (or gamma) on the y-axis versus velocity ratio v/c (from 0 to 1) on the x-axis. The curve starts at (0,1) and shoots asymptotically upward to infinity as v/c approaches 1.

Interpret: Shows that time dilation effects are negligible at classical speeds (v/c < 0.1) but diverge rapidly as an object approaches the speed of light.

Exam use: Frequently tested in MCQs to identify the correct qualitative curve or to calculate gamma at specific fractions of c (e.g., v = 0.6c or 0.8c).

Rossi-Hall Experiment

Draw: Schematic setup of particle detectors placed at different altitudes on a mountain to count the flux of cosmic-ray muons.

Interpret: Demonstrates that the flux of muons measured at lower altitude is significantly higher than what would be predicted without relativistic time dilation, confirming the stretching of their lifetime.

Exam use: Tested conceptually in PGT exams as experimental proof of special relativity.

Hafele-Keating Experiment

Draw: A conceptual flow diagram showing atomic clocks flown eastward and westward around the Earth compared to a reference clock at the US Naval Observatory.

Interpret: Combines special relativistic time dilation (due to velocity) and general relativistic time dilation (due to gravitational potential) to confirm predicted clock offsets.

Exam use: Used in advanced PGT questions integrating special and general relativity.

Solved numericals

TGT core

A space rocket moves with a velocity of 0.6c relative to the Earth. A clock on the rocket ticks every 1 second according to an astronaut on board. What is the time interval for one tick as measured by an observer on Earth?

  • Identify the given values: proper time $t_0 = 1 s$, velocity v = 0.6c.
  • Recall the time dilation formula: $t = t_0 / \sqrt(1 - v^2/c^2)$.
  • Calculate the velocity squared term: $v^2/c^2 = (0.6c)^2 / c^2 = 0.36$.
  • Evaluate the denominator: sqrt(1 - 0.36) = sqrt(0.64) = 0.8.
  • Calculate the dilated time: $t = 1 / 0.8 = 1.25 s$.

Answer: 1.25 s

PGT extension

At what fraction of the speed of light must a clock move so that its rate is slowed down by a factor of 2? (i.e., measured time interval is twice the proper time).

  • Set the condition: t / $t_0 = \gamma = 2$.
  • Write the definition of the Lorentz factor: 1 / sqrt(1 - $v^2/c^2) = 2$.
  • Square both sides: 1 / (1 - $v^2/c^2) = 4$.
  • Invert both sides: 1 - $v^2/c^2 = 1/4 = 0.25$.
  • Rearrange to solve for v^2/c^2: $v^2/c^2 = 1 - 0.25 = 0.75$.
  • Take the square root: v/$c = \sqrt(3)/2$.

Answer: 0.866 c (or sqrt(3)/2 c)

PGT extension

A particle has a proper lifetime of 2 x 10^{-8} s. If it travels through the laboratory at a speed of 0.8c, what distance does it travel in the laboratory frame before decaying?

  • Calculate the Lorentz factor for $v = 0.8c: \gamma = 1 / \sqrt(1 - (0.8)^2) = 1 / \sqrt(1 - 0.64) = 1 / 0.6 = 5/3$.
  • Calculate the dilated lifetime in the laboratory frame: $t = \gamma * t_0 = (5/3) * (2 x 10^{-8} s) = 10/3 x 10^{-8} s$.
  • Calculate the distance traveled in the lab frame: $d = v * t$.
  • Substitute $v = 0.8 * (3 x 10^8 m/s) = 2.4 x 10^8 m/s$.
  • $d = (2.4 x 10^8 m/s) * (10/3 x 10^{-8} s) = 0.8 x 10 / 1 = 8 meters$.

Answer: 8 m

Assumptions and limitations

  • Assumption: Relative motion between inertial frames is uniform and rectilinear. Limitation: Does not account for accelerating reference frames without invoking general relativity or instantaneous inertial frames.

Common misconceptions

Common error: Time actually passes slower for the moving astronaut, so they age slower biologically because their internal chemical processes physically change.

Correct: Time itself in that reference frame runs slower; all physical, biological, and chemical processes proceed at the standard rate when measured in their own proper time.

Time dilation is a property of spacetime geometry, not a mechanical disruption of clocks or biological systems.

Common error: Time dilation only applies to mechanical or light clocks.

Correct: Time dilation applies equally to all physical processes, including radioactive decay, chemical reactions, and atomic transitions.

All phenomena in a given inertial frame share the same coordinate time framework.

Quick revision

  • Moving clocks run slow: $t = \gamma t_0$ where gamma >= 1.
  • Time Dilation Relation: $t = \frac{t_0}{\sqrt{1 - \frac{v^2}{c^2}}}$
  • Lorentz Factor: gamm$a = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 - \frac{v^2}{c^2}}}$

Must remember

  • Proper time ($t_0$) is measured by a clock at rest relative to the events, representing the shortest time interval between two events in inertial frames.
  • The Lorentz factor $\gamma = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 - \frac{v^2}{c^2}}}$ governs the magnitude of relativistic time dilation.
  • Time dilation is completely reciprocal between two inertially moving observers, meaning each sees the other's clock running slow.
  • Atmospheric muon decay serves as direct experimental verification of time dilation, allowing high-speed cosmic ray muons to reach sea level.
  • The Twin Paradox is resolved because the traveling twin undergoes acceleration and changes inertial frames, breaking the initial symmetry.

Glossary

Proper Time ($t_0$)The time interval measured by a clock that is at rest relative to the events being observed, occurring at the same spatial point in that frame.
Coordinate Time ($t$)The time interval measured by an observer in relative motion with respect to the clock, always greater than the proper time interval.
Lorentz Factor ($\gamma$)The dimensionless scaling factor $\frac{1}{\sqrt{1 - v^2/c^2}}$ by which time, length, and relativistic mass change.
Twin ParadoxA thought experiment in special relativity involving identical twins, one of whom makes a journey into space in a high-speed rocket and returns to find the stay-at-home twin has aged more.

Practice Quiz

Choose an option to reveal the explanation.

1. What is defined as the time interval measured by a clock that is at rest relative to the events being observed?

Explanation: Proper time ($t_0$) is specifically the time interval measured by a clock at rest relative to the events, occurring at the same spatial point in that reference frame.

2. The Lorentz factor $\gamma$ is expressed as:

Explanation: The Lorentz factor is defined by the formula $\gamma = \left(1 - \frac{v^2}{c^2}\right)^{-\frac{1}{2}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 - \frac{v^2}{c^2}}}.

3. If a clock moves at a constant velocity $v$ relative to an observer, the relationship between coordinate time $t$ and proper time $t_0$ is:

Explanation: The time dilation formula states that $t = \gamma t_0$, meaning the measured time interval in the moving frame is greater than the proper time.

4. What happens to the Lorentz factor $\gamma$ as the relative velocity $v$ approaches the speed of light $c$?

Explanation: As $v \to c$, the denominator $\sqrt{1 - v^2/c^2}$ approaches 0, causing the Lorentz factor $\gamma$ to approach infinity.

5. For speeds much less than the speed of light ($v \ll c$), what does the Lorentz factor approximately equal?

Explanation: When $v \ll c$, $v^2/c^2 \approx 0$, making $\gamma \approx 1$, which recovers classical Newtonian mechanics.

6. Atmospheric muons provide experimental verification of time dilation. What is their proper mean lifetime?

Explanation: Atmospheric muons produced by cosmic rays have a proper mean lifetime of about $2.2 \text{ microseconds}$ ($2.2 \times 10^{-6} \text{ s}$).

7. Why are atmospheric muons able to reach the Earth's surface despite their short lifetime?

Explanation: Due to relativistic time dilation at high speeds ($v \approx 0.99c$), their lifetime as measured by Earth observers is extended by the Lorentz factor, allowing them to reach sea level.

8. Which of the following statements about the reciprocity of time dilation is correct?

Explanation: Time dilation is completely reciprocal between two inertial frames: observer A sees B's clock running slow, and observer B sees A's clock running slow.

9. How is the apparent asymmetry in the Twin Paradox resolved?

Explanation: The Twin Paradox is resolved because the traveling twin must accelerate and switch inertial frames, thereby breaking the symmetry between the two twins.

10. Using the binomial approximation for low speeds ($v \ll c$), the dilated time $t$ can be expressed as:

Explanation: Using binomial expansion on $\left(1 - \frac{v^2}{c^2}\right)^{-1/2}$, for $v \ll c$, we get $t \approx t_0 \left(1 + \frac{1}{2}\frac{v^2}{c^2}\right)$.

11. What is the velocity of a particle if its dilated time $t$ is twice its proper time $t_0$ ($t = 2t_0$)?

Explanation: Using $v = c\sqrt{1 - (t_0/t)^2}$, substituting $t = 2t_0$ gives $v = c\sqrt{1 - (1/2)^2} = c\sqrt{3/4} = \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}c$.

12. Which of the following physical processes is subject to time dilation?

Explanation: Time dilation is a property of spacetime geometry, affecting all physical processes equally, including atomic transitions, chemical reactions, and radioactive decay.

13. What is the proper time interval $\tau$ along a general worldline for a non-inertial or moving object with instantaneous velocity $v(t)$?

Explanation: The proper time along an arbitrary path in spacetime is given by the integral $\tau = \int \sqrt{1 - \frac{v(t)^2}{c^2}} dt$.

14. How does observed frequency $\nu$ relate to proper frequency $\nu_0$ in relativistic Doppler / time dilation relations?

Explanation: The observed frequency is given by $\nu = \nu_0 \sqrt{1 - \frac{v^2}{c^2}}$, reflecting the inverse relationship between time intervals and frequency.

15. What is the common misconception regarding biological aging during time dilation?

Explanation: Biological processes proceed normally when measured in their own proper time; the apparent slowing is a kinematic consequence of spacetime geometry, not a disruption of biology.

PYQ Patterns

These are pattern-based practice questions. Authentic year labels are only used when verified from a source.

1. At what speed must a clock move so that it runs at half the rate of a stationary clock?

Explanation: Running at half the rate means $t = 2t_0$. Using $t = \gamma t_0$, $\gamma = 2$, so $\frac{1}{\sqrt{1 - v^2/c^2}} = 2 \implies 1 - \frac{v^2}{c^2} = \frac{1}{4} \implies v = \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}c$.

2. If the proper lifetime of a particle is $10 \text{ ns}$, what is its lifetime when moving with a velocity of $0.8c$?

Explanation: Here $\gamma = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 - (0.8c)^2/c^2}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 - 0.64}} = \frac{1}{0.6} = \frac{5}{3}$. Thus $t = \gamma t_0 = \frac{5}{3} \times 10 \text{ ns} = 16.67 \text{ ns}$.

3. The percentage increase in time period of a clock moving with velocity $v \ll c$ compared to its rest period $t_0$ is:

Explanation: Using the binomial approximation $t \approx t_0 \left(1 + \frac{1}{2}\frac{v^2}{c^2}\right)$, the fractional change $\frac{t - t_0}{t_0} = \frac{1}{2}\frac{v^2}{c^2}$, which gives a percentage increase of $\frac{1}{2}\frac{v^2}{c^2} \times 100\%.$

4. A spaceship travels at $0.6c$. An observer on Earth measures a time interval of $20 \text{ hours}$ for an event on the spaceship. What is the proper time interval measured by the clock on the spaceship?

Explanation: Here $t = 20 \text{ hours}$ is the coordinate time. With $v = 0.6c$, $\gamma = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 - 0.36}} = \frac{1}{0.8} = 1.25$. Proper time $t_0 = \frac{t}{\gamma} = \frac{20}{1.25} = 16 \text{ hours}$? Wait: $20 / 1.25 = 16$. Let's check options: 12, 16, 25, 33.3. Correct is 16 hours. (Note: $\gamma = 1.25 = 5/4$, so $t_0 = 20 \times 4/5 = 16 \text{ hours}$).

5. What is the kinetic energy of a particle of rest mass $m_0$ moving at a velocity where its time dilation factor is $\gamma$?

Explanation: Total relativistic energy is $E = \gamma m_0 c^2$ and rest energy is $m_0 c^2$. Kinetic energy is $K = E - m_0 c^2 = (\gamma - 1)m_0 c^2$.

6. Two identical clocks are synchronized on Earth. One is placed on a supersonic jet moving at constant speed $v$, and the other remains on Earth. After a time $t$, the fractional time difference $\frac{\Delta t}{t}$ is proportional to:

Explanation: Since $t = \gamma t_0 \approx t_0 \left(1 + \frac{1}{2}\frac{v^2}{c^2}\right)$, the fractional difference $\frac{t - t_0}{t_0} \approx \frac{1}{2}\frac{v^2}{c^2}$, which is proportional to $\left(\frac{v}{c}\right)^2$.

Explanation: Proper time is measured by a clock present at both events (or at rest relative to them, occurring at the same spatial point in that frame).

Explanation: The spacetime interval $ds^2 = c^2 dt^2 - dx^2$ (or proper time invariant) is invariant under Lorentz transformations for all inertial observers.

Explanation: Since $\gamma = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 - v^2/c^2}} = 5$, we have $1 - v^2/c^2 = \frac{1}{25} = 0.04$, so $v^2/c^2 = 0.96$, and $v = \sqrt{0.96}c \approx 0.9798c \approx 0.98c$ (Wait, $\sqrt{0.96} \approx 0.9798c$, let's check correct option D).

5. Why is time dilation considered a symmetrical phenomenon between two inertial observers?

Explanation: By the principle of relativity, all inertial frames are equivalent; thus, each observer considers themselves stationary and sees the other's clock ticking more slowly.

6. If proper time $t_0 = 5 \text{ s}$ and dilated time $t = 10 \text{ s}$, what is the relative velocity $v$?

Explanation: Using $v = c\sqrt{1 - (t_0/t)^2} = c\sqrt{1 - (5/10)^2} = c\sqrt{1 - 1/4} = \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}c$.

7. What is the physical meaning of the worldline in relation to proper time?

Explanation: In Minkowski spacetime, proper time interval along a particle's worldline is proportional to the geometric arc length of that path.

8. Which experiment historically confirmed relativistic time dilation using fast-moving particles in accelerators?

Explanation: The Ives-Stilwell experiment (along with muon decay measurements) directly verified relativistic time dilation and the transverse Doppler effect.

9. If an observer measures time intervals to be 3 times longer than proper time ($\gamma = 3$), what is the kinetic energy of a particle of rest mass $m_0$?

Explanation: Kinetic energy $K = (\gamma - 1)m_0 c^2$. Since $\gamma = 3$, $K = (3 - 1)m_0 c^2 = 2 m_0 c^2$.

10. What primary conclusion can be drawn from the existence of time dilation in special relativity?

Explanation: Time dilation demonstrates that time is not absolute; time intervals depend dynamically on the relative motion between reference frames.
