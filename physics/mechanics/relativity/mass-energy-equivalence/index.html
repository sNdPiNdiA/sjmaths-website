Mass-Energy Equivalence Relation

Mass-energy equivalence establishes that mass and energy are manifestations of the same underlying physical entity, connected by the square of the speed of light in a vacuum. It forms a foundational pillar of special relativity and governs both nuclear transformations and relativistic dynamics.

Scope and prerequisites

TGT core

Understanding the formula E = mc&sup2; and its application in basic mass defect and binding energy calculations for atomic nuclei.

PGT extension

Derivation of the relativistic mass-energy relation using momentum and velocity transformations, total energy, kinetic energy, and the relativistic energy-momentum invariant relation.

Prerequisites

  • Conservation of linear momentum and energy in collisions
  • Lorentz transformation and relativistic mass variation
  • Work-energy theorem in classical mechanics

Inertial Mass and Rest Energy

  • Every object of rest mass m0 possesses an intrinsic rest energy given by E0 = m0c².
  • Mass is not conserved independently in nuclear and subatomic reactions; rather, the total relativistic energy is conserved.
  • The rest energy represents the internal energy content of a system when it is stationary in a given inertial frame.

Relativistic Total Energy and Kinetic Energy

  • The total relativistic energy of a particle moving with velocity v is E = gamma m0c², where gamma = (1 - v²/c²)-1/2 is the Lorentz factor.
  • Relativistic kinetic energy K is defined as the difference between total energy and rest energy: K = E - E0 = (gamma - 1)m0c².
  • In the limit v << c, the relativistic kinetic energy reduces to the classical expression (1/2)m0v² via binomial expansion.

Energy-Momentum Invariant Relation

  • The total energy E and relativistic momentum p of a particle satisfy the invariant relation E² = p²c² + (m0c²)².
  • For massless particles such as photons, m0 = 0, which leads directly to the relation E = pc.
  • The quantity E² - p²c² is invariant under Lorentz transformations for all observers in inertial frames.

Mass Defect and Nuclear Binding Energy

  • The mass of an atomic nucleus is always less than the sum of the masses of its constituent free nucleons (protons and neutrons).
  • The mass difference Delta m, known as mass defect, is equivalent to the total binding energy B of the nucleus released during formation: B = Delta mc².
  • Positive binding energy signifies a stable bound system, requiring external energy input to separate the nucleons.

Derivations

Derivation of Relativistic Kinetic Energy and Mass-Energy Equivalence

Conditions: A particle of rest mass m<sub>0</sub> accelerated by a force F along a straight line in an inertial frame.

  • Start with the work-energy theorem: Work done W = int F dx = Delta K, where F = dp/dt = d(mv)/dt.
  • Substitute force into the work integral: K = int v d(mv) = int v d(gamma m0 v).
  • Expand the differential using the product rule: d(gamma m0 v) = m0 v dgamma + gamma m0 dv.
  • Substitute gamma = (1 - v²/c²)-1/2 to find v dgamma = gamma^3 (v^3/c^2) dv and integrate to obtain K = (gamma - 1)m0c².
  • Define total energy E as K + m0c², yielding E = gamma m0c² = mc².

Result: E = mc² and K = (gamma - 1)m0c²

Derivation of the Energy-Momentum Invariant Relation

Conditions: Relativistic particle with rest mass m<sub>0</sub>, total energy E, and momentum p.

  • Write the expression for total energy: E = gamma m0c².
  • Write the expression for relativistic momentum: p = gamma m0v.
  • Square both quantities: E² = gamma^2 m_0^2 c^4 and p²c² = gamma^2 m_0^2 v^2 c^2.
  • Subtract p²c² from E²: E² - p²c² = gamma^2 m_0^2 c^4 (1 - v^2/c^2).
  • Use the definition gamma^2 = (1 - v²/c²)-1 to simplify the right-hand side to (m0c²)².

Result: E² = p²c² + (m0c²)²

Graphs and experiments

Kinetic Energy vs Velocity (Classical vs Relativistic)

Draw: A plot of kinetic energy on the y-axis against velocity v/c on the x-axis ranging from 0 to 1. Show two curves: a parabolic curve for classical kinetic energy (1/2)m_0v^2 and an asymptotic curve shooting to infinity at v = c for the relativistic kinetic energy.

Interpret: At low speeds (v << c), classical and relativistic kinetic energies coincide. As v approaches c, the relativistic kinetic energy diverges, demonstrating that infinite energy is required to accelerate a massive particle to the speed of light.

Exam use: Tested conceptually in multiple-choice questions regarding the failure of classical mechanics at high velocities.

Binding Energy Per Nucleon vs Mass Number Curve

Draw: A curve plotting B/A (Binding energy per nucleon) on the y-axis against mass number A on the x-axis, peaking around A = 50 to 60 (Iron group) with a value of ~8.8 MeV/nucleon, and sloping downwards for light and heavy nuclei.

Interpret: The peak at intermediate mass numbers explains why nuclear fusion of light nuclei and nuclear fission of heavy nuclei both result in an increase in binding energy per nucleon, releasing mass energy in the process.

Exam use: Frequently used in PGT and TGT questions to analyze stability, fission, and fusion reactions.

Cockcroft-Walton Proton-Lithium Particle Accelerator Experiment

Draw: Schematic diagram of a proton beam striking a lithium target resulting in two alpha particles, with a corresponding energy balance equation showing mass converted to kinetic energy.

Interpret: Direct experimental verification of E = mc&sup2; where the kinetic energy of the resulting alpha particles exceeds the kinetic energy of the incident proton by exactly the mass-energy equivalent of the mass defect of the nuclear reaction.

Exam use: Cited in descriptive questions or numerical problems regarding quantitative verification of mass-energy equivalence in nuclear reactions.

Solved numericals

TGT core

Calculate the energy released in joules when 1 microgram (1 mu g) of mass is completely converted into energy.

  • Identify given values: mass m = 1 mu g = 1 imes 10^{-9} kg, speed of light c = 3 imes 10^8 m/s.
  • Apply the mass-energy equivalence formula: E = mc^2.
  • Substitute the values: E = (1 \times 10^{-9} \text{ kg}) \times (3 \times 10^8 \text{ m/s})^2.
  • Calculate the final product: E = 1 \times 10^{-9} \times 9 \times 10^{16} = 9 \times 10^7 \text{ J}.

Answer: 9 \times 10^7 \text{ J}

PGT extension

Find the momentum of an electron whose kinetic energy is equal to its rest mass energy.

  • Given condition: Kinetic energy K = m_0 c^2.
  • Total energy E = K + m_0 c^2 = m_0 c^2 + m_0 c^2 = 2 m_0 c^2.
  • Use the energy-momentum relation: E^2 = p^2 c^2 + (m_0 c^2)^2.
  • Substitute E: (2 m_0 c^2)^2 = p^2 c^2 + (m_0 c^2)^2.
  • Expand and rearrange for momentum: 4 (m_0 c^2)^2 - (m_0 c^2)^2 = p^2 c^2 implies 3 (m_0 c^2)^2 = p^2 c^2.
  • Solve for p: p = \frac{\sqrt{3} m_0 c^2}{c} = \sqrt{3} m_0 c.

Answer: \sqrt{3} m_0 c

PGT extension

A particle of rest mass m_0 moves with a velocity v = 0.8c. Calculate its total relativistic energy and its relativistic momentum in terms of m_0 and c.

  • Calculate the Lorentz factor \gamma: \gamma = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 - v^2/c^2}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 - (0.8)^2}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 - 0.64}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{0.36}} = \frac{1}{0.6} = \frac{5}{3}.
  • Calculate total energy E = \gamma m_0 c^2 = \frac{5}{3} m_0 c^2.
  • Calculate relativistic momentum p = \gamma m_0 v = \frac{5}{3} m_0 (0.8 c) = \frac{5}{3} \cdot \frac{4}{5} m_0 c = \frac{4}{3} m_0 c.

Answer: E = \frac{5}{3} m_0 c^2 \text{ and } p = \frac{4}{3} m_0 c

Assumptions and limitations

  • The relation E = mc² applies strictly in inertial reference frames.
  • In non-inertial frames, pseudo-forces must be accounted for, modifying the simple form of relativistic energy equations.
  • The speed of light c is assumed to be an absolute universal constant in all inertial frames as per Einstein's second postulate.

Common misconceptions

Common error: Mass and energy are two completely different physical quantities that happen to be proportional.

Correct: Mass and energy are two different measures of the exact same physical property of a system.

E = mc&sup2; implies that energy has mass and mass has energy; they are interchangeable manifestations.

Common error: The formula E = mc&sup2; can be used for any object regardless of whether it is at rest or in motion.

Correct: E = mc&sup2; strictly represents rest energy when m is rest mass, or total energy when m is relativistic mass.

For a moving particle, the complete energy equation is E = \gamma m_0 c^2 or E^2 = p^2 c^2 + (m_0 c^2)^2.

Common error: A massive particle can reach the speed of light if given infinite kinetic energy.

Correct: A massive particle cannot reach or exceed the speed of light.

As velocity approaches c, the Lorentz factor and required kinetic energy approach infinity.

Quick revision

  • Rest energy is given by E_0 = m_0 c^2, and total relativistic energy is E = \gamma m_0 c^2.
  • Relativistic kinetic energy is K = ( \gamma - 1 ) m_0 c^2.
  • The fundamental energy-momentum invariant is E^2 - p^2 c^2 = (m_0 c^2)^2.
  • Mass defect in nuclei directly accounts for the nuclear binding energy via B = \Delta m c^2.

Must remember

  • Every object of rest mass $m_0$ possesses an intrinsic rest energy given by $E_0 = m_0 c^2$.
  • The total relativistic energy of a moving particle is given by $E = \gamma m_0 c^2$, where $\gamma = (1 - v^2/c^2)^{-1/2}$.
  • Relativistic kinetic energy is defined as $K = E - E_0 = (\gamma - 1)m_0 c^2$.
  • The energy-momentum invariant relation is $E^2 = p^2 c^2 + (m_0 c^2)^2$.
  • For massless particles like photons ($m_0 = 0$), the energy-momentum relation reduces to $E = pc$.
  • Mass defect $\Delta m$ is the difference between the mass of constituent free nucleons and the nuclear mass, related to binding energy by $B = \Delta m c^2$.
  • 1 amu of mass defect is energetically equivalent to approximately $931.5 \text{ MeV}$.

Glossary

Rest Mass ($m_0$)The invariant mass of a particle measured in its rest frame.
Lorentz Factor ($\gamma$)The factor by which time, length, and relativistic mass change for an object moving at speed $v$, given by $\gamma = (1 - v^2/c^2)^{-1/2}$.
Mass Defect ($\Delta m$)The difference between the total mass of the separate nucleons composing a nucleus and the rest mass of the nucleus itself.
Nuclear Binding Energy ($B$)The energy required to break a nucleus into its constituent free protons and neutrons, equal to $\Delta m c^2$.

Practice Quiz

Choose an option to reveal the explanation.

1. What is the intrinsic rest energy of an object of rest mass $m_0$?

Explanation: Every object of rest mass $m_0$ possesses an intrinsic rest energy given by $E_0 = m_0 c^2$ when stationary in a given inertial frame.

2. How is the relativistic kinetic energy $K$ defined in terms of total energy $E$ and rest energy $E_0$?

Explanation: Relativistic kinetic energy $K$ is defined as the difference between total relativistic energy and rest energy: $K = E - E_0 = (\$\gamma$ - 1)m_0 c^2$.

3. Which of the following represents the correct energy-momentum invariant relation for a particle of rest mass $m_0$?

Explanation: The total energy $E$ and relativistic momentum $p$ satisfy the invariant relation $E^2 = p^2 c^2 + (m_0 c^2)^2$.

4. For a massless particle such as a photon, what does the energy-momentum relation simplify to?

Explanation: For massless particles, $m_0 = 0$, which leads directly from $E^2 = p^2 c^2 + (m_0 c^2)^2$ to $E = pc$.

5. What is the mass defect $\Delta m$ of an atomic nucleus of mass $M_{nucleus}$ containing $Z$ protons (mass $m_p$) and $A-Z$ neutrons (mass $m_n$)?

Explanation: The mass of an atomic nucleus is always less than the sum of the masses of its constituent free nucleons. The mass defect is $\Delta m = [Z m_p + (A - Z) m_n] - M_{nucleus}$.

6. In standard nuclear energy calculations, 1 amu is energetically equivalent to approximately how much energy?

Explanation: 1 amu is equivalent to $931.5 \text{ MeV}$ when applying mass-energy conversion for nuclear calculations.

7. In the limit $v \ll c$, what does the relativistic kinetic energy expression reduce to?

Explanation: In the limit $v \ll c$, the relativistic kinetic energy reduces to the classical expression $\frac{1}{2}m_0 v^2$ via binomial expansion.

8. What is the physical significance of a positive nuclear binding energy?

Explanation: Positive binding energy signifies a stable bound system, requiring external energy input to separate the constituent nucleons.

9. What does the quantity $E^2 - p^2 c^2$ represent under Lorentz transformations?

Explanation: The quantity $E^2 - p^2 c^2 = (m_0 c^2)^2$ is invariant under Lorentz transformations for all observers in inertial frames.

10. Why can a massive particle not reach or exceed the speed of light?

Explanation: As velocity approaches $c$, $\$\gamma$ = (1 - v^2/c^2)^{-1/2}$ approaches infinity, meaning infinite energy would be required to accelerate a massive particle to $c$.

11. What is the expression for the Lorentz factor $\$\gamma$$?

Explanation: The Lorentz factor is defined as $\$\gamma$ = (1 - v^2/c^2)^{-1/2}$.

12. Is mass conserved independently in nuclear and subatomic reactions?

Explanation: Mass is not conserved independently in nuclear and subatomic reactions; rather, total relativistic energy (which includes rest mass energy) is conserved.

13. What does the formula $E = mc^2$ strictly represent when $m$ is the rest mass and the object is stationary?

Explanation: When $m$ is rest mass and the object is at rest, $E = m_0 c^2$ represents the intrinsic rest energy of the system.

14. How is nuclear binding energy $B$ calculated from mass defect $\Delta m$?

Explanation: The binding energy is equivalent to the mass defect multiplied by the square of the speed of light: $B = \Delta m \cdot c^2$.

15. What does the rest energy of a system represent?

Explanation: The rest energy represents the internal energy content of a system when it is stationary in a given inertial frame.

PYQ Patterns

These are pattern-based practice questions. Authentic year labels are only used when verified from a source.

1. If the rest mass of a particle is $m_0$ and it moves with a velocity $v = \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}c$, what is its total relativistic energy?

Explanation: For $v = \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}c$, the Lorentz factor is $\$\gamma$ = (1 - \frac{3}{4})^{-1/2} = (1/4)^{-1/2} = 2$. Thus, total energy $E = \$\gamma$ m_0 c^2 = 2 m_0 c^2$.

2. A nucleus has a mass defect of $0.03 \text{ amu}$. What is its approximate nuclear binding energy in $\text{MeV}$?

Explanation: Binding energy $B = \Delta m \times 931.5 \text{ MeV} = 0.03 \times 931.5 = 27.945 \text{ MeV} \approx 27.95 \text{ MeV}$.

3. What is the relativistic momentum of a particle of rest mass $m_0$ moving at speed $v = 0.6c$?

Explanation: Lorentz factor $\$\gamma$ = (1 - 0.6^2)^{-1/2} = (1 - 0.36)^{-1/2} = (0.64)^{-1/2} = \frac{1}{0.8} = 1.25$. Momentum $p = \$\gamma$ m_0 v = 1.25 \times m_0 \times (0.6c) = 0.75 m_0 c$.

4. If a particle's total energy is three times its rest energy ($E = 3E_0$), what is its speed in terms of $c$?

Explanation: $E = \$\gamma$ m_0 c^2 = 3 m_0 c^2 \implies \$\gamma$ = 3$. Since $\$\gamma$ = (1 - v^2/c^2)^{-1/2} = 3$, squaring gives $1 - v^2/c^2 = \frac{1}{9}$, so $v^2/c^2 = \frac{8}{9} \implies v = \frac{\sqrt{8}}{3}c$.

5. Which relation correctly connects total energy $E$, rest energy $E_0$, and relativistic kinetic energy $K$?

Explanation: By definition, kinetic energy is $K = E - E_0$, which rearranges to $E = K + E_0$.

6. If the binding energy per nucleon of a nucleus is $8 \text{ MeV}$ and it has a mass number $A = 50$, what is the total binding energy of the nucleus?

Explanation: Total binding energy = (Binding energy per nucleon) $\times A = 8 \text{ MeV} \times 50 = 400 \text{ MeV}$.

Timed Topic Test

Attempt these questions under a ten-minute limit using a separate timer.

1. What is the rest energy of an electron with rest mass $m_0 \approx 9.11 \times 10^{-31} \text{ kg}$ in Joules?

Explanation: $E_0 = m_0 c^2 = (9.11 \times 10^{-31} \text{ kg}) \times (3 \times 10^8 \text{ m/s})^2 = 8.199 \times 10^{-14} \text{ J} \approx 8.20 \times 10^{-14} \text{ J}$.

2. For a particle moving at $v = 0.8c$, what is the value of the Lorentz factor $\$\gamma$$?

Explanation: $\$\gamma$ = (1 - 0.8^2)^{-1/2} = (1 - 0.64)^{-1/2} = (0.36)^{-1/2} = \frac{1}{0.6} = 1.67$.

3. Which of the following statements about mass and energy is correct according to modern physics?

Explanation: $E = mc^2$ demonstrates that mass and energy are equivalent, meaning energy has mass and mass has energy, and they can be converted into one another.

4. What is the relativistic kinetic energy of a particle when its velocity $v$ is very small compared to $c$ ($v \ll c$)?

Explanation: Using binomial expansion on $(\$\gamma$ - 1)m_0 c^2$ for $v \ll c$, the relativistic kinetic energy reduces to the classical expression $\frac{1}{2}m_0 v^2$.

5. If a system absorbs energy $\Delta E$ while remaining at rest in an inertial frame, how does its rest mass change?

Explanation: Since $E_0 = m_0 c^2$, an increase in internal rest energy $\Delta E$ corresponds to an increase in rest mass by $\Delta m = \frac{\Delta E}{c^2}$.

6. What is the momentum of a photon of energy $E$?

Explanation: For a photon ($m_0 = 0$), $E = pc$, which means momentum $p = \frac{E}{c}$.

7. Why is the mass of a stable nucleus always less than the sum of the masses of its individual nucleons?

Explanation: When nucleons bind together to form a nucleus, binding energy is released. By mass-energy equivalence, this released energy corresponds to a mass defect $\Delta m = \frac{B}{c^2}$.

8. What happens to the relativistic mass of an object as its speed approaches the speed of light $c$?

Explanation: Relativistic mass $m = \$\gamma$ m_0$ approaches infinity as $v$ approaches $c$ because the Lorentz factor $\$\gamma$$ diverges to infinity.

9. Using the invariant relation $E^2 = p^2 c^2 + (m_0 c^2)^2$, what is the rest energy of a particle with momentum $p = 4 \text{ MeV}/c$ and total energy $E = 5 \text{ MeV}$?

Explanation: $(m_0 c^2)^2 = E^2 - p^2 c^2 = 5^2 - 4^2 = 25 - 16 = 9 \implies m_0 c^2 = 3 \text{ MeV}$.

10. Which UP PGT/TGT concept best explains why nuclear fission and fusion release tremendous amounts of energy?

Explanation: Fission and fusion involve changes in nuclear binding energy, resulting in a mass defect $\Delta m$ that is converted into energy according to $E = \Delta m c^2$ (since $c^2$ is extremely large).
