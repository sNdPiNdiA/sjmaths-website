Variation of Mass with Velocity in Special Relativity

According to the special theory of relativity, the mass of an object depends on its relative speed with respect to an observer. The relativistic mass increases without bound as the velocity of the object approaches the speed of light in a vacuum.

Qualitative understanding of relativistic mass increase, energy-mass equivalence relation, and basic high-velocity particle behavior.

Prerequisites

  • Postulates of Special Theory of Relativity
  • Lorentz transformations for space and time
  • Conservation of momentum and energy in mechanics

Concept of Rest Mass versus Relativistic Mass

  • Rest mass (m_0) is the invariant mass of an object measured in the frame of reference where the object is at rest.
  • Relativistic mass (m) is the effective mass of a moving object as measured by an observer in a frame relative to which the object moves with velocity v.
  • The relation is given by $m = m_0 / \sqrt(1 - v^2/c^2)$, where c is the speed of light.

Behavior at Extreme Velocities

  • For speeds much smaller than c (v << c), the relativistic mass reduces to the classical Newtonian mass (m ≈ m_0).
  • As the velocity v approaches c, the denominator approaches zero, causing the relativistic mass to approach infinity.
  • Because an infinite force and energy would be required to accelerate a massive object to the speed of light, material particles can never reach or exceed c.

Transverse and Longitudinal Mass

  • In advanced relativistic dynamics, force and acceleration are not necessarily collinear.
  • Longitudinal mass ($m_L = m_0 / (1 - v^2/c^2)^(3/2)) governs acceleration parallel$ to the velocity vector.
  • Transverse mass ($m_T = m_0 / (1 - v^2/c^2)^(1/2)) governs acceleration perpendicular$ to the velocity vector.

Equivalence of Mass and Energy

  • The total relativistic energy of a free particle is expressed as $E = mc^2$.
  • The kinetic energy (K) is the difference between total energy and rest energy: $K = (m - m_0)c^2$.
  • In high-energy particle accelerators, increase in input energy manifests primarily as an increase in relativistic mass rather than classical velocity increments.

Derivations

Derivation of Relativistic Mass Variation

Conditions: Using conservation of linear momentum in two reference frames during a symmetric elastic-plastic collision.

  • Consider two identical bodies A and B moving with equal and opposite velocities u_y and -u_y along the y-axis in frame S', and frame S moving with velocity v along the x-axis.
  • Body A is thrown from S' and body B from S such that they collide perfectly inelastically at the origin of S and come to rest together in S.
  • Using velocity addition formulas for the y-component of velocity in frame S: $u_{y} = u'_{y} \sqrt(1 - v^2/c^2)$.
  • Applying conservation of momentum in frame S: m_A u_{yA} + m_B $u_{yB} = (m_A + m_B) u_{yfinal}$.
  • Solving for mass as a function of velocity yields m(v) = m_0 / sqrt(1 - v^2/c^2).

Result: $m = \frac{m_0}{\sqrt{1 - \frac{v^2}{c^2}}}$

Derivation of Relativistic Kinetic Energy

Conditions: Work-energy theorem applied under relativistic mechanics.

  • Work done W to increase velocity from 0 to v is equal to change in kinetic energy: K = int F dx = int \frac{d(mv)}{dt} dx = int v d(mv).
  • Substitute $m = m_0 / \sqrt(1 - v^2/c^2)$ and integrate by parts.
  • $K = \int_{0}^{v} v d\left(\frac{m_0 v}{\sqrt{1 - v^2/c^2}}\right)$.
  • Evaluation of the integral gives $K = \frac{m_0 c^2}{\sqrt{1 - v^2/c^2}} - m_0 c^2$.
  • Expressing in terms of relativistic mass: $K = mc^2 - m_0 c^2 = E - E_0$.

Result: $K = (\frac{1}{\sqrt{1 - \frac{v^2}{c^2}}} - 1) m_0 c^2$

Solved numericals

TGT core

At what fraction of the speed of light must a particle move so that its relativistic mass is twice its rest mass?

  • Write the given condition: $m = 2m_0$.
  • Use the mass-velocity relation: $m = m_0 / \sqrt(1 - v^2/c^2)$.
  • Substitute m: 2$m_0 = m_0 / \sqrt(1 - v^2/c^2) => 2 = 1 / \sqrt(1 - v^2/c^2)$.
  • Square both sides: $4 = 1 / (1 - v^2/c^2) => 1 - v^2/c^2 = 1/4$.
  • Solve for v: $v^2/c^2 = 3/4 => v = (\sqrt(3)/2)c$.

Answer: v = \frac{sqrt{3}}{2} c approx 0.866 c

PGT extension

Calculate the kinetic energy of an electron (rest mass $m_0 = 9.1 \times 10^{-31} kg) moving with a velocity of 0.6c$. Express the answer in Joules and electron-volts (eV). (Take $c = 3 \times 10^8 m/s)$

  • Identify rest mass $m_0 = 9.1 \times 10^{-31} kg$ and v = 0.6c.
  • Calculate the Lorentz factor gamm$a = 1 / \sqrt(1 - (0.6c/c)^2) = 1 / \sqrt(1 - 0.36) = 1 / \sqrt(0.64) = 1 / 0.8 = 1.25$.
  • Use kinetic energy formula: $K = (\gamma - 1)m_0 c^2$.
  • Substitute values: $K = (1.25 - 1)(9.1 \times 10^{-31})(3 \times 10^8)^2 = 0.25 \times (9.1 \times 10^{-31}) \times (9 \times 10^{16})$.
  • Calculate Joules: $K = 0.25 \times 8.19 \times 10^{-14} = 2.0475 \times 10^{-14} J$.
  • Convert to eV by dividing by electronic charge $e = 1.6 \times 10^{-19} C: K = (2.0475 \times 10^{-14}) / (1.6 \times 10^{-19}) = 1.28 \times 10^{5} eV = 128 keV$.

Answer: 2.05 \times 10^{-14} J (or 128 keV)

PGT extension

Find the momentum of a proton whose kinetic energy is equal to its rest mass energy. (Rest mass of proto$n = m_0$, speed of light = c)

  • Given condition: Kinetic Energy $K = E_0 = m_0 c^2$.
  • Total relativistic energy $E = K + E_0 = m_0 c^2 + m_0 c^2 = 2m_0 c^2$.
  • Use the energy-momentum relation: $E^2 = p^2 c^2 + (m_0 c^2)^2$.
  • Substitute E: (2m_0 $c^2)^2 = p^2 c^2 + (m_0 c^2)^2$.
  • 4(m_0 c^2)^2 - (m_0 $c^2)^2 = p^2 c^2 => 3(m_0 c^2)^2 = p^2 c^2$.
  • Solve for momentum p: $p^2 = 3 (m_0 c)^2 => p = \sqrt{3} m_0 c$.

Answer: $p = \sqrt{3} m_0 c$

Assumptions and limitations

  • Special relativity assumes inertial frames of reference moving at constant relative velocities in the absence of strong gravitational fields.
  • The concept of 'relativistic mass' is mathematically valid and useful in dynamics, though modern theoretical physics frequently prefers keeping mass strictly as the invariant rest mass (m_0) and expressing velocity dependence entirely through momentum and energy.

Common misconceptions

Common error: An object's rest mass increases when it moves fast.

Correct: Rest mass is an invariant scalar property; only the relativistic mass (or measured inertia) increases with velocity.

Rest mass is measured in the object's proper frame where v = 0, hence it remains constant regardless of the observer's frame.

Common error: Objects can exceed the speed of light if given infinite energy.

Correct: As v approaches c, the relativistic mass and required energy approach infinity, making it impossible for massive particles to reach or exceed c.

The denominator sqrt(1 - v^2/c^2) tends to zero, creating an insurmountable asymptotic barrier.

Common error: Mass and weight are the same thing in relativistic transformations.

Correct: Mass is an intrinsic measure of inertia, whereas weight depends on gravitational acceleration and metric properties in general relativity.

Relativity alters inertial mass with velocity, while gravitational mass behavior requires general relativistic treatments.

Quick revision

  • Relativistic mass increases with velocity according to $m = m_0 / \sqrt(1 - v^2/c^2)$.
  • Total energy $E = mc^2$, rest energy $E_0 = m_0 c^2$, and kinetic energy $K = (m - m_0)c^2$.
  • Momentum is given by $p = mv = m_0 v / \sqrt(1 - v^2/c^2)$.
  • The energy-momentum invariant relation is $E^2 = p^2 c^2 + (m_0 c^2)^2$.
  • A massive particle can never attain the speed of light because its mass and required energy would become infinite.

Must remember

  • Rest mass ($m_0$) is an invariant scalar quantity measured in the object's proper frame of reference.
  • Relativistic mass ($m$) increases with velocity according to $m = \frac{m_0}{\sqrt{1 - \frac{v^2}{c^2}}}$.
  • As an object's velocity $v$ approaches the speed of light $c$, its relativistic mass approaches infinity.
  • Total relativistic energy is given by $E = mc^2$, and rest mass energy is $E_0 = m_0 c^2$.
  • Relativistic kinetic energy is $K = (m - m_0)c^2 = \left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{1 - \frac{v^2}{c^2}}} - 1\right)m_0 c^2$.
  • Longitudinal mass ($m_L = \frac{m_0}{(1 - v^2/c^2)^{3/2}}$) governs acceleration parallel to velocity.
  • Transverse mass ($m_T = \frac{m_0}{(1 - v^2/c^2)^{1/2}}$) governs acceleration perpendicular to velocity.
  • The energy-momentum invariant relation is universal: $E^2 = p^2 c^2 + (m_0 c^2)^2$.

Glossary

Rest Mass ($m_0$)The invariant mass of an object measured in the frame of reference where the object is at rest.
Relativistic Mass ($m$)The effective inertial mass of a moving object as measured by an observer relative to whom the object moves with velocity $v$.
Longitudinal Mass ($m_L$)The mass parameter governing acceleration parallel to the velocity vector, expressed as $m_0 / (1 - v^2/c^2)^{3/2}$.
Transverse Mass ($m_T$)The mass parameter governing acceleration perpendicular to the velocity vector, expressed as $m_0 / (1 - v^2/c^2)^{1/2}$.
Rest Energy ($E_0$)The intrinsic energy content of a stationary particle given by $E_0 = m_0 c^2$.
Total Relativistic Energy ($E$)The total energy of a free moving particle expressed as $E = mc^2$.

Practice Quiz

Choose an option to reveal the explanation.

1. What happens to the relativistic mass of an object as its velocity $v$ approaches the speed of light $c$?

Explanation: According to the formula $m = \frac{m_0}{\sqrt{1 - v^2/c^2}}$, as $v$ approaches $c$, the denominator approaches zero, causing the relativistic mass $m$ to approach infinity.

2. What is the rest energy $E_0$ of a particle with rest mass $m_0$?

Explanation: The rest energy is defined as the energy of a particle when it is at rest ($v = 0$), given by Einstein's famous relation $E_0 = m_0 c^2$.

3. How is the relativistic kinetic energy $K$ of a free particle correctly expressed?

Explanation: Relativistic kinetic energy is the difference between total relativistic energy ($mc^2$) and rest energy ($m_0 c^2$), yielding $K = (m - m_0)c^2$.

4. Which of the following represents the correct expression for longitudinal mass $m_L$?

Explanation: Longitudinal mass governs acceleration parallel to the velocity vector and is given by $m_L = \frac{m_0}{(1 - v^2/c^2)^{3/2}}$.

5. What is the transverse mass $m_T$ equal to in special relativity?

Explanation: Transverse mass governs acceleration perpendicular to velocity and is equal to the standard relativistic mass $m_T = m = \frac{m_0}{\sqrt{1 - v^2/c^2}}$.

6. What is the invariant relation connecting total energy $E$, momentum $p$, and rest mass $m_0$?

Explanation: The energy-momentum invariant relation for any free particle is $E^2 = p^2 c^2 + (m_0 c^2)^2$, which holds across all inertial frames.

7. Why can material particles never attain the speed of light in a vacuum?

Explanation: As $v$ approaches $c$, relativistic mass approaches infinity. Accelerating an infinite mass requires infinite energy and force, creating an insurmountable barrier.

8. If an object moves at a velocity $v \ll c$, what does its relativistic mass approximately reduce to?

Explanation: When $v \ll c$, the term $v^2/c^2 \approx 0$, so $\sqrt{1 - v^2/c^2} \approx 1$, and relativistic mass $m$ reduces to the classical rest mass $m_0$.

9. What is the relativistic momentum $p$ of a particle of rest mass $m_0$ moving with velocity $v$?

Explanation: Relativistic momentum is defined as $p = mv = \frac{m_0 v}{\sqrt{1 - v^2/c^2}}$.

10. In high-energy particle accelerators, when additional energy is continuously supplied to a particle already moving close to $c$, what primarily increases?

Explanation: Particles moving near $c$ experience very little increase in velocity for added energy; instead, the input energy manifests as an increase in relativistic mass and total energy.

11. Which statement regarding rest mass is correct?

Explanation: Rest mass ($m_0$) is an invariant scalar quantity intrinsic to the particle, measured in its proper rest frame where $v = 0$.

12. If the velocity of a particle is $v = \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2} c$, what is its relativistic mass in terms of its rest mass $m_0$?

Explanation: Using $m = \frac{m_0}{\sqrt{1 - v^2/c^2}}$, substitute $v = \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}c$ to get $\sqrt{1 - 3/4} = \sqrt{1/4} = 1/2$. Thus $m = m_0 / (1/2) = 2 m_0$.

13. What is the ratio of transverse mass to longitudinal mass for a particle moving with velocity $v$?

Explanation: Since $m_T = \frac{m_0}{(1 - v^2/c^2)^{1/2}}$ and $m_L = \frac{m_0}{(1 - v^2/c^2)^{3/2}}$, their ratio is $m_T / m_L = 1 - v^2/c^2$.

14. Which misconception about mass in special relativity is fundamentally false?

Explanation: Rest mass is an invariant property and does not change with the object's velocity; only the relativistic mass increases.

15. For a massless particle (such as a photon, where $m_0 = 0$), what is the relationship between its energy $E$ and momentum $p$?

Explanation: From $E^2 = p^2 c^2 + (m_0 c^2)^2$, setting $m_0 = 0$ yields $E^2 = p^2 c^2$, which simplifies to $E = pc$.

PYQ Patterns

These are pattern-based practice questions. Authentic year labels are only used when verified from a source.

1. If the kinetic energy of a particle is equal to its rest energy, what is its velocity in terms of the speed of light $c$?

Explanation: Given $K = E_0$, so $(m - m_0)c^2 = m_0 c^2 \implies m = 2m_0$. Since $m = \frac{m_0}{\sqrt{1 - v^2/c^2}}$, we get $\frac{1}{\sqrt{1 - v^2/c^2}} = 2 \implies 1 - v^2/c^2 = \frac{1}{4} \implies v = \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}c$.

2. At what velocity does the relativistic mass of a particle become $1.5$ times its rest mass?

Explanation: Set $m = 1.5 m_0 = \frac{3}{2} m_0$. Then $\frac{1}{\sqrt{1 - v^2/c^2}} = \frac{3}{2} \implies 1 - \frac{v^2}{c^2} = \frac{4}{9} \implies \frac{v^2}{c^2} = \frac{5}{9} \implies v = \frac{\sqrt{5}}{3}c$.

3. The total energy of a particle is $3$ times its rest energy. What is its relativistic momentum?

Explanation: Given $E = 3 E_0 = 3 m_0 c^2$. From $E^2 = p^2 c^2 + (m_0 c^2)^2$, $(3 m_0 c^2)^2 = p^2 c^2 + (m_0 c^2)^2 \implies 9 (m_0 c^2)^2 - (m_0 c^2)^2 = p^2 c^2 \implies 8 (m_0 c^2)^2 = p^2 c^2 \implies p = 2\sqrt{2} m_0 c$.

4. What is the percentage increase in the mass of a body when it is accelerated to a velocity of $0.6 c$?

Explanation: For $v = 0.6c$, $m = \frac{m_0}{\sqrt{1 - (0.6)^2}} = \frac{m_0}{\sqrt{1 - 0.36}} = \frac{m_0}{\sqrt{0.64}} = \frac{m_0}{0.8} = 1.25 m_0$. The increase is $1.25 m_0 - m_0 = 0.25 m_0$, which is $25 \%$.

5. If the rest mass of an electron is $m_0$ and it moves with velocity $v = 0.8 c$, what is its longitudinal mass $m_L$?

Explanation: Longitudinal mass is $m_L = \frac{m_0}{(1 - v^2/c^2)^{3/2}}$. For $v = 0.8c$, $1 - v^2/c^2 = 1 - 0.64 = 0.36 = \left(\frac{3}{5}\right)^2$. Raising to the power $3/2$ gives $\left(\frac{3}{5}\right)^3 = \frac{27}{125}$. Thus $m_L = \frac{125}{27} m_0$.

6. A particle of rest mass $m_0$ has a momentum $p = m_0 c$. What is its total relativistic energy $E$?

Explanation: Using $E^2 = p^2 c^2 + (m_0 c^2)^2$ and substituting $p = m_0 c$: $E^2 = (m_0 c)^2 c^2 + (m_0 c^2)^2 = (m_0 c^2)^2 + (m_0 c^2)^2 = 2 (m_0 c^2)^2$. Therefore, $E = \sqrt{2} m_0 c^2$.

Timed Topic Test

Attempt these questions under a ten-minute limit using a separate timer.

1. Which of the following expressions correctly defines the relativistic mass $m$ in terms of rest mass $m_0$ and velocity $v$?

Explanation: The standard relativistic mass variation formula is $m = \frac{m_0}{\sqrt{1 - v^2/c^2}}$.

2. What is the kinetic energy of a particle expressed in terms of its relativistic mass $m$ and rest mass $m_0$?

Explanation: Relativistic kinetic energy $K$ is the difference between total energy ($mc^2$) and rest energy ($m_0 c^2$), which is $(m - m_0)c^2$.

3. For a particle moving at $v = \frac{\sqrt{15}}{4} c$, what is the ratio of its relativistic mass to its rest mass?

Explanation: $1 - v^2/c^2 = 1 - 15/16 = 1/16$. Taking the square root of the reciprocal gives $\frac{1}{\sqrt{1/16}} = 4$.

4. Which mass formulation governs the acceleration of a charged particle when the accelerating electric field is perpendicular to its velocity?

Explanation: Transverse mass ($m_T$) governs forces and accelerations perpendicular to the velocity vector.

5. What happens to the denominator term $\sqrt{1 - v^2/c^2}$ as an object's speed decreases toward zero ($v \to 0$)?

Explanation: As $v \to 0$, $v^2/c^2 \to 0$, so $\sqrt{1 - 0} = 1$.

6. Which of the following correctly characterizes rest mass?

Explanation: Rest mass $m_0$ is an invariant scalar quantity intrinsic to the particle, measured where $v = 0$.

7. If an object's total energy is double its rest energy ($E = 2 E_0$), what is its velocity?

Explanation: $E = 2 m_0 c^2 \implies mc^2 = 2 m_0 c^2 \implies m = 2 m_0 \implies \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 - v^2/c^2}} = 2 \implies v = \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}c$.

8. What is the correct dimensional unit for relativistic mass and rest mass?

Explanation: Mass, whether rest mass or relativistic mass, is measured in kilograms (kg).

9. Which of the following statements about longitudinal mass ($m_L$) and transverse mass ($m_T$) is correct?

Explanation: Since $m_L = m_0 \gamma^3$ and $m_T = m_0 \gamma$, $m_L = m_T \gamma^2$, making longitudinal mass larger than transverse mass.

10. According to special relativity, can a particle with non-zero rest mass travel at the speed of light $c$?

Explanation: Reaching $c$ requires infinite energy because relativistic mass approaches infinity as $v \to c$, making it physically impossible for massive particles.
