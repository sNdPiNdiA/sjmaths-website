Perpendicular-Axis Theorem

The perpendicular-axis theorem states that the moment of inertia of a plane lamina about an axis perpendicular to its plane is equal to the sum of the moments of inertia about two mutually perpendicular axes concurrent with the perpendicular axis and lying in the plane of the lamina. It applies exclusively to two-dimensional planar bodies (laminae).

Scope and prerequisites

TGT core

Direct statement of the theorem, application to simple planar bodies such as thin circular rings and discs to find the moment of inertia about an axis perpendicular to the plane passing through the center.

PGT extension

Rigorous derivation using coordinate systems, combination with parallel-axis theorem for composite planar and non-planar structures, and extension to continuous mass distribution integrations over area density.

Prerequisites

  • Moment of inertia definition and physical significance
  • Cartesian coordinate systems in two dimensions
  • Integration techniques for continuous mass distributions

Statement and Mathematical Expression

  • The theorem is mathematically expressed as I_z = I_x + I_y, where I_z is the moment of inertia about an axis perpendicular to the lamina, and I_x and I_y are moments of inertia about mutually perpendicular axes lying within the plane.
  • All three axes—x, y, and z—must be mutually orthogonal and intersect at a common point located on the plane of the lamina.
  • The intersection point of the axes does not necessarily have to be the center of mass or the geometric center of the lamina.

Geometric Restriction to Planar Bodies

  • The theorem is strictly valid only for plane laminae (two-dimensional objects where the thickness along the z-axis is negligible or zero).
  • For any true three-dimensional solid body (such as a solid sphere, cylinder, or cube), the perpendicular-axis theorem cannot be applied directly because the mass coordinate along the perpendicular axis z is not identically zero (z = 0).
  • However, elements of 3D bodies that possess planar symmetry can utilize the principle by treating them as an assembly of infinitesimal laminae.

Choice of In-Plane Axes

  • The axes x and y can be any two mutually perpendicular lines lying entirely in the plane of the body, intersecting at the origin where the z-axis passes.
  • They do not need to be principal axes of symmetry, though choosing symmetry axes often simplifies the evaluation of I_x and I_y.
  • Rotating the x and y axes within the plane about the z-axis changes individual values of I_x and I_y, but their sum I_z remains invariant.

Synergy with the Parallel-Axis Theorem

  • The perpendicular-axis theorem is frequently combined with the parallel-axis theorem to find moments of inertia about axes that neither lie in the plane nor pass through the center of mass directly.
  • For example, finding the moment of inertia of a circular disc about a tangent in its own plane requires both theorems sequentially.
  • The parallel-axis theorem shifts axes across space, while the perpendicular-axis theorem relates orthogonal axes within the same plane.

Derivations

Derivation of the Perpendicular-Axis Theorem

Conditions: Planar lamina lying in the XY plane, with the Z-axis perpendicular to the plane passing through the origin.

  • Consider a thin planar lamina of mass M lying in the XY plane. Let an element of mass dm be located at coordinates (x, y) relative to the origin.
  • The perpendicular distance of this mass element dm from the Z-axis (passing through the origin perpendicular to the XY plane) is given by r^2 = x^2 + y^2.
  • By definition, the moment of inertia of the lamina about the Z-axis is I_z = integral (r^2) dm = integral (x^2 + y^2) dm.
  • Split the integral into two parts: I_z = integral (x^2) dm + integral (y^2) dm.
  • Recognize that integral (y^2) dm represents the moment of inertia about the X-axis (I_x) because y is the perpendicular distance of dm from the X-axis.
  • Recognize that integral (x^2) dm represents the moment of inertia about the Y-axis (I_y) because x is the perpendicular distance of dm from the Y-axis.
  • Substitute these to obtain the final relation: I_z = I_x + I_y.

Result: I_z = I_x + I_y

Solved numericals

TGT core

Find the moment of inertia of a uniform circular disc of mass 2 kg and radius 0.5 m about a diameter lying in its plane.

  • Identify the formula for the moment of inertia of a disc about a perpendicular axis through its center: I_z = (1/2) M R^2.
  • Calculate I_z: I_z = 0.5 * 2 kg * (0.5 m)^2 = 0.5 * 2 * 0.25 = 0.25 kg m^2.
  • Use the perpendicular-axis theorem for a symmetrical disc where I_x = I_y = I_diameter, so I_z = 2 * I_diameter.
  • Therefore, I_diameter = I_z / 2 = 0.25 / 2 = 0.125 kg m^2.

Answer: 0.125 kg m^2

PGT extension

A thin uniform rectangular lamina has mass M, length l, and breadth b. Using the perpendicular-axis theorem, find its moment of inertia about an axis passing through its center and perpendicular to its plane.

  • For a rectangular lamina in the XY plane with center at the origin, the in-plane axes parallel to sides b and l are chosen as X and Y axes.
  • The moment of inertia about the X-axis (parallel to side l, with breadth b distributed across y) is I_x = (1/12) M b^2.
  • The moment of inertia about the Y-axis (parallel to side b, with length l distributed across x) is I_y = (1/12) M l^2.
  • Apply the perpendicular-axis theorem: I_z = I_x + I_y.
  • Substitute the values: I_z = (1/12) M b^2 + (1/12) M l^2 = (1/12) M (l^2 + b^2).

Answer: (1/12) M (l^2 + b^2)

PGT extension

Determine the moment of inertia of a uniform circular ring of mass M and radius R about a tangent lying in the plane of the ring.

  • First, find the moment of inertia of the ring about an in-plane diameter. For a ring, I_z = M R^2 about the perpendicular central axis.
  • Since I_z = I_x + I_y and I_x = I_y for a ring, the moment of inertia about any diameter is I_d = I_z / 2 = (1/2) M R^2.
  • The desired tangent is in the plane of the ring and is parallel to a diameter of the ring.
  • Apply the parallel-axis theorem between the in-plane diameter axis and the in-plane tangent axis, where the separation distance is R.
  • I_tangent = I_d + M R^2 = (1/2) M R^2 + M R^2 = (3/2) M R^2.

Answer: 1.5 M R^2

Assumptions and limitations

  • The body must be strictly two-dimensional (a lamina); application to 3D solid bodies without integration over thickness leads to severe errors.

Common misconceptions

Common error: The perpendicular-axis theorem can be applied to any three-dimensional object like a solid sphere or a solid cylinder.

Correct: It is strictly applicable only to two-dimensional planar bodies (laminae).

The derivation assumes that all mass elements lie in a single plane, making the z-coordinate of every mass element zero (z = 0), which is false for 3D bodies.

Common error: The three axes in the theorem must intersect at the center of mass.

Correct: The three mutually perpendicular axes must intersect at any common point on the plane of the lamina, not necessarily the center of mass.

The geometric proof relies only on orthogonal distance squares (r^2 = x^2 + y^2) relative to the intersection origin, regardless of whether it is the center of mass.

Common error: I_x and I_y must always be equal to each other.

Correct: I_x and I_y are equal only if the planar body possesses high geometric symmetry about both axes (such as a circular ring or disc).

For asymmetric laminae like a rectangular plate with unequal sides, I_x and I_y have distinct values based on their respective mass distributions.

Quick revision

  • Perpendicular-axis theorem formula: I_z = I_x + I_y for 2D laminae.
  • Perpendicular-Axis Theorem Relation: I_z = I_x + I_y
  • Moment of Inertia of Circular Ring (Perpendicular Axis): I_z = M R^2

Must remember

  • The perpendicular-axis theorem is strictly valid only for two-dimensional plane laminae ($z = 0$).
  • The mathematical expression is $I_z = I_x + I_y$, where $x$, $y$, and $z$ are mutually orthogonal and intersect at a common point on the plane.
  • The sum $I_z = I_x + I_y$ remains invariant when the in-plane axes $x$ and $y$ are rotated about the $z$-axis.
  • For a thin circular ring of mass $M$ and radius $R$, the in-plane diameter moment of inertia is $I_x = I_y = \frac{1}{2} M R^2$.
  • For a thin circular disc of mass $M$ and radius $R$, the in-plane diameter moment of inertia is $I_x = I_y = \frac{1}{4} M R^2$.
  • The intersection point of the three axes does not necessarily have to be the center of mass or geometric center.

Practice Quiz

Choose an option to reveal the explanation.

1. What is the primary geometric restriction for the applicability of the perpendicular-axis theorem?

Explanation: The perpendicular-axis theorem is strictly valid only for plane laminae (two-dimensional objects) where the thickness along the $z$-axis is negligible or zero, ensuring $z = 0$ for all mass elements.

2. The mathematical expression for the perpendicular-axis theorem is given by:

Explanation: The theorem is mathematically expressed as $$I_{z}$ = $I_{x}$ + $I_{y}$$, where $$I_{z}$$ is about the perpendicular axis and $$I_{x}$, $I_{y}$$ are about mutually perpendicular in-plane axes.

3. Can the perpendicular-axis theorem be applied directly to a solid sphere or solid cylinder?

Explanation: For any true three-dimensional solid body like a solid sphere or cylinder, the theorem cannot be applied directly because the mass coordinate along the perpendicular axis is not identically zero ($z \neq 0$).

4. In the relation $$I_{z}$ = $I_{x}$ + $I_{y}$$, where must the three axes intersect?

Explanation: All three axes ($x$, $y$, and $z$) must be mutually orthogonal and intersect at a common point located on the plane of the lamina, which does not necessarily have to be the center of mass.

5. What happens to the sum $$I_{x}$ + $I_{y}$$ if the in-plane $x$ and $y$ axes are rotated about the $z$-axis within the plane of the lamina?

Explanation: Rotating the $x$ and $y$ axes within the plane about the $z$-axis changes the individual values of $$I_{x}$$ and $$I_{y}$$, but their sum $$I_{z}$$ remains invariant.

6. What is the moment of inertia of a thin circular ring of mass $M$ and radius $R$ about an in-plane diameter?

Explanation: For a circular ring, $$I_{z}$ = M R^2$. Since $$I_{x}$ = $I_{y}$$ by symmetry, $$I_{x}$ = \frac{1}{2} $I_{z}$ = \frac{1}{2} M R^2$.

7. What is the moment of inertia of a thin circular disc of mass $M$ and radius $R$ about an axis perpendicular to the disc passing through its center ($$I_{z}$$)?

Explanation: For a thin circular disc, the moment of inertia about a perpendicular axis passing through its center is $$I_{z}$ = \frac{1}{2} M R^2$.

8. Using the perpendicular-axis theorem, find the moment of inertia of a thin circular disc of mass $M$ and radius $R$ about an in-plane diameter.

Explanation: For a circular disc, $$I_{z}$ = \frac{1}{2} M R^2$. Since $$I_{x}$ = $I_{y}$$ along orthogonal diameters, $$I_{x}$ = \frac{1}{2} $I_{z}$ = \frac{1}{4} M R^2$.

9. Which theorem is required in combination with the perpendicular-axis theorem to find the moment of inertia of a circular disc about a tangent in its own plane?

Explanation: Finding the moment of inertia about a tangent in its own plane requires first finding the diameter MOI using the perpendicular-axis theorem, and then shifting it using the parallel-axis theorem.

10. For a rectangular lamina of mass $M$ with in-plane dimensions $a$ and $b$, what is the moment of inertia about the perpendicular axis passing through the center of mass ($$I_{z}$$)?

Explanation: For a rectangular lamina with sides $a$ and $b$ in-plane, $$I_{x}$ = \frac{1}{12} M b^2$ and $$I_{y}$ = \frac{1}{12} M a^2$, giving $$I_{z}$ = $I_{x}$ + $I_{y}$ = \frac{1}{12} M (a^2 + b^2)$.

11. Do the in-plane axes $x$ and $y$ chosen for the perpendicular-axis theorem need to be principal axes of symmetry?

Explanation: The axes $x$ and $y$ do not need to be principal axes of symmetry, although choosing symmetry axes often simplifies the evaluation of $$I_{x}$$ and $$I_{y}$$.

12. What is the common misconception regarding the intersection point of the three axes in the perpendicular-axis theorem?

Explanation: A common misconception is that the axes must intersect at the center of mass; however, they can intersect at any common point on the plane of the lamina.

13. How can elements of three-dimensional bodies utilize the perpendicular-axis theorem?

Explanation: Elements of 3D bodies that possess planar symmetry can utilize the principle by treating them as an assembly of infinitesimal laminae where each lamina satisfies $z = 0$ locally.

14. In the formula $$I_{x}$ = \frac{1}{12} M b^2$ for a rectangular lamina, what does $b$ represent?

Explanation: For an in-plane $x$-axis passing through the center of a rectangle with sides $a$ and $b$ (where $a$ is along $x$ and $b$ is perpendicular to $x$), $$I_{x}$ = \frac{1}{12} M b^2$.

15. What is the physical significance of the parallel-axis theorem when used alongside the perpendicular-axis theorem?

Explanation: The parallel-axis theorem shifts axes across space, whereas the perpendicular-axis theorem relates orthogonal axes within the same plane, making them complementary.

PYQ Patterns

These are pattern-based practice questions. Authentic year labels are only used when verified from a source.

1. A thin circular ring has mass $M$ and radius $R$. What is its moment of inertia about an axis passing through its center and perpendicular to its plane?

Explanation: For a thin circular ring, every mass element is at a distance $R$ from the center axis, hence $$I_{z}$ = \sum m_i R^2 = M R^2$.

2. If the moment of inertia of a plane lamina about two mutually perpendicular axes in its plane are $$I_{x}$ = 3 \text{ kg m}^2$ and $$I_{y}$ = 4 \text{ kg m}^2$, what is its moment of inertia about an axis perpendicular to the lamina and passing through their intersection?

Explanation: According to the perpendicular-axis theorem, $$I_{z}$ = $I_{x}$ + $I_{y}$ = 3 + 4 = 7 \text{ kg m}^2$.

3. What is the moment of inertia of a thin circular disc about a diameter lying in its plane, given that its mass is $M$ and radius is $R$?

Explanation: For a circular disc, $$I_{z}$ = \frac{1}{2} M R^2$. By the perpendicular-axis theorem and symmetry, $$I_{x}$ = $I_{y}$ = \frac{1}{2} $I_{z}$ = \frac{1}{4} M R^2$.

4. Which of the following bodies allows the direct application of the perpendicular-axis theorem?

Explanation: A thin rectangular sheet is a two-dimensional plane lamina, which satisfies the strict geometric restriction for the direct application of the perpendicular-axis theorem.

5. Let $$I_{x}$$ and $$I_{y}$$ be the moments of inertia of a plane lamina about two orthogonal axes in its plane. If the axes are rotated by an angle $\$\theta$$ about the perpendicular $z$-axis, what happens to $$I_{z}$ = $I_{x}$ + $I_{y}$$?

Explanation: The sum $$I_{x}$ + $I_{y}$ = $I_{z}$$ is rotationally invariant; rotating the in-plane axes changes $$I_{x}$$ and $$I_{y}$$ individually, but their sum remains constant.

6. A rectangular lamina has sides $a = 3\text{ m}$ and $b = 4\text{ m}$ and mass $M$. What is $$I_{z}$$ about the perpendicular axis through its center?

Explanation: Using $$I_{z}$ = \frac{1}{12} M (a^2 + b^2)$, we substitute $a = 3$ and $b = 4$ to get $\frac{1}{12} M (9 + 16) = \frac{25}{12} M$.

Timed Topic Test

Attempt these questions under a ten-minute limit using a separate timer.

1. If a planar lamina lies in the $xy$-plane, what is the coordinate condition assumed for all its mass elements in the derivation of the perpendicular-axis theorem?

Explanation: Since the body is a 2D plane lamina lying in the $xy$-plane, every mass element has a perpendicular coordinate of $z = 0$.

2. If the moment of inertia of a circular ring about an in-plane diameter is $I$, what is its moment of inertia about a tangent parallel to that diameter?

Explanation: By the parallel-axis theorem, $I_{tangent} = I_{diameter} + M R^2$. Since $I_{diameter} = \frac{1}{2} M R^2$, $I_{tangent} = \frac{1}{2} M R^2 + M R^2 = \frac{3}{2} M R^2 = 3 \left(\frac{1}{2} M R^2\right) = 3I$.

3. Which statement correctly contrasts the requirements of the parallel-axis and perpendicular-axis theorems?

Explanation: The parallel-axis theorem relates any two parallel axes (one through CM) for any body, whereas the perpendicular-axis theorem requires three mutually orthogonal axes in a 2D plane lamina.

4. What is the ratio of $$I_{z}$$ (perpendicular axis) to $$I_{x}$$ (in-plane diameter) for a thin circular ring?

Explanation: For a ring, $$I_{z}$ = M R^2$ and $$I_{x}$ = \frac{1}{2} M R^2$. Thus, the ratio $$I_{z}$ : $I_{x}$ = 1 : \frac{1}{2} = 2:1$.

5. What is the ratio of $$I_{z}$$ (perpendicular axis) to $$I_{x}$$ (in-plane diameter) for a thin circular disc?

Explanation: For a disc, $$I_{z}$ = \frac{1}{2} M R^2$ and $$I_{x}$ = \frac{1}{4} M R^2$. Thus, the ratio $$I_{z}$ : $I_{x}$ = \frac{1}{2} : \frac{1}{4} = 2:1$.

6. If a lamina has $$I_{x}$ = 5 \text{ kg m}^2$ and $$I_{z}$ = 13 \text{ kg m}^2$, what is the moment of inertia $$I_{y}$$ about the other orthogonal in-plane axis?

Explanation: From $$I_{z}$ = $I_{x}$ + $I_{y}$$, we get $$I_{y}$ = $I_{z}$ - $I_{x}$ = 13 - 5 = 8 \text{ kg m}^2$.

7. Why does the perpendicular-axis theorem fail when applied directly to a solid sphere?

Explanation: A solid sphere has mass distributed along the $z$-axis ($z \neq 0$), violating the essential 2D lamina requirement where all mass resides in a single plane.

8. Consider a thin square lamina of mass $M$ and side $a$. What is the moment of inertia about an in-plane axis parallel to one of its sides passing through the center?

Explanation: For a square lamina with side $a$, substituting $b = a$ into the rectangular in-plane formula $$I_{x}$ = \frac{1}{12} M b^2$ gives $\frac{1}{12} M a^2$.

9. Which of the following combinations correctly gives the perpendicular axis moment of inertia $$I_{z}$$ for a square lamina of side $a$ using symmetry?

Explanation: For a square lamina, by symmetry, $$I_{x}$ = $I_{y}$$. Therefore, $$I_{z}$ = $I_{x}$ + $I_{y}$ = 2 $I_{x}$$.

10. In applying the perpendicular-axis theorem, can the intersection point of the axes be located at a corner of the lamina rather than its center?

Explanation: The theorem requires only that the orthogonal axes intersect at a common point located on the plane of the lamina; it does not need to be the center of mass or geometric center.
