Kepler's Laws of Planetary Motion

Kepler's laws are three empirical laws describing the kinematic properties of planetary motion around the Sun, formulated by Johannes Kepler using Tycho Brahe's astronomical data. They form the foundational bridge between observational astronomy and Newtonian mechanics.

Scope and prerequisites

TGT core

Qualitative statements of the three laws, calculation of time periods using Kepler's Third Law (T&sup2; is proportional to r&sup3;), identification of orbital parameters like semi-major axis, and conservation of angular momentum in central orbits.

PGT extension

Rigorous derivation of Kepler's Second Law as a consequence of conservation of angular momentum, derivation of Kepler's Third Law for circular and elliptical orbits using Newton's law of gravitation, total energy of an orbiting body, and effective potential energy.

Prerequisites

  • Newton's Law of Gravitation
  • Conservation of Angular Momentum
  • Conic Sections (Ellipse, Parabola, Hyperbola)

Kepler's First Law (Law of Orbits)

  • All planets move in elliptical orbits with the Sun located at one of the two foci.
  • The eccentricity (e) of an ellipse lies between 0 and 1 (0 < e < 1). For a circle, e = 0.
  • The closest point of approach to the Sun is the perihelion, and the farthest point is the aphelion.

Kepler's Second Law (Law of Areas)

  • A line segment joining a planet and the Sun sweeps out equal areas in equal intervals of time.
  • This law is a direct consequence of the conservation of angular momentum, as the gravitational force is a central force exerting zero torque.
  • Planets move fastest at the perihelion and slowest at the aphelion.

Kepler's Third Law (Law of Periods)

  • The square of the time period of revolution of a planet is directly proportional to the cube of the semi-major axis of its elliptical orbit.
  • Expressed mathematically as T² = k r³, where k depends only on the mass of the central body (the Sun).
  • It establishes that outer planets take significantly longer to complete one orbit around the Sun.

Connection to Central Forces and Newtonian Mechanics

  • Newton demonstrated that Kepler's laws imply an inverse-square central gravitational force F = -GMm/r² r̂.
  • The motion occurs entirely in a plane due to zero net external torque acting about the Sun.
  • The total mechanical energy of a bound planetary orbit is negative (E < 0).

Derivations

Derivation of Kepler's Second Law from Conservation of Angular Momentum

Conditions: Central force field acting on a planet of mass m

  • The areal velocity of a planet vectorially is given by dA/dt = rac{1}{2} |r imes v|.
  • Substitute linear momentum p = mv into the cross product to get dA/dt = rac{1}{2m} |r imes p|.
  • Since r imes p is the angular momentum L, dA/dt = rac{L}{2m}.
  • Because gravitational force is a central force, torque τ = rac{dL}{dt} = 0, meaning L is constant.
  • Therefore, the areal velocity rac{dA}{dt} is constant, proving Kepler's Second Law.

Result: \frac{dA}{dt} = \frac{L}{2m} = \text{constant}

Derivation of Kepler's Third Law for Circular Orbits

Conditions: Planet of mass m moving in a circular orbit of radius r around a Sun of mass M

  • Equate the gravitational force to the required centripetal force: \frac{GMm}{r^2} = \frac{mv^2}{r}.
  • Express velocity v in terms of distance r and time period T as v = \frac{2\pi r}{T}.
  • Substitute the expression for v into the force balance equation: \frac{GM}{r^2} = \frac{1}{r} \left(\frac{2\pi r}{T}\right)^2.
  • Simplify the equation: \frac{GM}{r} = \frac{4\pi^2 r^2}{T^2}.
  • Rearrange to solve for T^2: T^2 = \left(\frac{4\pi^2}{GM}\right) r^3.

Result: T^2 = \left(\frac{4\pi^2}{GM}\right) r^3

Solved numericals

TGT core

If the distance of a planet from the Sun becomes half of its present distance, what will be the new time period of revolution?

  • State Kepler's Third Law: T² is proportional to r³.
  • Set up the ratio: (T_2 / T_1)² = (r_2 / r_1)³.
  • Substitute r_2 = r_1 / 2 into the equation: (T_2 / T_1)² = (1 / 2)³ = 1 / 8.
  • Take the square root on both sides: T_2 / T_1 = \sqrt{1 / 8} = \frac{1}{2\sqrt{2}}.
  • Write the final answer in terms of T_1: T_2 = \frac{T_1}{2\sqrt{2}}.

Answer: T / 2\sqrt{2}

PGT extension

A satellite orbits a planet of mass M in an elliptical orbit. Its closest distance is r_1 and farthest distance is r_2. Find the ratio of its speeds at these two extreme positions (v_1 / v_2).

  • Apply the principle of conservation of angular momentum about the center of force (focus): m r_1 v_1 = m r_2 v_2.
  • Cancel mass m from both sides: r_1 v_1 = r_2 v_2.
  • Rearrange to find the ratio of velocities v_1 / v_2: \frac{v_1}{v_2} = \frac{r_2}{r_1}.
  • Interpret the physical result: speed is inversely proportional to distance from the center.

Answer: r_2 / r_1

PGT extension

Calculate the total mechanical energy of an artificial satellite of mass 200 kg orbiting the Earth (mass M = 6 \times 10^{24} kg, radius R = 6400 km) in a circular orbit at a height equal to the Earth's radius.

  • Identify the orbital radius r = R + h = R + R = 2R = 2 \times 6.4 \times 10^6 m = 1.28 \times 10^7 m.
  • State the total energy formula for a circular orbit: E = -\frac{GMm}{2r}.
  • Substitute the given values (G = 6.67 \times 10^{-11} N m^2/kg^2, M = 6 \times 10^{24} kg, m = 200 kg):
  • E = -\frac{(6.67 \times 10^{-11}) \times (6 \times 10^{24}) \times 200}{2 \times (1.28 \times 10^7)}.
  • Simplify the numerator: 6.67 \times 6 \times 200 \times 10^{13} = 8.004 \times 10^{18}.
  • Simplify the denominator: 2.56 \times 10^7.
  • Calculate final numerical value: E = -3.126 \times 10^9 J.

Answer: -3.13 \times 10^9 J

Assumptions and limitations

  • Kepler's laws assume a two-body system (Sun and a single planet) and neglect gravitational perturbations caused by other planets.
  • Planets are treated as point masses or spherically symmetric bodies, ignoring oblateness and non-uniform density distributions.
  • Relativistic corrections (such as perihelion precession explained by General Relativity) are ignored in classical Keplerian motion.

Common misconceptions

Common error: The Sun is located at the center of the elliptical orbit.

Correct: The Sun is located at one of the two foci of the ellipse, not the geometric center.

An ellipse has two foci; the geometric center is equidistant from the vertices, whereas the Sun occupies only one focus point.

Common error: Kepler's laws apply only to planets orbiting the Sun.

Correct: Kepler's laws apply to any system bound by an inverse-square central force, including satellites around Earth and binary star systems.

Newton generalized Kepler's third law to include the mass of both interacting bodies (M + m).

Common error: A planet moves with constant speed throughout its elliptical orbit.

Correct: A planet's speed continuously changes, being maximum at perihelion and minimum at aphelion.

Conservation of angular momentum requires that as the radius vector changes, the linear speed must change inversely to keep areal velocity constant.

Quick revision

  • Kepler's 1st Law: Orbits are ellipses with the Sun at one focus.
  • Kepler's 2nd Law: Areal velocity (dA/dt = L/2m) is constant, reflecting conservation of angular momentum.
  • Kepler's 3rd Law: T² is proportional to r³.
  • Gravitational force is an inverse-square central force producing zero net torque.

  • Kepler's Third Law states that $T^2 = \left(\frac{4\pi^2}{GM}\right) r^3$, relating time period to the semi-major axis.
  • Planets travel fastest at the perihelion ($r_p = a(1-e)$) and slowest at the aphelion ($r_a = a(1+e)$).
  • The total mechanical energy of a bound planetary orbit is negative ($E = -\frac{GMm}{2a}$).
  • Kepler's laws imply an inverse-square central gravitational force $F = -\frac{GMm}{r^2} \hat{r}$.
  • Orbital motion under central forces occurs entirely in a single plane due to zero net external torque.
  • The areal velocity of a planet remains constant throughout its elliptical orbit.
  • The eccentricity for a perfect circle is $e = 0$.
  • Newton generalized Kepler's third law to apply to any system bound by an inverse-square central force.

Glossary

PerihelionThe closest point of approach of a planet to the Sun in its elliptical orbit, given by $r_p = a(1-e)$.
AphelionThe farthest point of a planet from the Sun in its elliptical orbit, given by $r_a = a(1+e)$.
Eccentricity ($e$)A parameter that determines the amount by which an orbit deviates from a circle; for an ellipse, $0 < e < 1$.
Semi-major axis ($a$)The longest semi-diameter of an elliptical orbit, representing the average distance from the central body.
Areal Velocity ($\frac{dA}{dt}$)The area swept out by the line joining a planet and the Sun per unit time, equal to $\frac{L}{2m}$.
Kepler Constant ($K_s$)The ratio $\frac{T^2}{r^3} = \frac{4\pi^2}{GM_s}$ which is constant for all planets orbiting the Sun.

Practice Quiz

Choose an option to reveal the explanation.

1. According to Kepler's First Law, where is the Sun located relative to a planet's elliptical orbit?

Explanation: Kepler's First Law states that planets move in elliptical orbits with the Sun located at one of the two foci.

2. What is the eccentricity ($e$) range for a planetary elliptical orbit?

Explanation: For any non-circular elliptical orbit, the eccentricity lies strictly between $0$ and $1$ ($0 < e < 1$). For a circle, $e = 0$.

3. Kepler's Second Law (Law of Areas) is a direct consequence of the conservation of which physical quantity?

Explanation: Because gravitational force is a central force, the torque acting on the planet is zero, leading directly to the conservation of angular momentum and constant areal velocity.

4. At which point in its elliptical orbit does a planet travel with maximum speed?

Explanation: By conservation of angular momentum ($m r v = \text{constant}$), as the distance $r$ is minimum at the perihelion ($r_p = a(1-e)$), the speed $v$ must be maximum.

5. What does Kepler's Third Law mathematically state regarding the time period ($T$) and semi-major axis ($r$)?

Explanation: Kepler's Third Law establishes that the square of the time period is directly proportional to the cube of the semi-major axis, $T^2 = k r^3$.

6. What is the expression for the areal velocity $\frac{dA}{dt}$ of a planet of mass $m$ and angular momentum $L$?

Explanation: The areal velocity in central force motion is given by $\frac{dA}{dt} = \frac{L}{2m}$.

7. If the perihelion distance is $r_p = a(1-e)$ and the aphelion distance is $r_a = a(1+e)$, what is the ratio $\frac{r_p}{r_a}$ in terms of eccentricity $e$?

Explanation: Dividing $r_p = a(1-e)$ by $r_a = a(1+e)$ gives $\frac{1-e}{1+e}$ after canceling $a$.

8. What is the total mechanical energy $E$ of a bound planetary orbit with semi-major axis $a$?

Explanation: The total mechanical energy of a bound orbit in an inverse-square gravitational field is negative and equal to $E = -\frac{GMm}{2a}$.

9. Which of the following correctly represents the central gravitational force law deduced from Kepler's laws?

Explanation: Newton demonstrated that Kepler's laws imply an inverse-square central gravitational force $F = -\frac{GMm}{r^2} \hat{r}$.

10. Why does planetary motion occur entirely within a single plane?

Explanation: Zero net external torque about the Sun ensures that angular momentum vector remains constant in direction, confining motion to a plane.

11. What is the orbital velocity $v$ of a planet at instantaneous distance $r$ in an elliptical orbit with semi-major axis $a$?

Explanation: From energy conservation (kinetic + potential energy equals total energy $- \frac{GMm}{2a}$), we get $v^2 = GM \left(\frac{2}{r} - \frac{1}{a}\right)$.

12. If a planet's distance from the Sun increases by a factor of $4$, by what factor does its orbital period increase according to Kepler's Third Law?

Explanation: Using $T^2 \propto r^3$, if $r$ increases by $4$, then $T \propto (4)^{3/2} = 8$.

13. Which misconception about Kepler's laws is scientifically incorrect?

Explanation: The Sun is located at one of the two foci of the ellipse, not at its geometric center.

14. What does the Kepler constant $K_s$ for the solar system equal in terms of solar mass $M_s$?

Explanation: From Kepler's Third Law, $T^2 = \left(\frac{4\$\pi$^2}{GM_s}\right)r^3$, so $K_s = \frac{T^2}{r^3} = \frac{4\$\pi$^2}{GM_s}$.

15. What is the conservation relation of linear momentum at perihelion and aphelion for a planet of mass $m$?

Explanation: Angular momentum conservation gives $m r_p v_p = m r_a v_a$, which simplifies to $r_p v_p = r_a v_a$ since mass $m$ cancels out.

These are pattern-based practice questions. Authentic year labels are only used when verified from a source.

1. The ratio of the kinetic energy of a planet at perihelion to that at aphelion can be expressed in terms of perihelion distance $r_p$ and aphelion distance $r_a$ as:

Explanation: By conservation of angular momentum, $r_p v_p = r_a v_a$, so $\frac{v_p}{v_a} = \frac{r_a}{r_p}$. The ratio of kinetic energies is $\frac{\frac{1}{2}mv_p^2}{\frac{1}{2}mv_a^2} = \left(\frac{v_p}{v_a}\right)^2 = \left(\frac{r_a}{r_p}\right)^2$ (Wait, velocity ratio squared is $(r_a/r_p)^2$? Let's verify: $v_p/v_a = r_a/r_p$, so $KE_p/KE_a = (r_a/r_p)^2$). Let's check options: option A is $\frac{r_a}{r_p}$, wait, $(v_p/v_a)^2 = (r_a/r_p)^2$. Thus the ratio of kinetic energies is $(r_a/r_p)^2$, which corresponds to option C.

2. If the semi-major axis of an orbit is doubled, the time period of revolution around the Sun becomes approximately how many times the original time period?

Explanation: According to Kepler's Third Law, $T \propto r^{3/2}$. If $r$ becomes $2r$, then $T' \propto (2)^{3/2} r^{3/2} = 2\sqrt{2} T$.

3. A planet is revolving in an elliptical orbit. At what point is its total mechanical energy equal to the sum of potential and kinetic energies?

Explanation: Total mechanical energy is conserved throughout the entire elliptical orbit, meaning $E = K + U$ is constant at every point.

4. For a planet in an elliptical orbit with eccentricity $e = 0.5$, the ratio of the perihelion distance to the aphelion distance is:

Explanation: Perihelion $r_p = a(1 - 0.5) = 0.5a$. Aphelion $r_a = a(1 + 0.5) = 1.5a$. The ratio $\frac{r_p}{r_a} = \frac{0.5a}{1.5a} = \frac{1}{3}$, i.e., $1:3$.

5. Which of the following laws or principles directly dictates that planetary orbits lie in a single plane?

Explanation: Since the gravitational force is central, torque $\vec{\tau} = \vec{r} \times \vec{F} = 0$, keeping the angular momentum vector constant in orientation, which confines the motion to a fixed plane.

6. The potential energy of a planet of mass $m$ at distance $r$ from the Sun (mass $M$) is given by $U = -\frac{GMm}{r}$. What is the ratio of potential energy to kinetic energy for a circular orbit?

Explanation: For a circular orbit, $KE = \frac{GMm}{2r}$ and $PE = -\frac{GMm}{r}$. Thus, $\frac{PE}{KE} = \frac{-\frac{GMm}{r}}{\frac{GMm}{2r}} = -2$.

Timed Topic Test

Attempt these questions under a ten-minute limit using a separate timer.

1. What is the eccentricity of a circular orbit according to Kepler's First Law?

Explanation: A circle is a special case of an ellipse where both foci coincide with the center, resulting in an eccentricity of zero ($e = 0$).

2. If a satellite orbits a planet at a semi-major axis $r$, and another satellite orbits at $4r$, what is the ratio of their time periods $T_1 / T_2$?

Explanation: By Kepler's Third Law, $\frac{T_1}{T_2} = \left(\frac{r}{4r} g ight)^{3/2} = \left(\frac{1}{4} g ight)^{3/2} = \frac{1}{8}$.

3. In an elliptical orbit, the instantaneous speed of a planet is given by $v^2 = GM \left(\frac{2}{r} - \frac{1}{a}\right)$. At what location is the orbital speed equal to $\sqrt{\frac{GM}{a}}$?

Explanation: Setting $r = a$ in the velocity equation gives $v^2 = GM(\frac{2}{a} - \frac{1}{a}) = \frac{GM}{a}$, so $v = \sqrt{\frac{GM}{a}}$. This occurs at the semi-minor axis endpoints where $r = a$.

4. What is the physical significance of a negative total mechanical energy ($E < 0$) for a planet-Sun system?

Explanation: A negative total mechanical energy signifies a bound system where the planet remains gravitationally trapped in orbit around the Sun.

5. A planet sweeps out an area $A_1$ in time $t_1$ and $A_2$ in time $t_2$. According to Kepler's Second Law, if $t_1 = t_2$, what is the relationship between $A_1$ and $A_2$?

Explanation: Kepler's Second Law states that areal velocity $\frac{dA}{dt}$ is constant, meaning equal areas are swept out in equal intervals of time ($A_1 = A_2$ when $t_1 = t_2$).

6. Which of the following quantities remains absolutely constant throughout the motion of a planet in an elliptical orbit?

Explanation: Under a central gravitational force, torque is zero, making total angular momentum a conserved constant of motion.

7. If the mass of the Sun suddenly doubled while planetary orbital semi-major axes remained unchanged, what would happen to the square of the time periods $T^2$?

Explanation: Since $T^2 = \frac{4\$\pi$^2}{GM} r^3$, if $M$ is doubled, the term $\frac{4\$\pi$^2}{GM}$ is halved, so $T^2$ is halved.

8. What is the expression for the perihelion distance $r_p$ of an elliptical orbit in terms of semi-major axis $a$ and eccentricity $e$?

Explanation: The closest point of approach (perihelion) is given by $r_p = a(1-e)$, while the aphelion is $r_a = a(1+e)$.

9. Why is Newton's formulation of Kepler's Third Law more general than Kepler's original statement?

Explanation: Newton's generalized form includes both the central body and the orbiting body's mass ($M + m$), making it applicable to binary star systems and moons alike.

10. If the areal velocity of a planet is $5 \times 10^{15} \text{ m}^2/\text{s}$, what is the angular momentum per unit mass ($L/m$) of the planet?

Explanation: Areal velocity is given by $\frac{dA}{dt} = \frac{L}{2m}$. Therefore, $\frac{L}{m} = 2 \times \frac{dA}{dt} = 2 \times 5 \times 10^{15} = 1 \times 10^{16} \text{ m}^2/\text{s}$.
