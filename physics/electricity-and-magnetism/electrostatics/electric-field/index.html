Electricity and Magnetism

Electric Field and Electric Field Intensity

The electric field is a vector space property surrounding a source charge that exerts a force on any other test charge placed within it. It is defined quantitatively as the electrostatic force experienced per unit positive test charge.

Scope and prerequisites

TGT core

Calculation of electric field due to point charges, discrete charge distributions, linear, surface, and volume charge distributions, and basic superposition principle.

PGT extension

Continuous charge distributions, Gauss's law applications for symmetric charge systems (spherical, cylindrical, planar), and differential form of Gauss's law.

Prerequisites

  • Coulomb's Law and vector form
  • Principle of superposition of forces
  • Integration techniques for continuous charge distributions

Definition and Physical Significance

  • Electric field intensity (E) at a point is the limit of the ratio of electrostatic force (F) to a vanishingly small positive test charge (q_0).
  • It is measured in Newtons per Coulomb (N/C) or Volts per meter (V/m), with dimensions [M L T^-3 A^-1].
  • The electric field acts as a mediator for electrostatic interactions, traveling at the speed of light in vacuum.

Electric Field Lines

  • Imaginary lines drawn to represent the direction and magnitude of the electric field in space.
  • They originate on positive charges and terminate on negative charges, never forming closed loops.
  • The tangent to a field line at any point gives the direction of the electric field vector at that point, and their relative density represents field magnitude.

Superposition Principle for Electric Field

  • The net electric field at a point due to a system of discrete charges is the vector sum of the electric fields produced by each individual charge.
  • E_net = E_1 + E_2 + ... + E_n = sum over i of E_i.
  • This principle holds because Maxwell's equations and Coulomb's law are linear.

Gauss's Law and Symmetry

  • Relates the total electric flux passing through a closed hypothetical surface (Gaussian surface) to the net charge enclosed by that surface.
  • Expressed mathematically as the surface integral of E dot dA equals q_enclosed divided by epsilon_0.
  • Simplifies the calculation of electric fields for highly symmetric charge distributions like spherical, cylindrical, and planar geometries.

Derivations

Electric Field due to a Point Charge

Conditions: A stationary point charge q in vacuum or free space.

  • Place a test charge q_0 at a distance r from the source charge q.
  • Apply Coulomb's law to find the force between the charges: F = \frac{1}{4piepsilon_0} \frac{q q_0}{r^2} \hat{r}.
  • Use the definition of electric field E = \frac{F}{q_0}.
  • Substitute the force equation into the definition to cancel q_0.

Result: E = \frac{1}{4piepsilon_0} \frac{q}{r^2} \hat{r}

Electric Field due to an Infinite Thin Plane Sheet of Charge

Conditions: Uniform surface charge density sigma on an infinite, non-conducting plane sheet.

  • Construct a cylindrical Gaussian surface of cross-sectional area A penetrating perpendicularly through the sheet.
  • Evaluate the electric flux through the two flat end-caps of the cylinder: \Phi = 2 E A.
  • Calculate the total charge enclosed within the Gaussian surface: q_enclosed = \sigma A.
  • Apply Gauss's law: \Phi = \frac{q_enclosed}{\epsilon_0} \implies 2 E A = \frac{\sigma A}{\epsilon_0}.

Result: E = \frac{\sigma}{2epsilon_0}

Graphs and experiments

E vs r Graph for a Point Charge

Draw: A rectangular hyperbola curve showing E decreasing non-linearly as 1/r^2 with increasing distance r.

Interpret: Demonstrates the inverse-square law relationship between electric field magnitude and distance from a point source.

Exam use: Frequently asked in MCQs to identify correct graphical trends for Coulombic fields versus dipole fields (1/r^3).

E vs r Graph for a Solid Conducting Sphere

Draw: E = 0 from r = 0 up to r = R, followed by a sharp step-up at r = R, then falling off as 1/r^2 for r > R.

Interpret: Reflects the physical principle that static electric field inside a conductor is zero and charge resides entirely on the surface.

Exam use: Tested in conceptual conceptual matching and numerical threshold questions.

Millikan's Oil Drop Experiment

Draw: Schematic of an atomizer, viewing microscope, parallel capacitor plates, and ionizing X-ray source.

Interpret: Measures the elementary electronic charge e by balancing gravitational force, buoyant force, viscous drag, and upward electric force (qE = mg).

Exam use: Historical experiment questions regarding charge quantization and electric field balance.

Solved numericals

TGT core

Calculate the magnitude of the electric field at a distance of 20 cm from a point charge of 4 micro-C in vacuum.

  • Identify given values: q = 4 \times 10^{-6} C, r = 0.2 m, 1/(4\pi\epsilon_0) = 9 \times 10^9 N m^2/C^2.
  • Substitute values into the formula E = \frac{1}{4piepsilon_0} \frac{q}{r^2}.
  • E = (9 \times 10^9) \times \frac{4 \times 10^{-6}}{(0.2)^2}.
  • E = (9 \times 10^9) \times \frac{4 \times 10^{-6}}{0.04} = 9 \times 10^5 N/C.

Answer: 9 x 10^5 N/C

PGT extension

An infinite non-conducting sheet of charge has a uniform surface charge density of 17.7 micro-C/m^2. Calculate the electric field intensity near the sheet.

  • Identify given parameters: \sigma = 17.7 \times 10^{-6} C/m^2, \epsilon_0 = 8.85 \times 10^{-12} C^2/(N m^2).
  • Use the formula for an infinite sheet: E = \frac{\sigma}{2epsilon_0}.
  • Substitute the values: E = \frac{17.7 \times 10^{-6}}{2 \times 8.85 \times 10^{-12}}.
  • E = \frac{17.7 \times 10^{-6}}{17.7 \times 10^{-12}} = 10^6 N/C.

Answer: 10^6 N/C

PGT extension

A uniform electric field E = 3 x 10^3 i-hat N/C passes through a surface of area 10 m^2 parallel to the yz-plane. What is the electric flux through the surface?

  • Identify field vector E = 3 \times 10^3 \hat{i} N/C and area vector A = 10 \hat{i} m^2 (since surface is parallel to yz-plane, normal is along x-axis).
  • Apply definition of electric flux: \Phi = E \cdot A.
  • Calculate dot product: \Phi = (3 \times 10^3 \hat{i}) \cdot (10 \hat{i}) = 3 \times 10^4 N m^2/C.

Answer: 3 x 10^4 N m^2/C

Assumptions and limitations

  • Coulomb's law and standard electric field formulas assume stationary point charges (electrostatic regime), ignoring relativistic magnetic effects of moving charges.
  • Infinite sheet and line charge formulas are idealized approximations valid only when the distance from the edges is much smaller than the physical dimensions of the charged object.

Common misconceptions

Common error: Electric field lines physically exist as tangible threads in space.

Correct: Electric field lines are merely a geometrical visualization tool to represent field direction and relative intensity.

They do not have a physical substance; space between them is continuous.

Common error: A test charge must always be positive and static.

Correct: A test charge is an idealized mathematical point charge assumed to be infinitesimally small so it does not disturb the source charge distribution.

Its sign only determines the conventional direction of the force vector, but the field itself exists independently of the test charge.

Quick revision

  • Electric field direction is always along the tangent to the electric field line at any point.
  • Electric Field Definition: E = \lim_{q_0 \to 0} \frac{F}{q_0}
  • Electric Field of a Point Charge: E = \frac{1}{4piepsilon_0} \frac{q}{r^2}

Must remember

  • Electric field intensity $E$ at a point is defined as the limit of the ratio of electrostatic force $F$ to a vanishingly small positive test charge $q_0$ ($E = \lim_{q_0 \to 0} \frac{F}{q_0}$).
  • Dimensional formula of electric field intensity is $[M L T^{-3} A^{-1}]$ and units are $N/C$ or $V/m$.
  • Electric field lines originate on positive charges, terminate on negative charges, and never form closed loops.
  • The net electric field at any point for a system of discrete charges is the vector sum of individual electric fields ($E_{net} = \sum E_i$).
  • Gauss's law relates total electric flux through a closed surface to the net charge enclosed: $\oint E \cdot dA = \frac{q_{enclosed}}{\epsilon_0}$.
  • Electric field inside a uniform spherical shell or hollow charged conductor is zero ($E = 0$ for $r < R$).
  • Electric field near the surface of any charged conductor is given by $E = \frac{\sigma}{\epsilon_0}$, whereas for a non-conducting infinite plane sheet it is $E = \frac{\sigma}{2\epsilon_0}$.
  • Electric field due to an infinite line charge varies inversely with radial distance ($E = \frac{\lambda}{2\pi\epsilon_0 r}$).
  • A test charge is an idealized mathematical point charge chosen to be infinitesimally small so it does not disturb the source charge distribution.
  • Superposition principle holds true because Coulomb's law and Maxwell's equations are linear equations.

Glossary

Electric Field Intensity ($E$)Force experienced per unit positive test charge placed at that point in space.
Test Charge ($q_0$)An infinitesimally small positive charge used to probe the electric field without distorting the source charge.
Gaussian SurfaceA hypothetical closed surface chosen to calculate electric flux and electric field using Gauss's law.
Linear Charge Density ($\lambda$)Charge per unit length, measured in Coulombs per meter ($C/m$).
Surface Charge Density ($\sigma$)Charge per unit area, measured in Coulombs per square meter ($C/m^2$).
Electric FluxThe measure of flow of the electric field through a given surface area.

Practice Quiz

Choose an option to reveal the explanation.

1. What is the dimensional formula of electric field intensity?

Explanation: Electric field intensity is force per unit charge ($E = F/q_0$). Dimensions of force are $[M L T^{-2}]$ and charge is $[A T]$, yielding $[M L T^{-3} A^{-1}]$.

2. Which of the following statements about electric field lines is INCORRECT?

Explanation: Electric field lines are continuous curves that start on positive charges and end on negative charges; electrostatic field lines never form closed loops because electrostatic field is conservative.

3. What is the electric field intensity inside a hollow charged spherical conductor of radius $R$ at a distance $r < R$?

Explanation: By Gauss's Law, any Gaussian surface drawn inside a hollow charged conductor encloses zero net charge ($q_{enclosed} = 0$), hence the electric field inside is zero.

4. The electric field due to an infinite line charge with linear charge density $\lambda$ at a radial distance $r$ is given by:

Explanation: Applying Gauss's law with a cylindrical Gaussian surface around an infinite line charge yields $E \cdot (2\$\pi$ r L) = \frac{\lambda L}{\epsilon_0}$, which simplifies to $E = \frac{\lambda}{2\$\pi$\epsilon_0 r}$.

5. What is the SI unit of electric field intensity?

Explanation: Electric field intensity is measured in both Newtons per Coulomb ($N/C$) from force per charge definition and Volts per meter ($V/m$) from potential gradient.

6. The superposition principle for electric fields is valid because:

Explanation: The linear nature of Coulomb's law and Maxwell's equations ensures that the total field is the direct vector sum of individual electric fields.

7. What is the electric field near a non-conducting infinite plane sheet having surface charge density $\sigma$?

Explanation: For a non-conducting uniform plane sheet, application of Gauss's law gives $E = \frac{\sigma}{2\epsilon_0}$.

8. Why can two electric field lines never intersect each other?

Explanation: If two field lines intersected, the electric field vector at that single point would have two distinct directions, violating the uniqueness of the electric field vector.

9. What is the electric field at a distance $r$ ($r > R$) outside a uniform spherical shell of total charge $Q$ and radius $R$?

Explanation: For points outside a uniform spherical shell ($r > R$), the shell behaves as if all its charge is concentrated at its center, giving $E = \frac{1}{4\$\pi$\epsilon_0} \frac{Q}{r^2}$.

10. What is the mathematical expression for Gauss's Law in electrostatics?

Explanation: Gauss's law states that the surface integral of the electric field over a closed surface equals $\frac{q_{enclosed}}{\epsilon_0}$.

11. An ideal test charge $q_0$ used in defining electric field intensity must be:

Explanation: A test charge is chosen to be infinitesimally small so that it does not disturb or alter the position of the source charge distribution.

12. What is the electric field near the surface of any charged conductor with surface charge density $\sigma$?

Explanation: For any charged conductor, the electric field right at its surface is directed normally and has a magnitude of $E = \frac{\sigma}{\epsilon_0}$.

13. If a point charge $q$ is placed at the center of a cube of side length $a$, what is the total electric flux passing through all six faces of the cube?

Explanation: According to Gauss's Law, the total electric flux through any closed surface enclosing a charge $q$ depends only on the enclosed charge, which is $\$\phi$ = \frac{q}{\epsilon_0}$.

14. Which of the following physical quantities acts as a mediator for electrostatic interactions traveling at the speed of light in vacuum?

Explanation: The electric field acts as a physical mediator for electrostatic interactions, propagating through space at the speed of light in vacuum.

15. What is the electric field due to a point charge $q$ at a distance $r$ in vacuum?

Explanation: From Coulomb's law, the electric field of a point charge is $E = \frac{1}{4\$\pi$\epsilon_0} \frac{q}{r^2}$.

PYQ Patterns

These are pattern-based practice questions. Authentic year labels are only used when verified from a source.

1. Two point charges of $+2\mu C$ and $-8\mu C$ are separated by a distance in air. At what point on the line joining them is the net electric field zero?

Explanation: For the net electric field to be zero, the individual fields must oppose each other. This can only happen outside the charges on the side of smaller magnitude, i.e., closer to the $+2\mu C$ charge.

2. A spherical conductor of radius $R$ has a charge $Q$. The electric field at a distance $r$ ($r < R$) from its center is:

Explanation: The electric field inside any charged conductor (solid or hollow) in electrostatic equilibrium is always zero.

3. If the electric flux entering and leaving a closed surface are $\$\phi_{1}$$ and $\$\phi_{2}$$ respectively, the net charge enclosed by the surface is:

Explanation: Net flux $\Phi_{net} = \$\phi_{2}$ - \$\phi_{1}$ = \frac{q_{enclosed}}{\epsilon_0}$, which implies $q_{enclosed} = (\$\phi_{2}$ - \$\phi_{1}$)\epsilon_0$.

4. An infinite line charge produces a field of $9 \times 10^4 N/C$ at a distance of $2\,cm$. What is the linear charge density $\lambda$?

Explanation: Using $E = \frac{\lambda}{2\$\pi$\epsilon_0 r} \implies \lambda = E \cdot (2\$\pi$\epsilon_0 r) = 2\$\pi$\epsilon_0 \times (9 \times 10^4) \times (0.02) = \frac{9 \times 10^4 \times 0.04}{9 \times 10^9} = 0.2 \times 10^{-6}\,C/m = 0.2\,\mu C/m$.

5. A charged particle of mass $m$ and charge $q$ is suspended in equilibrium in a uniform vertical electric field $E$. What is the magnitude of the electric field?

Explanation: For equilibrium, downward gravitational force $mg$ must balance upward electrostatic force $qE$, hence $qE = mg \implies E = mg/q$.

6. The electric field at the surface of a charged conductor is given by $E = \frac{\sigma}{\epsilon_0}\hat{n}$. What is the direction of this field vector?

Explanation: The electric field at the surface of any charged conductor is always directed perpendicular (normal) to the surface.

Timed Topic Test

Attempt these questions under a ten-minute limit using a separate timer.

1. Calculate the electric field intensity due to a point charge of $4\mu C$ at a distance of $0.2\,m$ in vacuum.

Explanation: $E = \frac{1}{4\$\pi$\epsilon_0} \frac{q}{r^2} = \frac{9 \times 10^9 \times 4 \times 10^{-6}}{(0.2)^2} = \frac{3.6 \times 10^4}{0.04} = 9 \times 10^5\,N/C$? Wait, $3.6 \times 10^4 / 0.04 = 9 \times 10^5$, let's check: $9 \times 10^9 \times 4 \times 10^{-6} = 36000$. $0.2^2 = 0.04$. $36000 / 0.04 = 900,000 = 9 \times 10^5$. Correct option is index 0 ($9 \times 10^5\, N/C$).

2. What happens to the electric field intensity at a point if the distance from a point charge is doubled?

Explanation: Since $E \propto \frac{1}{r^2}$, doubling the distance ($r \to 2r$) reduces the electric field to $\frac{1}{4}$th of its original value (quartered).

3. An infinite plane sheet of charge has a surface charge density of $8.85 \times 10^{-12}\, C/m^2$. What is the electric field near the sheet?

Explanation: For a non-conducting sheet, $E = \frac{\sigma}{2\epsilon_0} = \frac{8.85 \times 10^{-12}}{2 \times 8.854 \times 10^{-12}} \approx 0.5\, N/C$.

4. The surface integral of an electric field over a closed surface depends upon:

Explanation: According to Gauss's law, $\oint E \cdot dA = \frac{q_{enclosed}}{\epsilon_0}$, which depends solely on the net charge enclosed by the Gaussian surface.

5. If the electric field is given by $E = 3i + 4j$, what is the magnitude of the electric field intensity?

Explanation: Magnitude $|E| = \sqrt{3^2 + 4^2} = \sqrt{9 + 16} = \sqrt{25} = 5\, N/C$.

6. Which of the following distributions has an electric field inversely proportional to radial distance $r$ instead of $r^2$?

Explanation: For an infinite line charge, $E = \frac{\lambda}{2\$\pi$\epsilon_0 r}$, showing inverse proportionality to $r$ ($r^1$). Point charges and spherical shells vary as $1/r^2$, while plane sheet field is uniform (independent of $r$).

7. A charge $q$ is placed at the corner of a cube of side $a$. What is the electric flux passing through the entire cube?

Explanation: A corner of a cube is shared by 8 adjacent cubes in a symmetrical lattice. Thus, only $\frac{1}{8}$th of the total flux from the charge passes through the given cube, giving $\frac{q}{8\epsilon_0}$.

8. What is the nature of the force experienced by a positive test charge placed in an electric field pointing in the positive x-direction?

Explanation: A positive test charge experiences a force in the exact same direction as the electric field vector ($F = q_0 E$). Thus, it moves along the positive x-direction.

9. What is the electric field inside a solid conducting sphere carrying a net static charge $Q$?

Explanation: In electrostatic equilibrium, excess charge on a conductor resides entirely on its outer surface, making the electric field everywhere inside the solid conductor zero.

10. Why are electric field lines orthogonal to the surface of a charged conductor in electrostatic equilibrium?

Explanation: If there were a tangential component of the electric field at the surface of a conductor, free electrons would experience a force and move, contradicting the condition of electrostatic equilibrium.
