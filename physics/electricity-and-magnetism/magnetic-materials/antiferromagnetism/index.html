Electricity and Magnetism

Antiferromagnetism

Antiferromagnetism is a magnetic phenomenon in which adjacent atomic magnetic moments spontaneously align in opposite directions, resulting in zero net macroscopic magnetization below a characteristic temperature known as the Néel temperature. Above this temperature, the material transitions to a paramagnetic state.

Scope and prerequisites

TGT core

Qualitative understanding of atomic magnetic dipole alignment, definition of Néel temperature, comparison with ferromagnetism and paramagnetism, and the behavior of magnetic susceptibility above the Néel temperature (Curie-Weiss law modification).

PGT extension

Quantitative treatment using molecular field theory, derivation of magnetic susceptibility below and above the Néel temperature, sub-lattice magnetization behavior, and temperature dependence of susceptibility.

Prerequisites

  • Origin of magnetic moments in atoms and Hund's rules
  • Exchange interaction and Heisenberg Hamiltonian
  • Curie-Weiss law in paramagnetism and ferromagnetism

Microscopic Origin of Antiferromagnetism

  • Adjacent magnetic moments are coupled via an exchange interaction that favors antiparallel alignment due to the overlap of electronic wavefunctions.
  • The exchange integral (J) is negative, distinguishing it from ferromagnetism where J is positive.
  • The crystal lattice can be visualized as two interpenetrating sub-lattices (A and B) with opposite magnetization vectors (MA and MB).

Temperature Dependence and Néel Temperature (T<sub>N</sub>)

  • Below the Néel temperature (TN), thermal agitation is insufficient to disrupt the antiparallel ordering, yielding zero net magnetization in the absence of an external field.
  • As temperature increases toward TN, the sub-lattice magnetizations decrease gradually to zero.
  • For T > TN, the thermal energy completely overcomes the short-range exchange forces, and the material exhibits paramagnetic behavior.

Magnetic Susceptibility Behavior

  • Unlike ferromagnets whose susceptibility follows the Curie-Weiss law with a positive paramagnetic Curie temperature, antiferromagnets exhibit a susceptibility that decreases with decreasing temperature below TN.
  • At $T = T_{N}$, the magnetic susceptibility reaches a sharp maximum.
  • Above TN, the susceptibility follows the modified Curie-Weiss law: $\chi = C / (T + \theta)$, where θ is the paramagnetic Curie temperature.

Comparison with Other Magnetic Materials

  • Antiferromagnetic materials have zero spontaneous macroscopic magnetization, unlike ferromagnets and ferrimagnets.
  • Examples of antiferromagnetic materials include Manganese oxide (MnO), Nickel oxide (NiO), and Chromium (Cr).
  • They show no magnetic hysteresis loops under normal conditions because domain walls do not contribute to net moments without an applied asymmetric field.

Derivations

Molecular Field Theory for Antiferromagnetic Susceptibility (T > T_N)

Conditions: Temperature T greater than the Néel temperature T_N, two-sublattice model

  • Consider two interpenetrating sub-lattices A and B with magnetizations MA and MB under an applied magnetic field B.
  • The effective magnetic field acting on sub-lattice A is given by Beff,A = B - λ MB, where λ is the molecular field coefficient (λ > 0).
  • Similarly, for sub-lattice B, Beff,B = B - λ MA.
  • Using the Curie law for each sub-lattice in the paramagnetic region, MA = [C / (2T)] (B - λ MB) and MB = [C / (2T)] (B - λ MA), where C is the Curie constant for the whole system.
  • Adding and solving these simultaneous equations for total magnetization $M = M_{A} + M_{B} yields the susceptibility \chi = M / B = C / (T + \lambdaC)$.

Result: $\chi = \frac{C}{T + \theta} \text{}$ where } \theta = \lambda C

Susceptibility at Absolute Zero (T = 0 K) for Parallel Field

Conditions: Applied field parallel to the easy axis of antiferromagnetic alignment at T = 0 K

  • At T = 0 K, sub-lattice magnetizations are fully saturated at M0.
  • When a weak magnetic field B is applied parallel to the alignment axis, it tilts both sub-lattice vectors slightly by an angle θ toward the field direction.
  • The component of magnetization along the field is $M = 2 M_{0} \sin(\theta)$.
  • The torque balance per sub-lattice equates the external magnetic torque (M0 B cos(θ)) to the molecular field restoring torque (λ M02 sin(θ)).
  • Solving for sin(θ) in the small angle limit gives sin(θ) = B / (λ M0), leading to $M = 2 M_{0} B / (\lambda M_{0}) = 2 B / \lambda$.

Result: \chi_{\parallel}(0) = \frac{2}{\lambda}

Graphs and experiments

Susceptibility vs Temperature (&chi;-T) Curve for Antiferromagnetic Material

Draw: A curve showing susceptibility increasing linearly with temperature in the paramagnetic region (T > T_N), reaching a sharp cusp or maximum at $T = T_N$, and then decreasing as T approaches 0 K.

Interpret: The sharp peak marks the Néel temperature where antiparallel ordering sets in. Below T_N, thermal fluctuations alter the alignment differently depending on field orientation.

Exam use: Frequently asked in multiple-choice questions to identify the characteristic susceptibility graph of antiferromagnetic substances compared to ferromagnetic and paramagnetic graphs.

Sub-lattice Magnetization vs Temperature

Draw: A plot starting at maximum value M0 at T = 0 K, curving downwards smoothly with increasing temperature, and dropping sharply to zero exactly at $T = T_N$.

Interpret: Represents the thermal disordering of individual sub-lattice alignment vectors, mirroring Brillouin function behavior in ferromagnets.

Exam use: Used to test understanding of order-disorder phase transitions in magnetic lattices.

Neutron Diffraction Experiment Setup

Draw: Schematic of a neutron beam incident on an antiferromagnetic powder sample, producing distinct magnetic Bragg peaks in addition to nuclear scattering peaks.

Interpret: Because neutrons possess a magnetic moment and a wavelength comparable to interatomic spacing, they can probe the alternating magnetic unit cell, which has a lattice parameter twice that of the chemical unit cell.

Exam use: Asked in PGT examinations as the primary experimental proof verifying antiferromagnetic atomic ordering.

Solved numericals

TGT core

An antiferromagnetic material has a paramagnetic Curie temperature $\theta = 60 K$ and a Néel temperature $T_N = 50 K$. Calculate the ratio θ / T_N and state if this is typical.

  • Identify given values: $\theta = 60 K$, $T_N = 50 K$.
  • Compute the ratio: θ / $T_N = 60 / 50 = 1.2$.
  • Conclusion: In many real antiferromagnetic crystals, θ is roughly equal to or slightly greater than T_N due to next-nearest-neighbor interactions.

Answer: 1.2 (Typical of simple antiferromagnets)

PGT extension

The magnetic susceptibility of an antiferromagnetic material above its Néel temperature is given by $\chi = C / (T + \theta)$. If C = 1.5 K and $\theta = 30 K$, calculate the susceptibility at T = 70 K.

  • Identify the formula: $\chi = C / (T + \theta)$.
  • Substitute the given values: C = 1.5 K, T = 70 K, $\theta = 30 K$.
  • Evaluate the denominator: T + $\theta = 70 + 30 = 100 K$.
  • Calculate susceptibility: $\chi = 1.5 / 100 = 0.015$.

Answer: 0.015 (Dimensionless)

PGT extension

Using the molecular field approximation, if the molecular field constant λ for an antiferromagnetic solid is 500 mol/m3, determine the perpendicular susceptibility χ⊥ at T = 0 K.

  • Recall the relation for perpendicular susceptibility at $T = 0 K: \chi_{\perp } = 1 / \lambda$.
  • Substitute $\lambda = 500 mol/m^{3} (assuming standard SI volume normalization$, or unitless convention where λ is dimensionless volumetric parameter; here use standard dimensionless SI susceptibility relations where χ⊥ = 1 / λ).
  • Calculate: χ⊥ = 1 / 500 = 0.002.

Answer: 0.002

Assumptions and limitations

  • The molecular field theory assumes a uniform average magnetic field acting on each sub-lattice, which neglects local spatial fluctuations of spins.
  • It assumes localized magnetic moments (Heisenberg model with fixed localized spins), which fails for itinerant (band) antiferromagnets like chromium.

Common misconceptions

Common error: Antiferromagnetic materials have permanent net macroscopic magnetization.

Correct: Antiferromagnetic materials have zero net macroscopic magnetization below the Néel temperature.

The opposing magnetic moments of adjacent atoms cancel each other out completely.

Common error: Susceptibility increases continuously with temperature in antiferromagnets.

Correct: Susceptibility increases with temperature only below the Néel temperature; above it, susceptibility decreases according to the Curie-Weiss law.

The Néel temperature represents a critical transition point where thermal energy begins to dominate over exchange coupling.

Common error: Antiferromagnetism exhibits strong magnetic hysteresis like ferromagnetism.

Correct: Antiferromagnets show practically linear field dependence and no remanent magnetization or coercivity.

The absence of net moment prevents typical ferromagnetic domain wall pinning hysteresis effects.

Quick revision

  • Antiferromagnetism is characterized by antiparallel alignment of adjacent atomic magnetic moments and zero net magnetization.
  • The transition temperature is called the Néel temperature (T_N).
  • Magnetic susceptibility reaches a maximum at $T = T_N$ and follows $\chi = C / (T + \theta)$ for T > T_N.

Must remember

  • In antiferromagnetic materials, adjacent magnetic moments are coupled via an exchange interaction that favors antiparallel alignment due to the negative sign of the exchange integral ($J < 0$).
  • The critical temperature below which antiferromagnetic ordering occurs is known as the Néel temperature ($T_N$).
  • Below $T_N$, the magnetic susceptibility ($\chi$) decreases with decreasing temperature, reaching zero at $0\text{ K}$ for parallel susceptibility.
  • Above $T_N$, antiferromagnetic materials follow the modified Curie-Weiss law given by $\chi = \frac{C}{T + \theta}$, where $\theta$ is the paramagnetic Curie temperature.
  • Classic examples of antiferromagnetic materials include Manganese oxide ($\text{MnO}$), Nickel oxide ($\text{NiO}$), and Chromium ($\text{Cr}$).
  • Antiferromagnets exhibit zero net spontaneous macroscopic magnetization because the two interpenetrating sub-lattices ($M_A$ and $M_B$) have equal and opposite magnetization vectors.

Glossary

Néel Temperature ($T_N$)The critical temperature above which an antiferromagnetic material becomes paramagnetic.
Exchange Integral ($J$)A quantum mechanical parameter that determines the coupling between adjacent spins; negative for antiferromagnetism.
Paramagnetic Curie Temperature ($\theta$)A constant appearing in the modified Curie-Weiss law for antiferromagnetic materials above $T_N$.
Sub-lattice Magnetization ($M_A, M_B$)The net magnetization vectors of the two interpenetrating sub-lattices into which an antiferromagnetic crystal is divided.
Modified Curie-Weiss LawThe relation $\chi = \frac{C}{T + \theta}$ governing magnetic susceptibility in the paramagnetic region above $T_N$.

1. What is the sign of the exchange integral ($J$) in antiferromagnetic materials?

Explanation: The exchange integral ($J$) is negative ($J < 0$) in antiferromagnetic materials, which favors the antiparallel alignment of adjacent magnetic moments.

2. What is the characteristic temperature below which antiferromagnetic ordering is established?

Explanation: The Néel temperature ($T_N$) is the critical temperature below which thermal agitation is insufficient to disrupt the antiparallel ordering in antiferromagnets.

3. Which of the following equations represents the modified Curie-Weiss law for antiferromagnetic materials above the Néel temperature?

Explanation: Above $T_N$, antiferromagnetic susceptibility follows the modified Curie-Weiss law given by $\chi = \frac{C}{T + \theta}$, where $\theta$ is positive.

4. What is the net macroscopic magnetization of an antiferromagnetic material below the Néel temperature in the absence of an external field?

Explanation: Due to the equal and opposite alignment of the two sub-lattice magnetizations ($M_A + M_B = 0$), the net macroscopic magnetization is zero.

5. Which of the following materials is a classic example of an antiferromagnetic substance?

Explanation: Manganese oxide ($\text{MnO}$), Nickel oxide ($\text{NiO}$), and Chromium ($\text{Cr}$) are well-known examples of antiferromagnetic materials.

6. How does magnetic susceptibility behave at the Néel temperature ($T_N$)?

Explanation: At $T = T_N$, the magnetic susceptibility of an antiferromagnetic material reaches a sharp maximum.

7. What happens to an antiferromagnetic material when heated above its Néel temperature?

Explanation: For $T > T_N$, thermal energy completely overcomes the short-range exchange forces, causing the material to exhibit paramagnetic behavior.

8. Which model is commonly used to describe antiferromagnetism in terms of interpenetrating sub-lattices?

Explanation: The crystal lattice in antiferromagnetism is typically visualized using a two-sublattice model with opposite magnetization vectors $M_A$ and $M_B$.

Explanation: The absence of net macroscopic moments and symmetric antiparallel alignment prevents typical domain wall pinning hysteresis effects under normal conditions.

Explanation: In the two-sublattice molecular field approximation, the Néel temperature is given by $T_N = \lambda C'$.

12. Below the Néel temperature, how does the magnetic susceptibility of an antiferromagnet change with decreasing temperature?

Explanation: Unlike ferromagnets, antiferromagnetic susceptibility decreases with decreasing temperature below $T_N$ as thermal agitation reduces.

1. The magnetic susceptibility of an antiferromagnetic material above its Néel temperature follows which law?

Explanation: Above the Néel temperature $T_N$, antiferromagnets exhibit paramagnetic behavior governed by the modified Curie-Weiss law $\chi = \frac{C}{T + \theta}$ with a positive paramagnetic Curie temperature $\theta$.

2. At the Néel temperature ($T_N$), the magnetic susceptibility of an antiferromagnetic material shows:

Explanation: The magnetic susceptibility curve of an antiferromagnetic material exhibits a distinct sharp peak (maximum) exactly at the Néel temperature $T_N$.

Explanation: The exchange interaction favoring antiparallel alignment arises from the overlap of electronic wavefunctions where the exchange integral $J$ is negative.

4. What is the net spontaneous magnetization of an antiferromagnetic substance below the Néel temperature?

Explanation: Due to complete cancellation between the two interpenetrating sub-lattices ($M_A$ and $M_B$), the net spontaneous macroscopic magnetization is zero.

Explanation: Manganese oxide ($\text{MnO}$) as well as $\text{NiO}$ are standard textbook examples of antiferromagnetic materials.

Timed Topic Test

Attempt these questions under a ten-minute limit using a separate timer.

1. What physical parameter dictates the antiparallel spin coupling in the Heisenberg model of antiferromagnetism?

Explanation: The exchange energy expression $E = -2 \sum J_{ij} \vec{S}_i \cdot \vec{S}_j$ requires $J < 0$ (negative exchange integral) to minimize energy when adjacent spins are antiparallel.

2. For temperatures $T > T_N$, antiferromagnetic materials behave as:

Explanation: Above the Néel temperature, thermal energy overcomes exchange coupling and the material exhibits standard paramagnetic behavior.

3. In the two-sublattice molecular field model, the susceptibility at the Néel temperature is given by:

Explanation: From molecular field theory, the susceptibility evaluated at $T = T_N$ is $\chi(T_N) = \frac{C}{2 T_N + \theta}$.

4. Why do antiferromagnets exhibit linear field dependence of magnetization without hysteresis?

Explanation: Symmetric antiparallel arrangement means domain walls do not undergo typical pinning/hysteresis shifts, resulting in near-linear response and zero remanence.

5. What is the perpendicular susceptibility ($\chi_{\perp}$) at $0\text{ K}$ in terms of the molecular field coefficient $\lambda$?

Explanation: Theoretical derivation shows that perpendicular susceptibility at $0\text{ K}$ is given by $\chi_{\perp} = \frac{1}{\lambda}$.

6. Which of the following statements is a common misconception regarding antiferromagnetism?

Explanation: Believing that antiferromagnets possess permanent net macroscopic magnetization is incorrect; their opposing moments cancel out completely.

7. In the expression for the Curie constant per mole, $C = \frac{N_A \mu_0 \mu_B^2 g^2 S(S+1)}{3 k_B}$, what does $S$ represent?

Explanation: Here, $S$ denotes the spin quantum number of the magnetic ion.

8. How does the sub-lattice magnetization ($M_A$ and $M_B$) vary as temperature increases from absolute zero towards $T_N$?

Explanation: Thermal agitation progressively disrupts the sub-lattice alignment, causing $M_A$ and $M_B$ to decrease gradually until they vanish at $T_N$.

9. Which material from the following list is NOT antiferromagnetic?

Explanation: Iron (Fe) is a classic ferromagnetic material, whereas MnO, NiO, and Cr are antiferromagnetic.

10. What does the paramagnetic Curie temperature $\theta$ signify in the modified Curie-Weiss law for antiferromagnets?

Explanation: The parameter $\theta$ in $\chi = \frac{C}{T + \theta}$ acts as a positive intercept representing the strength of the internal antiparallel molecular field.
