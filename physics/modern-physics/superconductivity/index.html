Elementary Concept of Superconductivity

Superconductivity is a macroscopic quantum phenomenon characterized by the complete disappearance of electrical resistivity and the exclusion of magnetic fields below a critical temperature. It arises from the condensation of conduction electrons into bound pairs known as Cooper pairs.

Phenomenology of Superconductivity

  • Superconductivity is characterized by zero electrical DC resistance when a material is cooled below its characteristic critical temperature Tc.
  • Persistent currents can circulate in a superconducting closed loop indefinitely without any measurable decay, confirming true zero resistance.
  • The transition at Tc is typically a thermodynamic second-order phase transition in the absence of a magnetic field, showing a discontinuity in specific heat.

The Meissner Effect

  • A superconductor acts as a perfect diamagnet, expelling all magnetic flux from its interior upon transitioning to the superconducting state, a phenomenon known as the Meissner effect.
  • The magnetic induction B inside a bulk superconductor is identically zero (B = 0), distinct from a hypothetical perfect conductor which would merely trap existing magnetic flux.
  • The shielding currents flow exclusively within a very thin surface layer characterized by the London penetration depth λ.

Type-I and Type-II Superconductors

  • Type-I superconductors exhibit complete Meissner effect up to a single critical magnetic field Hc, above which they abruptly return to the normal state.
  • Type-II superconductors possess two critical fields: lower critical field Hc1 and upper critical field Hc2, with a mixed state or vortex state existing between them.
  • Type-II materials maintain zero resistance up to much higher magnetic fields and temperatures, making them technically superior for high-field electromagnets.

Microscopic Origin: BCS Theory Essentials

  • At the microscopic level, superconductivity is explained by the Bardeen-Cooper-Schrieffer (BCS) theory involving the pairing of electrons mediated by lattice vibrations (phonons).
  • An attractive interaction between electrons via virtual phonon exchange overcomes Coulomb repulsion, forming weakly bound composite bosons called Cooper pairs.
  • An energy gap 2Δ opens up at the Fermi surface, requiring a finite thermal or optical energy to break the Cooper pairs and create quasi-particle excitations.

Derivations

London Equations and Magnetic Field Penetration

Conditions: Isotropic, homogeneous superconductor subjected to a static weak magnetic field

  • Assume superconducting electrons move with velocity vs under electric field E without scattering, giving m(dvs/dt) = eE.
  • Express the superconducting current density as Js = ns e vs, leading to the first London equation: dJs/d$t = (n_{s} e_{2}/m)E$.
  • Take the curl of the first London equation and use Maxwell's equation curl $E = -(\partial B/\partial t)$ to obtain d(curl Js)/d$t = -(n_{s} e_{2}/m)(\partial B/\partial t)$.
  • Integrate with respect to time and set integration constants to zero to yield the second London equation: curl Js = -(ns e2/m)B.
  • Combine with Ampere's law (curl $B = \mu_{0} J_{s})$ to arrive at the vector Laplacian relation ∇2$B = B / \lambda^{2}$.

Result: B(x) = B(0) exp(-x / \lambda), where $\lambda = \sqrt{\frac{m}{\mu_0 n_s e^2}}$

Critical Magnetic Field Temperature Dependence

Conditions: Thermodynamic derivation using free energy difference between normal and superconducting states

  • Write the difference in Helmholtz free energy between the normal state (Gn) and superconducting state (Gs) in an applied magnetic field H as Gn(T) - Gs(T, H) = (μ0 Hc2) / 2.
  • Use the Gibbs-Helmholtz thermodynamic relation and entropy differences to approximate the temperature dependence of the critical field.
  • Apply the empirical parabolic law observed experimentally for elemental superconductors.

Result: $H_c(T) = H_c(0) \left[ 1 - \left(\frac{T}{T_c}\right)^2 \right]$

Resistance vs Temperature Graph (Onnes Effect)

Draw: A plot of electrical resistance R against absolute temperature T, showing a sharp, vertical drop to absolute zero at T_c for a superconductor compared to a linear residual resistance drop for a normal metal.

Interpret: Demonstrates the sudden loss of electrical resistivity at the critical temperature T_c.

Magnetization vs Applied Magnetic Field (Type-I vs Type-II)

Draw: Two plots: Type-I showing linear diamagnetic increase up to H_c with sudden drop to zero magnetization; Type-II showing linear diamagnetic increase up to H_c1, mixed state up to H_c2, and then zero magnetization.

Interpret: Differentiates complete magnetic flux exclusion (Meissner effect) in Type-I from partial vortex penetration in Type-II superconductors.

Critical Field Curve (H_c vs T)

Draw: A parabolic curve starting at H_c(0) on the vertical axis and intersecting the horizontal temperature axis at $T = T_c$.

Interpret: Shows that superconductivity is destroyed if either temperature exceeds T_c or magnetic field exceeds H_c(T).

Exam use: Numerical problems involving calculation of H_c at given temperatures.

Solved numericals

TGT core

The critical temperature of lead is 7.2 K. If the critical magnetic field at 0 K is 8.0 x 10^4 A/m, calculate the critical magnetic field at 5.0 K.

  • Identify given parameters: $T_c = 7.2 K$, $H_c(0) = 8.0 x 10^4 A/m$, T = 5.0 K.
  • Use the empirical critical field formula: $H_c(T) = H_c(0) [1 - (T/T_c)^2]$.
  • Substitute the values: H_c(5.0) = 8.0 x 10^4 [1 - (5.0 / 7.2)^2].
  • Calculate the ratio: (5.0 / 7.2)^2 = 25 / 51.84 = 0.4822$.
  • Evaluate H_c(5.0) = 8.0 x 10^4 (1 - 0.4822) = 8.0 x 10^4 (0.5178) = 4.14 x 10^4 A/m.

Answer: 4.14 \times 10^4 \text{ A/m}

PGT extension

A superconducting material has a London penetration depth of 50 nm at T = 0 K. Calculate the penetration depth at a temperature where (T/$T_c)^2 = 0.19$.

  • Recall the empirical Gorter-Casimir temperature dependence of penetration depth: \lambda(T) = \lambda(0) / \sqrt{1 - (T/T_c)^4}. Note: For simple estimations near low T, often approximated or used via \lambda(T) = \lambda(0) [1 - (T/T_c)^4]^{-1/2}.
  • Substitute \lambda(0) = 50 nm and (T/$T_c)^2 = 0.19$, so (T/$T_c)^4 = (0.19)^2 = 0.0361$.
  • Evaluate the denominator term: 1 - 0.0361 = 0.9639.
  • Calculate \sqrt{0.9639} = 0.9818.
  • Compute \lambda(T) = 50 / 0.9818 = 50.92 nm.

Answer: 50.92 \text{ nm}

PGT extension

Calculate the superconducting electron density n_s in a specimen given its London penetration depth $\lambda = 3.5 \times 10^{-8} m$, mass of electron $m = 9.11 \times 10^{-31} kg$, and permeability of free space \m$u_0 = 4\pi \times 10^{-7} H/m$.

  • Start with the London penetration depth formula: $\lambda = \sqrt{m / (\mu_0 n_s e^2)}$.
  • Rearrange to solve for n_s: $n_s = m / (\mu_0 e^2 \lambda^2)$.
  • Substitute the values: $m = 9.11 \times 10^{-31}$, \m$u_0 = 4\pi \times 10^{-7}$, $e = 1.6 \times 10^{-19}$, $\lambda = 3.5 \times 10^{-8}$.
  • Calculate $e^2 = 2.56 \times 10^{-38}$, \lambd$a^2 = 1.225 \times 10^{-15}$.
  • Denominato$r = (4 \times 3.1416 \times 10^{-7}) \times (2.56 \times 10^{-38}) \times (1.225 \times 10^{-15}) = 3.929 \times 10^{-59}$.
  • Numerato$r = 9.11 \times 10^{-31}$.
  • $n_s = (9.11 \times 10^{-31}) / (3.929 \times 10^{-59}) = 2.32 \times 10^{28} \text{ m}^{-3}$.

Answer: 2.32 \times 10^{28} \text{ m}^{-3}

Assumptions and limitations

  • The London theory assumes local electrodynamics where current density is proportional to the vector potential at the exact same point.
  • BCS theory assumes a weak-coupling approximation and isotropic Fermi surfaces, which fails for strongly coupled or high-T_c cuprate superconductors.

Common misconceptions

Common error: A superconductor is simply a perfect conductor with zero resistance.

Correct: A superconductor is a thermodynamic state characterized by zero resistance AND perfect diamagnetism (Meissner effect).

A perfect conductor would trap existing magnetic flux due to Lenz's law, whereas a superconductor expels all magnetic flux regardless of historical cooling path.

Common error: Superconductivity occurs only at temperatures near absolute zero.

Correct: Many high-temperature superconductors (like cuprates) exhibit superconductivity at temperatures well above liquid nitrogen temperature (77 K).

Discovery of high-T_c superconductors shifted the operational limits far beyond liquid helium temperatures.

Quick revision

  • Superconductivity involves zero resistivity and complete magnetic flux expulsion (Meissner effect) below T_c.
  • London Penetration Depth: $\lambda = \sqrt{\frac{m}{\mu_0 n_s e^2}}$
  • First London Equation: \frac{\partial J_s}{\partial t} = \frac{n_s e^2}{m} E

Must remember

  • Superconductivity is characterized by zero electrical DC resistance and complete diamagnetism ($B = 0$, Meissner effect) below a critical temperature $T_c$.
  • The transition at $T_c$ in the absence of a magnetic field is typically a thermodynamic second-order phase transition.
  • Type-I superconductors exhibit a complete Meissner effect up to a single critical field $H_c$, while Type-II superconductors have two critical fields ($H_{c1}$ and $H_{c2}$) with a mixed vortex state in between.
  • BCS theory explains superconductivity via lattice-vibration (phonon) mediated pairing of electrons into weakly bound Cooper pairs.
  • The magnetic flux inside a superconductor is quantized in units of $\Phi_0 = \frac{h}{2e}$.

Glossary

Critical Temperature ($T_c$)The characteristic temperature below which a material transitions into the superconducting state.
Meissner EffectThe complete expulsion of magnetic flux from the interior of a superconductor as it transitions to the superconducting state.
London Penetration Depth ($\lambda$)The characteristic length over which a magnetic field decays inside the surface of a superconductor.
Cooper PairA bound pair of electrons formed through attractive phonon-mediated interaction in superconductors, acting as a composite boson.
Isotope EffectThe dependence of the critical temperature on the isotopic mass of the superconducting element, given by $T_c M^\alpha = \text{constant}$.

Practice Quiz

Choose an option to reveal the explanation.

1. What is the primary characteristic of a material in its superconducting state?

Explanation: Superconductivity is fundamentally characterized by zero electrical DC resistance and complete expulsion of magnetic fields (Meissner effect / perfect diamagnetism).

2. The transition from the normal state to the superconducting state in zero magnetic field is typically a:

Explanation: In the absence of a magnetic field, the superconducting transition at $T_c$ is a thermodynamic second-order phase transition characterized by a discontinuity in specific heat.

3. What is the value of the magnetic induction $B$ inside a bulk superconductor?

Explanation: Due to the Meissner effect, a bulk superconductor completely expels magnetic flux, resulting in an internal magnetic induction $B = 0$.

4. Shielding currents in a superconductor flow exclusively within a very thin surface layer characterized by which parameter?

Explanation: The screening currents that generate the Meissner effect flow within a thin surface layer whose thickness is defined by the London penetration depth $\lambda$.

5. Type-I superconductors are characterized by:

Explanation: Type-I superconductors exhibit a complete Meissner effect up to a single critical magnetic field $H_c$, above which they abruptly return to the normal state.

6. What exists between the lower critical field $H_{c1}$ and upper critical field $H_{c2}$ in Type-II superconductors?

Explanation: Type-II superconductors possess a mixed state (or Shubnikov phase / vortex state) between $H_{c1}$ and $H_{c2}$ where magnetic flux penetrates in quantized flux lines.

7. According to the BCS theory, what mediates the attractive interaction between electrons to form Cooper pairs?

Explanation: In BCS theory, superconductivity arises from electron pairing mediated by the exchange of virtual phonons (lattice vibrations).

8. What is the formula for the London Penetration Depth $\lambda$?

Explanation: The London penetration depth is derived as $\lambda = \sqrt{\frac{m}{\mu_0 n_s e^2}}$, where $m$ is electron mass, $n_s$ is superconducting electron density, and $e$ is electronic charge.

9. The isotope effect relation in conventional superconductors is given by $T_c M^\alpha = \text{constant}$. What does $M$ represent?

Explanation: In the relation $T_c M^\alpha = \text{constant}$, $M$ represents the isotopic mass of the element, proving the lattice vibration involvement.

10. What is the relation for the BCS energy gap at absolute zero temperature in the weak-coupling limit?

Explanation: In the weak-coupling limit of BCS theory, the zero-temperature energy gap is related to the critical temperature by $2\Delta(0) = 3.52 k_B T_c$.

11. What is the quantum of magnetic flux $\Phi_0$ expressed in terms of fundamental constants?

Explanation: Magnetic flux quantization in superconducting rings dictates that the flux quantum is $\Phi_0 = \frac{h}{2e}$, reflecting the charge $2e$ of Cooper pairs.

12. How does the critical magnetic field $H_c(T)$ vary with temperature according to the empirical relation?

Explanation: The empirical relation for the temperature dependence of the critical field is $H_c(T) = H_c(0) \left( 1 - \frac{T^2}{T_c^2} \right)$.

13. What is the fundamental misconception corrected by the definition of a superconductor versus a perfect conductor?

Explanation: A perfect conductor maintains constant magnetic flux due to Lenz's law ($E=0, \frac{dB}{dt}=0$), whereas a true superconductor expels all magnetic flux ($B=0$) regardless of cooling history.

14. What does the Second London Equation ($\nabla \times J_s = -\frac{n_s e^2}{m} B$) mathematically describe?

Explanation: The Second London equation, when combined with Maxwell's equations, leads to $\nabla^2 B = \frac{B}{\lambda^2}$, showing exponential decay of magnetic fields inside a superconductor.

15. Which type of superconductors are technically superior for fabricating high-field electromagnets?

Explanation: Type-II superconductors maintain zero resistance up to very high upper critical fields ($H_{c2}$), making them ideal for high-field superconducting magnets.

PYQ Patterns

These are pattern-based practice questions. Authentic year labels are only used when verified from a source.

1. The critical temperature of mercury is approximately $4.2 \text{ K}$. If the critical magnetic field at $0 \text{ K}$ is $H_c(0)$, what is the critical field at $T = 2.1 \text{ K}$?

Explanation: Using $H_c(T) = H_c(0) \left( 1 - \frac{T^2}{T_c^2} \right)$, with $T = \frac{T_c}{2}$, we get $H_c(T) = H_c(0) \left( 1 - \frac{(T_c/2)^2}{T_c^2} \right) = H_c(0) \left( 1 - \frac{1}{4} \right) = 0.75 H_c(0)$.

2. Which of the following statements correctly distinguishes between Type-I and Type-II superconductors regarding magnetic response?

Explanation: Type-II superconductors exhibit a mixed/vortex state between lower ($H_{c1}$) and upper ($H_{c2}$) critical fields, whereas Type-I exhibits a complete Meissner effect up to a single $H_c$.

3. In BCS theory, what is the nature of the pairing mechanism responsible for superconductivity?

Explanation: BCS theory establishes that superconductivity is caused by an attractive interaction between electrons mediated by the exchange of virtual phonons.

4. The London penetration depth $\lambda$ varies with superconducting electron density $n_s$ as:

Explanation: From the formula $\lambda = \sqrt{\frac{m}{\mu_0 n_s e^2}}$, the penetration depth is inversely proportional to the square root of the superconducting electron density ($n_s^{-1/2}$).

5. The isotope effect in superconductors gives experimental evidence for:

Explanation: The dependence of $T_c$ on the isotopic mass ($T_c M^\alpha = \text{constant}$) confirms that lattice vibrations (phonons) play a fundamental role in superconductivity.

6. What is the difference in Gibbs free energy density between the normal state and the superconducting state at temperature $T$ in zero magnetic field?

Explanation: The thermodynamic energy balance equates the stabilization energy of the superconducting state to the magnetic energy needed to restore the normal state: $G_n(T) - G_s(T,0) = \frac{1}{2} \mu_0 H_c^2(T)$.

Timed Topic Test

Attempt these questions under a ten-minute limit using a separate timer.

1. What thermodynamic phase transition order best describes the superconducting transition in zero magnetic field?

Explanation: In the absence of a magnetic field, the transition at $T_c$ is a classic thermodynamic second-order phase transition.

2. Which of the following equations represents the First London Equation?

Explanation: The First London equation relates the time derivative of superconducting current density to the electric field: $\frac{\partial J_s}{\partial t} = \frac{n_s e^2}{m} E$.

3. In a Type-II superconductor, what occurs when the external magnetic field exceeds the upper critical field $H_{c2}$?

Explanation: Above the upper critical field $H_{c2}$, the magnetic flux penetrates completely, destroying superconductivity and returning the material to the normal state.

4. What is the physical significance of the energy gap $2\Delta$ in BCS theory?

Explanation: An energy gap $2\Delta$ opens up at the Fermi surface, representing the minimum energy required to break up a Cooper pair into unbound electronic excitations (quasi-particles).

5. If an isotope of a superconducting element is replaced by a heavier isotope, how does the critical temperature $T_c$ change?

Explanation: According to the isotope effect relation $T_c M^\alpha = \text{constant}$, $T_c$ varies inversely with isotopic mass $M$, so a heavier isotope results in a lower $T_c$.

6. Why is a superconductor not merely a 'perfect conductor'?

Explanation: A perfect conductor maintains trapped magnetic flux due to Lenz's law when cooled in a magnetic field, whereas a superconductor expels all magnetic flux (Meissner effect).

7. What are the units of the London Penetration Depth $\lambda$?

Explanation: The London penetration depth represents a spatial decay length, and its SI unit is meters (m).

8. What is the typical value of the isotope coefficient $\alpha$ in conventional BCS superconductors?

Explanation: In simple phonon-mediated conventional superconductors, the isotope coefficient $\alpha$ is approximately 0.5.

9. Which of the following materials is most likely to exhibit Type-I superconductivity?

Explanation: Type-I superconductivity is predominantly observed in pure, soft elemental metals like mercury, lead, and tin.

10. What happens to the Cooper pair binding energy as the temperature approaches the critical temperature $T_c$?

Explanation: As temperature increases towards $T_c$, thermal energy breaks more Cooper pairs, and the superconducting energy gap $\Delta(T)$ decreases continuously to zero at $T_c$.
