Semiconductor Physics and Electronic Devices

Semiconductors are solid-state materials whose electrical conductivity lies between that of conductors and insulators, typically ranging from 10<sup>-5</sup> to 10<sup>6</sup> S m<sup>-1</sup>. Their properties are governed by energy band structures with a narrow forbidden energy gap and can be modulated through thermal excitation or controlled chemical doping.

Prerequisites

  • Bohr's theory of hydrogen atom and discrete energy levels
  • Pauli's exclusion principle and electron spin
  • Electrostatics and current electricity

Energy Band Structure in Solids

  • According to Bloch's theorem and the Kronig-Penney model, periodic potential of a crystal lattice splits discrete atomic energy levels into continuous energy bands.
  • The valence band is completely or partially filled with electrons at absolute zero, while the conduction band is essentially empty.
  • The energy separation between the valence band maximum and conduction band minimum is the forbidden energy gap (Eg).

Intrinsic and Extrinsic Semiconductors

  • Pure semiconductors (e.g., Germanium, Silicon) are intrinsic, where thermal generation creates equal numbers of electrons in the conduction band and holes in the valence band (ni = pi).
  • Doping an intrinsic semiconductor with group V elements (pentavalent, e.g., Phosphorus) yields n-type semiconductors with electrons as majority carriers.
  • Doping with group III elements (trivalent, e.g., Boron) yields p-type semiconductors with holes as majority carriers, governed by the mass-action law n * p = ni2.

Carrier Transport Mechanisms

  • Drift current arises from the directed motion of charge carriers under the influence of an applied electric field, quantified by carrier mobilities (μn and μp).
  • Diffusion current occurs due to spatial concentration gradients of charge carriers, causing net movement from high to low concentration regions.
  • The total current density in a semiconductor is the sum of drift and diffusion current densities for both electrons and holes.

PN Junction Diode Operation

  • At the interface of p-type and n-type regions, carrier diffusion sets up a depletion region devoid of mobile charge carriers, creating an internal built-in electric field (V0).
  • Forward biasing reduces the potential barrier, facilitating large injection current; reverse biasing widens the depletion region, permitting only a minute reverse saturation current.
  • The diode current follows the Shockley diode equation, demonstrating exponential dependence on forward bias voltage.

Derivations

Law of Mass Action

Conditions: Thermal equilibrium in a homogeneous semiconductor

  • Write the thermal generation rate of electron-hole pairs as Rth = α ni2, where α is the recombination probability constant.
  • Write the recombination rate as Rrec = α n p, where n and p are the total concentrations of electrons and holes under non-equilibrium or equilibrium conditions.
  • At thermal equilibrium, the rate of thermal generation equals the rate of recombination: α ni2 = α n p.
  • Cancel the common proportionality constant α to obtain the final relation.

Result: n * p = n_i^2

Conductivity of an Intrinsic Semiconductor

Conditions: Uniform temperature and low electric field

  • Express the total electrical conductivity as σ = e (n μ_n + p μ_p), where e is the elementary charge, μ_n and μ_p are electron and hole mobilities.
  • For an intrinsic semiconductor, substitute n = p = n_i.
  • Factor out the intrinsic carrier concentration n_i from the expression.

Result: \sigma = e n_i (\mu_n + \mu_p)

Solved numericals

TGT core

In a pure silicon crystal at 300 K, the number density of conduction electrons is 1.5 x 10^16 m^-3. If the crystal is doped with phosphorus at a concentration of 1.0 x 10^20 m^-3, calculate the new hole concentration.

  • Identify given values: n_i = 1.5 x 10^16 m^-3, N_D = 1.0 x 10^20 m^-3 (n-type doping).
  • Apply the mass-action law: n * p = n_i^2.
  • Since donor concentration is much larger than intrinsic concentration, electron concentration n ≈ N_D = 1.0 x 10^20 m^-3.
  • Calculate hole concentration: p = n_i^2 / N_D = (1.5 x 10^16)^2 / (1.0 x 10^20).
  • p = (2.25 x 10^32) / (1.0 x 10^20) = 2.25 x 10^12 m^-3.

Answer: 2.25 x 10^12 m^-3

PGT extension

A germanium sample is doped with donor impurity to a concentration of 10^21 m^-3. Given the intrinsic carrier concentration for germanium at 300 K is 2.5 x 10^19 m^-3, and mobilities of electrons and holes are 0.39 m^2 V^-1 s^-1 and 0.19 m^2 V^-1 s^-1 respectively, calculate the electrical conductivity of the doped sample.

  • Identify parameters: N_D = 10^21 m^-3, n_i = 2.5 x 10^19 m^-3, μ_n = 0.39, μ_p = 0.19, e = 1.6 x 10^-19 C.
  • Since N_D >> n_i, majority carrier electron concentration n ≈ N_D = 10^21 m^-3.
  • Minority carrier hole concentration p = n_i^2 / N_D = (2.5 x 10^19)^2 / 10^21 = (6.25 x 10^38) / 10^21 = 6.25 x 10^17 m^-3.
  • Calculate conductivity using σ = e (n μ_n + p μ_p).
  • σ = (1.6 x 10^-19) * [ (10^21 * 0.39) + (6.25 x 10^17 * 0.19) ].
  • σ ≈ (1.6 x 10^-19) * (3.9 x 10^20) = 62.4 S m^-1.

Answer: 62.4 S m^-1

PGT extension

In a transistor, the collector current is 9.8 mA and the emitter current is 10 mA. Find the base current and the current amplification factor β (beta).

  • Given: I_C = 9.8 mA, I_E = 10 mA.
  • Use transistor current relation: I_E = I_B + I_C.
  • Calculate base current: I_B = I_E - I_C = 10 mA - 9.8 mA = 0.2 mA.
  • Calculate current amplification factor β = I_C / I_B.
  • β = 9.8 mA / 0.2 mA = 49.

Answer: I_B = 0.2 mA, β = 49

Assumptions and limitations

  • The Maxwell-Boltzmann statistics approximation is often used for non-degenerate semiconductors when carrier densities are much less than the effective density of states.
  • Impurities are assumed to be 100% ionized at room temperature.
  • The band gap is assumed constant with temperature within narrow operating ranges.

Common misconceptions

Common error: Extrinsic semiconductors carry a net electric charge due to doping.

Correct: Extrinsic semiconductors are electrically neutral overall.

Added donor or trivalent impurity atoms are neutral prior to lattice incorporation; doping introduces localized energy states without altering overall net charge.

Common error: Holes actually move through the crystal lattice like physical positive particles.

Correct: A hole represents the absence of an electron in the valence band, and its movement is an abstraction caused by successive jumping of valence electrons.

Physical mass transport is performed by electrons moving in the opposite direction.

Quick revision

  • Intrinsic carrier concentration satisfies n * p = n_i^2.
  • n-type doping uses pentavalent impurities; p-type uses trivalent impurities.
  • Forward bias decreases depletion width and barrier potential, whereas reverse bias increases both.

Must remember

  • According to the Kronig-Penney model and Bloch's theorem, periodic crystal potentials split discrete atomic levels into continuous energy bands separated by forbidden energy gaps ($E_g$).
  • In intrinsic semiconductors at thermal equilibrium, the concentration of conduction electrons equals that of holes ($n_i = p_i$).
  • The mass-action law states that the product of electron and hole concentrations is constant at a given temperature: $n \cdot p = n_i^2$.
  • Total current density in a semiconductor is the linear combination of drift current density and diffusion current density for both electrons and holes: $J = J_{drift} + J_{diff}$.
  • Extrinsic semiconductors remain electrically neutral overall despite doping with pentavalent (n-type) or trivalent (p-type) impurity atoms.
  • A hole is not a physical positive particle, but represents the absence of an electron in the valence band moving via successive electron jumps.
  • The Shockley diode equation describes the exponential forward-bias characteristic: $I = I_0 \left(\exp\frac{e V}{\eta k_B T} - 1\right)$.

Glossary

Forbidden Energy Gap ($E_g$)The energy separation between the valence band maximum and conduction band minimum where no electron quantum states can exist.
Mass-Action LawThe relation $n \cdot p = n_i^2$ governing thermal equilibrium carrier concentrations in semiconductors.
Drift CurrentDirected motion of charge carriers resulting from an applied electric field, quantified by mobilities $\mu_n$ and $\mu_p$.
Diffusion CurrentNet movement of charge carriers driven by spatial concentration gradients from high to low concentration regions.
Fermi-Dirac Distribution ($f(E)$)The probability that an available energy state at energy $E$ is occupied by an electron at absolute temperature $T$.

Practice Quiz

Choose an option to reveal the explanation.

1. According to the Kronig-Penney model, periodic potential in a crystal lattice causes discrete atomic energy levels to form:

Explanation: Bloch's theorem and the Kronig-Penney model demonstrate that the periodic potential of a crystal lattice splits discrete atomic energy levels into continuous allowed energy bands separated by forbidden gaps.

2. At absolute zero temperature ($T = 0\text{ K}$), the valence band in an intrinsic semiconductor is:

Explanation: At absolute zero, valence electrons completely fill the valence band, whereas the conduction band remains entirely empty.

3. Which impurity atom type is added to intrinsic silicon to yield an n-type semiconductor?

Explanation: Doping an intrinsic semiconductor with group V pentavalent elements provides extra electrons, producing an n-type semiconductor with electrons as majority carriers.

4. The mass-action law for semiconductors in thermal equilibrium is expressed as:

Explanation: The mass-action law states that the product of free electron concentration ($n$) and hole concentration ($p$) equals the square of the intrinsic carrier concentration ($n_i^2$) at a given temperature.

5. Drift current in a semiconductor arises due to:

Explanation: Drift current is driven by an applied electric field imparting a net drift velocity to charge carriers, quantified by mobilities $\mu_n$ and $\mu_p$.

6. Diffusion current occurs as a direct consequence of:

Explanation: Diffusion current is driven by spatial concentration gradients ($\frac{dn}{dx}$ or $\frac{dp}{dx}$), causing net carrier transport from high to low concentration regions.

7. The electrical conductivity $\sigma$ of a homogeneous semiconductor is given by:

Explanation: Electrical conductivity accounts for contributions from both electron and hole carriers: $\sigma = e(n\mu_n + p\mu_p)$.

8. What is the physical nature of a 'hole' in the valence band of a semiconductor?

Explanation: A hole is not a distinct elementary particle, but represents the absence of an electron; its apparent movement results from successive valence electrons filling vacant states.

9. Extrinsic semiconductors doped with donor or acceptor impurities:

Explanation: Extrinsic semiconductors are electrically neutral overall because implanted impurity atoms are neutral prior to incorporation and introduce localized energy states without altering overall charge balance.

10. The Fermi-Dirac distribution function $f(E)$ represents:

Explanation: The Fermi-Dirac distribution $f(E) = \frac{1}{1 + \exp(\frac{E - E_F}{k_B T})}$ gives the probability that an available energy state at energy $E$ is occupied by an electron.

11. In a PN junction diode, forward biasing results in:

Explanation: Forward biasing opposes and reduces the built-in potential barrier, narrowing the depletion region and facilitating large injection current.

12. The Shockley diode equation describes diode current as an exponential function of:

Explanation: The Shockley equation $I = $I_{0}$ \left(\exp\frac{e V}{\eta k_B T} - 1\right)$ shows exponential dependence on the applied forward voltage $V$.

13. The built-in potential $$V_{0}$$ of an abrupt PN junction depends logarithmically on:

Explanation: The built-in potential is expressed as $$V_{0}$ = \frac{k_B T}{e} \ln\left(\frac{N_A N_D}{n_i^2}\right)$, scaling with dopant concentrations $N_A$ and $N_D$.

14. For a bipolar junction transistor operating in the active mode, the emitter current $$I_{E}$$ satisfies:

Explanation: In any transistor configuration, Kirchhoff's current law at the terminals dictates that emitter current equals the sum of base and collector currents: $$I_{E}$ = $I_{B}$ + $I_{C}$$.

15. How does the depletion layer width $W$ of a PN junction change under reverse biasing?

Explanation: Reverse biasing reinforces the built-in potential barrier, pulling mobile carriers away from the interface and causing the depletion layer width $W$ to expand.

PYQ Patterns

These are pattern-based practice questions. Authentic year labels are only used when verified from a source.

1. In an intrinsic semiconductor at room temperature, if the thermal generation rate increases, what happens to the intrinsic carrier concentration $n_i$ and forbidden gap $E_g$?

Explanation: Raising the temperature increases thermal agitation, elevating more electrons across the bandgap, thus increasing $n_i$, while lattice expansion slightly narrows the forbidden energy gap $E_g$.

2. Calculate the electrical conductivity of an intrinsic silicon sample at room temperature given $n_i = 1.5 \times 10^{16}\text{ m}^{-3}$, $\mu_n = 0.13\text{ m}^2\text{V}^{-1}\text{s}^{-1}$, and $\mu_p = 0.05\text{ m}^2\text{V}^{-1}\text{s}^{-1}$ ($e = 1.6 \times 10^{-19}\text{ C}$):

Explanation: Using $\sigma = e n_i (\mu_n + \mu_p) = (1.6 \times 10^{-19}) (1.5 \times 10^{16}) (0.13 + 0.05) = (2.4 \times 10^{-3}) (0.18) = 4.32 \times 10^{-4}\text{ S m}^{-1}$.

3. In a p-type semiconductor, if the acceptor concentration $N_A = 10^{21}\text{ m}^{-3}$ and intrinsic concentration $n_i = 10^{16}\text{ m}^{-3}$, the minority carrier electron concentration $n$ is:

Explanation: From the mass-action law $n \cdot p = n_i^2$, with $p \approx N_A$, we get $n = \frac{n_i^2}{N_A} = \frac{(10^{16})^2}{10^{21}} = \frac{10^{32}}{10^{21}} = 10^{11}\text{ m}^{-3}$.

4. Which of the following transport mechanisms is responsible for minority carrier injection across a forward-biased PN junction?

Explanation: Forward biasing lowers the potential barrier, allowing majority carriers from each side to diffuse across the junction, becoming minority carriers on the opposing side (minority carrier injection).

5. The depletion layer width $W$ of a PN junction under zero or reverse bias is proportional to:

Explanation: The formula $W = \sqrt{\frac{2 \epsilon_s ($V_{0}$ - $V_{R}$)}{e} \left(\frac{N_A + N_D}{N_A N_D}\right)}$ shows that the depletion width varies directly as the square root of the net reverse voltage.

6. According to the Fermi-Dirac distribution, when the Fermi energy level $E_F$ coincides exactly with energy $E$ at absolute temperature $T > 0\text{ K}$, the probability of occupation $f(E)$ is:

Explanation: When $E = E_F$, the exponent $\frac{E - E_F}{k_B T} = 0$, giving $f(E) = \frac{1}{1 + e^0} = \frac{1}{1 + 1} = 0.5$ (exactly 50% probability).

Timed Topic Test

Attempt these questions under a ten-minute limit using a separate timer.

1. The forbidden energy gap $E_g$ is largest in which of the following materials?

Explanation: Diamond is a wide-bandgap insulator with $E_g \approx 5.5\text{ eV}$, significantly larger than Silicon ($~1.1\text{ eV}$) or Germanium ($~0.7\text{ eV}$). Metals have zero bandgap.

2. In an extrinsic semiconductor, if the temperature is raised significantly, the semiconductor eventually behaves like:

Explanation: At very high temperatures, thermally generated intrinsic carrier concentration vastly exceeds the fixed dopant concentration, causing the semiconductor to exhibit intrinsic behavior.

3. Diffusion current density $J_{diff}$ for electrons in the presence of a concentration gradient is proportional to:

Explanation: Fick's law dictates that electron diffusion current density is proportional to the spatial concentration gradient: $J_{diff, n} = e D_n \frac{dn}{dx}$.

4. What is the primary effect of increasing donor concentration $N_D$ on the Fermi level position in an n-type semiconductor?

Explanation: Increasing donor concentration increases electron occupancy, shifting the Fermi energy level upward toward the conduction band edge.

5. In the Shockley diode equation, if the ideality factor $\eta = 1$ at room temperature ($T = 300\text{ K}$), the thermal voltage $\frac{k_B T}{e}$ is approximately:

Explanation: The thermal voltage $$V_{T}$ = \frac{k_B T}{e} \approx \frac{(1.38 \times 10^{-23}\text{ J/K}) (300\text{ K})}{1.6 \times 10^{-19}\text{ C}} \approx 0.0259\text{ V} \approx 26\text{ mV}$.

6. Under heavy reverse bias, a PN junction diode primarily permits:

Explanation: Reverse biasing blocks majority carrier flow, permitting only a very small reverse saturation current ($$I_{0}$$) carried by thermally generated minority carriers.

7. The unit of the diffusion coefficient $D_n$ or $D_p$ is:

Explanation: Diffusion coefficients have dimensions of area per unit time, expressed in SI units as $\text{m}^2\text{ s}^{-1}$.

8. Einstein's relation connecting carrier mobility $\mu$ and diffusion coefficient $D$ in semiconductors is given by:

Explanation: Einstein's relation establishes that $\frac{D_n}{\mu_n} = \frac{D_p}{\mu_p} = \frac{k_B T}{e}$.

9. Which of the following statements about extrinsic semiconductors is correct?

Explanation: In an n-type semiconductor, electron concentration exceeds hole concentration, placing the Fermi level closer to the conduction band and above the intrinsic Fermi level.

10. In a bipolar junction transistor, why is the base region intentionally kept very thin and lightly doped?

Explanation: Keeping the base region extremely thin and lightly doped minimizes recombination of injected carriers in the base, thereby maximizing common-base current gain ($\$\alpha$$) and collector current.
