Energy Bands in Solids

Energy bands arise from the splitting of discrete atomic energy levels into continuous bands of allowed energies due to the strong interatomic interactions in a crystal lattice. They provide the fundamental theoretical framework for classifying solids into conductors, semiconductors, and insulators.

Scope and prerequisites

TGT core

Qualitative classification of solids (conductors, semiconductors, insulators) using valence and conduction bands, forbidden energy gaps, and basic effect of temperature.

PGT extension

Kronig-Penney model (periodic potential), effective mass of electrons, Bloch's theorem, Fermi-Dirac distribution function, and intrinsic/extrinsic carrier concentration calculations.

Prerequisites

  • Bohr's theory of hydrogen atom and atomic energy levels
  • Pauli's exclusion principle and electron spin
  • Wave-particle duality and de Broglie wavelength

Origin of Energy Bands

  • When isolated atoms are brought together to form a crystal, outer shell electrons interact strongly with neighboring atomic potentials.
  • According to Pauli's exclusion principle, identical electrons cannot occupy the same quantum state, causing degenerate energy levels to split.
  • For N atoms, each atomic level splits into N closely spaced energy levels forming a continuous energy band.

Classification of Solids

  • Insulators possess a large forbidden energy gap (Eg > 3 eV) with a completely filled valence band and empty conduction band.
  • Semiconductors have a narrow forbidden gap (Eg &approx; 1 eV), allowing thermal excitation of electrons from the valence to the conduction band.
  • Conductors feature overlapping valence and conduction bands or a half-filled band, ensuring free movement of charge carriers under an electric field.

Bloch Theorem and Periodic Potential

  • The potential energy V(x) experienced by an electron in a crystal lattice is periodic with the lattice constant a, such that V(x) = V(x + a).
  • Solutions to the Schrödinger equation for a periodic potential are modulated plane waves known as Bloch functions: ψk(x) = uk(x)exp(i k x).
  • The periodic potential leads to the formation of allowed energy bands separated by forbidden energy gaps in the E-k dispersion relation.

Effective Mass of Electrons

  • Inside a crystal lattice, an electron responds to external electric fields as if it possesses an effective mass m* rather than its rest mass m0.
  • The effective mass is defined by the curvature of the E-k band: m* = ℏ2 / (d2E / dk2).
  • Near the bottom of an energy band, m* is positive, whereas near the top of a band, the curvature is negative, yielding a negative effective mass interpreted as holes.

Derivations

Kronig-Penney Model Energy Dispersion Relation

Conditions: 1D rectangular periodic potential well of height V0 and width b, barrier width a-b.

  • Apply Bloch's theorem to the Schrödinger equation in regions of zero potential and square-well potential.
  • Match the wavefunctions and their first derivatives at the boundary x = b.
  • Apply boundary conditions and simplify using the approximation of a rectangular delta-function potential where V0 → ∞ and b → 0 keeping $P = (m V_{0} b a) / \hbar^{2} constant$.
  • Obtain the transcendental equation: (P / α a) sin(α a) + cos(α a) = cos(k a), where α2 = 2mE / ℏ2.

Result: The transcendental equation constrains allowed values of αa such that the left-hand side must lie strictly between -1 and +1, yielding allowed energy bands and forbidden gaps.

Density of States in a 3D Energy Band

Conditions: Free electron approximation in a 3D box of volume V.

  • Write the energy of an electron as $E = (\hbar^{2} k^{2}) / (2m*)$, where k2 = kx2 + ky2 + kz2.
  • Calculate the number of allowed k-states in a spherical shell of radius k and thickness dk in k-space as N(k) d$k = 2 * (4 \pi k^{2} dk) / (8 \pi^{3} / V)$.
  • Differentiate the energy relation to find dk/dE and substitute it into the state density expression N(E)dE = N(k)dk.

Result: N(E) = \frac{V}{2 \pi^{2}} \left(\frac{2m^{*}}{\hbar^{2}}\right)^{3/2} E^{1/2}

Graphs and experiments

E vs k Dispersion Relation

Draw: A plot of energy E versus wave vector k showing reduced zone scheme with Brillouin zones, separated by forbidden energy gaps.

Interpret: Discontinuities in the E-k curve occur at $k = \pm n\pi/a$, which correspond to Bragg reflection planes defining the boundaries of forbidden energy bands.

Exam use: Tested for identifying zone boundaries, forbidden gaps, and regions of positive/negative effective mass.

Fermi-Dirac Distribution Function vs Energy

Draw: Curves of f(E) versus E for T = 0 K (step function) and T > 0 K (smoothly sloping curve passing through f(EF) = 0.5).

Interpret: At T = 0 K, all states below EF are completely filled and those above are empty. As temperature rises, thermal excitation smears the occupation probability around EF.

Exam use: Explaining thermal generation of carriers and temperature dependence of semiconductor Fermi levels.

Hall Effect Experiment Setup

Draw: A semiconductor slab carrying current Ix placed in a transverse magnetic field Bz, generating a transverse Hall voltage VH.

Interpret: The sign of the Hall coefficient RH confirms whether the majority charge carriers are electrons (negative) or holes (positive), validating band theory predictions.

Exam use: Used to determine carrier concentration, sign of charge carriers, and mobility in solids.

Solved numericals

TGT core

A solid has a forbidden energy gap of 1.2 eV. Calculate the maximum wavelength of a photon that can excite an electron from the valence band to the conduction band.

  • Identify given values: $E_g = 1.2 eV = 1.2 * 1.6 * 10^-19 J$.
  • Use the photon energy relation: $E_g = \frac{hc}{\lambda}$.
  • Substitute constants $h = 6.63 * 10^-34 J s$ and $c = 3 * 10^8 m/s$.
  • Calculate $\lambda = \frac{hc}{E_g} = \frac{(6.63 \times 10^{-34}) (3 \times 10^{8})}{1.2 \times 1.6 \times 10^{-19}}$.

Answer: 1.036 \mu m

PGT extension

The E-k relationship for electrons in a one-dimensional crystal band is given by E(k) = E_0 - 2A \cos(a k), where A and a are constants. Find the expression for the effective mass m* of the electron at the bottom of the band (k = 0).

  • State the effective mass formula: m* = \hbar^{2} / (d^{2}E / dk^{2}).
  • Differentiate E(k) with respect to k once: \frac{dE}{dk} = 2Aa \sin(ak).
  • Differentiate a second time: \frac{d^{2}E}{dk^{2}} = 2Aa^{2} \cos(ak).
  • Evaluate at the bottom of the band where k = 0: \left.\frac{d^{2}E}{dk^{2}}\right|_{$k=0 = 2Aa^{2}$.
  • Substitute into the effective mass expression: m* = \hbar^{2} / (2Aa^{2}).

Answer: m* = \hbar^{2} / (2Aa^{2})

PGT extension

In an intrinsic semiconductor with a band gap of 0.7 eV, calculate the probability of an energy state being occupied by an electron at 0.3 eV above the Fermi level at a temperature of 300 K. (Take $k_B = 8.62 * 10^-5 eV/K)$

  • Identify given values: E - $E_F = 0.3 eV$, T = 300 K, $k_B = 8.62 * 10^-5 eV/K$.
  • Write down the Fermi-Dirac distribution function: f(E) = 1 / [exp((E - E_F) / (k_B T)) + 1].
  • Calculate the exponent term: (E - E_F) / (k_B T) = 0.3 / (8.62 \times 10^{-5} \times 300) = 0.3 / 0.02586 = 11.60.
  • Evaluate the exponential: exp(11.60) \approx 108804.
  • Calculate probability: f(E) = 1 / (108804 + 1) \approx 9.19 \times 10^{-6}.

Answer: 9.19 \times 10^{-6}

Assumptions and limitations

  • The one-electron approximation assumes each electron moves in an average effective potential created by the ionic cores and all other electrons.
  • The adiabatic (Born-Oppenheimer) approximation assumes atomic nuclei are stationary compared to rapidly moving electrons, which fails at high temperatures where lattice vibrations (phonons) strongly couple with electrons.

Common misconceptions

Common error: Electrons in a completely filled valence band can contribute to electrical conduction.

Correct: Completely filled valence bands do not conduct electricity.

Under an applied electric field, every electron moving in one direction has a corresponding electron moving in the opposite direction, resulting in zero net current.

Common error: The effective mass of an electron in a crystal is always positive and equal to its rest mass.

Correct: The effective mass can be negative or different in magnitude from the rest mass.

Effective mass depends on the local curvature of the E-k dispersion curve, which is dictated by the periodic crystal potential.

Quick revision

Must remember

  • According to Pauli's exclusion principle, identical electrons cannot occupy the same quantum state, causing degenerate energy levels to split into energy bands when atoms form a crystal.
  • Insulators possess a large forbidden energy gap ($E_g > 3\text{ eV}$) with a completely filled valence band and an empty conduction band.
  • Semiconductors have a narrow forbidden gap ($E_g \approx 1\text{ eV}$), enabling thermal excitation of charge carriers.
  • Conductors feature overlapping valence and conduction bands or a half-filled band allowing free movement of electrons.
  • Bloch's theorem states that solutions to the Schrödinger equation for a periodic potential are modulated plane waves: $\psi_k(x) = u_k(x)e^{i k x}$.
  • The effective mass of an electron in a crystal lattice is defined by the curvature of the E-k band: $m^* = \hbar^2 / (d^2E/dk^2)$.
  • Near the top of an energy band, the band curvature is negative, giving rise to a negative effective mass interpreted as holes.
  • The Fermi-Dirac distribution function governs the probability of occupancy of an energy state: $f(E) = \frac{1}{e^{(E - E_F)/kT} + 1}$.

Glossary

Energy BandA continuous range of allowed energy levels formed due to the splitting of discrete atomic energy levels in a crystal lattice.
Forbidden Energy Gap ($E_g$)The energy range between the valence band and conduction band where no electron quantum states are allowed.
Bloch FunctionThe wavefunction of an electron in a periodic potential, represented as a product of a plane wave and a lattice-periodic function.
Effective Mass ($m^*$)The apparent mass of an electron moving inside a crystal lattice, accounting for internal periodic potentials.
Fermi Energy ($E_F$)The energy level at which the probability of occupation by an electron is exactly $0.5$ at thermal equilibrium.
Intrinsic Carrier Concentration ($n_i$)The number density of thermally generated electrons in the conduction band (or holes in the valence band) of an intrinsic semiconductor.

Practice Quiz

Choose an option to reveal the explanation.

1. When $N$ isolated atoms are brought together to form a crystal, each atomic energy level splits into how many closely spaced energy levels?

Explanation: According to quantum mechanics and Pauli's exclusion principle, when $N$ identical atoms interact to form a crystal lattice, each discrete atomic energy level splits into $N$ closely spaced discrete levels forming an energy band.

2. What is the approximate magnitude of the forbidden energy gap in typical semiconductors like silicon or germanium?

Explanation: Semiconductors possess a narrow forbidden energy gap $E_g$ typically around $1\text{ eV}$ (e.g., $\approx 1.1\text{ eV}$ for silicon and $\approx 0.7\text{ eV}$ for germanium at room temperature).

3. Why do completely filled valence bands not contribute to electrical conduction?

Explanation: In a completely filled valence band, under an applied electric field, every electron moving in one direction is balanced by an electron moving in the opposite direction, resulting in zero net electric current.

4. Bloch's theorem states that the wavefunction of an electron in a periodic potential is modulated by which type of function?

Explanation: Solutions to the Schrödinger equation for a periodic potential are Bloch functions given by $\psi_k(x) = u_k(x)e^{i k x}$, where $u_k(x)$ has the periodicity of the crystal lattice.

5. The effective mass $m^*$ of an electron inside a crystal lattice is inversely proportional to which quantity?

Explanation: The effective mass is defined as $m^* = \hbar^2 / (d^2E / dk^2)$, meaning it is inversely proportional to the curvature ($d^2E/dk^2$) of the E-k band.

6. Near the top of an energy band, the curvature of the E-k dispersion curve is negative. What does this imply for the effective mass?

Explanation: Where the band curvature $d^2E/dk^2$ is negative (near the top of an energy band), the effective mass $m^*$ is negative, which physically manifests as mobile positive charge carriers known as holes.

7. What does the Fermi-Dirac distribution function $f(E)$ represent?

Explanation: The Fermi-Dirac function $f(E) = \frac{1}{e^{(E-E_F)/kT} + 1}$ gives the exact thermal equilibrium probability that an electronic state with energy $E$ is occupied.

8. At absolute zero temperature ($T = 0\text{ K}$), what is the value of the Fermi-Dirac distribution function $f(E)$ for energy levels above the Fermi energy ($E > E_F$)?

Explanation: At $T = 0\text{ K}$, all energy states below $E_F$ are completely filled ($f(E) = 1$) and all states above $E_F$ are completely empty ($f(E) = 0$).

9. How does the electrical conductivity of an intrinsic semiconductor vary with an increase in temperature?

Explanation: As temperature increases, more electrons are thermally excited across the narrow forbidden gap from the valence band to the conduction band, increasing carrier concentration and electrical conductivity.

10. In the Kronig-Penney model, what type of potential energy function is assumed for an electron in a crystal?

Explanation: The Kronig-Penney model simplifies the complex 3D crystal lattice potential into a 1D periodic square-well potential to analytically demonstrate band formation.

11. What is the relationship between the intrinsic carrier concentration $n_i$, effective density of states $N_c, N_v$, and the band gap $E_g$?

Explanation: The mass-action law relation for intrinsic semiconductors is given by $n_i^2 = N_c N_v \exp(-E_g / kT)$, showing exponential dependence on band gap and temperature.

12. Which of the following solids features overlapping valence and conduction bands?

Explanation: Conductors feature overlapping valence and conduction bands (or a half-filled band), meaning there is no forbidden gap, allowing electrons to move freely under an electric field.

13. What does the potential periodicity condition $V(x) = V(x + a)$ signify in a crystal lattice?

Explanation: The condition $V(x) = V(x + a)$ expresses spatial periodicity where $a$ is the lattice constant of the periodic crystal lattice.

14. If the effective mass of electrons $m_n^*$ equals the effective mass of holes $m_p^*$ in an intrinsic semiconductor, where does the Fermi level lie?

Explanation: The Fermi level in an intrinsic semiconductor is given by $E_F = \frac{E_c + E_v}{2} + \frac{3}{4}kT \ln(m_p^*/m_n^*)$. If $m_n^* = m_p^*$, the logarithmic term vanishes, placing $E_F$ precisely at the middle of the gap.

15. What is the unit of electrical conductivity $\sigma$ in terms of fundamental SI units?

Explanation: Electrical conductivity $\sigma = e(n\mu_n + p\mu_p)$ is measured in Siemens per meter ($\text{S}/\text{m}$) or inverse ohm-meters ($\Omega^{-1}\text{m}^{-1}$).

PYQ Patterns

These are pattern-based practice questions. Authentic year labels are only used when verified from a source.

1. According to the band theory of solids, the forbidden energy gap in insulators is typically:

Explanation: Insulators are characterized by a large forbidden energy gap ($E_g > 3\text{ eV}$), preventing thermal excitation of electrons from the valence band to the conduction band at normal temperatures.

2. The effective mass of an electron in an energy band is negative:

Explanation: Near the top of an energy band, the E-k curve curves downward ($d^2E/dk^2 < 0$), resulting in a negative effective mass which corresponds physically to the behavior of holes.

3. What is the probability of finding an electron in an energy state exactly equal to the Fermi energy ($E = E_F$) at any temperature $T > 0\text{ K}$?

Explanation: Substituting $E = E_F$ into the Fermi-Dirac distribution yields $f(E_F) = \frac{1}{e^0 + 1} = \frac{1}{1+1} = 0.5$, regardless of the temperature.

4. In a semiconductor, electrical conductivity is expressed as $\sigma = e(n\mu_n + p\mu_p)$. Here $\mu_n$ and $\mu_p$ represent:

Explanation: In the conductivity equation, $\mu_n$ and $\mu_p$ denote the mobilities of electrons and holes respectively, defined as drift velocity per unit electric field.

5. Bloch functions used in solving the Schrödinger equation for a periodic lattice are products of a plane wave and:

Explanation: Bloch's theorem establishes that wavefunctions in a periodic potential have the form $\psi_k(x) = u_k(x)e^{i k x}$, where $u_k(x)$ is periodic with the lattice spacing.

6. How does the band gap $E_g(T)$ of a semiconductor generally change with an increase in absolute temperature $T$?

Explanation: Empirically and theoretically, the band gap of semiconductors decreases with increasing temperature due to thermal expansion of the lattice and electron-phonon interactions (Varshni relation).

Timed Topic Test

Attempt these questions under a ten-minute limit using a separate timer.

1. Which physical principle is primarily responsible for the splitting of discrete atomic energy levels into energy bands in a crystal?

Explanation: Pauli's exclusion principle forbids identical electrons from occupying the exact same quantum state simultaneously, forcing degenerate atomic levels to split into closely spaced energy bands.

2. What is the condition for the Kronig-Penney model in a 1D periodic square-well potential?

Explanation: The dispersion relation resulting from the Kronig-Penney Kronig boundary matching condition is $\frac{P}{\alpha a}\sin(\alpha a) + \cos(\alpha a) = \cos(k a)$.

3. In an intrinsic semiconductor, if the effective density of states in the conduction and valence bands are $N_c$ and $N_v$, what is the intrinsic carrier concentration $n_i$ proportional to?

Explanation: Since $n_i^2 = N_c N_v \exp(-E_g / kT)$, taking the square root gives $n_i = \sqrt{N_c N_v} \exp(-E_g / 2kT)$, meaning $n_i$ is proportional to $\exp(-E_g / 2kT)$.

4. Which of the following describes the behavior of holes in a semiconductor valence band?

Explanation: A vacant electron state near the top of the valence band (where $m^* < 0$) acts dynamically as a positive charge carrier (hole) with a positive effective mass.

5. What is the value of the Fermi-Dirac distribution function $f(E)$ when $E - E_F = kT$?

Explanation: For $E - E_F = kT$, $f(E) = \frac{1}{e^{(kT)/kT} + 1} = \frac{1}{e^1 + 1} = \frac{1}{2.718 + 1} \approx 0.269$.

6. Under what condition can a solid with a half-filled energy band act as a good conductor?

Explanation: A half-filled band has plenty of empty states adjacent to occupied states. An electric field gives electrons thermal/kinetic promotion into these empty states, causing a net current.

7. What is the physical significance of the wave vector $k$ in Bloch functions for electrons in crystals?

Explanation: The wave vector $k$ labels the Bloch states and is directly proportional to the crystal momentum ($hbar k$) of the electron in the periodic lattice.

8. If the band gap $E_g$ of a material is $0.67\text{ eV}$ (typical for germanium), approximately how does thermal excitation compare to an insulator with $E_g = 5\text{ eV}$?

Explanation: Because the exponential factor $\exp(-E_g / 2kT)$ is much larger for a smaller band gap ($0.67\text{ eV}$ vs $5\text{ eV}$), germanium exhibits significantly higher thermal carrier generation and conductivity.

9. What happens to the effective mass $m^*$ of an electron at an inflection point of the E-k dispersion curve where $d^2E/dk^2 = 0$?

Explanation: Since $m^* = \hbar^2 / (d^2E / dk^2)$, when the second derivative $d^2E / dk^2$ approaches zero at an inflection point, the effective mass approaches infinity ($m^* \to \infty$).

10. Which parameter in the Kronig-Penney potential model represents the scattering strength of the rectangular potential barriers?

Explanation: In the Kronig-Penney model equation, $P = \frac{m V_0 b a}{\hbar^2}$ represents the scattering strength parameter of the barrier of height $V_0$ and width $b$.
