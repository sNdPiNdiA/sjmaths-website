Metals, Semiconductors, and Insulators

Solids are classified into metals, semiconductors, and insulators based on the energy band structure formed by overlapping atomic orbitals according to Pauli's exclusion principle. The electrical behavior of these materials is governed by the occupancy of the valence band and conduction band, separated by a forbidden energy gap.

Scope and prerequisites

Prerequisites

  • Pauli's exclusion principle
  • Bohr's theory of hydrogen atom and atomic orbital overlapping
  • Band theory of solids (Bloch theorem concepts)

Energy Band Formation in Solids

  • According to Pauli's exclusion principle, when isolated atoms are brought together to form a solid, discrete atomic energy levels split into closely spaced continuous bands.
  • The valence band contains the highest occupied energy levels at absolute zero temperature, while the conduction band contains unoccupied or partially occupied levels responsible for electrical conduction.
  • The forbidden energy gap (Eg) is the energy range between the top of the valence band and the bottom of the conduction band where no electron quantum states exist.

Metals (Conductors)

  • In metals, the valence band and conduction band either overlap or the conduction band is only partially filled, resulting in a zero forbidden energy gap (Eg = 0).
  • An immense number of free electrons are available for conduction under the application of a weak electric field.
  • The electrical resistivity of metals increases with temperature due to increased lattice vibrations (phonon scattering), yielding a positive temperature coefficient of resistance.

Semiconductors

  • Semiconductors possess a narrow forbidden energy gap, typically around Eg ≈ 1.1 eV for silicon and 0.7 eV for germanium at room temperature.
  • At absolute zero temperature (T = 0 K), semiconductors behave as perfect insulators because the valence band is completely filled and the conduction band is empty.
  • As temperature increases or upon doping, covalent bonds break, generating electron-hole pairs and imparting a negative temperature coefficient of resistance.

Insulators

  • Insulators feature a very large forbidden energy gap, generally Eg > 3 eV (e.g., diamond has Eg ≈ 5 eV).
  • Thermal energy at room temperature is insufficient to thermally excite electrons across such a large band gap from the valence band to the conduction band.
  • Insulators maintain extremely high resistivity and do not conduct electricity under normal electric field strengths.

Derivations

Position of Fermi Level in an Intrinsic Semiconductor

Conditions: Intrinsic semiconductor at thermal equilibrium

  • Write the concentration of electrons in the conduction band: n = Nc exp(-(Ec - Ef)/(kT))
  • Write the concentration of holes in the valence band: p = Nv exp(-(Ef - Ev)/(kT))
  • Equate electron and hole concentrations for an intrinsic semiconductor (n = p): Nc exp(-(Ec - Ef)/(kT)) = Nv exp(-(Ef - Ev)/(kT))
  • Take the natural logarithm of both sides and solve for the Fermi energy Ef: Ef = (Ec + Ev)/2 + (kT/2) ln(Nv/Nc)

Result: Ef = (Ec + Ev)/2 + (kT/2) ln(Nv/Nc), which lies very close to the middle of the forbidden energy gap when effective masses of electrons and holes are nearly equal.

Electrical Conductivity of Semiconductors

Conditions: Presence of both electrons and holes as charge carriers

  • Express total current density J as the sum of electron and hole current densities: J = J_n + J_p = e n v_d_n + e p v_d_p
  • Substitute drift velocity in terms of mobility (v_d = μ E): J = e n μ_n E + e p μ_p E
  • Factor out the electric field E to obtain conductivity σ = J/E: σ = e (n μ_n + p μ_p)
  • For intrinsic semiconductors where n = p = n_i, substitute to get intrinsic conductivity: σ_i = e n_i (μ_n + μ_p)

Result: \sigma = e(n\mu_n + p\mu_p)

Solved numericals

TGT core

A piece of germanium has a forbidden energy gap of 0.72 eV. What is the maximum wavelength of electromagnetic radiation that can generate an electron-hole pair in germanium?

  • Convert band gap energy from eV to Joules: E_g = 0.72 eV = 0.72 × 1.6 × 10^-19 J = 1.152 × 10^-19 J
  • Use the relation between energy and wavelength: E_g = hc / λ_max
  • Rearrange for wavelength: λ_max = hc / E_g
  • Substitute constants (h = 6.63 × 10^-34 J·s, c = 3 × 10^8 m/s): λ_max = (6.63 × 10^-34 × 3 × 10^8) / (1.152 × 10^-19)
  • Calculate the value: λ_max = 1.989 × 10^-25 / 1.152 × 10^-19 = 1.726 × 10^-6 m

Answer: 1.726 μm (or 1726 nm)

PGT extension

In an intrinsic semiconductor with a band gap of 0.7 eV, calculate the probability that a state 0.35 eV above the Fermi level is occupied by an electron at temperature T = 300 K. (Take k = 8.62 &times; 10^-5 eV/K)

  • Identify given parameters: E - E_f = 0.35 eV, T = 300 K, k = 8.62 × 10^-5 eV/K
  • Calculate the exponent term: (E - E_f)/(kT) = 0.35 / (8.62 × 10^-5 × 300)
  • Compute denominator value: 8.62 × 10^-5 × 300 = 0.02586 eV
  • Evaluate exponent: (E - E_f)/(kT) = 0.35 / 0.02586 = 13.534
  • Calculate Fermi-Dirac probability: f(E) = 1 / (1 + exp(13.534)) ≈ 1 / (1 + 754670) ≈ 1.32 × 10^-6

Answer: 1.32 × 10^-6 (extremely low probability)

PGT extension

An intrinsic silicon semiconductor at 300 K has carrier concentration n_i = 1.5 &times; 10^16 m^-3. If the electron mobility &mu;_n is 0.13 m^2/(V·s) and hole mobility &mu;_p is 0.05 m^2/(V·s), find the electrical conductivity of silicon.

  • Use the intrinsic conductivity formula: σ_i = e n_i (μ_n + μ_p)
  • Substitute the given values: e = 1.6 × 10^-19 C, n_i = 1.5 × 10^16 m^-3, μ_n = 0.13, μ_p = 0.05
  • Sum mobilities: μ_n + μ_p = 0.13 + 0.05 = 0.18 m^2/(V·s)
  • Multiply terms: σ_i = (1.6 × 10^-19) × (1.5 × 10^16) × 0.18
  • Calculate final value: σ_i = 2.4 × 10^-3 × 0.18 = 4.32 × 10^-4 S/m

Answer: 4.32 × 10^-4 S/m

Assumptions and limitations

  • Band theory assumes a periodic potential of the crystal lattice (Kronig-Penney model approximation) which neglects lattice imperfections and impurities in pure crystals.
  • Effective mass approximation treats electrons inside the periodic lattice as free particles with an effective mass m* that accounts for internal forces.

Common misconceptions

Common error: Semiconductors conduct better at lower temperatures because thermal agitation decreases.

Correct: Semiconductors conduct better at higher temperatures.

Thermal energy breaks covalent bonds to generate mobile charge carriers (electron-hole pairs), overriding thermal scattering effects.

Common error: The Fermi level always lies exactly at the center of the forbidden energy gap for all semiconductors.

Correct: The Fermi level lies near the center only for pure intrinsic semiconductors with nearly equal effective masses; in doped semiconductors, it shifts toward the conduction or valence band.

Doping alters carrier concentrations, requiring a shift in the chemical potential (Fermi level) to maintain charge neutrality.

Common error: Metals have a negative temperature coefficient of resistance.

Correct: Metals have a positive temperature coefficient of resistance.

In metals, carrier concentration remains nearly constant while lattice vibrations increase scattering with temperature, raising resistance.

Quick revision

  • Metals have overlapping energy bands (E_g = 0) and positive temperature coefficients of resistance.
  • Semiconductors possess a narrow band gap (~1 eV) and negative temperature coefficients of resistance.
  • Insulators have wide band gaps (E_g > 3 eV) preventing thermal carrier excitation.
  • Intrinsic semiconductor conductivity depends directly on carrier concentration and mobility: σ = e n_i (μ_n + μ_p).

Must remember

  • In metals, the valence band and conduction band overlap or the conduction band is partially filled, resulting in a zero forbidden energy gap ($E_g = 0$).
  • Semiconductors possess a narrow forbidden energy gap, typically around $1.1\text{ eV}$ for silicon and $0.7\text{ eV}$ for germanium at room temperature.
  • At $T = 0\text{ K}$, semiconductors behave as perfect insulators because the valence band is completely filled and the conduction band is empty.
  • Insulators feature a very large forbidden energy gap, generally $E_g > 3\text{ eV}$ (e.g., diamond has $E_g \approx 5\text{ eV}$).
  • Electrical conductivity in semiconductors is given by $\sigma = n e \mu_n + p e \mu_p$.
  • The Fermi-Dirac distribution function is $f(E) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{(E - E_f)/kT}}$.

Glossary

Forbidden Energy Gap ($E_g$)The energy range between the top of the valence band and the bottom of the conduction band where no electron quantum states exist.
Fermi Energy Level ($E_f$)The energy level at which the probability of occupation by an electron is exactly $0.5$ at thermal equilibrium.
Intrinsic Carrier Concentration ($n_i$)The concentration of electrons or holes in a pure, undoped semiconductor in thermal equilibrium.
Drift Mobility ($\mu$)The ratio of the drift velocity of charge carriers to the applied electric field, expressed in $\text{m}^2/(\text{V}\cdot\text{s})$.

Practice Quiz

Choose an option to reveal the explanation.

1. What is the approximate forbidden energy gap ($E_g$) for silicon at room temperature?

Explanation: Silicon has a narrow forbidden energy gap of approximately $1.1\text{ eV}$ at room temperature, while germanium is around $0.7\text{ eV}$.

2. How do semiconductors behave at absolute zero temperature ($T = 0\text{ K}$)?

Explanation: At absolute zero temperature ($T = 0\text{ K}$), valence bands are completely filled and conduction bands are empty, making semiconductors behave as perfect insulators.

3. What is the temperature coefficient of resistance for metals?

Explanation: The electrical resistivity and resistance of metals increase with temperature due to increased lattice vibrations (phonon scattering), yielding a positive temperature coefficient of resistance.

4. What is the forbidden energy gap ($E_g$) value range that characterizes insulators?

Explanation: Insulators feature a very large forbidden energy gap, generally $E_g > 3\text{ eV}$ (e.g., diamond has $E_g \approx 5\text{ eV}$).

5. What does the Fermi-Dirac distribution function $f(E)$ represent?

Explanation: The Fermi-Dirac distribution function $f(E) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{(E - E_f)/kT}}$ gives the probability that an energy state $E$ is occupied by an electron.

6. According to the law of mass action for semiconductors in thermal equilibrium, which relation holds true?

Explanation: The law of mass action states that $n_i^2 = n p$, where $n_i$ is the intrinsic carrier concentration, $n$ is electron density, and $p$ is hole density.

7. What is the expression for electrical conductivity $\sigma$ in terms of carrier concentrations and mobilities?

Explanation: The total electrical conductivity is given by $\sigma = n e \mu_n + p e \mu_p$, accounting for both electron and hole contributions.

8. Why do semiconductors have a negative temperature coefficient of resistance?

Explanation: As temperature increases, thermal energy breaks covalent bonds to generate mobile charge carriers (electron-hole pairs), rapidly increasing conductivity and thus reducing resistance.

9. For an intrinsic semiconductor, where does the Fermi level $E_f$ ideally lie at $0\text{ K}$?

Explanation: For an intrinsic semiconductor with nearly equal effective masses for electrons and holes, the intrinsic Fermi level lies near the center of the forbidden energy gap.

10. What is the unit of drift mobility ($\mu$)?

Explanation: Drift mobility is defined as $\mu = v_d / E$, where $v_d$ is drift velocity ($\text{m}/\text{s}$) and $E$ is electric field ($\text{V}/\text{m}$), yielding units of $\text{m}^2/(\text{V}\cdot\text{s})$.

11. In an n-type extrinsic semiconductor under moderate temperatures with complete ionization, what is the electron concentration $n$ approximately equal to?

Explanation: For an n-type semiconductor with complete ionization at moderate temperatures, the electron concentration is approximately equal to the donor impurity concentration ($n \approx N_d$).

12. What happens to the resistance of a metal when its temperature is increased?

Explanation: In metals, electrical resistivity increases with temperature because increased thermal lattice vibrations cause more frequent scattering of conduction electrons.

13. According to Pauli's exclusion principle, when isolated atoms are brought together to form a solid, what happens to discrete atomic energy levels?

Explanation: When isolated atoms form a solid, interacting valence electrons experience splitting of discrete atomic energy levels into closely spaced continuous bands according to Pauli's exclusion principle.

14. What is the approximate band gap value for germanium at room temperature?

Explanation: Germanium possesses a narrow forbidden energy gap of approximately $0.7\text{ eV}$ at room temperature.

15. Which of the following materials has a forbidden energy gap $E_g \approx 5\text{ eV}$?

Explanation: Diamond is an insulator with a very large forbidden energy gap of approximately $5\text{ eV}$.

PYQ Patterns

These are pattern-based practice questions. Authentic year labels are only used when verified from a source.

1. If the Fermi level $E_f$ lies exactly in the middle of the forbidden band of a semiconductor, the semiconductor is:

Explanation: For an intrinsic semiconductor, the Fermi level lies near the center of the forbidden energy gap when effective masses of electrons and holes are comparable.

2. The electrical conductivity of an intrinsic semiconductor at $0\text{ K}$ is:

Explanation: At absolute zero temperature ($0\text{ K}$), intrinsic semiconductors have no thermally generated charge carriers and act as perfect insulators with zero conductivity.

3. In a p-type semiconductor, the Fermi level shifts towards:

Explanation: Acceptor doping in p-type semiconductors increases hole concentration, lowering the chemical potential and shifting the Fermi level closer to the valence band.

4. Which band is responsible for electrical conduction in solids?

Explanation: The conduction band contains unoccupied or partially occupied levels that allow electrons to move freely under an electric field, enabling electrical conduction.

5. The resistance of a semiconductor decreases with an increase in temperature. This behavior is attributed to:

Explanation: The dominant factor in semiconductors with rising temperature is the rapid exponential generation of electron-hole pairs, which outweighs scattering effects.

6. What is the value of the Fermi-Dirac distribution function $f(E)$ when the energy state $E$ is equal to the Fermi energy $E_f$ at any temperature $T > 0\text{ K}$?

Explanation: When $E = E_f$, the exponent $(E - E_f)/kT = 0$, making $f(E_f) = 1 / (1 + e^0) = 1 / 2 = 0.5$.

Timed Topic Test

Attempt these questions under a ten-minute limit using a separate timer.

1. Which of the following statements is correct regarding metals?

Explanation: In metals, valence and conduction bands overlap or the conduction band is partially filled, yielding $E_g = 0$.

2. The resistance change formula $$R_{T}$ = $R_{0}$(1 + \$\alpha$ \Delta T)$ applies best to:

Explanation: This linear approximation for resistance variation with temperature is standard for metals under moderate temperature changes.

3. If the effective density of states in the conduction band $N_c$ is greater than in the valence band $N_v$, where does the intrinsic Fermi level lie relative to mid-gap?

Explanation: From the formula $E_f = \frac{E_c + E_v}{2} + \frac{kT}{2} \ln\left(\frac{N_v}{N_c}\right)$, if $N_c > N_v$, the logarithmic term is negative, shifting $E_f$ slightly below the mid-gap.

4. What primary mechanism causes the resistivity of metals to increase with temperature?

Explanation: Carrier concentration in metals is essentially constant; resistance increases due to increased lattice vibrations causing greater scattering of conduction electrons.

5. Which material classification features an energy gap $E_g \approx 1.1\text{ eV}$ at room temperature?

Explanation: Silicon is well known for having an energy gap of approximately $1.1\text{ eV}$ at room temperature.

6. At absolute zero ($0\text{ K}$), what is the value of the Fermi-Dirac distribution function $f(E)$ for energy levels $E < E_f$?

Explanation: At $T = 0\text{ K}$, all energy states below the Fermi level are completely filled, so $f(E) = 1$ for $E < E_f$.

7. What is the consequence of a very large forbidden energy gap ($E_g > 3\text{ eV}$) in insulators?

Explanation: Due to the large band gap ($E_g > 3\text{ eV}$), room temperature thermal energy is far too small to promote electrons from the valence to the conduction band.

8. In an extrinsic n-type semiconductor, the majority charge carriers are:

Explanation: Donor doping introduces an excess of conduction electrons, making electrons the majority charge carriers in n-type semiconductors.

9. According to Varshni's empirical relation, how does the band gap $E_g(T)$ change with temperature?

Explanation: Varshni's relation $E_g(T) = E_g(0) - \frac{\$\alpha$ T^2}{T + \$\beta$}$ shows that semiconductor band gaps generally decrease as temperature increases.

10. What role do valence bands play in electrical conduction?

Explanation: The valence band contains the highest occupied states at $0\text{ K}$. Conduction occurs when electrons leave this band (leaving holes) or are excited into the conduction band.
