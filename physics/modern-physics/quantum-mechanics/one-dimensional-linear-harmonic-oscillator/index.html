One-Dimensional Linear Harmonic Oscillator in Quantum Mechanics

The one-dimensional linear harmonic oscillator describes a particle subject to a restoring force proportional to its displacement from an equilibrium position under quantum mechanics. Its potential energy is given by V(x) = &frac{1}{2}m&omega;<sup>2</sup>x<sup>2</sup>, serving as a fundamental model for molecular vibrations and lattice dynamics.

Potential Energy Function and Hamiltonian

  • The classical potential for a linear harmonic oscillator is V(x) = &frac{1}{2}mω2x2, where m is the mass and ω is the angular frequency.
  • The quantum mechanical Hamiltonian operator is H = -&frac{hbar2}{2m}&frac{d2}{dx2} + &frac{1}{2}mω2x2.
  • This potential is symmetric about x = 0 and confines the particle to a finite region for any finite energy state, though quantum mechanically tunneling occurs.

Energy Eigenvalues and Zero-Point Energy

  • The allowed energy eigenvalues are quantized and given by En = (n + &frac{1}{2})ℏω, where n = 0, 1, 2, 3, ...
  • For the ground state (n = 0), the energy is E0 = &frac{1}{2}ℏω, which is known as the zero-point energy.
  • The zero-point energy is a direct consequence of Heisenberg's uncertainty principle, preventing the particle from resting motionless at the potential minimum.

Wavefunctions and Hermite Polynomials

  • The spatial wavefunctions ψn(x) are expressed in terms of Hermite polynomials Hn(αx) multiplied by a Gaussian decay factor.
  • The wavefunctions alternate in parity: even n corresponds to even parity functions, and odd n corresponds to odd parity functions.
  • Adjacent wavefunctions satisfy the orthogonality condition over the domain from -∞ to +∞.

Quantum vs. Classical Turning Points

  • Classically, a particle cannot exist in regions where its total energy is less than its potential energy (E < V).
  • Quantum mechanically, the wavefunction penetrates into the classically forbidden region, leading to a non-zero probability density beyond the turning points.
  • In the classical limit (as n → ∞), the quantum probability distribution approaches the classical probability distribution.

Derivations

Derivation of Energy Eigenvalues using Series Solution

Conditions: Time-independent Schrödinger equation for V(x) = 0.5 m &omega;<sup>2</sup> x<sup>2</sup>

  • Write the time-independent Schrödinger equation: -&frac{hbar2}{2m}&frac{d2ψ}{dx2} + &frac{1}{2}mω2x2ψ = Eψ
  • Introduce dimensionless coordinates ξ = αx where α = √(&frac{mω}{hbar}) and dimensionless energy λ = &frac{2E}{hbarω}.
  • Transform the differential equation to &frac{d2ψ}{dξ2} + (λ - ξ2)ψ = 0.
  • Assume a solution of the form ψ(ξ) = h(ξ)exp(-ξ2/2) and substitute into the differential equation to obtain Hermite's differential equation for h(ξ).
  • Apply the condition that the series must terminate to prevent the wavefunction from diverging at infinity, yielding λ = 2n + 1.

Result: E_n = (n + 1/2)ℏω

Derivation of Ground State Wavefunction &psi;<sub>0</sub>(x)

Conditions: For n = 0 using the differential equation and normalization condition

  • For n = 0, the Hermite polynomial is H_0(ξ) = 1.
  • The trial wavefunction for the ground state is ψ0(ξ) = N_0 exp(-ξ2/2), where N_0 is the normalization constant.
  • Apply the normalization condition ∫-∞∞ |ψ0(x)|2 dx = 1.
  • Evaluate the Gaussian integral ∫-∞∞ exp(-α2x2) dx = √(π)/α.
  • Solve for the normalization constant N_0 = (&frac{alpha}{pi})1/4.

Result: ψ_0(x) = (&frac{mω}{pihbar})1/4 exp(-&frac{mωx2}{2hbar})

Solved numericals

TGT core

Calculate the zero-point energy of a linear harmonic oscillator whose frequency is 1.0 &times; 10<sup>13</sup> Hz. Given h = 6.63 &times; 10<sup>-34</sup> J s.

  • Identify the formula for zero-point energy: E_0 = 0.5 ℏ ω = 0.5 h f.
  • Substitute the given values: h = 6.63 × 10-34 J s and f = 1.0 × 1013 Hz.
  • E_0 = 0.5 × (6.63 × 10-34) × (1.0 × 1013).
  • E_0 = 3.315 × 10-21 J.

Answer: 3.315 imes 10^{-21} J

PGT extension

For a one-dimensional harmonic oscillator of mass 9.1 &times; 10<sup>-31</sup> kg and angular frequency 2.0 &times; 10<sup>14</sup> rad/s, find the root-mean-square displacement &radic;&lang;x<sup>2</sup>&rang; for the ground state.

  • Recognize that for the ground state, ⟨x⟩ = 0, so √⟨x2⟩ = √(⟨x2⟩).
  • Use the expectation value relation for position in the ground state: ⟨x2⟩ = &frac{hbar}{2momega}.
  • Substitute the values: ℏ = 1.054 × 10-34 J s, m = 9.1 × 10-31 kg, ω = 2.0 × 1014 rad/s.
  • ⟨x2⟩ = rac{1.054 imes 10^{-34}}{2 imes (9.1 imes 10^{-31}) imes (2.0 imes 10^{14})} = 2.895 × 10-20 m2.
  • Take the square root: √⟨x2⟩ = √(2.895 × 10-20) = 1.7 × 10-10 m.

Answer: 1.7 imes 10^{-10} m

PGT extension

Find the energy difference between the fourth excited state and the ground state for a harmonic oscillator with vibrational frequency f = 3.0 &times; 10<sup>12</sup> Hz.

  • Identify the quantum numbers: ground state n = 0, fourth excited state n = 4.
  • Energy formula: E_n = (n + 0.5) h f.
  • Calculate E_0 = (0 + 0.5) h f = 0.5 h f.
  • Calculate E_4 = (4 + 0.5) h f = 4.5 h f.
  • Find the difference ΔE = E_4 - E_0 = 4.0 h f = 4 × (6.63 × 10-34) × (3.0 × 1012).
  • ΔE = 7.956 × 10-21 J.

Answer: 7.956 imes 10^{-21} J

Assumptions and limitations

  • Assumption: The restoring force strictly obeys Hooke's law (F = -kx) indefinitely for all displacements. Limitation: Real molecular potentials (e.g., Morse potential) deviate from harmonic behavior at large amplitudes due to anharmonicity and dissociation.

Common misconceptions

Common error: The minimum energy of a quantum harmonic oscillator is zero.

Correct: The minimum energy is E_0 = 0.5 &hbar; &omega; (zero-point energy).

A zero energy state would violate the Heisenberg uncertainty principle by allowing simultaneous exact localization and zero momentum.

Common error: A quantum harmonic oscillator particle cannot cross the classical turning points.

Correct: The particle has a non-zero probability of being found in the classically forbidden region.

Quantum wavefunctions decay exponentially rather than dropping abruptly to zero at the turning points.

Quick revision

  • Energy levels are equally spaced by ΔE = ℏω with ground state energy &frac{1}{2}ℏω.
  • Potential Energy: V(x) = rac{1}{2}momega^2x^2
  • Hamiltonian Operator: H = - rac{hbar^2}{2m} rac{d^2}{dx^2} + rac{1}{2}momega^2x^2

Must remember

  • The potential energy for a one-directional linear harmonic oscillator is given by $V(x) = \frac{1}{2}m\omega^2x^2$.
  • The allowed energy eigenvalues are quantized as $E_n = \left(n + \frac{1}{2}\right)\hbar\omega$ for $n = 0, 1, 2, \dots$.
  • The zero-point energy of the oscillator is $E_0 = \frac{1}{2}\hbar\omega$, which is a direct consequence of Heisenberg's uncertainty principle.
  • The spatial wavefunctions $\psi_n(x)$ are expressed using Hermite polynomials multiplied by a Gaussian decay factor.
  • Quantum mechanically, a particle can penetrate into the classically forbidden region where $E < V$, unlike in classical mechanics.

Practice Quiz

Choose an option to reveal the explanation.

1. What is the classical potential energy function for a one-dimensional linear harmonic oscillator of mass $m$ and angular frequency $\$\omega$$?

Explanation: The classical potential energy for a linear harmonic oscillator is defined as $V(x) = \frac{1}{2}m\$\omega$^2x^2$.

2. The allowed energy eigenvalues for a quantum harmonic oscillator are given by:

Explanation: The energy eigenvalues are quantized as $E_n = \left(n + \frac{1}{2}\right)\hbar\$\omega$$ where $n = 0, 1, 2, \dots$.

3. What is the zero-point energy of a one-dimensional quantum harmonic oscillator?

Explanation: For the ground state ($n = 0$), the energy is $E_0 = \frac{1}{2}\hbar\$\omega$$, which is known as the zero-point energy.

4. The zero-point energy in a quantum harmonic oscillator is a direct consequence of:

Explanation: The zero-point energy prevents the particle from resting motionless at the potential minimum, directly arising from Heisenberg's uncertainty principle.

5. What is the correct expression for the Hamiltonian operator $H$ of a 1D linear harmonic oscillator?

Explanation: The Hamiltonian includes the kinetic energy operator $-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{d^2}{dx^2}$ and the potential energy term $\frac{1}{2}m\$\omega$^2x^2$.

6. The spatial wavefunctions $\psi_n(x)$ of a harmonic oscillator are expressed using Hermite polynomials multiplied by which decay factor?

Explanation: Wavefunctions are formed by Hermite polynomials $H_n(\$\alpha$ x)$ multiplied by a Gaussian decay factor.

7. What is the parity of the ground state wavefunction $\psi_0(x)$ of a harmonic oscillator?

Explanation: Even quantum numbers $n$ correspond to even parity functions. For the ground state $n = 0$, it is even ($n=0$ is even).

8. In the context of harmonic oscillator scaling, what is the dimension of the dimensionless scale parameter $\$\alpha$ = \sqrt{\frac{m\$\omega$}{\hbar}}$?

Explanation: The parameter $\$\alpha$$ has units of inverse meters ($mathrm{m}^{-1}$) to scale the spatial coordinate $x$.

9. Classically, a particle cannot exist in regions where:

Explanation: Classically, kinetic energy cannot be negative, meaning the particle cannot enter regions where total energy is less than potential energy ($E < V$).

10. Quantum mechanically, what happens to the particle's probability density beyond the classical turning points?

Explanation: The wavefunction penetrates into the classically forbidden region ($E < V$), leading to a non-zero probability density beyond the turning points.

11. As the quantum number $n \to \infty$, what does the quantum probability distribution of a harmonic oscillator approach?

Explanation: According to the correspondence principle, in the classical limit ($n \to \infty$), the quantum probability distribution approaches the classical probability distribution.

12. Which operator acts as the raising operator (creation operator) for the energy states of a harmonic oscillator?

Explanation: The creation operator $a^\dagger$ raises the energy state by one quantum.

13. What is the function of the lowering operator (annihilation operator) $a$?

Explanation: The annihilation operator $a$ lowers the energy state, reducing the energy eigenvalue by $\hbar\$\omega$$.

14. Adjacent wavefunctions of the harmonic oscillator over the domain from $-\infty$ to $+\infty$ satisfy which condition?

Explanation: Adjacent wavefunctions satisfy the orthogonality condition over the entire domain from $-\infty$ to $+\infty$.

15. What is the spatial parity of the first excited state ($n = 1$) wavefunction of a linear harmonic oscillator?

Explanation: Wavefunctions alternate in parity: even $n$ corresponds to even parity, and odd $n$ corresponds to odd parity. Thus, $n=1$ is odd.

PYQ Patterns

These are pattern-based practice questions. Authentic year labels are only used when verified from a source.

1. The energy of the ground state of a one-dimensional simple harmonic oscillator is $\frac{1}{2}\hbar\$\omega$$. This implies that:

Explanation: The minimum energy is non-zero (zero-point energy), which prevents simultaneous exact localization and zero momentum, complying with Heisenberg's uncertainty principle.

2. What is the energy difference $\Delta E$ between two adjacent energy levels $E_n$ and $E_{n+1}$ of a quantum harmonic oscillator?

Explanation: Since $E_n = \left(n + \frac{1}{2}\right)\hbar\$\omega$$, the difference is $E_{n+1} - E_n = \left(n + 1 + \frac{1}{2}\right)\hbar\$\omega$ - \left(n + \frac{1}{2}\right)\hbar\$\omega$ = \hbar\$\omega$$.

3. Which of the following represents the correct normalized ground state wavefunction $\psi_0(x)$ for a 1D harmonic oscillator with parameter $\$\alpha$ = \sqrt{\frac{m\$\omega$}{\hbar}}$?

Explanation: Substituting $\$\alpha$ = \sqrt{\frac{m\$\omega$}{\hbar}}$, the ground state wavefunction expression transforms to $\left(\frac{\$\alpha$^2}{\$\pi$}\right)^{1/4} e^{-\frac{\$\alpha$^2 x^2}{2}}$.

4. In quantum mechanics, the turning points for a harmonic oscillator of energy $E_n$ are given by the points where:

Explanation: Turning points occur where the total energy equals the potential energy, i.e., $E_n = V(x) = \frac{1}{2}m\$\omega$^2x^2$.

5. The parity of the wave function $\psi_n(x)$ of a one-dimensional harmonic oscillator is:

Explanation: The wavefunctions of a harmonic oscillator alternate in parity, being even when $n$ is even and odd when $n$ is odd.

6. What is the commutator $[x, p]$ between the position operator $x$ and the momentum operator $p$?

Explanation: The canonical commutation relation in quantum mechanics is $[x, p] = i\hbar$.

Timed Topic Test

Attempt these questions under a ten-minute limit using a separate timer.

1. For a harmonic oscillator, if the angular frequency $\$\omega$$ is doubled while keeping mass constant, the zero-point energy becomes:

Explanation: Zero-point energy is $E_0 = \frac{1}{2}\hbar\$\omega$$. Since $E_0 \propto \$\omega$$, doubling $\$\omega$$ doubles the zero-point energy.

2. The Hermite polynomial $H_0(\$\alpha$ x)$ for the ground state of a harmonic oscillator is equal to:

Explanation: The zeroth Hermite polynomial is identically equal to 1 ($H_0(y) = 1$).

3. What is the Hermite polynomial $H_1(\$\alpha$ x)$ for the first excited state?

Explanation: The first-order Hermite polynomial is $H_1(y) = 2y$, so $H_1(\$\alpha$ x) = 2\$\alpha$ x$.

4. Which of the following operators can be expressed in terms of creation and annihilation operators to write the position operator $x$?

Explanation: The position operator in terms of ladder operators is $x = \sqrt{\frac{\hbar}{2m\$\omega$}}(a + a^\dagger)$.

5. The number operator $N$ is defined as $N = a^\dagger a$. What is the eigenvalue of $N$ for the state $\psi_n$?

Explanation: The number operator $N = a^\dagger a$ counts the quanta of excitation, yielding $N\psi_n = n\psi_n$.

6. What is the expectation value of the potential energy $\langle V \rangle$ for the ground state of a harmonic oscillator?

Explanation: By the virial theorem for harmonic potentials, the average kinetic energy equals the average potential energy, each being half of the total ground state energy $\frac{1}{2}E_0 = \frac{1}{4}\hbar\$\omega$$.

7. The classical frequency of a harmonic oscillator is $\nu = \frac{\$\omega$}{2\$\pi$}$. What is the energy spacing between adjacent quantum states in terms of $\nu$?

Explanation: Since $\hbar = \frac{h}{2pi}$ and $\$\omega$ = 2\$\pi$\nu$, the energy spacing is $\hbar\$\omega$ = \left(\frac{h}{2\$\pi$}\right)(2\$\pi$\nu) = h\nu$.

8. What is the effect of applying the creation operator $a^\dagger$ on the energy eigenstate $|n\rangle$?

Explanation: The creation operator action is given by $a^\dagger |n\rangle = \sqrt{n+1} |n+1\rangle$.

9. Which of the following quantum states of a harmonic oscillator represents the ground state wavefunction profile?

Explanation: The ground state wavefunction $\psi_0(x)$ is proportional to $e^{-\frac{m\$\omega$ x^2}{2\hbar}}$, which is a bell-shaped Gaussian curve.

10. Why is quantum mechanical tunneling possible in a harmonic oscillator potential?

Explanation: Tunneling occurs because the quantum wavefunction penetrates into the classically forbidden region ($E < V$) with a non-zero amplitude before decaying.
