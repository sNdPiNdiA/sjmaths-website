Concepts of Quantum Mechanics

Quantum mechanics provides the fundamental framework for describing physical systems at atomic and subatomic scales where classical mechanics fails. It introduces wave-particle duality, operator algebra, and probabilistic interpretation of physical observables.

Scope and prerequisites

TGT core

Basic postulates of wave-particle duality, de Broglie wavelength, Heisenberg's Uncertainty Principle, and elementary properties of wave functions.

PGT extension

Time-dependent and time-independent Schrödinger wave equations, expectation values, Hermitian operators, normalization conditions, and probability current density.

Prerequisites

  • Wave motion and superposition principle
  • Classical mechanics and Hamiltonian formulation
  • Differential equations and complex numbers

Wave-Particle Duality and de Broglie Hypothesis

  • All material particles in motion possess an associated wave nature with wavelength inversely proportional to linear momentum.
  • The de Broglie wavelength is expressed as $\lambda = h / p$, where h is Planck's constant.
  • This duality bridges corpuscular and undulatory descriptions, forming the foundation of matter waves.

Heisenberg's Uncertainty Principle

  • It is fundamentally impossible to simultaneously measure certain pairs of conjugate variables, such as position and momentum, with arbitrary precision.
  • The minimum uncertainty product is given by \Delta x \Delta p_x \ge \frac{\hbar}{2}.
  • Energy and time also satisfy an uncertainty relation \Delta E \Delta t \ge \frac{\hbar}{2}, explaining natural line broadening.

Schrödinger Wave Equation and Wave Function

  • The state of a quantum system is completely specified by a wave function \Psi(r, t), which contains all accessible physical information.
  • Max Born's statistical interpretation states that the absolute square |\Psi|^2 represents the probability density of finding a particle at a given point.
  • The time-independent Schrödinger equation governs stationary states of a quantum system under a time-independent potential.

Operators and Expectation Values

  • Every observable in quantum mechanics is represented by a linear, Hermitian operator acting on the wave function.
  • Eigenvalues of an operator correspond to the measurable values of the physical observable.
  • The expectation value \langle A \rangle represents the statistical average outcome of numerous measurements performed on identically prepared systems.

Derivations

Time-Independent Schrödinger Equation

Conditions: Conservative system with time-independent potential energy V(r)

  • Start with the general wave function separation of variables: \Psi(r, t) = \psi(r) e^{-i \frac{E}{\hbar} t}.
  • Substitute \Psi(r, t) into the time-dependent Schrödinger equation: - \frac{\hbar^2}{2m} \nabla^2 \Psi + V \Psi = i \hbar \frac{\partial \Psi}{\partial t}.
  • Evaluate the time derivative yielding E \psi(r) e^{-i \frac{E}{\hbar} t} on the right-hand side.
  • Divide both sides by the exponential term e^{-i \frac{E}{\hbar} t} to obtain the time-independent equation.

Result: - \frac{\hbar^2}{2m} \nabla^2 \psi(r) + V(r) \psi(r) = E \psi(r)

de Broglie Wavelength of an Accelerated Electron

Conditions: Non-relativistic electron accelerated from rest through potential difference V

  • Equate the kinetic energy gained to electrical potential energy: \frac{1}{2} m$v^2 = eV$.
  • Express momentum p in terms of kinetic energy: p = mv = \sqrt{2mE} = \sqrt{2meV}.
  • Substitute momentum into the de Broglie relation \lambda = \frac{h}{p}.
  • Substitute standard values of h, m, and e to obtain the working formula in terms of V.

Result: \lambda = \frac{12.27}{\sqrt{V}} \text{ \AA}

Solved numericals

TGT core

Calculate the de Broglie wavelength of a proton accelerated through a potential difference of 100 V. (Mass of proto$n = 1.67 \times 10^{-27} kg$, $h = 6.63 \times 10^{-34} J s)$

  • Identify formula: \lambda = \frac{h}{\sqrt{2meV}}.
  • Substitute values: $h = 6.63 \times 10^{-34}$, $m = 1.67 \times 10^{-27}$, $e = 1.6 \times 10^{-19}$, V = 100.
  • Calculate denominator: 2 \times (1.67 \times 10^{-27}) \times (1.6 \times 10^{-19}) \times $100 = 5.344 \times 10^{-44}$.
  • Take square root: \sqrt{5.344 \times 10^{-44}} = 2.31 \times 10^{-22}.
  • Compute \lambda: \frac{6.63 \times 10^{-34}}{2.31 \times 10^{-22}} = 2.87 \times 10^{-12}\text{ m}.

Answer: 2.87 \times 10^{-12} m (or 0.0287 \text{ \AA})

PGT extension

A particle of mass m is moving in a 1D infinite potential well of width L. If its wave function is given by \psi(x) = \sqrt{\frac{2}{L}} \sin\left(\frac{\pi x}{L}\right) for 0 \le x \le L, find its expectation value of position \langle x \rangle.

  • Set up the expectation integral: \langle x $\rangle = \int_{0}^{L} \psi^*(x) x \psi(x) \$, dx.
  • Substitute wave function: \langle x $\rangle = \frac{2}{L} \int_{0}^{L} x \sin^2\left(\frac{\pi x}{L}\right) dx$.
  • Use trigonometric identity \si$n^2\theta = \frac{1 - \cos(2\theta)}{2}: \langle x \rangle = \frac{2}{L} \int_{0}^{L} x \left[ \frac{1 - \cos(\frac{2\pi x}{L})}{2} \right] dx$.
  • Integrate term by term: \frac{1}{L} \left[ \frac{x^2}{2} \right]_0^L - \frac{1}{L} \int_{0}^{L} x \cos\left(\frac{2\pi x}{L}\right) dx.
  • The integral of x \cos(kx) over a full period evaluates to zero by symmetry.
  • Result simplifies to: \langle x $\rangle = \frac{1}{L} \left( \frac{L^2}{2} \right) = \frac{L}{2}$.

Answer: L / 2

PGT extension

An electron is confined to a region of size 1 \text{ \AA}. Estimate the minimum uncertainty in its velocity.

  • Use position uncertainty \Delta $x = 1 \text{ \AA} = 10^{-10} \text{ m}$.
  • Apply Heisenberg uncertainty principle for momentum: \Delta p_x \ge \frac{\hbar}{2 \Delta x}.
  • Substitute $\hbar = 1.054 \times 10^{-34} \text{ J s}: \Delta p_x = \frac{1.054 \times 10^{-34}}{2 \times 10^{-10}} = 5.27 \times 10^{-25} \text{ kg m s}^{-1}\text{}$.
  • Relate momentum uncertainty to velocity uncertainty: \Delta $v_x = \frac{\Delta p_x}{m}$.
  • Substitute electron mass $m = 9.1 \times 10^{-31} \text{ kg}: \Delta v_x = \frac{5.27 \times 10^{-25}}{9.1 \times 10^{-10}} \text{ (wait}$, mass is 9.1e-31)} \Rightarrow \frac{5.27 \times 10^{-25}}{9.1 \times 10^{-31}} = 5.79 \times 10^5 \text{ m s^{-1}}.

Answer: 5.79 \times 10^5 \text{ m s^{-1}}

Assumptions and limitations

  • Non-relativistic quantum mechanics assumes particle velocities are much smaller than the speed of light, failing for high-energy subatomic processes requiring Dirac's relativistic wave equation.
  • Potential functions V(r) are treated as externally prescribed classical fields, ignoring back-reaction of the quantum particle on the field in elementary formulations.

Common misconceptions

Common error: The wave function \Psi itself is a physical wave propagating in space like a sound or light wave.

Correct: The wave function is a mathematical probability amplitude whose square modulus represents probability density.

\Psi can take complex values and does not transport physical energy or mass directly.

Common error: Heisenberg's Uncertainty Principle is caused by mechanical disturbance of the instrument during measurement.

Correct: It is an inherent intrinsic property of quantum states arising from the non-commuting nature of operators.

Even with infinitely sensitive, hypothetical disturbance-free instruments, the fundamental wave packet spread persists.

Quick revision

  • Matter waves have wavelength $\lambda = h / p$, verified by Davisson and Germer.
  • Conjugate variables obey \Delta A \Delta B \ge \hbar / 2.
  • Stationary states satisfy the time-independent Schrödinger equation H\psi = E\psi.
  • The wave function must be single-valued, continuous, and normalizable.

Must remember

  • The de Broglie wavelength is inversely proportional to the linear momentum of the particle: $\lambda = \frac{h}{p}$.
  • Heisenberg's Uncertainty Principle states that the minimum uncertainty product for position and momentum is $\Delta x \Delta p_x \ge \frac{\hbar}{2}$.
  • Max Born's interpretation dictates that the probability density of finding a particle is given by $|\psi(x)|^2 = \psi^*(x)\psi(x)$.
  • A physically acceptable wave function must be single-valued, continuous, finite, and square-integrable (normalized).
  • Every measurable physical observable in quantum mechanics is represented by a linear, Hermitian operator whose eigenvalues yield possible measurement outcomes.

Glossary

de Broglie WavelengthThe wavelength $\lambda$ associated with a moving material particle, given by $\lambda = \frac{h}{mv}$.
Wave FunctionA complex mathematical function $\Psi(r, t)$ that contains all accessible dynamical information of a quantum system.
Hermitian OperatorAn operator $\hat{A}$ satisfying $\int \psi_1^* \hat{A} \psi_2 dV = \int (\hat{A} \psi_1)^* \psi_2 dV$, ensuring real eigenvalues.
Expectation ValueThe statistical average of multiple measurements of an observable on identically prepared systems, denoted $\langle A \rangle$.
Zero-Point EnergyThe lowest possible energy of a quantum system, non-zero in systems like the harmonic oscillator due to uncertainty principles.

Practice Quiz

Choose an option to reveal the explanation.

1. What is the de Broglie wavelength of an electron accelerated through a potential difference of $V$ volts?

Explanation: Using $\lambda = \frac{h}{\sqrt{2meV}}$, substituting constants yields $\lambda \approx \frac{12.27}{\sqrt{V}}$ Angstroms for an electron.

2. Which of the following conditions must be satisfied by a physically acceptable wave function $\psi(x)$?

Explanation: A valid wave function must be single-valued, continuous, finite, and square-integrable to ensure a consistent probability interpretation.

3. The momentum operator in one-dimensional position space is represented as:

Explanation: The quantum mechanical momentum operator in position space is $\hat{p}_x = -i\hbar \frac{\partial}{\partial x}$.

4. Heisenberg's Uncertainty Principle sets a lower limit for the product of uncertainties in position and momentum as:

Explanation: The standard minimum uncertainty relation is $\Delta x \Delta p_x \ge \frac{\hbar}{2}$, where $\hbar = \frac{h}{2\pi}$.

5. What does the absolute square of the wave function, $|\psi(x)|^2$, represent according to Max Born?

Explanation: Max Born's statistical interpretation states that $|\psi(x)|^2$ represents the probability density of finding the particle at position $x$.

6. The time-independent Schrödinger equation for a 1D system is given by:

Explanation: The time-independent Schrödinger equation relates the Hamiltonian operator acting on $\psi$ to the total energy $E$ times $\psi$.

7. For a normalized wave function $\psi(x)$, the normalization condition is expressed as:

Explanation: Normalization requires that the total probability of finding the particle somewhere in all space equals 1: $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} |\psi(x)|^2 dx = 1$.

8. Which mathematical property must operators corresponding to observable physical quantities possess?

Explanation: Operators representing physical observables must be Hermitian so that their eigenvalues (measurable values) are strictly real numbers.

9. What is the de Broglie wavelength of a neutron of mass $m$ and kinetic energy $E$?

Explanation: Since $p = \sqrt{2mE}$, the de Broglie wavelength is $\lambda = \frac{h}{p} = \frac{h}{\sqrt{2mE}}$.

10. The energy-time uncertainty relation is given by:

Explanation: Energy and time satisfy the uncertainty relation $\Delta E \Delta t \ge \frac{\hbar}{2}$, which accounts for natural line broadening in excited states.

11. The expectation value of an observable represented by operator $\hat{A}$ for a normalized state $\psi$ is:

Explanation: The expectation value is defined as $\langle A \rangle = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \psi^* \hat{A} \psi \, dx$.

12. Why is the wave function $\Psi$ itself generally a complex quantity?

Explanation: Complex wave functions (containing phase factors like $e^{i(kx-\omega t)}$) are necessary to describe wave propagation, momentum states, and interference.

13. If a particle's position is measured with complete certainty ($\Delta x = 0$), what is the uncertainty in its momentum ($\Delta p_x$)?

Explanation: From $\Delta x \Delta p_x \ge \frac{\hbar}{2}$, if $\Delta x \to 0$, then $\Delta p_x \to \infty$.

14. What is the physical significance of the Schrödinger wave equation?

Explanation: The Schrödinger equation is the fundamental equation of motion in quantum mechanics, governing the spatial and temporal evolution of wave functions.

15. Which of the following statements regarding de Broglie waves is INCORRECT?

Explanation: De Broglie matter waves are not electromagnetic waves; they are probability matter waves associated with moving mass particles.

PYQ Patterns

These are pattern-based practice questions. Authentic year labels are only used when verified from a source.

1. The de Broglie wavelength of an $\alpha$ particle accelerated through a potential difference of $V$ volts is proportional to:

Explanation: Since $\lambda = \frac{h}{\sqrt{2mqV}}$, and for an $\alpha$ particle $m$ and $q$ are constant, $\lambda \propto \frac{1}{\sqrt{V}}$ or $V^{-1/2}$.

2. If the position uncertainty of an electron is equal to its de Broglie wavelength, the minimum uncertainty in its momentum $\Delta p_x$ is of the order of:

Explanation: From $\Delta x \Delta p_x \approx \hbar$ and $\lambda = \frac{h}{p} = \frac{2\pi \hbar}{p}$, setting $\Delta x = \lambda$ gives $\Delta p_x \approx \frac{p}{2\pi}$, which is of the same order as momentum $p$.

3. Which operator corresponds to the total energy (Hamiltonian) of a conservative system in quantum mechanics?

Explanation: The Hamiltonian operator is the sum of the kinetic energy operator and potential energy: $\hat{H} = \hat{T} + \hat{V} = -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m} \frac{d^2}{dx^2} + V(x)$.

4. The expectation value of momentum $\langle \hat{p}_x \rangle$ for a particle described by a real wave function $\psi(x)$ is always:

Explanation: If $\psi$ is real, $\psi^* = \psi$. The integral for momentum expectation value involves an antisymmetric derivative operator resulting in zero for real wave functions.

5. An electron and a proton have the same kinetic energy. Which of them has a greater de Broglie wavelength?

Explanation: Since $\lambda = \frac{h}{\sqrt{2mE}}$ and $m_{\text{electron}} < m_{\text{proton}}$, the electron has a smaller mass, resulting in a larger de Broglie wavelength.

6. The commutation relation $[x, \hat{p}_x]$ between position $x$ and momentum operator $\hat{p}_x$ is equal to:

Explanation: Evaluating $[x, \hat{p}_x]\psi = x(-i\hbar \frac{\partial}{\partial x}\psi) - (-i\hbar \frac{\partial}{\partial x}(x\psi)) = i\hbar \psi$, so $[x, \hat{p}_x] = i\hbar$.

Timed Topic Test

Attempt these questions under a ten-minute limit using a separate timer.

1. What is the de Broglie wavelength of a thermal neutron at temperature $T$ ($k_B$ is Boltzmann's constant)?

Explanation: Kinetic energy of a thermal neutron in 3D is $E = \frac{3}{2}k_BT$, so $p = \sqrt{2m(\frac{3}{2}k_BT)} = \sqrt{3mk_BT}$, giving $\lambda = \frac{h}{\sqrt{3mk_BT}}$.

2. If a wave function is given by $\psi(x) = A e^{ikx}$ in a region, what is the probability current density?

Explanation: Probability current density is $J = \frac{\hbar}{2mi}(\psi^* \frac{\partial \psi}{\partial x} - \psi \frac{\partial \psi^*}{\partial x}) = \frac{\hbar k}{m} |A|^2$.

3. The eigenvalues of a Hermitian operator are always:

Explanation: A defining property of Hermitian operators in quantum mechanics is that all their eigenvalues are strictly real, corresponding to measurable physical quantities.

4. An excited atom has a lifetime of $10^{-8}\text{ s}$. The minimum uncertainty in its energy state is approximately:

Explanation: Using $\Delta E \ge \frac{\hbar}{2 \Delta t}$, with $\hbar \approx 1.054 \times 10^{-34} \text{ J s}$ and $\Delta t = 10^{-8} \text{ s}$, $\Delta E \ge \frac{1.054 \times 10^{-34}}{2 \times 10^{-8}} \approx 5.27 \times 10^{-27} \text{ J}$.

5. Which of the following functions cannot be a valid wave function across all space?

Explanation: $\psi(x) = \frac{A}{x}$ goes to infinity at $x = 0$, violating the finiteness and square-integrability requirements of a valid wave function.

6. If $\hat{A}$ and $\hat{B}$ are two commuting observables (i.e., $[\hat{A}, \hat{B}] = 0$), what physical implication does this have?

Explanation: Commuting observables can be measured simultaneously with arbitrary precision and share a common set of eigenfunctions.

7. The phase velocity $v_p$ and group velocity $v_g$ of a de Broglie wave associated with a particle of velocity $v$ satisfy:

Explanation: The group velocity of de Broglie waves equals the classical particle velocity ($v_g = v$), whereas the phase velocity $v_p = \frac{c^2}{v}$ exceeds the speed of light.

8. What is the normalization constant $A$ for the wave function $\psi(x) = A e^{-ax}$ defined over the domain $x \in [0, \infty)$?

Explanation: Integrating $\int_{0}^{\infty} |A|^2 e^{-2ax} dx = |A|^2 \frac{1}{2a} = 1 \implies A = \sqrt{2a}$.

9. According to the correspondence principle, quantum mechanics must reduce to classical mechanics in what limit?

Explanation: In the limit of large quantum numbers (macroscopic systems), quantum mechanical predictions merge smoothly with classical physics.

10. The parity operator $\hat{P}$ reflects spatial coordinates ($x \to -x$). For a wave function with even parity, $\hat{P}\psi(x)$ equals:

Explanation: An even parity wave function is symmetric under spatial inversion, meaning $\psi(-x) = \psi(x)$.
