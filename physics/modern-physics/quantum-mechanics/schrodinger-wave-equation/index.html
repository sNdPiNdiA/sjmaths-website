Schrödinger Wave Equation

The Schrödinger wave equation is a fundamental partial differential equation that describes how the quantum state of a physical system changes over time. It forms the foundation of non-relativistic quantum mechanics, replacing Newtonian mechanics for microscopic systems.

Scope and prerequisites

TGT core

Basic understanding of time-independent Schrödinger wave equation, physical interpretation of wave function &psi;, probability density |&psi;|&sup2;, and normalization condition.

PGT extension

Derivation of time-dependent and time-independent Schrödinger equations, operator formalism, expectation values, and application to particle in a 1D box.

Prerequisites

  • De Broglie wavelength and wave-particle duality
  • Partial differentiation and separation of variables
  • Complex numbers and Euler's formula

Time-Dependent Schrödinger Equation

  • Governs the evolution of a quantum state over time for a system under potential energy V(x,t).
  • Linear and homogeneous differential equation ensuring the superposition principle holds in quantum mechanics.
  • Expressed mathematically as ihbar \frac{\partial}{\partial t} \Psi(x,t) = \left( -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m} \frac{\partial^2}{\partial x^2} + V(x,t) \right) \Psi(x,t).

Time-Independent Schrödinger Equation

  • Applicable when the potential energy V(x) is independent of time.
  • Obtained via the method of separation of variables: \Psi(x,t) = \psi(x)\phi(t).
  • Yields stationary states where probability density |\Psi(x,t)|^2 = |\psi(x)|^2 is invariant with time.

Physical Interpretation of the Wave Function (&psi;)

  • The wave function itself is generally a complex quantity and does not directly correspond to a measurable physical quantity.
  • Max Born's statistical interpretation states that the squared modulus |\psi(x)|^2 represents the probability density of finding the particle at position x.
  • To be physically acceptable, ψ must be single-valued, continuous, finite everywhere, and normalizable.

Normalization and Expectation Values

  • The total probability of finding the particle somewhere in space must equal 1, expressed as \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} |\psi(x)|^2 dx = 1.
  • An operator A corresponds to every observable, and its expectation value is given by \langle A \rangle = \int \psi^* \hat{A} \psi dx.
  • Eigenvalues of the Hamiltonian operator represent the allowed energy levels of the quantum system.

Derivations

Derivation of Time-Independent Schrödinger Equation

Conditions: Non-relativistic particle of mass m in a time-independent potential V(x)

  • Start with the free-particle de Broglie wave expression: \Psi(x,t) = \psi(x) e^{-iEt/\hbar}.
  • Differentiate the wave function twice with respect to spatial coordinate x to get \frac{d^2\psi}{dx^2} = - \frac{p^2}{\hbar^2} \psi.
  • Substitute the momentum relation using total energy E = \frac{p^2}{2m} + V(x) to yield the final differential equation.

Result: \frac{d^2\psi}{dx^2} + \frac{2m}{\hbar^2}(E - V)\psi = 0

Particle in a 1D Infinite Potential Well

Conditions: V(x) = 0 for 0 < x < L, and V(x) = &infin; outside this region

  • Inside the box, V = 0, so the time-independent equation reduces to \frac{d^2\psi}{dx^2} + k^2\psi = 0 where k^2 = \frac{2mE}{\hbar^2}.
  • Apply boundary conditions: \psi(0) = 0 and \psi(L) = 0, which eliminates the cosine term and gives \psi(x) = A \sin(kx).
  • Applying \psi(L) = 0 yields kL = n\pi, leading to quantized energy states E_n = \frac{n^2 \pi^2 \hbar^2}{2mL^2}.

Result: \psi_n(x) = \sqrt{\frac{2}{L}} \sin\left(\frac{n\pi x}{L}\right), \quad E_n = \frac{n^2h^2}{8mL^2}

Solved numericals

TGT core

An electron is confined in a 1D box of length 1 nm. Calculate the energy of the electron in its ground state.

  • Identify given values: L = 1 nm = 10^{-9} m, mass of electron m = 9.1 \times 10^{-31} kg, h = 6.626 \times 10^{-34} J s, n = 1.
  • Substitute into energy formula: E_1 = \frac{1^2 \times (6.626 \times 10^{-34})^2}{8 \times (9.1 \times 10^{-31}) \times (10^{-9})^2}.
  • Calculate value: E_1 = \frac{4.39 \times 10^{-67}}{7.28 \times 10^{-48}} = 6.03 \times 10^{-20} J.

Answer: 6.03 \times 10^{-20} J (or 0.38 eV)

PGT extension

The wave function of a particle in a 1D box of length L is given by \psi(x) = A \sin(\frac{3\pi x}{L}) for 0 < x < L. Find the normalization constant A.

  • Apply normalization condition: \int_{0}^{L} |\psi(x)|^2 dx = 1.
  • Substitute wave function: \int_{0}^{L} A^2 \sin^2\left(\frac{3\pi x}{L}\right) dx = 1.
  • Evaluate integral: A^2 \left( \frac{L}{2} \right) = 1 \implies A = \sqrt{\frac{2}{L}}.

Answer: \sqrt{\frac{2}{L}}

PGT extension

Find the expectation value of position \langle x \rangle for a particle in the ground state of an infinite potential well of width L (0 \le x \le L).

  • Write formula for expectation value: \langle x \rangle = \int_{0}^{L} \psi^* x \psi dx.
  • Substitute ground state wave function \psi = \sqrt{\frac{2}{L}} \sin(\frac{\pi x}{L}): \langle x \rangle = \frac{2}{L} \int_{0}^{L} x \sin^2\left(\frac{\pi x}{L}\right) dx.
  • Use standard integral result \int x \sin^2(cx) dx = \frac{x^2}{4} - \frac{x \sin(2cx)}{4c} - \frac{\cos(2cx)}{8c^2} evaluated from 0 to L to obtain L/2.

Answer: \frac{L}{2}

Assumptions and limitations

  • Assumes non-relativistic speeds (v << c); fails for high-energy particles where the Dirac equation is required.
  • Assumes potentials are time-independent or vary smoothly in non-relativistic quantum domains.
  • Does not inherently account for electron spin without incorporating Pauli spin matrices (Pauli equation).

Common misconceptions

Common error: The wave function &psi;(x,t) is a directly measurable physical wave like a sound or light wave.

Correct: The wave function is a mathematical construct representing probability amplitudes; only its squared modulus has physical meaning.

Wave functions can take complex values, whereas physical measurements are always real numbers.

Common error: A particle in a box can have zero energy in its ground state (n = 0).

Correct: The minimum energy (ground state) for a particle in a box corresponds to n = 1, giving a non-zero energy.

Zero energy would violate the Heisenberg Uncertainty Principle, as it would imply exact localization and zero momentum simultaneously.

Quick revision

  • The Schrödinger equation is the quantum mechanical equivalent of Newton's second law in classical mechanics.
  • Stationary states have time-independent probability densities.
  • The wave function must be single-valued, continuous, and finite for all space.
  • Ground state energy of a particle in a 1D box is proportional to 1/L^2.

Must remember

  • The time-dependent Schrödinger equation is given by $i\hbar \frac{\partial \Psi}{\partial t} = \left( -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m} \frac{\partial^2}{\partial x^2} + V \right) \Psi$.
  • Max Born's interpretation states that $|\psi(x)|^2$ represents the probability density of finding a particle at position $x$.
  • For a wave function to be physically acceptable, it must be single-valued, continuous, finite everywhere, and normalizable.
  • The normalization condition requires that $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} |\psi(x)|^2 dx = 1$.
  • The momentum operator in position representation is defined as $\hat{p}_x = -i\hbar \frac{\partial}{\partial x}$.
  • The energy eigenvalues for a particle in a 1D infinite potential well of width $L$ are given by $E_n = \frac{n^2 h^2}{8 m L^2}$ where $n = 1, 2, 3, \dots$.

Glossary

Schrödinger Wave EquationA fundamental equation in quantum mechanics that describes how the quantum state of a physical system changes in time.
Wave Function ($\Psi$)A mathematical function describing the quantum state of a system, whose squared modulus gives probability density.
Hamiltonian Operator ($\hat{H}$)The operator corresponding to the total energy of the system, combining kinetic and potential energy operators.
Expectation ValueThe probabilistic expected value of the result of a measurement of an observable, calculated via $\langle A \rangle = \int \psi^* \hat{A} \psi dx$.
Stationary StateA quantum state described by the time-independent Schrödinger equation where probability density is invariant with time.

Practice Quiz

Choose an option to reveal the explanation.

1. What is the physical significance of the squared modulus of the wave function $|\psi(x)|^2$ according to Max Born?

Explanation: Max Born's statistical interpretation states that $|\psi(x)|^2$ represents the probability density of locating the particle at position $x$.

2. Which of the following conditions is NOT required for a physically acceptable wave function?

Explanation: Wave functions can be complex quantities; they do not need to be purely real, but they must be single-valued, continuous, finite, and normalizable.

3. What is the momentum operator $\hat{p}_x$ in the position representation?

Explanation: The quantum mechanical momentum operator along the $x$-axis is given by $\hat{p}_x = -i\hbar \frac{\partial}{\partial x}$.

4. For a normalized wave function $\psi(x)$, what does the integral $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} |\psi(x)|^2 dx$ evaluate to?

Explanation: Normalization means the total probability of finding the particle somewhere in space is equal to $1$, hence the integral equals $1$.

5. What is the ground state energy of a particle of mass $m$ confined in a 1D infinite potential well of width $L$?

Explanation: Using the energy eigenvalue formula $E_n = \frac{n^2 h^2}{8 m L^2}$, the ground state corresponds to $n = 1$, giving $E_1 = \frac{h^2}{8mL^2}$.

6. Which differential equation represents the time-independent Schrödinger equation for a 1D system?

Explanation: The time-independent Schrödinger equation rearranges to $\frac{d^2\psi}{dx^2} + \frac{2m}{\hbar^2}(E - V)\psi = 0$.

7. What is the Hamiltonian operator $\hat{H}$ for a non-relativistic particle in one dimension with potential energy $V(x)$?

Explanation: The Hamiltonian operator is the sum of kinetic energy operator ($-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{d^2}{dx^2}$) and potential energy ($V(x)$).

8. Why can a particle in a box not have a zero ground state energy ($n = 0$)?

Explanation: If $n=0$, energy would be zero, implying zero momentum and complete localization, violating the Heisenberg Uncertainty Principle.

9. How is the time-dependent Schrödinger equation obtained or related to stationary states when $V$ is independent of time?

Explanation: The time-independent equation is derived from the time-dependent equation using separation of variables: $\Psi(x,t) = \psi(x)\$\phi$(t)$.

10. What is the expectation value formula for an observable represented by operator $\hat{A}$?

Explanation: The expectation value is given by the integral $\langle A \rangle = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \psi^* \hat{A} \psi dx$.

11. What is the spatial wave function $\psi_n(x)$ for a particle in a 1D box of length $L$ from $x=0$ to $x=L$?

Explanation: The normalized wave function for a particle in a 1D box with rigid walls at $x=0$ and $x=L$ is $\psi_n(x) = \sqrt{\frac{2}{L}} \sin\left(\frac{n\$\pi$ x}{L}\right)$.

12. Which property of the time-dependent Schrödinger equation ensures that linear combinations of solutions are also solutions?

Explanation: The time-dependent Schrödinger equation is a linear and homogeneous differential equation, which guarantees the validity of the superposition principle.

13. What are the units of the spatial wave function $\psi(x)$ in one dimension?

Explanation: Since $\int |\psi(x)|^2 dx = 1$ is dimensionless, $|\psi(x)|^2$ has units of $\text{m}^{-1}$, meaning $\psi(x)$ has units of $\text{m}^{-1/2}$.

14. In stationary states, how does the probability density $|\Psi(x,t)|^2$ behave with respect to time?

Explanation: For stationary states, the time-dependent phase factor cancels out in the squared modulus, making the probability density $|\psi(x)|^2$ invariant with time.

15. What does the Hamiltonian operator represent when acting on a wave function of a stationary state?

Explanation: The time-independent Schrödinger equation can be written as $\hat{H}\psi = E\psi$, meaning the Hamiltonian acting on $\psi$ yields the total energy $E$ times $\psi$.

PYQ Patterns

These are pattern-based practice questions. Authentic year labels are only used when verified from a source.

1. The time-independent Schrödinger wave equation for a particle of mass $m$ and total energy $E$ in a potential $V$ is expressed as:

Explanation: Rearranging the terms from $\hat{H}\psi = E\psi$ yields $\frac{d^2\psi}{dx^2} + \frac{2m}{\hbar^2}(E - V)\psi = 0$.

2. If a wave function $\psi(x)$ is given for a 1D box of length $L$ as $\psi(x) = \sqrt{\frac{2}{L}}\sin\left(\frac{\$\pi$ x}{L}\right)$, what is the probability of finding the particle in the region $0 \le x \le L/2$?

Explanation: Integrating $|\psi(x)|^2 = \frac{2}{L}\sin^2(\$\pi$ x / L)$ from $0$ to $L/2$ yields $\frac{1}{2}$, representing half of the total probability $1$.

3. Which of the following operators is Hermitian?

Explanation: In quantum mechanics, all observables—including position, momentum, and Hamiltonian—are represented by Hermitian operators whose eigenvalues are real.

4. The energy of the second excited state ($n = 3$) of a particle in a 1D box of length $L$ is:

Explanation: The ground state is $n=1$, the first excited state is $n=2$, and the second excited state is $n=3$. Substituting $n=3$ into $E_n = \frac{n^2 h^2}{8mL^2}$ gives $\frac{9h^2}{8mL^2}$.

5. What is the commutator $[x, \hat{p}_x]$ between the position operator $x$ and the momentum operator $\hat{p}_x$?

Explanation: Evaluating $[x, \hat{p}_x]\psi = \left(x(-i\hbar\frac{\partial}{\partial x}) - (-i\hbar\frac{\partial}{\partial x})x\right)\psi = i\hbar \psi$, so $[x, \hat{p}_x] = i\hbar$.

6. For a free particle where potential $V(x) = 0$, the time-independent Schrödinger equation becomes:

Explanation: Setting $V=0$ in $\frac{d^2\psi}{dx^2} + \frac{2m}{\hbar^2}E\psi = 0$ yields the standard harmonic wave equation $\frac{d^2\psi}{dx^2} + k^2\psi = 0$ with $k = \sqrt{2mE}/\hbar$.

Timed Topic Test

Attempt these questions under a ten-minute limit using a separate timer.

1. If a wave function is given by $\psi(x) = A e^{-ax}$, over $0 \le x < \infty$, what is the normalization constant $A$?

Explanation: Integrating $\int_{0}^{\infty} |A|^2 e^{-2ax} dx = |A|^2 \frac{1}{2a} = 1$, which gives $A = \sqrt{2a}$.

2. What does an eigenvalue of the Hamiltonian operator represent in quantum mechanics?

Explanation: Eigenvalues of the Hamiltonian operator $\hat{H}\psi_n = E_n\psi_n$ correspond to the allowed quantized energy levels of the system.

3. The expectation value of momentum $\langle p_x \qangle$ for a stationary state wave function of a particle in a 1D box is:

Explanation: Stationary states in a real potential box can be expressed as standing waves composed of equal and opposite momentum components, making the expectation value of momentum zero.

4. Which operator corresponds to kinetic energy in one dimension?

Explanation: The kinetic energy operator is derived from classical momentum $p^2/2m$ by substituting $\hat{p} = -i\hbar \frac{d}{dx}$, giving $-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m} \frac{d^2}{dx^2}$.

5. What is the number of nodes (excluding boundaries) in the wave function of the $n$-th energy state for a particle in a 1D box?

Explanation: The wave function $\psi_n(x) = \sqrt{\frac{2}{L}} \sin(n\$\pi$ x / L)$ has $n-1$ interior nodes where the wave function vanishes.

6. If the time-independent wave function of a system is real, what can be said about its probability current density?

Explanation: Probability current is proportional to $\psi^* \frac{d\psi}{dx} - \psi \frac{d\psi^*}{dx}$. If $\psi$ is real, this expression evaluates to zero.

7. What is the dimension of the wave function $\Psi(x,t)$ in three dimensions?

Explanation: In 3D, the normalization integral is $\int |\Psi|^2 dV = 1$. Since volume element has units $\text{m}^3$, $|\Psi|^2$ has units $\text{m}^{-3}$, making $\Psi$ have units $\text{m}^{-3/2}$.

8. According to Ehrenfest's theorem, how does the expectation value of quantum operators relate to classical mechanics?

Explanation: Ehrenfest's theorem proves that expectation values of quantum mechanical operators satisfy equations that are identical to classical Newtonian equations of motion.

9. Which of the following potentials allows bound state solutions for all positive energies?

Explanation: A free particle ($V=0$) admits continuous energy eigenvalues and scattering/unbound wave solutions for all positive energies.

10. What is the primary reason wave functions must be continuous and have continuous derivatives everywhere (except where $V$ is infinite)?

Explanation: Continuity of $\psi$ and its first derivative ensures that the Schrödinger equation yields a finite second derivative $\frac{d^2\psi}{dx^2}$, corresponding to finite kinetic energy.
