Elementary Reactor Physics

Elementary reactor physics deals with the neutron chain reaction, moderation, and the steady-state conditions necessary for controlled nuclear fission energy generation. It utilizes neutron transport and diffusion parameters to determine critical sizes of nuclear reactors.

Scope and prerequisites

Basic concepts of nuclear fission, chain reactions, role of moderators (graphite, heavy water), control rods, and prompt versus delayed neutrons.

Multi-group diffusion theory, neutron life cycle (four-factor formula), effective multiplication factor, Fermi age theory, and reactor kinetics.

Prerequisites

  • Binding energy per nucleon curve and mass defect
  • Nuclear cross-section and mean free path
  • Neutron thermalization and Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution

Nuclear Fission and Neutron Production

  • Absorption of a thermal neutron by a fissile nucleus such as 235U produces an excited compound nucleus 236U* which undergoes asymmetric fission.
  • Fission releases an average of ~2.4 to 2.5 fast neutrons per absorption event alongside fission fragments and ~200 MeV of energy.
  • Most neutrons released are prompt (emitted within 10-14 s), while a small fraction (~0.65% for 235U) are delayed neutrons emitted by fission product decay.

The Four-Factor Formula (Fermi's Theory)

  • The infinite multiplication factor (kinf) is the ratio of thermal neutrons produced in generation (n+1) to thermal neutrons in generation (n) in an infinite medium.
  • It is expressed as kinf = ε p f η, where ε is the fast fission factor, p is the resonance escape probability, f is the thermal utilization factor, and η is the reproduction factor.
  • For a finite reactor, leakage must be accounted for, yielding the effective multiplication factor keff = kinf Lf L_t, where L_f and L_t are non-leakage probabilities for fast and thermal neutrons respectively.

Neutron Moderation and Thermalization

  • Fast neutrons lose kinetic energy predominantly through elastic scattering collisions with light moderator nuclei like hydrogen, deuterium, or carbon.
  • Good moderators possess a high scattering cross-section (σs), a low absorption cross-section (σa), and a large average logarithmic energy decrement per collision (ξ).
  • Thermal neutrons reach thermal equilibrium with the moderator atoms at ambient temperature, possessing a most probable energy of ~0.025 eV.

Reactor Control and Kinetics

  • Reactor operation is governed by the effective multiplication factor: keff = 1 (critical, steady state), keff > 1 (supercritical, power rising), and keff < 1 (subcritical, power falling).
  • Control rods composed of strong neutron absorbers like boron, cadmium, or hafnium are inserted or withdrawn to adjust the thermal utilization factor (f) and maintain criticality.
  • Delayed neutrons play a vital role in reactor safety by extending the effective neutron generation time, making manual and automatic control feasible.

Derivations

Derivation of the Four-Factor Formula for Infinite Multiplication Factor

Conditions: Infinite thermal reactor containing natural or enriched uranium fuel and a moderator.

  • Consider NT thermal neutrons initiating fission in generation n. These neutrons produce NT η fast neutrons, where η is the number of fast neutrons produced per thermal neutron absorbed in fuel.
  • Due to fast fission of 238U by unmoderated neutrons, the total number of fast neutrons becomes NT η ε, where ε is the fast fission factor (ε > 1).
  • These fast neutrons slow down through the resonance absorption region of 238U. A fraction p, called the resonance escape probability, avoids capture and reaches thermal energies.
  • The number of thermalized neutrons is therefore NT η ε p.
  • Out of these thermalized neutrons, a fraction f (thermal utilization factor) is absorbed specifically by the fuel rather than the moderator, structural materials, or fission products.
  • The total number of thermal neutrons in generation (n+1) is NT+1 = NT ε p f η.
  • By definition, the infinite multiplication factor kinf is NT+1} / NT.

Result: $k_inf = \epsilon \cdot p \cdot f \cdot \eta$

Condition for Criticality in a Finite Reactor

Conditions: Homogeneous thermal reactor considering neutron leakage.

  • The effective multiplication factor keff accounts for both neutron production/absorption in an infinite medium and neutron leakage from the finite boundary.
  • Let Lf be the probability that a fast neutron does not leak out during slowing down, and Lt be the probability that a thermal neutron does not leak out before absorption.
  • Thus, keff = k_{inf} cdot L_f cdot L_t.
  • Using Fermi age theory, the fast non-leakage probability is approximated as $L_f = e^{-B^2 \tau}$, where B2 is the buckling of the reactor and τ is the Fermi age.
  • Using one-group diffusion theory, the thermal non-leakage probability is approximated as $L_t = \frac{1}{1 + B^2 L_t^2}$, where Lt is the thermal diffusion length.
  • Combining these yields the critical equation: $k_{inf} = (1 + B^2 \tau)(1 + B^2 L_t^2) e^{B^2 \tau} (or simplified$ as k_{inf} / (1 + B^2 $M^2) = 1$ where M is the migration length).

Result: $k_{eff} = \frac{k_{inf}}{(1 + B^2 L_t^2)(1 + B^2 L_f^2)} = 1$

Solved numericals

TGT core

In a thermal nuclear reactor, if the reproduction factor η is 1.33, the thermal utilization factor f is 0.85, the resonance escape probability p is 0.90, and the fast fission factor ε is 1.03, calculate the infinite multiplication factor (k_inf).

  • Write down the formula for the infinite multiplication factor: $k_{inf} = \epsilon \cdot p \cdot f \cdot \eta$
  • Substitute the given values: $\epsilon = 1.03$, p = 0.90, f = 0.85, $\eta = 1.33$
  • Calculate the product: $k_{inf} = 1.03 \times 0.90 \times 0.85 \times 1.33$
  • Compute final numerical value: k_{inf} approx 1.045

Answer: 1.045

PGT extension

A large thermal reactor has an infinite multiplication factor $k_{inf} = 1.08$. The thermal diffusion length L_t is 5 cm and the Fermi age τ is 35 cm2. Assuming critical condition ($k_{eff} = 1)$ and using one-group diffusion theory with L_f ≈ 1 - B2τ, find the geometric buckling B2.

  • Write the critical equation relation: $k_{eff} = \frac{k_{inf}}{(1 + B^2 L_t^2)(1 + B^2 \tau)} = 1$
  • Rearrange to get: (1 + B^2 L_t^2)(1 + B^2 \tau) = k_{inf}
  • Expand the binomial product, neglecting higher-order terms in B2 if B2 is small: 1 + B^2(L_t^2 + \tau) = k_{inf}
  • Substitute M2 = L_t^2 + $\tau = 5^2 + 35 = 25 + 35 = 60 cm^{2}$
  • Substitute $k_{inf} = 1.08: 1 + B^2 (60) = 1.08$
  • Solve for B2: 60 $B^2 = 0.08 => B^2 = \frac{0.08}{60} = 1.333 \times 10^{-3} cm^{-2}$

Answer: 1.333 \times 10^{-3} cm^{-2}

PGT extension

Calculate the reactivity ρ of a reactor whose effective multiplication factor k_{eff} is 1.05. Express your answer in dimensionless units and in percent.

  • Recall the formula for reactivity: $\rho = \frac{k_{eff} - 1}{k_{eff}}$
  • Substitute the given value: $k_{eff} = 1.05$
  • Calculate the numerator: 1.05 - 1 = 0.05
  • Divide by k_{eff}: \rho = \frac{0.05}{1.05} approx 0.0476
  • Convert to percentage: 4.76%

Answer: 0.0476 (or 4.76%)

Assumptions and limitations

  • One-group diffusion theory assumes all neutrons are monoenergetic at thermal energy, neglecting the thermal Maxwellian energy spectrum.
  • Fermi age theory assumes slowing down occurs by continuous moderation (continuous slowing down approximation), which is inaccurate for hydrogen where energy loss per collision is large.

Common misconceptions

Common error: Prompt neutrons alone determine the steady-state control time scale of a nuclear reactor.

Correct: Delayed neutrons dictate the effective reactor period and make safe mechanical control possible.

Prompt neutron generation time is on the order of microseconds, making manual or mechanical feedback far too slow without delayed neutrons.

Common error: A reactor with k_{inf} > 1 is always critical regardless of its physical dimensions.

Correct: A reactor must exceed its critical size so that neutron production overcomes leakage ($k_{eff} = 1)$.

If a reactor is too small, high neutron leakage causes k_{eff} to drop below 1 even if k_{inf} > 1.

Quick revision

  • Fission of 235U produces ~2.5 neutrons per absorption and releases ~200 MeV energy.
  • Four-factor formula: $k_{inf} = \epsilon p f \eta$.
  • Criticality condition: $k_{eff} = 1; Subcritical: k_{eff} < 1; Supercritical: k_{eff} > 1$.
  • Good moderators have high scattering cross-section, low absorption cross-section, and large ξ.
  • Migration length squared equals thermal diffusion length squared plus Fermi age: M2 = L_t^2 + \tau.

Must remember

  • Absorption of a thermal neutron by $^{235}\text{U}$ produces an excited compound nucleus $^{236}\text{U}^*$ which undergoes asymmetric fission, releasing ~200 MeV and an average of 2.4 to 2.5 fast neutrons.
  • Fermi's Four-Factor Formula for an infinite medium is given by $k_{inf} = \epsilon \cdot p \cdot f \cdot \eta$.
  • For a finite reactor, leakage must be factored in, yielding the effective multiplication factor $k_{eff} = k_{inf} \cdot L_f \cdot L_t$.
  • Reactivity is defined as $\rho = \frac{k_{eff} - 1}{k_{eff}}$, where $\rho = 0$ corresponds to critical ($k_{eff} = 1$), $\rho > 0$ to supercritical, and $\rho < 0$ to subcritical.
  • Delayed neutrons (~0.65% for $^{235}\text{U}$) extend the effective generation time, making reactor control feasible.
  • Good moderators require a high scattering cross-section $\sigma_s$, a low absorption cross-section $\sigma_a$, and a large logarithmic energy decrement $\xi$.

Glossary

Infinite Multiplication Factor ($k_{inf}$)The ratio of thermal neutrons produced in generation $(n+1)$ to those in generation $(n)$ in an infinite medium.
Effective Multiplication Factor ($k_{eff}$)The ratio of total neutrons in one generation to the total in the preceding generation in a finite reactor, incorporating leakage.
Thermal Utilization Factor ($f$)The fraction of thermal neutrons absorbed in the nuclear fuel compared to all thermal neutrons absorbed in the core.
Resonance Escape Probability ($p$)The probability that a fast neutron slows down to thermal energies without being captured in resonance absorption peaks of fertile nuclei.
Fast Fission Factor ($\epsilon$)The ratio of total fast neutrons produced by fissions at all energies to the fast neutrons produced by thermal fissions alone.
Reproduction Factor ($\eta$)The average number of fast fission neutrons produced per thermal neutron absorbed in the fuel.
Migration Length ($M$)The square root of the sum of the thermal diffusion area ($L_t^2$) and the Fermi age ($\tau$), representing the total slowing down and diffusion distance.

Practice Quiz

Choose an option to reveal the explanation.

1. Which excited compound nucleus is formed immediately upon thermal neutron absorption by a $^{235}\text{U}$ nucleus?

Explanation: Absorption of a thermal neutron by $^{235}\text{U}$ adds one nucleon, producing an excited compound nucleus $^{236}\text{U}^*$ which subsequently undergoes asymmetric fission.

2. What is the approximate average energy released per nuclear fission event in a reactor?

Explanation: Fission of heavy nuclei like $^{235}\text{U}$ releases approximately 200 MeV of energy, distributed among kinetic energy of fission fragments, neutrons, $\gamma$ rays, and radioactive decay products.

3. What percentage of neutrons released during $^{235}\text{U}$ fission are delayed neutrons?

Explanation: A small fraction of neutrons (~0.65% for $^{235}\text{U}$) are delayed, emitted by the radioactive decay of fission product precursors.

4. Which of the following represents Fermi's Four-Factor Formula for the infinite multiplication factor $k_{inf}$?

Explanation: The infinite multiplication factor is the product of four factors: fast fission factor ($\epsilon$), resonance escape probability ($p$), thermal utilization factor ($f$), and reproduction factor ($\eta$).

5. How is the effective multiplication factor $k_{eff}$ related to the infinite multiplication factor $k_{inf}$ in a finite reactor?

Explanation: For a finite reactor, leakage is accounted for by multiplying $k_{inf}$ by the fast and thermal non-leakage probabilities ($L_f$ and $L_t$), yielding $k_{eff} = k_{inf} \cdot L_f \cdot L_t$.

6. What is the primary mechanism by which fast neutrons lose kinetic energy in a moderator?

Explanation: Fast neutrons lose kinetic energy predominantly through elastic scattering collisions with light nuclei such as hydrogen, deuterium, or carbon.

7. Which properties characterize a good neutron moderator?

Explanation: A good moderator must scatter neutrons effectively (high $\sigma_s$), absorb few neutrons (low $\sigma_a$), and lose significant energy per collision (large $\xi$).

8. What is the most probable energy of thermal neutrons in thermal equilibrium with moderator atoms at ambient temperature?

Explanation: Thermal neutrons at room temperature possess a Maxwellian velocity distribution corresponding to a most probable kinetic energy of ~0.025 eV.

9. What condition defines a critical reactor operating in a steady state?

Explanation: A critical reactor operates at a steady state where $k_{eff} = 1$, meaning each neutron generation produces exactly one subsequent generation.

10. Which materials are commonly used in reactor control rods due to their strong neutron absorption cross-sections?

Explanation: Control rods are made of strong neutron absorbers like boron, cadmium, or hafnium to adjust thermal utilization ($f$) and control reactivity.

11. How do delayed neutrons contribute to reactor safety and control?

Explanation: Because delayed neutrons are emitted over seconds or minutes, they dominate the effective neutron lifetime, preventing instantaneous power surges and enabling manageable reactor control.

12. What is the definition of reactivity $\rho$ in terms of the effective multiplication factor $k_{eff}$?

Explanation: Reactivity is defined as $\rho = \frac{k_{eff} - 1}{k_{eff}}$, providing a dimensionless measure of departure from criticality.

13. What is the physical meaning of the reproduction factor $\eta$?

Explanation: The reproduction factor $\eta = \nu \cdot \frac{\sigma_f^F}{\sigma_a^F}$ represents the average number of fission neutrons produced per thermal neutron absorbed in the fuel.

14. What does the migration length $M^2$ combine in reactor physics?

Explanation: The migration area is given by $M^2 = L_t^2 + \tau$, combining the thermal diffusion area ($L_t^2$) and the Fermi age ($\tau$) representing slowing down distance.

15. Why can a reactor with $k_{inf} > 1$ still be subcritical ($k_{eff} < 1$)?

Explanation: If a reactor core is too small, high neutron leakage causes $k_{eff} = k_{inf} \cdot L_f \cdot L_t$ to drop below 1 despite $k_{inf} > 1$.

PYQ Patterns

These are pattern-based practice questions. Authentic year labels are only used when verified from a source.

1. In a nuclear reactor, the thermal utilization factor $f$ is defined as the ratio of:

Explanation: The thermal utilization factor $f = \frac{\Sigma_a^F}{\Sigma_a^F + \Sigma_a^M + \Sigma_a^O}$ measures the fraction of thermal neutrons absorbed specifically by the fuel relative to all core absorptions.

2. If the effective multiplication factor of a nuclear reactor is $k_{eff} = 1.05$, the reactor is:

Explanation: A value of $k_{eff} > 1$ ($1.05$) indicates that neutron production exceeds losses, making the reactor supercritical with rising power.

3. Which of the following best describes the role of control rods containing cadmium or boron in a nuclear reactor?

Explanation: Cadmium and boron have very high thermal neutron absorption cross-sections, allowing control rods to regulate $f$ and maintain or alter criticality.

4. What is the primary reason why moderators should have a high logarithmic energy decrement $\xi$ and low atomic mass?

Explanation: Kinetic energy transfer in elastic scattering is most efficient when the target nucleus has a low mass number (like hydrogen or deuterium), leading to a large logarithmic energy decrement $\xi$.

5. In reactor kinetics, what is the significance of the effective delayed neutron fraction $\beta_{eff}$?

Explanation: The delayed neutron fraction defines the 'dollar' of reactivity; reactivity changes within $\beta_{eff}$ depend entirely on delayed neutron decay times, allowing safe mechanical control.

6. The migration length $M$ is related to the thermal diffusion length $L_t$ and the Fermi age $\tau$ by:

Explanation: By definition, the migration area is $M^2 = L_t^2 + \tau$, making the migration length $M = \sqrt{L_t^2 + \tau}$.

Timed Topic Test

Attempt these questions under a ten-minute limit using a separate timer.

1. Calculate the reproduction factor $\eta$ for a fuel where $\nu = 2.5$, the fission cross-section is $580$ barns, and the total absorption cross-section of the fuel is $680$ barns.

Explanation: Using $\eta = \nu \cdot \frac{\sigma_f^F}{\sigma_a^F} = 2.5 \cdot \frac{580}{680} \approx 2.13$.

2. If a reactor has an effective multiplication factor $k_{eff} = 0.98$, what is its reactivity $\rho$?

Explanation: Reactivity $\rho = \frac{k_{eff} - 1}{k_{eff}} = \frac{0.98 - 1}{0.98} = \frac{-0.02}{0.98} \approx -0.0204$.

3. In the steady-state monoenergetic neutron diffusion equation $D \nabla^2 \phi(\mathbf{r}) - \Sigma_a \phi(\mathbf{r}) + S(\mathbf{r}) = 0$, what does $D$ represent?

Explanation: In diffusion theory, $D$ is the neutron diffusion coefficient, which relates neutron current to the spatial gradient of the neutron flux.

4. Which factor in Fermi's Four-Factor Formula accounts for fast fissions occurring before neutrons slow down below the fission threshold of fertile isotopes like $^{238}\text{U}$?

Explanation: The fast fission factor $\epsilon$ accounts for the additional fast neutrons produced by fissions caused by unmoderated fast neutrons.

5. What is the primary purpose of the resonance escape probability $p$ in reactor physics?

Explanation: The resonance escape probability $p$ is the fraction of slowing-down neutrons that successfully bypass resonance absorption capture bands in fertile nuclei.

6. If a reactor's reactivity $\rho$ is equal to the effective delayed neutron fraction $\beta_{eff}$, the reactor is said to be:

Explanation: When reactivity equals $\beta_{eff}$ ($\rho = \beta_{eff}$), $k_{eff} = \frac{1}{1 - \beta_{eff}}$, making the reactor prompt critical where prompt neutrons alone can sustain the chain reaction.

7. Which of the following materials is classified as a fertile nucleus that can be converted into fissile $^{239}\text{Pu}$ upon neutron capture?

Explanation: $^{238}\text{U}$ is a fertile isotope that captures neutrons to form $^{239}\text{U}$, which $\beta$ decays into $^{239}\text{Pu}$.

8. In the context of neutron moderation, what does Fermi Age ($\tau$) physically represent?

Explanation: Fermi age $\tau$ (slowing down area) has dimensions of area ($\text{m}^2$) and measures the squared displacement of a neutron from birth to thermalization.

9. What happens to the neutron flux $\phi(\mathbf{r})$ in a subcritical reactor ($k_{eff} < 1$) operating with a steady external neutron source $S(\mathbf{r})$?

Explanation: Even though $k_{eff} < 1$, a steady external source maintains a constant subcritical flux level according to source-driven diffusion equations.

10. Why is heavy water ($\text{D}_2\text{O}$) a superior moderator compared to ordinary light water ($\text{H}_2\text{O}$) regarding neutron absorption?

Explanation: Although both have light nuclei, ordinary hydrogen in $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ has a significant capture cross-section to form deuterium, whereas deuterium in $\text{D}_2\text{O}$ has a much lower absorption cross-section.
