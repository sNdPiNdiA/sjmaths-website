Gamma Radiation

Gamma radiation consists of high-energy electromagnetic photons emitted from atomic nuclei during radioactive decay or nuclear transitions. These photons possess zero rest mass and zero charge, representing the excess energy of a nucleus in an excited state.

Origin and Nature of Gamma Radiation

  • Gamma rays are high-frequency electromagnetic waves emitted when a daughter nucleus is left in an excited state following alpha or beta decay.
  • Unlike alpha and beta particles, gamma emission does not change the atomic number (Z) or mass number (A) of the nucleus.
  • The energy of a emitted gamma photon corresponds to the exact difference between two nuclear energy states: $E = E_initial - E_final$.

Interaction of Gamma Radiation with Matter

  • Gamma rays interact with matter primarily through three mechanisms depending on photon energy: Photoelectric effect, Compton scattering, and Pair production.
  • Due to the absence of charge and mass, gamma rays have high penetrating power and low ionizing power compared to alpha and beta particles.
  • The intensity of gamma radiation decreases exponentially with the thickness of the absorber according to the Lambert-Beer law.

Internal Conversion

  • In competition with gamma emission, an excited nucleus may directly transfer its excitation energy to an orbital electron (usually K or L shell).
  • This ejected orbital electron is known as an internal conversion electron, possessing kinetic energy equal to the nuclear transition energy minus the electron binding energy.
  • Internal conversion is followed by the emission of characteristic X-rays or Auger electrons as outer shell electrons fill the vacancy.

Nuclear Isomerism

  • Some excited nuclear states have relatively long half-lives (ranging from microseconds to years) and are called nuclear isomers or metastable states.
  • Metastable states are denoted by the superscript 'm' next to the mass number (e.g., 99mTc).
  • The transition from a metastable state to the ground state via gamma emission is known as isomeric transition.

Derivations

Exponential Attenuation Law for Gamma Rays

Conditions: Monoenergetic gamma beam passing through a uniform absorber of thickness x

  • Consider a beam of gamma photons of intensity I passing through a thin absorber layer of thickness dx.
  • The decrease in intensity (-dI) is directly proportional to the incident intensity I and the thickness dx: -dI = \mu I dx, where \mu is the linear attenuation coefficient.
  • Rearranging terms: \frac{dI}{I} = -\mu dx.
  • Integrating both sides from initial intensity I_0 at x = 0 to intensity I at thickness x yields \ln(\frac{I}{I_0}) = -\mu x.

Result: $I = I_0 e^{-\mu x}$

Threshold Energy for Pair Production

Conditions: Photon interaction in the Coulomb field of a heavy nucleus where momentum and energy must be conserved

  • Pair production involves the conversion of a gamma photon into an electron-positron pair: \gamma \rightarrow e^- + e^+.
  • The minimum energy (threshold energy) required is when the created electron and positron are at rest relative to the recoiling nucleus.
  • By conservation of rest mass energy, the minimum energy is equal to twice the rest mass energy of an electron: $E_{min} = 2m_0 c^2$.
  • Taking into account the recoil energy transferred to the massive nucleus, the exact threshold is slightly higher, but for practical purposes E_{th} \approx 1.02 \text{ MeV}.

Result: $E_{th} = 2m_0 c^2 \approx 1.02 \text{ MeV}$

Solved numericals

TGT core

A gamma-ray beam has an initial intensity of 1000 units. After passing through a lead sheet of thickness 10 cm, its intensity drops to 125 units. Calculate the linear attenuation coefficient of lead.

  • Use the attenuation formula: $I = I_0 e^{-\mu x}$.
  • Substitute the given values: $125 = 1000 e^{-\mu (10)}$.
  • Simplify the equation: \frac{125}{1000} = e^{-10\mu} \implies \frac{1}{8} = e^{-10\mu}.
  • Take natural logarithms on both sides: \ln(1/8) = -10\mu \implies -\ln(8) = -10\mu \implies -$2.079 = -10\mu$.
  • Solve for \mu: $\mu = \frac{2.079}{10} = 0.2079 \text{ cm}^{-1}$.

Answer: 0.2079 cm^{-1}

TGT core

Find the wavelength of a gamma photon emitted during a nuclear transition with an energy release of 2.48 MeV.

  • Convert energy from MeV to Joules: $E = 2.48 \times 10^6 \times 1.6 \times 10^{-19} \text{ J} = 3.968 \times 10^{-13} \text{ J}$.
  • Use the Planck-Einstein relation: \lambda = \frac{hc}{E}.
  • Substitute constants $h = 6.63 \times 10^{-34} \text{ J s}$ and $c = 3 \times 10^8 \text{ m/s}: \lambda = \frac{(6.63 \times 10^{-34}) (3 \times 10^8)}{3.968 \times 10^{-13}}$.
  • Calculate the numerator: 1.989 \times 10^{-25}.
  • Divide by energy: $\lambda = \frac{1.989 \times 10^{-25}}{3.968 \times 10^{-13}} = 5.01 \times 10^{-13} \text{ m}$.

Answer: 5.01 \times 10^{-13} m

PGT extension

Calculate the half-value thickness (x_1_2) of a material whose linear attenuation coefficient is 0.0693 m^-1.

  • Recall the formula for half-value thickness: $x_{1/2} = \frac{\ln(2)}{\mu}$.
  • Substitute \ln(2) \approx 0.693 and $\mu = 0.0693 \text{ m}^{-1}$.
  • Calculate: $x_{1/2} = \frac{0.693}{0.0693} = 10 \text{ m}$.

Answer: 10 m

Assumptions and limitations

  • Gamma ray attenuation equations assume a parallel, monoenergetic, and narrow beam of photons; broad beam geometries require buildup factors to account for scattered radiation.

Common misconceptions

Common error: Gamma rays change the atomic number of the emitting nucleus.

Correct: Gamma emission leaves both the atomic number (Z) and mass number (A) unchanged.

Gamma rays are pure electromagnetic radiation emitted due to a drop in nuclear energy state, unlike alpha or beta decays which involve particle emission changing nuclear composition.

Common error: Gamma rays have mass and charge.

Correct: Gamma rays are massless and chargeless photons.

They are packets of electromagnetic energy (photons), possessing momentum and energy but no rest mass or electric charge.

Quick revision

  • Gamma rays are electromagnetic waves originating from nuclear de-excitation with zero mass and zero charge, traveling at the speed of light.
  • Gamma Photon Energy: E = h\nu = \frac{hc}{\lambda}
  • Radioactive Intensity Attenuation: $I = I_0 e^{-\mu x}$

Must remember

  • Gamma rays are high-frequency electromagnetic waves emitted when a daughter nucleus drops from an excited state to a lower energy state.
  • Gamma emission does not change the atomic number (Z) or mass number (A) of the emitting nucleus.
  • The energy of an emitted gamma photon corresponds exactly to the difference between two nuclear energy states: $\Delta E = E_i - E_f$.
  • Gamma rays interact with matter primarily via three mechanisms: Photoelectric effect, Compton scattering, and Pair production.
  • The intensity of gamma radiation decreases exponentially with absorber thickness according to the Lambert-Beer law: $I = I_0 e^{-\mu x}$.
  • Internal conversion is a competing process where an excited nucleus transfers its energy directly to an orbital electron instead of emitting a gamma photon.
  • Metastable states (nuclear isomers) have relatively long half-lives and are denoted by a superscript 'm' (e.g., $^{99m}\text{Tc}$).
  • Pair production requires a minimum threshold energy of $2m_0 c^2 \approx 1.02\text{ MeV}$ and occurs in the vicinity of a nucleus.
  • Half-value thickness is given by $x_{1/2} = \frac{0.693}{\mu}$.

Glossary

Gamma RayHigh-frequency, high-energy electromagnetic photon emitted by radioactive nuclei.
Linear Attenuation Coefficient ($\mu$)A parameter characterizing how easily a volume of material can be penetrated by a beam of light or other radiation.
Internal ConversionA radioactive decay process where an excited nucleus interacts electrostatically with one of the orbital electrons of the atom, causing the electron to be ejected.
Nuclear IsomerAn excited state of an atomic nucleus with a measurable half-life significantly longer than the typical half-life for nuclear excited states.
Compton ScatteringThe inelastic scattering of a high-energy photon by a free or loosely bound charged particle, typically an electron.
Pair ProductionThe creation of an elementary particle and its antiparticle, usually an electron and a positron, from a neutral boson such as a high-energy photon.

Practice Quiz

Choose an option to reveal the explanation.

1. What happens to the atomic number $Z$ and mass number $A$ of a nucleus when it emits a $\gamma$ photon?

Explanation: $\gamma$ emission is the release of electromagnetic energy from an excited nucleus, which alters neither the number of protons nor neutrons, leaving both $Z$ and $A$ unchanged.

2. The energy of an emitted $\gamma$ photon is given by which of the following relations?

Explanation: The energy of an emitted $\gamma$ photon corresponds to the exact difference between two nuclear energy states: $\Delta E = E_i - E_f = h\nu$.

3. Which of the following is the correct expression for the exponential attenuation of $\gamma$ ray intensity through an absorber of thickness $x$?

Explanation: According to the Lambert-Beer law, the intensity of monoenergetic $\gamma$ radiation decreases exponentially with absorber thickness according to $I = I_0 e^{-\mu x}$.

4. What is the half-value thickness ($x_{1/2}$) in terms of the linear attenuation coefficient $\mu$?

Explanation: Setting $I = I_0 / 2$ in the attenuation equation gives $I_0 / 2 = I_0 e^{-\mu x_{1/2}}$, leading to $x_{1/2} = \frac{\ln(2)}{\mu} = \frac{0.693}{\mu}$.

5. What is the minimum threshold energy required for pair production to occur in the field of a nucleus?

Explanation: Pair production involves the creation of an electron-positron pair, each having a rest mass energy of $m_0 c^2 \approx 0.511\text{ MeV}$, giving a total minimum threshold energy of $2m_0 c^2 \approx 1.02\text{ MeV}$.

6. In Compton scattering, what determines the wavelength shift $\Delta \lambda$ of the scattered photon?

Explanation: The Compton wavelength shift formula is $\Delta \lambda = \frac{h}{m_0 c} (1 - \cos\theta)$, which depends purely on the scattering angle $\theta$ and fundamental constants.

7. What is internal conversion?

Explanation: Internal conversion is a process where an excited nucleus directly transfers its excitation energy to an orbital electron (usually K or L shell), ejecting it from the atom.

8. How are metastable nuclear isomers denoted?

Explanation: Metastable nuclear states, which have relatively long half-lives, are denoted by the superscript 'm' next to the mass number, such as $^{99m}\text{Tc}$.

9. Which interaction mechanism of $\gamma$ rays with matter predominates at very low photon energies and involves the complete absorption of the photon by a bound electron?

Explanation: The photoelectric effect involves the absorption of a $\gamma$ photon by a bound electron, transferring all of its energy to the electron and ejecting it.

10. Why do $\gamma$ rays possess high penetrating power compared to $\alpha$ and $\beta$ particles?

Explanation: Because $\gamma$ rays are chargeless and massless photons, they interact much less frequently with the electrons and nuclei of matter, allowing them to penetrate deeply.

11. What is the unit of the mass attenuation coefficient $\mu_m$?

Explanation: The mass attenuation coefficient is defined as $\mu_m = \frac{\mu}{\rho}$. Since $\mu$ has units of $\text{m}^{-1}$ and density $\rho$ has units of $\text{kg}/\text{m}^3$, $\mu_m$ has units of $\text{m}^2/\text{kg}$.

12. Following internal conversion, what subsequent emissions frequently occur as outer shell electrons fill the vacancy?

Explanation: When an inner shell electron is ejected via internal conversion, outer shell electrons drop down to fill the vacancy, leading to the emission of characteristic X-rays or Auger electrons.

13. What is the transition from a metastable nuclear isomer to the ground state via $\gamma$ emission called?

Explanation: The radioactive decay of a metastable nuclear isomer to a lower energy state via the emission of a $\gamma$ photon is termed isomeric transition.

14. Which of the following correctly describes the rest mass and electric charge of a $\gamma$ ray photon?

Explanation: $\gamma$ rays are electromagnetic radiation packets (photons) which possess energy and momentum but have zero rest mass and zero electric charge.

15. In Compton scattering, what is the maximum wavelength shift $\Delta \lambda$ possible for a scattered photon?

Explanation: The wavelength shift formula is $\Delta \lambda = \frac{h}{m_0 c} (1 - \cos\theta)$. The maximum shift occurs when the scattering angle $\theta = 180^{\circ}$, giving $\Delta \lambda_{\max} = \frac{h}{m_0 c} (1 - (-1)) = \frac{2h}{m_0 c}$.

PYQ Patterns

These are pattern-based practice questions. Authentic year labels are only used when verified from a source.

1. If the linear attenuation coefficient $\mu$ of a lead sheet for a monoenergetic $\gamma$ beam is $23\text{ m}^{-1}$, what is the half-value thickness of the lead sheet?

Explanation: Using the half-value thickness formula $x_{1/2} = \frac{0.693}{\mu} = \frac{0.693}{23\text{ m}^{-1}} \approx 0.030\text{ m}$ (or $3.0\text{ cm}$).

2. A $\gamma$ ray photon undergoes Compton scattering at an angle of $90^{\circ}$ off a free electron. What is the wavelength shift $\Delta \lambda$ of the photon? (Given Compton wavelength $\frac{h}{m_0 c} \approx 2.43 \times 10^{-12}\text{ m}$)

Explanation: At $\theta = 90^{\circ}$, $\cos(90^{\circ}) = 0$. Therefore, $\Delta \lambda = \frac{h}{m_0 c} (1 - 0) = \frac{h}{m_0 c} = 2.43 \times 10^{-12}\text{ m}$.

3. In a nuclear transition, a nucleus drops from an excited state of energy $4.5\text{ MeV}$ to a ground state of $2.2\text{ MeV}$. What is the frequency of the emitted $\gamma$ photon? ($h \approx 6.63 \times 10^{-34}\text{ J}\cdot\text{s}$)

Explanation: Energy difference $\Delta E = 4.5 - 2.2 = 2.3\text{ MeV} = 2.3 \times 10^6 \times 1.6 \times 10^{-19}\text{ J} = 3.68 \times 10^{-13}\text{ J}$. Frequency $\nu = \frac{\Delta E}{h} = \frac{3.68 \times 10^{-13}}{6.63 \times 10^{-34}} \approx 5.55 \times 10^{20}\text{ Hz}$

4. The intensity of $\gamma$ radiation drops to $1/8^{\text{th}}$ of its initial incident value after passing through a certain absorber thickness. How many half-value thicknesses ($x_{1/2}$) of the absorber has the beam traversed?

Explanation: The transmitted intensity is given by $I = I_0 (1/2)^n$, where $n$ is the number of half-value layers. Here $I / I_0 = 1/8 = (1/2)^3$, so $n = 3$.

5. Which of the following processes represents direct competition with $\gamma$ ray emission from an excited nuclear state?

Explanation: Internal conversion is a direct alternative or competing process to $\gamma$ emission, whereby an excited nucleus transfers its energy straight to an orbital electron.

6. Why is the presence of a neighboring heavy nucleus mandatory for the pair production process by a $\gamma$ photon?

Explanation: A photon in free space cannot spontaneously convert into an electron-positron pair while conserving both energy and momentum. A neighboring nucleus is necessary to absorb a fraction of the momentum.

Timed Topic Test

Attempt these questions under a ten-minute limit using a separate timer.

1. If a $\gamma$ ray beam of initial intensity $100\text{ W/m}^2$ passes through an absorber and its intensity reduces to $25\text{ W/m}^2$ after traversing a thickness of $2\text{ cm}$, what is the linear attenuation coefficient $\mu$?

Explanation: $I / I_0 = 25 / 100 = 1/4 = e^{-\mu x}$. Taking natural logs: $\ln(1/4) = -\mu (2)$, so $-1.386 = -2\mu \implies \mu = 0.693\text{ cm}^{-1}$.

2. What is the kinetic energy of the ejected internal conversion electron if the nuclear transition energy is $300\text{ keV}$ and the binding energy of the K-shell electron is $50\text{ keV}$?

Explanation: The kinetic energy of an internal conversion electron equals the nuclear transition energy minus the electron binding energy: $E_k = 300\text{ keV} - 50\text{ keV} = 250\text{ keV}$.

3. In the interaction of $\gamma$ radiation with matter, which mechanism predominates at intermediate photon energies (e.g., $0.5\text{ MeV}$ to $5\text{ MeV}$) in light elements?

Explanation: Compton scattering is the primary interaction mechanism for $\gamma$ photons in the intermediate energy range across most materials.

4. Which of the following statements about $\gamma$ rays is incorrect?

Explanation: $\gamma$ rays are uncharged electromagnetic photons and therefore are not deflected by electric or magnetic fields.

5. If a $\gamma$ photon has a wavelength of $1.24 \times 10^{-12}\text{ m}$, what is its approximate energy in MeV? (Take $hc \approx 1240\text{ eV}\cdot\text{nm}$)

Explanation: $E = \frac{hc}{\lambda} = \frac{1240\text{ eV}\cdot\text{nm}}{1.24 \times 10^{-3}\text{ nm}} = 10^6\text{ eV} = 1.00\text{ MeV}$.

6. Metastable nuclear isomers return to the ground state predominantly by emitting:

Explanation: Metastable states decay to the ground state through the emission of $\gamma$ radiation in a process known as isomeric transition.

7. What happens to the linear attenuation coefficient $\mu$ of a material as the energy of the incident $\gamma$ rays increases from $0.1\text{ MeV}$ to $5\text{ MeV}$?

Explanation: As $\gamma$ photon energy increases, the probability of interaction via photoelectric and Compton effects decreases, resulting in a decrease in the linear attenuation coefficient $\mu$ until pair production begins to dominate at very high energies.

8. In Compton scattering, what is the scattered photon energy $E_\gamma'$ when the incident photon of energy $E_\gamma$ is scattered at $180^{\circ}$ and the recoil electron energy is $E_e$?

Explanation: By conservation of energy in Compton scattering, the incident photon energy equals the sum of the scattered photon energy and the recoil electron kinetic energy: $E_\gamma = E_\gamma' + E_e \implies E_\gamma' = E_\gamma - E_e$.

9. Which type of radiation shares an identical electromagnetic nature with $\gamma$ rays, differing only in its origin?

Explanation: X-rays and $\gamma$ rays are both electromagnetic waves of high frequency. They differ only in their origin: X-rays originate from electronic transitions in the atomic electron cloud, while $\gamma$ rays originate from nuclear transitions.

10. What is the approximate transmission factor ($I / I_0$) of a shield whose thickness is exactly equal to 2 half-value thicknesses ($2 x_{1/2}$)?

Explanation: After 1 half-value layer, intensity is $1/2$. After 2 half-value layers, intensity is $(1/2)^2 = 1/4 = 0.25$ of the initial incident value.
