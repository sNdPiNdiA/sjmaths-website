Nuclear Binding Energy and Mass Defect

Nuclear binding energy is the minimum energy required to completely separate the constituent nucleons of an atomic nucleus to infinite separation. It originates from the mass defect, reflecting the equivalence of mass and energy according to Einstein's relation.

Definition of mass defect, binding energy, mass-energy equivalence, and basic calculations involving atomic mass units (u).

Prerequisites

  • Mass-energy equivalence principle (E = mc&sup2;)
  • Structure of the atomic nucleus (protons and neutrons)
  • Radioactivity and nuclear stability

Mass Defect

  • The total mass of a stable nucleus is always less than the sum of the rest masses of its individual unbound constituent nucleons.
  • The difference between the total rest mass of the separate nucleons and the actual mass of the nucleus is defined as the mass defect (Δm).
  • Mathematically, Δm = [Z m_p + (A - Z) m_n] - M, where m_p is proton mass, m_n is neutron mass, and M is nuclear mass.

Nuclear Binding Energy

  • Binding energy (BE) is the energy equivalent of the mass defect, representing the work done by the attractive nuclear forces in assembling the nucleus.
  • Expressed via Einstein's relation as BE = Δm × c².
  • When expressed using neutral atomic masses, electron masses must be accounted for to avoid systematic errors.

Binding Energy per Nucleon

  • Binding energy per nucleon (BE/A) is a direct measure of the structural stability of a nucleus against disintegration.
  • The average value of BE/A is approximately 8 MeV per nucleon for intermediate mass nuclei (A ≈ 50 to 60, like Iron).
  • Lower values of BE/A at very low A (fusion domain) and very high A (fission domain) explain why both nuclear fusion and fission release energy.

Semi-Empirical Mass Formula (Weizsäcker's Formula)

  • The total binding energy of a nucleus is modeled using the liquid drop analogy with five distinct energy contributions.
  • Terms include the volume energy, surface energy, Coulomb electrostatic repulsion energy, asymmetry energy, and pairing energy.
  • Allows theoretical calculation of nuclear binding energies and prediction of beta stability lines without solving quantum many-body equations explicitly.

Derivations

Derivation of Mass-Energy Equivalence for Binding Energy

Conditions: Static nucleus at rest composed of Z protons and N neutrons (where N = A - Z).

  • Write the total rest mass of Z isolated protons and N isolated neutrons: M_total = Z m_p + (A - Z) m_n.
  • Define the mass defect Δm as the difference between the constituent mass and the actual nuclear mass M: Δm = [Z m_p + (A - Z) m_n] - M.
  • Apply Einstein's mass-energy relation E = mc² to convert the mass defect into energy units: BE = Δm c².
  • Substitute atomic masses by adding Z electron masses to both sides, utilizing atomic mass unit conversions (1 u ≈ 931.5 MeV).

Result: BE = [Z m_H + (A - Z) m_n - M_atom] c²

Volume Energy Term in Liquid Drop Model

Conditions: Nucleus considered as an incompressible fluid drop of nucleons interacting via short-range forces.

  • Assume the nuclear radius is proportional to A^(1/3), meaning nuclear volume V is proportional to mass number A.
  • Since nuclear density is approximately constant, the number of nearest-neighbor interactions for an interior nucleon is constant.
  • The total binding energy contributed by these saturated short-range forces is directly proportional to the total number of nucleons A.
  • Express this contribution as the volume energy term: E_v = a_v A, where a_v is a positive empirical constant.

Result: E_v = a_v A

Solved numericals

TGT core

Calculate the mass defect and binding energy of an alpha particle (&Omicron;e^4_2He), given that the mass of a proton is 1.007825 u, the mass of a neutron is 1.008665 u, and the mass of an alpha particle is 4.001506 u. (Use 1 u = 931.5 MeV)

  • Identify Z = 2 protons and N = 4 - 2 = 2 neutrons.
  • Calculate total mass of nucleons: M_nucleons = 2 × 1.007825 u + 2 × 1.008665 u = 2.015650 + 2.017330 = 4.032980 u.
  • Calculate mass defect Δm: Δm = 4.032980 u - 4.001506 u = 0.031474 u.
  • Calculate binding energy BE = 0.031474 u × 931.5 MeV/u = 29.31 MeV.

Answer: Mass defect = 0.031474 u, Binding energy = 29.31 MeV

PGT extension

Using the liquid drop model, find the volume energy and surface energy contributions to the total binding energy of a nucleus with A = 125, given volume coefficient a_v = 15.75 MeV and surface coefficient a_s = 17.8 MeV.

  • Recall volume energy formula: E_v = a_v A.
  • Substitute values: E_v = 15.75 MeV × 125 = 1968.75 MeV.
  • Recall surface energy formula: E_s = -a_s A^(2/3).
  • Calculate A^(2/3): (125)^(2/3) = (5^3)^(2/3) = 5^2 = 25.
  • Calculate surface energy: E_s = -17.8 MeV × 25 = -445.0 MeV.

Answer: Volume energy = 1968.75 MeV, Surface energy = -445.0 MeV

TGT core

Find the binding energy per nucleon for an oxygen nucleus (&lt;sub&gt;8&lt;/sub&gt;O&lt;sup&gt;16&lt;/sup&gt;) if its atomic mass is 15.994915 u. Given m_p = 1.007825 u and m_n = 1.008665 u.

  • Identify Z = 8 and N = 16 - 8 = 8.
  • Total mass of 8 protons and 8 neutrons = 8(1.007825 u) + 8(1.008665 u) = 8.062600 + 8.069320 = 16.131920 u.
  • Mass defect Δm = 16.131920 u - 15.994915 u = 0.137005 u.
  • Total Binding Energy BE = 0.137005 × 931.5 MeV = 127.62 MeV.
  • Binding energy per nucleon = BE / A = 127.62 MeV / 16 = 7.98 MeV/nucleon.

Answer: 7.98 MeV/nucleon

Assumptions and limitations

  • The liquid drop model assumes uniform nuclear density and incompressible nuclear fluid, which fails for very light or magic-number nuclei where shell effects dominate.

Common misconceptions

Common error: A nucleus is heavier than the sum of its constituent nucleons because energy is added during formation.

Correct: A nucleus is lighter than the sum of its constituent nucleons due to mass defect released as binding energy.

Energy release corresponds to mass loss according to Einstein's mass-energy equivalence (E = mc&sup2;).

Common error: Binding energy per nucleon increases continuously as the mass number increases.

Correct: Binding energy per nucleon peaks around A = 56 (Iron group) and then gradually decreases for heavier nuclei.

Electrostatic Coulomb repulsion among protons destabilizes heavy nuclei, reducing BE/A at high mass numbers.

Quick revision

  • Mass defect is the difference between total nucleon mass and nuclear mass, converted entirely into binding energy via BE = Δm c².
  • Mass Defect: \Delta m = Z m_p + (A-Z) m_n - M
  • Nuclear Binding Energy: BE = \Delta m \cdot c^2 = [Z m_p + (A-Z) m_n - M] c^2

Must remember

  • The total mass of a stable nucleus is always less than the sum of the rest masses of its individual unbound constituent nucleons.
  • Mass defect is given by $\Delta m = [Z m_p + (A - Z) m_n] - M$ for nuclear mass, or using neutral atomic masses as $\Delta m = [Z m(^1H) + (A - Z) m_n] - m(^AZ)$.
  • Einstein's mass-energy equivalence relates binding energy as $BE = \Delta m \cdot c^2$, with $1\text{ u} \approx 931.5\text{ MeV}$.
  • Binding energy per nucleon ($\bar{B} = BE / A$) peaks around $A \approx 56$ (Iron region) with an average value of approximately $8.5\text{ MeV/nucleon}$.
  • The semi-empirical mass formula incorporates five energy terms: volume, surface, Coulomb, asymmetry, and pairing energies based on the liquid drop model.

Glossary

Mass Defect ($\Delta m$)The difference between the total rest mass of separate unbound nucleons and the actual mass of the assembled nucleus.
Nuclear Binding Energy ($BE$)The energy equivalent of the mass defect representing the work done by attractive nuclear forces to form a nucleus.
Binding Energy per Nucleon ($\bar{B}$)The total binding energy divided by the mass number $A$, serving as a primary measure of nuclear stability.
Semi-Empirical Mass FormulaWeizsäcker's formula expressing nuclear binding energy using the liquid drop model analogy containing volume, surface, Coulomb, asymmetry, and pairing terms.
Volume Energy Term ($E_v$)Term proportional to mass number $A$ representing the short-range saturation property of nuclear forces.
Coulomb Energy Term ($E_c$)Electrostatic repulsion energy among protons that destabilizes heavy nuclei, proportional to $Z(Z-1)/A^{1/3}$.

Practice Quiz

Choose an option to reveal the explanation.

1. What is the physical origin of the mass defect in a stable atomic nucleus?

Explanation: According to Einstein's mass-energy equivalence ($E = mc^2$), energy is released when nucleons bind together via attractive nuclear forces, resulting in a lower total rest mass for the nucleus compared to the sum of individual nucleons.

2. If $m_p$ is the mass of a proton, $m_n$ is the mass of a neutron, and $M$ is the mass of a nucleus with atomic number $Z$ and mass number $A$, the mass defect $\Delta m$ is given by:

Explanation: Mass defect $\Delta m$ is the total mass of the separate constituent nucleons minus the mass of the nucleus: $\Delta m = [Z m_p + (A - Z) m_n] - M$.

3. What is the approximate average binding energy per nucleon for intermediate mass nuclei such as Iron ($A \approx 56$)?

Explanation: Intermediate mass nuclei like iron have a peak binding energy per nucleon of approximately $8.5\text{ MeV}$ per nucleon, making them the most stable nuclei in nature.

4. Why do both very light nuclei (fusion domain) and very heavy nuclei (fission domain) release energy when undergoing nuclear reactions?

Explanation: Nuclei at both extremes of mass numbers have lower binding energy per nucleon. Moving toward intermediate mass nuclei ($A \approx 56$) increases the binding energy per nucleon, releasing the excess energy.

5. In the semi-empirical mass formula, which term accounts for the finite size of the nucleus where boundary nucleons have fewer neighboring interactions?

Explanation: The surface energy term ($E_s = -a_s A^{2/3}$) acts as a negative correction to the volume energy because nucleons on the surface are surrounded by fewer nucleons and therefore contribute less binding energy.

6. The Coulomb energy term in Weizsäcker's semi-empirical mass formula is proportional to:

Explanation: The electrostatic repulsion among all proton pairs in a uniformly charged sphere of radius proportional to $A^{1/3}$ results in a Coulomb energy term proportional to $\frac{Z(Z-1)}{A^{1/3}}$.

7. What does the asymmetry energy term in the semi-empirical mass formula penalize?

Explanation: The asymmetry energy term penalizes neutron-proton imbalance based on the Pauli exclusion principle, reducing the binding energy when $N \neq Z$.

8. When calculating binding energy using neutral atomic masses instead of pure nuclear masses, what must be properly accounted for?

Explanation: Using neutral atomic masses $m(^AZ)$ and hydrogen atomic masses $m(^1H)$ automatically incorporates the mass of $Z$ electrons, though electron binding energy differences are typically negligible in standard calculations.

9. What is the volume energy term $E_v$ proportional to in the liquid drop model?

Explanation: Because nuclear density is nearly constant, the volume of a nucleus is proportional to mass number $A$, making the volume energy term directly proportional to $A$ ($E_v = a_v A$).

10. Which of the following nuclei has the highest binding energy per nucleon among light nuclei?

Explanation: $_2^4\text{He}$ ($\alpha$ particle) exhibits a remarkably high binding energy per nucleon ($\approx 7.07\text{ MeV}$) compared to its immediate neighbors ($^2\text{H}$, $^3\text{H}$, $^3\text{He}$), reflecting exceptional internal stability.

11. In the context of the liquid drop model, which energy term represents the primary attractive bulk binding of nucleons?

Explanation: The volume energy term represents the saturation property of the strong nuclear force, where each interior nucleon interacts with a fixed number of neighbors, providing bulk binding proportional to $A$.

12. What is the approximate energy equivalent of $1\text{ atomic mass unit (u)}$ in MeV?

Explanation: Using $E = \Delta m \cdot c^2$, $1\text{ u} \times c^2$ equals approximately $931.5\text{ MeV}$.

13. Why does the binding energy per nucleon decrease for very heavy nuclei like Uranium ($A = 238$)?

Explanation: While the short-range strong nuclear force saturates and scales with $A$, the repulsive Coulomb force among protons increases quadratically with $Z$, eventually destabilizing heavy nuclei and decreasing $\bar{B}$.

14. Which pairing energy term sign convention applies to even-even nuclei in the semi-empirical mass formula?

Explanation: Even-even nuclei have all protons and neutrons paired, which is energetically favorable and adds a positive pairing energy term ($+\delta$), whereas odd-odd nuclei have a negative pairing term.

15. What does a sharp drop in the binding energy per nucleon curve signify at specific magic numbers of nucleons?

Explanation: Peaks in binding energy and exceptional stability occur at 'magic numbers' of neutrons or protons, corresponding to filled quantum shells in the nuclear shell model.

PYQ Patterns

These are pattern-based practice questions. Authentic year labels are only used when verified from a source.

1. The mass defect for an oxygen isotope $_8^{16}\text{O}$ is given as $\Delta m$. If $m_p$ is proton mass, $m_n$ is neutron mass, and $M$ is the nuclear mass, which equation correctly represents $\Delta m$?

Explanation: For $_8^{16}\text{O}$, $Z = 8$ and $A - Z = 16 - 8 = 8$. Therefore, $\Delta m = 8m_p + 8m_n - M$.

2. In the semi-empirical mass formula, the surface energy term varies with mass number $A$ as:

Explanation: The surface energy term is $E_s = -a_s A^{2/3}$, accounting for uncompensated nucleons on the nuclear surface proportional to the surface area ($4\$\pi$ R^2 \propto A^{2/3}$).

3. Calculate the binding energy per nucleon for a nucleus whose total binding energy is $492\text{ MeV}$ and mass number is $12$.

Explanation: Binding energy per nucleon $\bar{B} = \frac{BE}{A} = \frac{492\text{ MeV}}{12} = 41\text{ MeV/nucleon}$ (Note: typical model calculation for practice).

4. Which of the following terms in the semi-empirical mass formula is responsible for making neutron-rich heavy nuclei unstable against $\beta$ decay?

Explanation: The asymmetry energy term penalizes $N \neq Z$, driving nuclei toward the line of $\beta$ stability where neutron-proton imbalance is minimized.

5. Why is the nuclear binding energy per nucleon approximately constant at about $8\text{ MeV}$ for nuclei with $A > 50$?

Explanation: Saturation of the strong nuclear force means each nucleon interacts only with its immediate neighbors, keeping the binding energy per nucleon roughly constant across medium and heavy nuclei.

6. If $1\text{ u}$ is equivalent to $931.5\text{ MeV}$, what is the binding energy of a nucleus with a mass defect of $0.030\text{ u}$?

Explanation: $BE = \Delta m \cdot c^2 = 0.030\text{ u} \times 931.5\text{ MeV/u} = 27.945\text{ MeV}$.

Timed Topic Test

Attempt these questions under a ten-minute limit using a separate timer.

1. The difference between the rest mass of constituent nucleons and the nuclear mass is called:

Explanation: Mass defect is explicitly defined as the difference between the total mass of separate nucleons and the rest mass of the nucleus.

2. What is the primary reason why heavy nuclei undergo fission rather than fusion?

Explanation: Coulomb repulsion scales as $Z(Z-1)/A^{1/3}$, making heavy nuclei unstable and causing them to split into smaller, more tightly bound fragments (fission).

3. In the semi-empirical mass formula, the volume coefficient $a_v$ and surface coefficient $a_s$ have dimensions of:

Explanation: Both $a_v$ and $a_s$ represent energy contributions measured in MeV.

4. For a nucleus with $Z$ protons and $A$ nucleons, the atomic mass form of binding energy using neutral atomic masses substitutes proton mass with:

Explanation: When using neutral atomic masses, $m_p$ is replaced by the mass of a hydrogen atom $m(^1H)$ to account for the $Z$ atomic electrons.

5. Which nuclear type possesses the highest pairing energy contribution in Weizsäcker's formula?

Explanation: Even-even nuclei have paired neutrons and paired protons, resulting in a positive pairing energy bonus that maximizes stability.

6. What does the saturation property of nuclear forces imply?

Explanation: Nuclear force saturation means each nucleon binds only to a limited number of adjacent neighbors, leading to constant nuclear density and volume energy proportional to $A$.

7. If the binding energy per nucleon of $_6^{12}\text{C}$ is $7.68\text{ MeV}$, what is its total binding energy?

Explanation: Total binding energy $BE = \bar{B} \times A = 7.68\text{ MeV/nucleon} \times 12 = 92.16\text{ MeV}$.

8. Which term in the semi-empirical mass formula decreases the total binding energy quadratically with neutron-proton asymmetry?

Explanation: The asymmetry term $E_a = -a_a \frac{(A-2Z)^2}{A}$ reduces binding energy when $N \neq Z$ based on quantum statistical mechanics.

9. The peak of the binding energy per nucleon curve occurs around $A \approx 56$. Which element corresponds to this peak?

Explanation: Iron ($^{56}\text{Fe}$) and nickel isotopes near $A = 56$ form the peak of the binding energy curve.

10. Why is the mass of a nucleus always less than the sum of masses of its constituent nucleons?

Explanation: Binding energy released during the formation of a bound nuclear system corresponds directly to a reduction in rest mass ($\Delta m = BE / c^2$).
