Spherical Mirrors: Reflection, Sign Convention, and Image Formation

Spherical mirrors are reflecting surfaces that form a part of a hollow sphere of glass or metal. They obey the laws of reflection and are categorized into concave and convex mirrors based on the curvature of their reflecting surfaces.

Scope and prerequisites

TGT core

Reflection laws, mirror formula, relation between focal length and radius of curvature, real and virtual image characteristics, linear magnification, and standard ray diagrams for concave and convex mirrors.

PGT extension

Longitudinal and areal magnification, velocity of image in moving mirror problems, aberration in spherical mirrors (spherical aberration), and optical bench methods for focal length determination.

Prerequisites

  • Laws of reflection of light
  • Geometry of circles and spheres (radius, pole, tangent)
  • Real and virtual images in geometrical optics

Sign Convention (New Cartesian Sign Convention)

  • All distances are measured from the pole (P) of the spherical mirror as the origin.
  • Distances measured in the direction of incident light are taken as positive, while those measured opposite to the direction of incident light are taken as negative.
  • Heights measured perpendicularly upwards from the principal axis are positive, and those measured downwards are negative.

Focus and Focal Length

  • Paraxial rays parallel to the principal axis converge to (concave) or appear to diverge from (convex) a fixed point on the principal axis called the principal focus (F).
  • The distance between the pole and the principal focus is the focal length (f).
  • For paraxial rays, the focal length is related to the radius of curvature (R) by the relation f = R / 2.

Nature of Images Formed

  • A concave mirror can form real, inverted images as well as virtual, erect images depending on the object position.
  • A convex mirror always forms a virtual, erect, and diminished image for any real position of the object.
  • Linear magnification (m) is positive for virtual images and negative for real images.

Spherical Aberration

  • Marginal rays falling at large heights from the principal axis do not converge at the principal focus F, a phenomenon known as spherical aberration.
  • This defect leads to a blurred image, which can be minimized by using parabolic mirrors or stop diaphragms to block marginal rays.
  • Paraxial rays alone strictly satisfy the mirror formula.

Derivations

Mirror Formula for a Concave Mirror (Real Image)

Conditions: Paraxial rays, object placed beyond the focus of a concave mirror forming a real image.

  • Consider a concave mirror with pole P, focus F, and center of curvature C. Let an object AB be placed beyond C.
  • Using similar triangles ABC and A'B'C, the ratio of heights gives AB / A'B' = BC / B'C.
  • Similarly, using triangles ABP and A'B'P, AB / A'B' = PB / PB'.
  • Equating the two ratios and substituting Cartesian coordinates (PB = -u, PB' = -v, PC = -R = -2f), we get (-u - (-2f)) / (-2f - (-v)) = -u / -v.
  • Simplifying the algebraic relation yields (1 / v) + (1 / u) = (1 / f).

Result: 1 / v + 1 / u = 1 / f

Relation between Focal Length and Radius of Curvature

Conditions: Paraxial rays incident on a concave mirror at small angles.

  • Let a ray parallel to the principal axis strike the concave mirror at point M at a height h from the pole P.
  • Let C be the center of curvature and angle MCP = theta. Since the ray is parallel, angle PMF = theta and angle MCF = 2theta.
  • In triangle MCP, tan(theta) = h / PC approx theta. In triangle MKF (where MK is perpendicular to principal axis), tan(2theta) = h / PF approx 2theta.
  • Substituting theta and 2theta gives h / PF = 2 (h / PC), which simplifies to PC = 2 PF.
  • Since PC = R and PF = f, we obtain R = 2f.

Result: f = R / 2

Graphs and experiments

Draw: A rectangular hyperbola plotted in the second quadrant where both u and v are negative, with asymptotes at u = f and v = f.

Interpret: Shows the inverse non-linear relationship between object distance and image distance. The intersection with the line v = u gives the center of curvature (u = -2f, v = -2f).

Exam use: Tested conceptually to identify focal length from intercept values or symmetry lines.

Magnification (m) vs Object Distance (u)

Draw: A curve showing m starting from 0 at u = infinity, going to -infinity as u approaches f from the left, and positive values for virtual images when u < f.

Interpret: Represents the transition from real inverted (negative m) to virtual erect (positive m) images.

Exam use: Used in numerical problems involving threshold object positions where magnification magnitude equals 1.

Optical Bench Experiment for Focal Length

Draw: Experimental setup schematic showing an illuminated pin, a concave mirror on a stand, and a screen mounted on an optical bench.

Interpret: Determines focal length by locating the coincidence position where the object pin and its real inverted image coincide at the center of curvature (u = v = R = 2f).

Exam use: Core practical physics question in viva and conceptual MCQ formats.

Solved numericals

TGT core

An object of height 2 cm is placed at a distance of 30 cm in front of a concave mirror of focal length 20 cm. Find the position, nature, and height of the image.

  • Identify given values using sign convention: u = -30 cm, f = -20 cm, ho = +2 cm.
  • Apply the mirror formula: 1/v + 1/u = 1/f => 1/v + 1/(-30) = 1/(-20).
  • Solve for v: 1/v = -1/20 + 1/30 = (-3 + 2) / 60 = -1/60, which gives v = -60 cm.
  • Calculate height using magnification: hi = - (v/u) * ho = - (-60 / -30) * 2 = -2 * 2 = -4 cm.

Answer: Image distance v = -60 cm (real and in front of mirror), height = -4 cm (inverted and enlarged).

PGT extension

A small rod of length 10 cm is placed along the principal axis of a concave mirror of focal length 30 cm, such that its nearer end is at a distance of 40 cm from the mirror. Find the length of the image of the rod.

  • Focal length f = -30 cm. Distance of nearer end u1 = -40 cm. Distance of farther end u2 = -(40 + 10) = -50 cm.
  • Calculate image position for u1 = -40 cm: 1/v1 + 1/(-40) = 1/(-30) => 1/v1 = -1/30 + 1/40 = -1/120 => v1 = -120 cm.
  • Calculate image position for u2 = -50 cm: 1/v2 + 1/(-50) = 1/(-30) => 1/v2 = -1/30 + 1/50 = -2/150 => v2 = -75 cm.
  • Length of the image is the distance between the two image positions: delta v = |v1 - v2| = |-120 - (-75)| = 45 cm.

Answer: 45 cm

PGT extension

A concave mirror has a focal length of 20 cm. At what distance from the mirror should an object be placed so that the size of the image is twice the size of the object, and the image is real?

  • For a real image formed by a concave mirror, magnification m = -2.
  • Use the magnification formula relating m, f, and u: m = f / (f - u).
  • Substitute values: -2 = -20 / (-20 - u).
  • Solve for u: -2 (-20 - u) = -20 => 40 + 2u = -20 => 2u = -60 => u = -30 cm.

Answer: 30 cm in front of the mirror (u = -30 cm)

Assumptions and limitations

  • Mirrors are assumed to have a small aperture so that paraxial ray approximations (sin(theta) approx theta) hold true.
  • The mirror formula fails for wide-aperture spherical mirrors due to spherical aberration.
  • Wavelength of light is assumed to be much smaller than the mirror dimensions, so diffraction effects can be neglected.

Common misconceptions

Common error: A convex mirror can produce a real image if the object is placed beyond its focus.

Correct: A convex mirror always produces a virtual, erect, and diminished image for any real object.

The reflecting surface bulges outward, causing parallel or convergent incident rays to always diverge after reflection.

Common error: The focal length of a concave mirror changes when it is immersed in water.

Correct: The focal length of a spherical mirror remains unchanged in water.

Reflection depends only on the law of reflection (angles of incidence and reflection), which is independent of the surrounding medium's refractive index.

Common error: Power of a mirror has units of dioptres only when focal length is in centimeters.

Correct: Power is measured in dioptres when the focal length is expressed strictly in meters (P = -1/f).

Dimensional consistency requires focal length to be in meters for the dioptre (m^-1) unit.

Quick revision

  • For a concave mirror, f is negative; for a convex mirror, f is positive.
  • Real images are always inverted and formed on the same side as the object; virtual images are erect and formed behind the mirror.
  • Longitudinal magnification is equal to the negative square of transverse magnification (-m^2).

Must remember

  • All distances in spherical mirrors are measured from the pole (P) following the New Cartesian Sign Convention.
  • The relation between focal length ($f$) and radius of curvature ($R$) is given by $f = \frac{R}{2}$ for paraxial rays.
  • The mirror formula is expressed as $\frac{1}{v} + \frac{1}{u} = \frac{1}{f}$.
  • A convex mirror always forms a virtual, erect, and diminished image for any real position of an object.
  • Transverse linear magnification is given by $m = \frac{h_i}{h_o} = -\frac{v}{u}$.
  • Longitudinal magnification for a small object along the principal axis is $m_L = -m^2$.
  • The focal length of a spherical mirror remains unchanged when immersed in water.
  • Spherical aberration caused by marginal rays can be minimized by using parabolic mirrors or stop diaphragms.
  • Power of a spherical mirror is defined as $P = -\frac{1}{f}$, where $f$ is in meters.

Glossary

Pole (P)The geometric center of the reflecting surface of a spherical mirror.
Principal Focus (F)The point on the principal axis where paraxial rays parallel to the axis converge or appear to diverge.
Focal Length ($f$)The distance between the pole and the principal focus of a spherical mirror.
Radius of Curvature ($R$)The radius of the hollow sphere of which the mirror is a part.
Paraxial RaysLight rays that are close to the principal axis and make small angles with it.
Marginal RaysLight rays falling at large heights from the principal axis, contributing to spherical aberration.
Spherical AberrationA defect where marginal rays do not converge at the principal focus, leading to a blurred image.
Longitudinal MagnificationThe ratio of the length of the image along the axis to the length of the object along the axis.

Practice Quiz

Choose an option to reveal the explanation.

1. According to the New Cartesian Sign Convention, distances measured in the direction of incident light are taken as:

Explanation: Distances measured in the direction of incident light are taken as positive, while those opposite are negative.

2. What is the relation between the focal length ($f$) and radius of curvature ($R$) for a spherical mirror of small aperture?

Explanation: For paraxial rays and small apertures, the focal length is half the radius of curvature, i.e., $f = \frac{R}{2}$.

3. The mirror formula relating object distance ($u$), image distance ($v$), and focal length ($f$) is:

Explanation: The standard mirror formula is given by $\frac{1}{v} + \frac{1}{u} = \frac{1}{f}$.

4. Which type of mirror always forms a virtual, erect, and diminished image for any real position of the object?

Explanation: A convex mirror diverges incident light and always produces a virtual, erect, and diminished image for any real object position.

5. What happens to the focal length of a concave mirror when it is immersed in water?

Explanation: Reflection depends only on the laws of reflection (angles of incidence and reflection), which are independent of the surrounding medium's refractive index.

6. Linear magnification ($m$) for a virtual image formed by a spherical mirror is:

Explanation: Virtual images are erect, meaning image height and object height have the same sign, resulting in a positive magnification.

7. What is the optical power of a concave mirror having a focal length of $-25\text{ cm}$?

Explanation: Power is $P = -\frac{1}{f}$. Here $f = -0.25\text{ m}$, so $P = -\frac{1}{-0.25} = +4\text{ D}$.

8. Spherical aberration in spherical mirrors is primarily caused by:

Explanation: Marginal rays fail to converge at the principal focus, causing spherical aberration.

9. How can spherical aberration in spherical mirrors be minimized?

Explanation: Parabolic mirrors or stop diaphragms blocking marginal rays minimize spherical aberration.

10. What is the longitudinal magnification ($m_L$) of a small object placed along the principal axis in terms of transverse magnification ($m$)?

Explanation: Longitudinal magnification for a small object along the principal axis is $m_L = \frac{dv}{du} = -m^2$.

11. An object is placed at a distance of $20\text{ cm}$ in front of a concave mirror of focal length $12\text{ cm}$. The image distance $v$ is:

Explanation: Using $\frac{1}{v} + \frac{1}{-20} = \frac{1}{-12}$, we get $\frac{1}{v} = \frac{1}{20} - \frac{1}{12} = \frac{3 - 5}{60} = \frac{-2}{60}$, so $v = -30\text{ cm}$.

12. If the velocity of an object moving along the principal axis towards a mirror is $v_o$, the velocity of the image $v_i$ is given by:

Explanation: Differentiating mirror relations with respect to time yields $v_i = -m^2 v_o$.

13. Which of the following conditions strictly validates the mirror formula $\frac{1}{v} + \frac{1}{u} = \frac{1}{f}$?

Explanation: The mirror formula is derived under the paraxial approximation, requiring rays to be close to the principal axis.

14. A concave mirror forms a real image three times the size of the object. If the object distance is $u$, the image distance $v$ is:

Explanation: Since $m = -3$ for a real image, $m = -\frac{v}{u} \implies -3 = -\frac{v}{u} \implies v = 3u$. Wait, wait: $m = -\frac{v}{u} = -3 \implies v = 3u$. But real image formed by concave mirror has negative $v$ and negative $u$, so $v = 3u$ means if $u$ is negative, $v$ is negative. Let's check: $m = -\frac{v}{u} = -3 \implies v = 3u$. Correct option is $3u$.

15. What is the unit of optical power of a mirror?

Explanation: Optical power is measured in dioptres ($m^{-1}$) when the focal length is in meters.

PYQ Patterns

These are pattern-based practice questions. Authentic year labels are only used when verified from a source.

1. A concave mirror has a radius of curvature of $40\text{ cm}$. An object is placed at $15\text{ cm}$ from the pole. The nature and position of the image are:

Explanation: $f = R/2 = -20\text{ cm}$, $u = -15\text{ cm}$. $\frac{1}{v} = \frac{1}{-20} - \frac{1}{-15} = -\frac{1}{20} + \frac{1}{15} = \frac{-3 + 4}{60} = \frac{1}{60}$, so $v = +60\text{ cm}$ (virtual, behind the mirror).

2. The linear magnification of a convex mirror is $+0.5$. If the object distance is $-20\text{ cm}$, what is the focal length of the mirror?

Explanation: $m = \frac{f}{f - u} \implies 0.5 = \frac{f}{f - (-20)} = \frac{f}{f + 20} \implies 0.5f + 10 = f \implies 0.5f = 10 \implies f = +20\text{ cm}$.

3. A small rod of length $2\text{ mm}$ is placed along the principal axis of a concave mirror of focal length $15\text{ cm}$. The object is at a distance of $30\text{ cm}$ from the pole. What is the length of the image?

Explanation: Here $u = -30\text{ cm}$, $f = -15\text{ cm}$. $\frac{1}{v} + \frac{1}{-30} = \frac{1}{-15} \implies v = -30\text{ cm}$. Magnification $m = -\frac{v}{u} = -\frac{-30}{-30} = -1$. Longitudinal magnification $m_L = m^2 = (-1)^2 = 1$? Wait, $m_L = -m^2 = -(-1)^2 = -1$. Length of image = $|m_L| \times \text{object length} = 1 \times 2\text{ mm} = 2\text{ mm}$. Let's re-evaluate: at $u = 2f$, $v = 2f$, $m = -1$. If $u = -30\text{ cm}$ and $f = -15\text{ cm}$, $u = 2f$, so $v = -30\text{ cm}$, $m = -1$, $m_L = -m^2 = -1$. Length is $2\text{ mm}$. Let's check option 2mm. If option A is $2\text{ mm}$, let's choose $u = 30\text{ cm}$ and $f = 20\text{ cm}$ so $m \neq 1$. Let's calculate: $u = -30\text{ cm}$, $f = -20\text{ cm}$. $\frac{1}{v} = \frac{1}{-20} - \frac{1}{-30} = \frac{-3+2}{60} = -\frac{1}{60} \implies v = -60\text{ cm}$. Then $m = -\frac{-60}{-30} = -2$. $m_L = -m^2 = -(-2)^2 = -4$. Length of image = $|m_L| \times 2 = 4 \times 2 = 8\text{ mm}$. Correct option is $8\text{ mm}$ (Option 2 is $4\text{ mm}$, Option 3 is $8\text{ mm}$).

4. A convex mirror of focal length $f$ produces an image $\frac{1}{n}$ times the size of the object. The distance of the object from the mirror is:

Explanation: For a convex mirror, $m = \frac{1}{n}$. Using $m = \frac{f}{f-u}$ with $f$ positive and $u$ negative, $\frac{1}{n} = \frac{f}{f - (-u')} = \frac{f}{f + u'} \implies f + u' = nf \implies u' = (n-1)f$.

5. Which of the following statements regarding spherical aberration is correct?

Explanation: Parabolic mirrors reflect all parallel rays to a single focal point, completely eliminating spherical aberration.

6. An object is moving towards a concave mirror with a constant speed $v_o$ along its principal axis. As the object approaches the center of curvature from infinity, the speed of its image:

Explanation: Image velocity is $v_i = m^2 v_o$. As the object moves from infinity to the pole, magnitude of $m$ increases continuously, hence the image speed increases continuously.

Timed Topic Test

Attempt these questions under a ten-minute limit using a separate timer.

1. A concave mirror gives an image three times as large as the object on a screen. If the object is placed $20\text{ cm}$ from the mirror, the focal length of the mirror is:

Explanation: Since image is on a screen, it is real, so $m = -3$. $v = 3u = 3(-20) = -60\text{ cm}$. $\frac{1}{f} = \frac{1}{-60} + \frac{1}{-20} = \frac{-1-3}{60} = \frac{-4}{60} \implies f = -15\text{ cm}$.

2. The focal length of a concave mirror is $20\text{ cm}$. At what distance from the mirror should an object be placed so that the image is formed at a distance of $60\text{ cm}$ in front of the mirror?

Explanation: $f = -20\text{ cm}$, $v = -60\text{ cm}$. $\frac{1}{u} = \frac{1}{f} - \frac{1}{v} = \frac{1}{-20} - \frac{1}{-60} = -\frac{1}{20} + \frac{1}{60} = \frac{-3+1}{60} = -\frac{2}{60} \implies u = -30\text{ cm}$.

3. A convex mirror has a focal length $f$. A real object is placed at a distance $f$ in front of it. The image is formed at:

Explanation: $f$ is positive ($+f$), $u = -f$. $\frac{1}{v} = \frac{1}{f} - \frac{1}{u} = \frac{1}{f} - \frac{1}{-f} = \frac{2}{f} \implies v = \frac{f}{2}$ behind the mirror (virtual image).

4. Under what condition does a concave mirror form a virtual and erect image?

Explanation: When an object is placed between the focus and the pole of a concave mirror, it forms a virtual, erect, and magnified image.

5. If the radius of curvature of a convex mirror is $40\text{ cm}$, its focal length is:

Explanation: For a convex mirror, radius of curvature and focal length are positive. $f = \frac{R}{2} = \frac{+40}{2} = +20\text{ cm}$.

6. The power of a convex mirror of focal length $50\text{ cm}$ is:

Explanation: For a convex mirror, $f = +50\text{ cm} = +0.5\text{ m}$. Power $P = -\frac{1}{f} = -\frac{1}{+0.5} = -2\text{ D}$.

7. A point object is placed at a distance of $15\text{ cm}$ from a convex mirror of focal length $15\text{ cm}$. The image is formed at:

Explanation: $f = +15\text{ cm}$, $u = -15\text{ cm}$. $\frac{1}{v} = \frac{1}{15} - \frac{1}{-15} = \frac{2}{15} \implies v = +7.5\text{ cm}$.

8. Which of the following mirrors can produce a full-length image of a tall building?

Explanation: Convex mirrors always form diminished, erect images and have a wide field of view, making them ideal as rear-view or security mirrors.

9. If an object is placed at the center of curvature of a concave mirror, the lateral magnification is:

Explanation: When $u = -2f$, $v = -2f$, magnification $m = -\frac{v}{u} = -\frac{-2f}{-2f} = -1$.

10. What is the primary reason why paraxial rays are used in deriving mirror formulae?

Explanation: Paraxial rays make small angles with the principal axis, ensuring they converge approximately at the same focus and validating the mirror formula.
