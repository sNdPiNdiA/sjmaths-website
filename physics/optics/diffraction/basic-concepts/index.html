Basic Concepts of Diffraction

Diffraction is the bending of light waves around the corners of an obstacle or aperture into the geometrical shadow region. It arises due to the secondary wavelets originating from different points of the same wavefront, as stated by Huygens-Fresnel principle.

Prerequisites

  • Phase difference and path difference relationships

Wavefront Subdivision and Huygens-Fresnel Principle

  • Every point on a propagating wavefront acts as a source of secondary spherical wavelets.
  • The resultant optical disturbance at any subsequent point is the superposition of all these secondary wavelets taking their respective phases and amplitudes into account.
  • Diffraction is fundamentally the interference of a continuous infinity of coherent secondary wavelets originating from the unobstructed portions of a single wavefront.

Classification of Diffraction: Fresnel and Fraunhofer

  • Fresnel diffraction occurs when either the source of light, the obstacle/aperture, or the screen (or any combination) is at a finite distance, making the wavefronts incident and emergent spherical or cylindrical.
  • Fraunhofer diffraction occurs when both the source and the observation screen are effectively at infinite distances from the aperture, achieved experimentally using lenses.
  • In Fraunhofer diffraction, the incident wavefronts are planar and the diffracted rays forming a particular parallel beam are brought to a focus on the screen.

Distinction Between Interference and Diffraction

  • Interference is the superposition of two or more distinct coherent wave trains originating from separate wavefronts (e.g., Young's double slit).
  • Diffraction is the superposition of continuous secondary wavelets originating from different parts of the same wavefront.
  • In interference, all bright fringes are generally of equal intensity, whereas in single-slit diffraction, the central maximum is brightest and widest, with subsidiary maxima falling off rapidly in intensity.

Conditions for Maxima and Minima in Single-Slit Diffraction

  • Secondary minima occur when the path difference between light rays originating from the top and bottom edges of a slit of width a is an integral multiple of the wavelength lambda.
  • Principal maxima occur approximately midway between the secondary minima, corresponding to odd multiples of lambda / 2 path difference for consecutive zones.
  • The central maximum is twice as wide as any of the secondary maxima and contains the vast majority of the incident optical energy.

Derivations

Fraunhofer Diffraction at a Single Slit

Conditions: Normal incidence of plane wavefront on a slit of width a, observation on a focal plane of a convex lens.

  • Consider a slit of width a divided into n equal strips. The path difference between rays from the extreme edges of the slit for a diffraction angle theta is given by delta = a sin(theta).
  • The phase difference between successive strips is beta = (2pi / lambda) (a/n) sin( heta). Let the total phase difference between the extreme rays be 2alpha = (2pi / lambda) a sin( heta).
  • Summing the amplitudes of n secondary wavelets using the polygon method of vector addition yields the resultant amplitude R = A0 (sin(alpha) / alpha), where A0 is the maximum amplitude.
  • The intensity is proportional to R^2, giving I = I0 (sin(alpha) / alpha)^2.

Position of Secondary Minima in Single-Slit Diffraction

Conditions: Destructive interference conditions across the slit width a.

  • Divide the slit width a into two equal halves of width a/2. For any ray from the upper half, a corresponding ray from the lower half can be found with a path difference of (lambda / 2).
  • This mutual cancellation leads to the first minimum when (a/2) sin heta = lambda / 2, or a sin heta = lambda.
  • Generalizing this subdivision into 2n equal parts results in destructive interference whenever corresponding destructive pairs cancel out.
  • Therefore, the condition for the m-th secondary minimum is a sin heta = mlambda, where m = pm 1, pm 2, pm 3, ...

Result: a sin heta = mlambda quad (m = pm 1, pm 2, pm 3, ...)

Solved numericals

TGT core

Light of wavelength 5000 Å falls normally on a single slit of width 0.2 mm. Calculate the angular position of the first secondary minimum.

  • Identify given parameters: lambda = 5000 Å = 5 x 10^-7 m, a = 0.2 mm = 2 x 10^-4 m, m = 1.
  • Use the diffraction minimum condition: a sin(theta) = m * lambda.
  • Substitute values: sin(theta) = (1 * 5 x 10^-7) / (2 x 10^-4) = 2.5 x 10^-3.
  • Since sin(theta) is very small, theta approx sin(theta) = 2.5 x 10^-3 radians.

Answer: 2.5 x 10^-3 rad

PGT extension

A slit of width 0.5 mm is illuminated normally by light of wavelength 589 nm. The diffraction pattern is observed on a screen placed 1.0 m away from the lens. Calculate the linear width of the central maximum.

  • Identify given data: a = 0.5 mm = 5 x 10^-4 m, lambda = 589 nm = 5.89 x 10^-7 m, f (or D) = 1.0 m.
  • Recall the formula for linear width of central maximum: beta_0 = 2 * lambda * f / a.
  • Substitute the values: beta_0 = (2 * 5.89 x 10^-7 * 1.0) / (5 x 10^-4).
  • Calculate the final numerical value: beta_0 = (11.78 x 10^-7) / (5 x 10^-4) = 2.356 x 10^-3 m.

Answer: 2.356 mm

PGT extension

In a single slit diffraction pattern, the angular width of the central maximum is measured to be 2 degrees when light of wavelength 600 nm is used. Find the width of the slit.

  • Identify the angular width relation: Total angular width = 2 * theta = 2 * (lambda / a).
  • Convert angle to radians: 2 degrees = 2 * (pi / 180) rad = (pi / 90) rad. Therefore, half-angle theta = pi / 180 rad.
  • Use theta = lambda / a to find a: a = lambda / theta.
  • Substitute lambda = 600 x 10^-9 m and theta = (pi / 180) rad: a = (600 x 10^-9 * 180) / pi.
  • Evaluate: a = (1.08 x 10^-4) / 3.1416 approx 3.438 x 10^-5 m = 0.0344 mm.

Answer: 3.44 x 10^-5 m (or 0.0344 mm)

Assumptions and limitations

  • Scalar diffraction theory assumes the light wave can be treated as a scalar wave, ignoring vector polarization effects, which holds well when the aperture dimensions are much larger than the wavelength.
  • Fraunhofer approximations assume that distances R >> a^2 / lambda, validating the neglect of quadratic phase terms across the aperture.

Common misconceptions

Common error: All bright fringes in a single-slit diffraction pattern have equal intensity.

Correct: Only the central maximum has maximum intensity; subsidiary maxima have drastically lower intensities.

Unlike interference fringes where amplitudes from two coherent slits remain uniform, diffraction involves varying fractional cancellation across a continuous aperture.

Common error: Diffraction only occurs when obstacles are microscopic.

Correct: Diffraction occurs for obstacles of any size, but it is pronounced only when the obstacle or aperture dimensions are comparable to the wavelength of the wave.

The diffraction angle theta approx lambda / a is too small to detect easily when aperture a is extremely large compared to lambda.

Quick revision

  • Diffraction is the bending of waves around edges governed by secondary wavelets from unblocked wavefronts.
  • Fraunhofer diffraction requires infinite source-screen distances, simulated in labs via lenses.
  • Minima condition for single slit: a sin(theta) = m*lambda (m != 0).
  • Central maximum width is twice that of any secondary maximum: beta_0 = 2*lambda*f / a.

Must remember

  • Diffraction is fundamentally the interference of a continuous infinity of coherent secondary wavelets originating from the unobstructed portions of a single wavefront.
  • Fresnel diffraction involves finite distances for source and/or screen, producing spherical or cylindrical wavefronts.
  • Fraunhofer diffraction requires both source and screen to be effectively at infinite distances, realized using lenses to handle planar wavefronts.
  • The condition for secondary minima in single-slit diffraction is $a \sin \theta = m\lambda$, where $m = \pm 1, \pm 2, \dots$.
  • The linear width of the central maximum on a screen placed at the focal plane of a converging lens is given by $\beta_0 = \frac{2f\lambda}{a}$.
  • Unlike interference fringes, single-slit diffraction subsidiary maxima fall off rapidly in intensity and the central maximum is twice as wide.

Practice Quiz

Choose an option to reveal the explanation.

1. What is the primary physical origin of diffraction according to the Huygens-Fresnel principle?

Explanation: Diffraction is fundamentally the interference of a continuous infinity of coherent secondary wavelets originating from the unobstructed portions of a single wavefront.

2. In which type of diffraction are the incident and emergent wavefronts planar?

Explanation: Fraunhofer diffraction occurs when both the source and the observation screen are effectively at infinite distances, so the incident wavefronts are planar.

3. What is the condition for secondary minima in single-slit diffraction of width $a$ for wavelength $\lambda$?

Explanation: Secondary minima occur when the path difference between extreme rays is an integral multiple of wavelength, given by $a \sin \$\theta$ = m\lambda$ ($m \neq 0$).

4. How does the intensity of subsidiary maxima compare to the central maximum in single-slit diffraction?

Explanation: In single-slit diffraction, the central maximum is brightest and widest, with subsidiary maxima falling off rapidly in intensity.

5. What is the linear width of the central maximum $\$\beta_{0}$$ on a screen placed at the focal plane of a lens of focal length $f$?

Explanation: The linear width of the central maximum is $\$\beta_{0}$ = \frac{2f\lambda}{a}$, where $a$ is the slit width.

6. Which diffraction class is characterized by finite distances between the source, aperture, and screen?

Explanation: Fresnel diffraction occurs when either the source, obstacle/aperture, or screen is at a finite distance, yielding spherical or cylindrical wavefronts.

7. What is the half-angular width of the central maximum for a narrow slit of width $a$ illuminated by light of wavelength $\lambda$?

Explanation: Using the small angle approximation for narrow slits, $\sin \$\theta$ \approx \$\theta$ = \frac{\lambda}{a}$.

8. Which of the following correctly defines the phase difference parameter $\$\alpha$$ in single-slit diffraction?

Explanation: The phase difference parameter is defined as $\$\alpha$ = \frac{\$\pi$ a \sin \$\theta$}{\lambda}$, representing the half-phase difference between extreme rays.

9. What is the intensity distribution formula for Fraunhofer single-slit diffraction?

Explanation: The intensity distribution is given by $I = $I_{0}$ \left(\frac{\sin \$\alpha$}{\$\alpha$}\right)^2$, where $\$\alpha$ = \frac{\$\pi$ a \sin \$\theta$}{\lambda}$.

10. Why is diffraction pronounced only when obstacle dimensions are comparable to the wavelength of the wave?

Explanation: When aperture $a$ is extremely large compared to $\lambda$, the diffraction angle $\$\theta$ \approx \frac{\lambda}{a}$ is vanishingly small, making the effect undetectable.

11. What is the path difference between extreme rays originating from the top and bottom edges of a slit of width $a$ at diffraction angle $\$\theta$$?

Explanation: The path difference between extreme rays for normal incidence is given by $\Delta = a \sin \$\theta$$.

12. In interference (e.g., Young's double slit), how do bright fringes compare in intensity?

Explanation: In interference, all bright fringes are generally of equal intensity, unlike single-slit diffraction where subsidiary maxima fall off rapidly.

13. What experimental arrangement is typically used to achieve Fraunhofer diffraction in a laboratory?

Explanation: Fraunhofer diffraction is achieved experimentally using lenses so that both the source and the observation screen are effectively at infinite distances.

14. What is the approximate condition for secondary maxima (excluding the central maximum) in single-slit diffraction?

Explanation: Principal/secondary maxima occur approximately midway between secondary minima, corresponding to odd multiples of $\frac{\lambda}{2}$, i.e., $a \sin \$\theta$ = \left(m + \frac{1}{2}\right)\lambda$.

15. Which of the following is a common misconception about single-slit diffraction?

Explanation: It is a common misconception that all bright fringes have equal intensity; in reality, subsidiary maxima drop off drastically in intensity compared to the central maximum.

PYQ Patterns

These are pattern-based practice questions. Authentic year labels are only used when verified from a source.

1. A slit of width $0.2 \text{ mm}$ is illuminated by monochromatic light of wavelength $5890 \text{ \AA}$. What is the angular half-width of the central maximum?

Explanation: Using $\$\theta$ = \frac{\lambda}{a} = \frac{5.890 \times 10^{-7} \text{ m}}{0.2 \times 10^{-3} \text{ m}} = 2.945 \times 10^{-3} \text{ rad}.

2. In a Fraunhofer diffraction pattern by a single slit, the first secondary minimum is observed at an angle of $30^\circ$ for a light of wavelength $5000 \text{ \AA}$. What is the width of the slit?

Explanation: For the first secondary minimum ($m=1$), $a \sin \$\theta$ = 1\lambda \implies a \sin(30^\circ) = 5000 \times 10^{-10} \implies a(0.5) = 5 \times 10^{-7} \implies a = 1 \times 10^{-6} \text{ m}.

3. What is the ratio of the width of the central maximum to that of any secondary maximum in single-slit diffraction?

Explanation: The angular width of the central maximum is $2\frac{\lambda}{a}$, while the angular width of any subsidiary maximum is $\frac{\lambda}{a}$. Thus, the ratio is $2:1$.

4. If the slit width in a single-slit diffraction experiment is halved, how does the angular width of the central maximum change?

Explanation: Since angular half-width $\$\theta$ = \frac{\lambda}{a}$, halving $a$ doubles the angular width of the central maximum.

5. Light of wavelength $6000 \text{ \AA}$ falls normally on a slit. If the first minimum is formed at $\$\theta$ = 30^\circ$, calculate the slit width $a$.

Explanation: Using $a \sin \$\theta$ = \lambda$ for $m=1$, $a \sin(30^\circ) = 6000 \times 10^{-10} \implies a(0.5) = 6 \times 10^{-7} \implies a = 1.2 \times 10^{-6} \text{ m}.

6. Which of the following statements correctly distinguishes diffraction from interference?

Explanation: Interference is the superposition of distinct coherent wave trains from separate wavefronts, while diffraction is the superposition of continuous secondary wavelets from different parts of the same wavefront.

Timed Topic Test

Attempt these questions under a ten-minute limit using a separate timer.

1. What happens to the diffraction pattern of a single slit if the monochromatic light source is replaced by a white light source?

Explanation: With white light, the central maximum for all wavelengths coincides at $\$\theta$ = 0$ forming a white fringe, while subsidiary maxima are dispersed into colored spectra because their positions depend on $\lambda$.

2. In Fraunhofer diffraction by a single slit, the intensity of the first subsidiary maximum is approximately what fraction of the central maximum intensity $$I_{0}$$?

Explanation: The first subsidiary maximum occurs at $\$\alpha$ \approx \frac{3\$\pi$}{2}$. Substituting into $I = $I_{0}$ \left(\frac{\sin \$\alpha$}{\$\alpha$}\right)^2$ gives $I \approx $I_{0}$ \left(\frac{1}{3\$\pi$/2}\right)^2 = \frac{4}{9\$\pi$^2} $I_{0}$$ (roughly $4.5\%$ of $$I_{0}$$).

3. What is the radius of the $n$-th Fresnel zone for a point source and screen setup given by $r_n = \sqrt{\frac{n \lambda b}{1 + b/a}}$? What does $b$ represent?

Explanation: In the Fresnel zone expression, $a$ represents the source distance and $b$ represents the screen (observation point) distance from the aperture.

4. If the wavelength of light used in a single-slit diffraction setup is doubled while keeping the slit width constant, what happens to the linear width of the central maximum?

Explanation: Since $\$\beta_{0}$ = \frac{2f\lambda}{a}$, the linear width is directly proportional to wavelength $\lambda$. Doubling $\lambda$ doubles the linear width.

5. Which condition separates Fresnel diffraction from Fraunhofer diffraction?

Explanation: Fresnel diffraction occurs at finite distances (spherical/cylindrical wavefronts), whereas Fraunhofer diffraction occurs at infinite distances (planar wavefronts, achieved via lenses).

6. In single-slit diffraction, what occurs at angles where $\$\alpha$$ is an integral multiple of $\$\pi$$ (excluding zero)?

Explanation: When $\$\alpha$ = m\$\pi$$ ($m \neq 0$), $\sin \$\alpha$ = 0$, which corresponds to secondary minima and zero optical intensity.

7. Why can sound waves bend around corners of a doorway more noticeably than light waves?

Explanation: Diffraction is prominent when obstacle dimensions are comparable to wavelength. Sound wavelengths are in meters (comparable to doors), whereas light wavelengths are in nanometers.

8. What is the effect on the diffraction pattern if a transparent thin film is introduced in front of one half of the single slit?

Explanation: Introducing a phase-changing thin film across part of the slit introduces an additional optical path difference, causing a lateral shift of the entire diffraction pattern on the screen.

9. What is the phase difference between wavelets originating from the top and bottom edges of a single slit when the path difference is equal to $\lambda$?

Explanation: A path difference of $\lambda$ corresponds to a phase difference of $2\$\pi$$ radians between extreme rays across the entire slit width $a$.

10. Which of the following parameters does NOT directly affect the angular position of secondary minima in single-slit Fraunhofer diffraction?

Explanation: The angular positions are given by $a \sin \$\theta$ = m\lambda$, which depends on $a$, $\lambda$, and $m$. The focal length $f$ affects linear positions on the focal plane, not angular positions.
