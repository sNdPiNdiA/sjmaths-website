Specific Heat of Gases: Molar Heat Capacities

The specific heat of a gas represents the amount of thermal energy required to raise the temperature of a unit mass or one mole of the gas by one kelvin. Gases possess two distinct molar heat capacities corresponding to constant volume and constant pressure processes.

Definition and Physical Meaning of C&lt;sub&gt;v&lt;/sub&gt; and C&lt;sub&gt;p&lt;/sub&gt;

  • Molar heat capacity at constant volume (Cv) is the heat supplied per mole per unit temperature rise when the volume of the gas is kept fixed, implying zero external work done.
  • Molar heat capacity at constant pressure (Cp) is the heat supplied per mole per unit temperature rise when the pressure is kept constant, during which the gas expands and performs mechanical work.
  • Since Cp accounts for both internal energy increase and external work done during expansion, Cp is always greater than Cv for any ideal gas.

Law of Equipartition of Energy

  • According to the law of equipartition of energy, in any dynamical system in thermal equilibrium, the total energy is shared equally among all active degrees of freedom.
  • Each translational and rotational degree of freedom contributes an average thermal energy of (1/2)kBT per molecule, or (1/2)RT per mole.
  • Each vibrational mode contributes two terms to the energy (kinetic and potential), giving an average energy of kBT per molecule or RT per mole.

Degrees of Freedom across Atomicity

  • Monoatomic gases (e.g., He, Ne) possess 3 translational degrees of freedom, giving Cv = (3/2)R and Cp = (5/2)R.
  • Rigid diatomic gases (e.g., O2, N2 at room temperature) possess 3 translational and 2 rotational degrees of freedom (total f = 5), yielding Cv = (5/2)R and Cp = (7/2)R.
  • Non-linear triatomic gases (e.g., H2O) possess 3 translational and 3 rotational degrees of freedom (total f = 6), giving Cv = 3R and Cp = 4R.

Ratio of Principal Specific Heats (&amp;gamma)

  • The adiabatic index or ratio of specific heats is defined as &gamma; = Cp / Cv = 1 + (2/f).
  • For monoatomic gases, &gamma; = 5/3 &approx; 1.67.
  • For rigid diatomic gases, &gamma; = 7/5 = 1.40, and for non-linear triatomic gases, &gamma; = 4/3 &approx; 1.33.

Derivations

Derivation of Mayer's Relation (C&lt;sub&gt;p&lt;/sub&gt; - C&lt;sub&gt;v&lt;/sub&gt; = R)

Conditions: One mole of an ideal gas undergoing heating at constant volume and constant pressure

  • From the first law of thermodynamics, d$Q = dU + dW$, where dQ is heat supplied, dU is change in internal energy, and dW is work done.
  • At constant volume, dV = 0, so dW = 0. Therefore, heat supplied is dQv = d$U = C_{v}dT$.
  • At constant pressure, heat supplied is dQp = Cpd$T = dU + PdV = C_{v}dT + PdV$.
  • For one mole of an ideal gas, PV = RT, differentiating gives PdV = RdT at constant pressure.
  • Substituting Pd$V = RdT into the constant pressure heat equation yields C_{p}dT = C_{v}dT + RdT$, which simplifies to Cp - Cv = R.

Result: Cp - Cv = R (For real gases, Cp - Cv = R[1 + 2a/(RTV)] approximately).

Specific Heat via Internal Energy and Degrees of Freedom

Conditions: Ideal gas with f degrees of freedom in thermal equilibrium

  • The total internal energy U of one mole of an ideal gas with f degrees of freedom is given by $U = (f/2)RT$, using the equipartition theorem.
  • The molar heat capacity at constant volume is obtained by differentiating internal energy with respect to temperature: Cv = dU/dT.
  • Differentiating (f/2)RT with respect to T gives Cv = (f/2)R.
  • Using Mayer's relation, the molar heat capacity at constant pressure is Cp = Cv + $R = (f/2)R + R = ((f + 2)/2)R$.

Result: Cv = (f/2)R and Cp = ((f + 2)/2)R

Calculate the molar heat capacity at constant volume (Cv) and constant pressure (Cp) for a monoatomic gas. Given $R = 8.31 J mol^-1 K^-1$.

  • Identify the atomicity of the gas: monoatomic gas.
  • Determine the degrees of freedom for a monoatomic gas: f = 3 (translational only).
  • Calculate Cv using the formula Cv = (f/2)$R = (3/2)R$.
  • Substitute $R = 8.31: C_{v} = 1.5 * 8.31 = 12.465 J mol^-1 K^-1$.
  • Calculate Cp using Mayer's relation: Cp = Cv + $R = (5/2)R = 2.5 * 8.31 = 20.775 J mol^-1 K^-1$.

Answer: Cv = 12.47 J mol^-1 K^-1, Cp = 20.78 J mol^-1 K^-1

Assumptions and limitations

  • Ideal gas laws assume point particles with no intermolecular forces, which fails at high pressures and low temperatures.
  • Equipartition theorem is a classical result; at very low temperatures, quantum mechanical zero-point energy and discrete energy level spacing cause degrees of freedom to freeze out.

Common misconceptions

Common error: Cp is greater than Cv because gases expand more at higher pressures.

Correct: Cp exceeds Cv because additional heat must be supplied at constant pressure to perform external work during expansion.

Work done during constant pressure expansion requires extra thermal energy input beyond merely raising the internal energy.

Common error: Degrees of freedom for a diatomic gas are always 5 regardless of temperature.

Correct: At high temperatures (>1000 K), vibrational degrees of freedom become active, making f = 7.

Thermal energy becomes comparable to the quantum vibrational spacing at high temperatures.

Quick revision

  • Cp - Cv = R for an ideal gas.
  • Cv = (f/2)R and Cp = ((f+2)/2)R.
  • &gamma; = Cp / Cv = 1 + (2/f).
  • Monoatomic: f=3, &gamma;=1.67; Diatomic: f=5, &gamma;=1.40; Triatomic non-linear: f=6, &gamma;=1.33.

Must remember

  • $C_p - C_v = R$ for an ideal gas, known as Mayer's relation.
  • The adiabatic index $\gamma = \frac{C_p}{C_v} = 1 + \frac{2}{f}$ depends only on the degrees of freedom $f$.
  • Monoatomic gases possess 3 degrees of freedom, giving $C_v = \frac{3}{2}R$ and $\gamma = 1.67$.
  • Rigid diatomic gases at room temperature possess 5 degrees of freedom, giving $C_v = \frac{5}{2}R$ and $\gamma = 1.40$.
  • Non-linear triatomic gases possess 6 degrees of freedom, giving $C_v = 3R$ and $\gamma = 1.33$.
  • At high temperatures, vibrational degrees of freedom activate, altering the total degrees of freedom.

Glossary

$C_v$Molar heat capacity at constant volume representing heat required per mole per unit temperature rise with fixed volume.
$C_p$Molar heat capacity at constant pressure representing heat required per mole per unit temperature rise with fixed pressure.
$R$Universal gas constant equal to $8.314\text{ J mol}^{-1}\text{ K}^{-1}$.
Degree of Freedom ($f$)Independent ways in which a molecule can store thermal energy (translational, rotational, vibrational).
Adiabatic Index ($\gamma$)Ratio of principal specific heats $\frac{C_p}{C_v}$, characterizing the compressibility and adiabatic behavior of gases.

Practice Quiz

Choose an option to reveal the explanation.

1. What is the relation between $C_p$ and $C_v$ for an ideal gas?

Explanation: Mayer's relation for an ideal gas is given by $C_p - C_v = R$, where $R$ is the universal gas constant.

2. For a monoatomic gas, the molar heat capacity at constant volume $C_v$ is:

Explanation: A monoatomic gas has $3$ translational degrees of freedom. Using $C_v = \frac{f}{2}R$, we get $C_v = \frac{3}{2}R$.

3. What is the ratio of specific heats $\gamma$ for a rigid diatomic gas?

Explanation: A rigid diatomic gas has $f = 5$. Thus, $\gamma = 1 + \frac{2}{5} = 1 + 0.4 = 1.40$.

4. Why is $C_p$ greater than $C_v$ for a gas?

Explanation: At constant pressure, when heat is supplied, the gas expands and performs external mechanical work, requiring extra heat input compared to constant volume.

5. What are the degrees of freedom for a non-linear triatomic gas like $H_2O$?

Explanation: A non-linear triatomic molecule has $3$ translational and $3$ rotational degrees of freedom, totaling $f = 6$.

6. According to the law of equipartition of energy, each active degree of freedom contributes an average thermal energy per mole equal to:

Explanation: Per mole, each active degree of freedom contributes $\frac{1}{2}RT$ (and per molecule, $\frac{1}{2}k_B T$).

7. What is the adiabatic index $\gamma$ for a monoatomic gas?

Explanation: For a monoatomic gas, $f = 3$, so $\gamma = 1 + \frac{2}{3} = \frac{5}{3} \approx 1.67$.

8. What happens to the degrees of freedom of a diatomic gas at very high temperatures (>1000 K)?

Explanation: At high temperatures, vibrational modes become active, contributing 2 additional terms (kinetic and potential), raising $f$ from $5$ to $7$.

9. What is the internal energy per mole of an ideal gas with $f$ degrees of freedom at absolute temperature $T$?

Explanation: Internal energy per mole is $U = \frac{f}{2}RT$, as each degree of freedom contributes $\frac{1}{2}RT$.

10. For a real gas obeying the van der Waals equation, Mayer's relation is modified to:

Explanation: For real gases, intermolecular forces and finite molecular volume modify Mayer's relation to $C_p - C_v = R\left(1 + \frac{2a}{RTV}\right)$.

11. What is the molar heat capacity at constant pressure $C_p$ for a diatomic gas at room temperature?

Explanation: For a rigid diatomic gas at room temperature, $f = 5$, so $C_p = C_v + R = \frac{5}{2}R + R = \frac{7}{2}R$.

12. How many vibrational degrees of freedom does a diatomic molecule have?

Explanation: A diatomic molecule has $1$ vibrational mode along the internuclear axis, contributing 2 terms to energy (kinetic and potential).

13. What is the specific heat per unit mass ($c$) related to molar heat capacity ($C$) and molar mass ($M$)?

Explanation: Specific heat capacity per unit mass is defined as $c = \frac{C}{M}$.

14. In a mixture of $n_1$ moles of gas 1 and $n_2$ moles of gas 2, the equivalent molar heat capacity $C_{v(mix)}$ is:

Explanation: The effective molar heat capacity at constant volume for a non-reacting gas mixture is weighted by the mole fractions: $C_{v(mix)} = \frac{n_1 C_{v1} + n_2 C_{v2}}{n_1 + n_2}$.

15. Which of the following gases has the highest adiabatic index $\gamma$?

Explanation: Monoatomic gases have the fewest degrees of freedom ($f = 3$), yielding the highest adiabatic index $\gamma = \frac{5}{3} \approx 1.67$.

Explanation: Since $C_v = \frac{f}{2}R$ and $C_p = \left(\frac{f}{2} + 1\right)R$, $\gamma = \frac{C_p}{C_v} = \frac{f/2 + 1}{f/2} = 1 + \frac{2}{f}$.

1. What is the molar heat capacity at constant volume $C_v$ for a diatomic gas whose molecules vibrate at high temperatures?

Explanation: At high temperatures, vibrational modes are active, giving $f = 7$ ($3$ translational, $2$ rotational, $2$ vibrational). Thus $C_v = \frac{7}{2}R$.

2. If Mayer's relation is $C_p - C_v = R$ for one mole of gas, what is the relation for $m$ grams of an ideal gas of molar mass $M$?

Explanation: Dividing molar heat capacities by molar mass $M$ gives specific heat capacities per unit mass: $c_p - c_v = \frac{R}{M}$.

3. What is the value of adiabatic index $\gamma$ for a non-linear triatomic gas like ozone ($O_3$) or water vapor ($H_2O$)?

Explanation: Non-linear triatomic gases possess $f = 6$, so $\gamma = 1 + \frac{2}{6} = 1 + \frac{1}{3} = \frac{4}{3} \approx 1.33$.

4. According to the law of equipartition of energy, how much total energy does $1$ mole of a monoatomic gas possess at absolute temperature $T$?

Explanation: A monoatomic gas has $3$ degrees of freedom, so its total internal energy per mole is $U = \frac{3}{2}RT$.

5. When heat is added to an ideal gas at constant pressure, what fraction of the supplied heat is typically converted into external work for a monoatomic gas?

Explanation: Heat supplied at constant pressure is $dQ = C_p dT = \frac{5}{2}R dT$. Work done is $dW = P dV = R dT$. Fraction converted to work is $\frac{R dT}{\frac{5}{2}R dT} = \frac{2}{5}$.

6. Find the equivalent adiabatic index $\gamma_{mix}$ for a mixture of $1$ mole of monoatomic gas and $1$ mole of diatomic gas (rigid).

Explanation: $C_{v(mix)} = \frac{1(\frac{3}{2}R) + 1(\frac{5}{2}R)}{1+1} = \frac{4R}{2} = 2R$. $C_{p(mix)} = C_{v(mix)} + R = 3R$. Thus $\gamma_{mix} = \frac{3R}{2R} = 1.50$.

7. Which gas molecule has $3$ translational and $2$ rotational degrees of freedom at room temperature?

Explanation: Rigid diatomic molecules like $O_2$ and $N_2$ have $3$ translational and $2$ rotational degrees of freedom at room temperature ($f = 5$).
