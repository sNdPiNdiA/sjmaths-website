Forced vibration – amplitude, phase, resonance

Day 3 of 129 Engineering Fundamentals Phase 1: Foundations 60-90 min Master Cycle

1. Understand (5–10 min)

In mechanical and aeronautical system dynamics, forced vibration occurs when a single-degree-of-freedom (SDOF) or multi-degree-of-freedom structure is subjected to a time-varying external excitation force or base motion. When the excitation force is harmonic, represented as $F(t) = F_0 \cos(\omega t)$ or $F(t) = F_0 \sin(\omega t)$, the structural response consists of two distinct components: a transient response (which decays exponentially over time due to damping) and a steady-state response (which persists at the excitation frequency $\omega$).

The fundamental equation of motion for a viscously damped SDOF system under direct harmonic forcing is defined as:

$$ m \ddot{x}(t) + c \dot{x}(t) + k x(t) = F_0 \cos(\omega t) $$

where $m$ is the system mass ($\text{kg}$), $c$ is the viscous damping coefficient ($\text{N}\cdot\text{s/m}$), $k$ is the structural stiffness ($\text{N/m}$), $F_0$ is the magnitude of the harmonic excitation force ($\text{N}$), and $\omega$ is the excitation circular frequency ($\text{rad/s}$).

The steady-state solution to this differential equation takes the form:

$$ x_p(t) = X \cos(\omega t - \phi) $$

where $X$ represents the steady-state displacement amplitude, and $\phi$ represents the phase angle by which the response lags behind the excitation force. The domain bounds and operational regimes are dictated by the frequency ratio $r = \frac{\omega}{\omega_n}$, where $\omega_n = \sqrt{\frac{k}{m}}$ is the undamped natural frequency, and the damping ratio $\zeta = \frac{c}{2\sqrt{km}} = \frac{c}{2m\omega_n}$.

Resonance is defined broadly as the physical state where the excitation frequency $\omega$ approaches or equals a characteristic frequency of the system, resulting in a dramatic amplification of response amplitude. In damped forced systems, exact resonance boundaries vary depending on whether the peak amplitude evaluated is displacement, velocity, or acceleration.

2. Learn the Concept (15–20 min)

To rigorously analyze forced vibration, we examine the governing differential equation expressed in standard canonical form by dividing through by mass $m$:

$$ \ddot{x}(t) + 2\zeta\omega_n \dot{x}(t) + \omega_n^2 x(t) = f_0 \cos(\omega t) $$

where $f_0 = \frac{F_0}{m} = \frac{F_0 \omega_n^2}{k}$.

1. Derivation of Steady-State Amplitude and Phase Angle

Using complex algebra, we express the harmonic forcing function as $\mathcal{F}(t) = F_0 e^{i\omega t}$ and assume a complex steady-state displacement response $\mathcal{x}_p(t) = \bar{X} e^{i\omega t}$, where $\bar{X} = X e^{-i\phi}$ is the complex amplitude. Substituting into the differential equation gives:

$$ \left[ -m\omega^2 + i c\omega + k \right] \bar{X} e^{i\omega t} = F_0 e^{i\omega t} $$ $$ \bar{X} = \frac{F_0}{(k - m\omega^2) + i(c\omega)} $$

Dividing numerator and denominator by $k$, and substituting $\omega_n^2 = \frac{k}{m}$, $r = \frac{\omega}{\omega_n}$, and $2\zeta\omega_n = \frac{c}{m}$:

$$ \bar{X} = \frac{F_0/k}{\left(1 - r^2\right) + i\left(2\zeta r\right)} $$

The magnitude $X = |\bar{X}|$ gives the steady-state displacement amplitude:

$$ X = \frac{F_0/k}{\sqrt{(1 - r^2)^2 + (2\zeta r)^2}} = x_{st} \cdot M $$

where $x_{st} = \frac{F_0}{k}$ is the zero-frequency static deflection under force $F_0$, and $M$ is the dimensionless Magnification Factor (or Dynamic Magnification):

$$ M(r, \zeta) = \frac{X}{x_{st}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\left(1 - r^2\right)^2 + \left(2\zeta r\right)^2}} $$ $$ \phi(r, \zeta) = \arctan\left( \frac{2\zeta r}{1 - r^2} \right) \quad (\text{where } \phi \in [0, \pi]) $$

2. Detailed Mathematical Analysis of Phase Lag ($\phi$)

The phase lag $\phi$ describes the physical delay between the application of peak force and peak displacement response:

  • Low Frequency Regime ($r \ll 1$): $1 - r^2 \approx 1 \implies \phi \to 0^\circ$. The system response is stiffness-dominated; force and displacement move in phase.
  • At Natural Frequency ($r = 1$): $1 - r^2 = 0 \implies \phi = 90^\circ = \frac{\pi}{2}\text{ rad}$. The system response is damping-dominated; excitation force strictly balances viscous damping force ($F_0 \cos\omega t = c\dot{x}$).
  • High Frequency Regime ($r \gg 1$): $1 - r^2 < 0 \implies \phi \to 180^\circ = \pi\text{ rad}$. The system response is inertia-dominated; motion is $180^\circ$ out of phase with excitation force.

3. Precise Conditions for Resonance and Peak Amplitudes

Resonance frequency differs depending on the physical parameter being maximized:

A. Displacement Amplitude Peak ($r_p$ or $\omega_{res, x}$):

To find the frequency ratio $r_p$ that maximizes $M(r, \zeta)$, we minimize the denominator function $D(r) = (1 - r^2)^2 + 4\zeta^2 r^2$:

$$ \frac{d D(r)}{dr} = 2(1 - r^2)(-2r) + 8\zeta^2 r = 0 \implies -4r \left[ (1 - r^2) - 2\zeta^2 \right] = 0 $$

For non-trivial solution $r \neq 0$:

$$ 1 - r_p^2 - 2\zeta^2 = 0 \implies r_p = \sqrt{1 - 2\zeta^2} $$ $$ \omega_{res, x} = \omega_n \sqrt{1 - 2\zeta^2} $$

Domain Bound Criterion: Real peak displacement resonance exists if and only if $1 - 2\zeta^2 > 0 \implies \zeta < \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \approx 0.7071$. For $\zeta \ge \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}$, peak amplitude occurs at $r = 0$ ($M \le 1$).

Substituting $r_p = \sqrt{1 - 2\zeta^2}$ into $M(r, \zeta)$ yields the maximum magnification factor:

$$ M_{max} = \frac{1}{2\zeta \sqrt{1 - \zeta^2}} $$

B. Velocity Amplitude Peak ($\omega_{res, v}$):

Velocity response amplitude is $V(\omega) = \omega X = \frac{\omega (F_0/k)}{\sqrt{(1-r^2)^2 + (2\zeta r)^2}} = \frac{(F_0/\sqrt{km}) r}{\sqrt{(1-r^2)^2 + (2\zeta r)^2}}$. Differentiating $V(r)$ with respect to $r$ yields maximum velocity amplitude strictly at $r = 1$ ($\omega = \omega_n$), independent of damping ratio $\zeta$.

C. Acceleration Amplitude Peak ($\omega_{res, a}$):

Acceleration amplitude $A(\omega) = \omega^2 X$. Differentiating $A(r)$ yields peak acceleration at $r_a = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 - 2\zeta^2}}$, or $\omega_{res, a} = \frac{\omega_n}{\sqrt{1 - 2\zeta^2}}$ (for $\zeta < \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}$).

4. Force Transmissibility ($TR$) and Vibration Isolation

When a structure is mounted on a foundation via spring $k$ and damper $c$, the total force transmitted to the foundation $F_T(t)$ is the vector sum of spring force and damping force:

$$ F_T(t) = k x_p(t) + c \dot{x}_p(t) $$

The force transmissibility ratio $TR$ is defined as:

$$ TR = \frac{|F_T|}{F_0} = \frac{\sqrt{1 + (2\zeta r)^2}}{\sqrt{(1 - r^2)^2 + (2\zeta r)^2}} $$

Key Isolation Properties of $TR$:

  • At $r = 0$, $TR = 1$.
  • At $r = \sqrt{2}$, $TR = 1$ for all values of damping ratio $\zeta$. The point $(r, TR) = (\sqrt{2}, 1)$ is the crossover point.
  • For $r < \sqrt{2}$, $TR > 1$ (amplification range). Damping decreases $TR$.
  • For $r > \sqrt{2}$, $TR < 1$ (vibration isolation range). High frequency isolation requires operating in this regime. However, in this regime ($r > \sqrt{2}$), increasing damping ratio $\zeta$ increases $TR$ (reduces isolation effectiveness).

5. Half-Power Bandwidth and Quality Factor ($Q$)

For lightly damped systems ($\zeta \ll 1$), the Quality Factor $Q$ measures sharp frequency selectivity at resonance ($r = 1$):

$$ Q = M|_{r=1} = \frac{1}{2\zeta} $$

The half-power frequencies $\omega_1$ and $\omega_2$ are frequencies where $M = \frac{M_{max}}{\sqrt{2}} \approx \frac{Q}{\sqrt{2}}$. The bandwidth $\Delta \omega = \omega_2 - \omega_1 = 2\zeta \omega_n$. Thus:

$$ Q = \frac{\omega_n}{\omega_2 - \omega_1} = \frac{\omega_n}{\Delta \omega} $$

3. Memorize Important Facts (10 min)

  • Magnification Factor (Displacement): $M = \frac{X}{x_{st}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{(1 - r^2)^2 + (2\zeta r)^2}}$
  • Phase Angle Equation: $\phi = \arctan\left(\frac{2\zeta r}{1 - r^2}\right)$ (At $r=1$, $\phi = 90^\circ$)
  • Displacement Resonant Frequency: $\omega_{res, x} = \omega_n \sqrt{1 - 2\zeta^2} \quad \left(\text{valid only for } \zeta < \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\right)$
  • Maximum Displacement Magnification Factor: $M_{max} = \frac{1}{2\zeta \sqrt{1 - \zeta^2}}$
  • Damped Natural Frequency (Free Vibration): $\omega_d = \omega_n \sqrt{1 - \zeta^2}$
  • Velocity Resonant Frequency: $\omega_{res, v} = \omega_n \quad (\text{for all } \zeta)$
  • Acceleration Resonant Frequency: $\omega_{res, a} = \frac{\omega_n}{\sqrt{1 - 2\zeta^2}}$
  • Force and Motion Transmissibility Ratio: $TR = \frac{\sqrt{1 + (2\zeta r)^2}}{\sqrt{(1 - r^2)^2 + (2\zeta r)^2}}$
  • Isolation Crossover Frequency Ratio: $r = \sqrt{2} \implies TR = 1$ (Isolation requires $r > \sqrt{2}$)
  • Quality Factor and Bandwidth: $Q = \frac{1}{2\zeta} = \frac{\omega_n}{\Delta \omega} \implies \Delta \omega = 2\zeta \omega_n$
  • Rotating Unbalance Amplitude: $X = \frac{m_e e}{m} \frac{r^2}{\sqrt{(1 - r^2)^2 + (2\zeta r)^2}}$

4. Aviation Application (5–10 min)

Forced vibration principles form the analytical core of aerospace structural dynamics, airworthiness certification, and aircraft accident investigation:

1. Aeroelastic Wing Flutter and Atmospheric Buffeting

Aircraft wings undergo unsteady aerodynamic forcing from atmospheric turbulence or vortex shedding. Flutter occurs when aerodynamic forces couple with natural bending and torsional modes. Air safety investigators evaluate structural responses using complex impedance $H(i\omega)$. When flight speeds approach flutter velocity, effective aerodynamic damping decreases ($\zeta \to 0$), driving magnification factors $M \to \infty$ and triggering catastrophic structural failure.

2. Turbofan Engine Unbalance and Engine Mount Isolation

A jet engine rotor possesses an inherent residual eccentric mass $m_e$ at radius $e$. During high-speed operation ($\omega$), it generates a centrifugal forcing function $F(t) = m_e e \omega^2 \sin(\omega t)$. Engine isolator mounts are engineered with stiffness $k$ and damping $c$ such that the cruise frequency ratio $r = \frac{\omega}{\omega_n} \ge 2.5$ to $3.0$. This ensures force transmissibility $TR \ll 1$, preventing high-g engine vibration from transmitting to fuselage frames and avionics bays.

3. Helicopter Rotor Vibration (N-per-Rev Harmonic Excitation)

Helicopter airframes experience intense periodic forcing generated at $N \times \Omega$ (where $N$ is blade count and $\Omega$ is rotor speed). Active vibration isolation systems (AVCS) and tuned mass dampers (TMDs) adjust local stiffness or mass to shift natural frequencies $\omega_n$ away from $N\Omega$, avoiding displacement resonance conditions ($\omega_{res} = \omega_n \sqrt{1 - 2\zeta^2}$).

4. Air Safety Investigation & Black Box Dynamic Analysis

In crash investigations involving in-flight structural breakup, Flight Data Recorders (FDR) and onboard accelerometers record vibration spectra. Signal processing routines apply Z-transforms and digital FFT filters to evaluate structural transfer functions. Identifying peak response frequencies near $\omega_n \sqrt{1 - 2\zeta^2}$ allows safety officers to differentiate between primary structural resonance/fatigue failure and secondary impact loads.

5. UPSC ASO Focus (10 min)

  • Frequently Asked Facts & Formulas:
    • At exact undamped resonance ($r=1, \zeta=0$), amplitude increases linearly with time as $x(t) = \frac{F_0}{2 m \omega_n} t \sin(\omega_n t)$.
    • At $r = 1$ with damping, $M = \frac{1}{2\zeta}$, phase angle $\phi = 90^\circ$, and energy dissipation per cycle $W_d = \pi c \omega X^2$ reaches maximum value.
    • Distinguish the three characteristic frequencies: Undamped Natural $\omega_n$, Damped Free $\omega_d = \omega_n \sqrt{1-\zeta^2}$, and Displacement Forced Resonant $\omega_p = \omega_n \sqrt{1-2\zeta^2}$. Hierarchy: $\omega_p < \omega_d < \omega_n$.
    • Transmissibility crossover occurs precisely at $r = \sqrt{2}$. For $r < \sqrt{2}$, adding damping reduces transmission; for $r > \sqrt{2}$, adding damping increases transmission.
  • Conceptual Traps & Pitfalls:
    • Trap 1: Confusing Damped Natural Frequency $\omega_d$ with Resonant Frequency $\omega_p$. Remember: $\omega_d = \omega_n \sqrt{1-\zeta^2}$ governs transient decay rate; $\omega_p = \omega_n \sqrt{1-2\zeta^2}$ governs steady-state displacement peak.
    • Trap 2: Assuming resonance ALWAYS occurs at $r=1$. Peak displacement occurs at $r = \sqrt{1-2\zeta^2} < 1$. Peak velocity occurs at $r=1$. Peak acceleration occurs at $r = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1-2\zeta^2}} > 1$.
    • Trap 3: Believing higher damping is ALWAYS better for isolation. In the isolation zone ($r > \sqrt{2}$), increasing damping ratio $\zeta$ actually INCREASES the force transmitted to the aircraft airframe!
    • Trap 4: Phase angle limits: $\phi$ transitions from $0^\circ$ at $r=0$, through $90^\circ$ at $r=1$, approaching $180^\circ$ as $r \to \infty$. Neglecting quadrant selection when $1-r^2 < 0$ causes $180^\circ$ phase errors.

6. Practice Questions (Exhaustive UPSC ASO MCQs)

Q1. A mechanical component of mass $m = 10\text{ kg}$ is supported by a spring of stiffness $k = 4000\text{ N/m}$ and a viscous damper with $c = 40\text{ N}\cdot\text{s/m}$. It is subjected to a direct harmonic force $F(t) = 100 \cos(10 t)\text{ N}$. Calculate the steady-state displacement amplitude $X$.
A) $0.020\text{ m}$
B) $0.0333\text{ m}$
C) $0.0167\text{ m}$
D) $0.0250\text{ m}$
Step-by-step Solution:
1. Calculate undamped natural frequency: $\omega_n = \sqrt{\frac{k}{m}} = \sqrt{\frac{4000}{10}} = \sqrt{400} = 20\text{ rad/s}$.
2. Static deflection $x_{st} = \frac{F_0}{k} = \frac{100}{4000} = 0.025\text{ m}$.
3. Damping ratio: Critical damping $c_c = 2\sqrt{km} = 2\sqrt{4000 \times 10} = 2\sqrt{40000} = 400\text{ N}\cdot\text{s/m}$.
$\zeta = \frac{c}{c_c} = \frac{40}{400} = 0.1$.
4. Frequency ratio $r = \frac{\omega}{\omega_n} = \frac{10}{20} = 0.5$.
5. Magnification Factor $M = \frac{1}{\sqrt{(1 - r^2)^2 + (2\zeta r)^2}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{(1 - 0.25)^2 + (2 \times 0.1 \times 0.5)^2}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{0.75^2 + 0.1^2}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{0.5625 + 0.01}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{0.5725}} \approx \frac{1}{0.7566} \approx 1.3216$.
6. Steady-state amplitude $X = x_{st} \times M = 0.025 \times 1.3216 \approx 0.03304\text{ m} \approx 0.0333\text{ m}$.
Q2. For a viscously damped single-degree-of-freedom system with natural frequency $\omega_n = 100\text{ rad/s}$ and damping ratio $\zeta = 0.3$, at what excitation frequency $\omega$ will the steady-state displacement amplitude reach its absolute maximum peak value?
A) $100.0\text{ rad/s}$
B) $95.39\text{ rad/s}$
C) $90.55\text{ rad/s}$
D) $105.41\text{ rad/s}$
Step-by-step Solution:
1. Resonant frequency for steady-state displacement amplitude is given by $\omega_{res, x} = \omega_n \sqrt{1 - 2\zeta^2}$.
2. Substitute $\omega_n = 100\text{ rad/s}$ and $\zeta = 0.3$:
$2\zeta^2 = 2(0.3)^2 = 2 \times 0.09 = 0.18$.
$\omega_{res, x} = 100 \sqrt{1 - 0.18} = 100 \sqrt{0.82} = 100 \times 0.90554 = 90.55\text{ rad/s}$.
Note: Do not confuse this with damped natural frequency $\omega_d = \omega_n \sqrt{1 - \zeta^2} = 100\sqrt{0.91} = 95.39\text{ rad/s}$.
Q3. A system under forced harmonic vibration has a damping ratio $\zeta = 0.25$. At an excitation frequency equal to its undamped natural frequency ($\omega = \omega_n$), what is the phase angle $\phi$ between the excitation force and the displacement response?
A) $0^\circ$
B) $45^\circ$
C) $90^\circ$
D) $180^\circ$
Step-by-step Solution:
1. Phase angle formula: $\tan\phi = \frac{2\zeta r}{1 - r^2}$.
2. Here $\omega = \omega_n \implies r = 1$.
3. The denominator becomes $1 - r^2 = 1 - 1 = 0$.
4. Therefore, $\tan\phi = \frac{2(0.25)(1)}{0} \to \infty \implies \phi = 90^\circ$ (or $\frac{\pi}{2}\text{ rad}$).
5. At $r = 1$, the response lags the force by exactly $90^\circ$ for ALL non-zero values of damping ratio $\zeta$.
Q4. An avionics instrument package mounted on an aircraft airframe requires dynamic isolation from engine vibration. The force transmissibility ratio $TR$ will be strictly LESS THAN 1 (providing vibration isolation) if and only if the frequency ratio $r = \omega/\omega_n$ satisfies:
A) $r < 1$
B) $r > 1$
C) $r > \sqrt{2}$
D) $1 < r < \sqrt{2}$
Step-by-step Solution:
1. Transmissibility formula: $TR = \frac{\sqrt{1 + (2\zeta r)^2}}{\sqrt{(1 - r^2)^2 + (2\zeta r)^2}}$.
2. Set $TR = 1$: $\sqrt{1 + (2\zeta r)^2} = \sqrt{(1 - r^2)^2 + (2\zeta r)^2}$.
3. Squaring both sides: $1 + (2\zeta r)^2 = (1 - r^2)^2 + (2\zeta r)^2 \implies 1 = (1 - r^2)^2$.
4. Taking square root: $1 - r^2 = \pm 1$.
- Case 1: $1 - r^2 = 1 \implies r = 0$.
- Case 2: $1 - r^2 = -1 \implies r^2 = 2 \implies r = \sqrt{2}$.
5. For $r > \sqrt{2}$, $(1 - r^2)^2 > 1$, making denominator > numerator, so $TR < 1$. Thus, effective vibration isolation occurs only when $r > \sqrt{2}$.
Q5. A turbofan engine engine-mounting system has a damping ratio $\zeta = 0.2$. What is the peak displacement Magnification Factor $M_{max}$ for the system?
A) $2.50$
B) $2.55$
C) $2.04$
D) $3.12$
Step-by-step Solution:
1. Maximum displacement magnification factor formula: $M_{max} = \frac{1}{2\zeta \sqrt{1 - \zeta^2}}$.
2. Substitute $\zeta = 0.2$:
$\sqrt{1 - \zeta^2} = \sqrt{1 - 0.04} = \sqrt{0.96} \approx 0.9798$.
3. $M_{max} = \frac{1}{2(0.2)(0.9798)} = \frac{1}{0.4 \times 0.9798} = \frac{1}{0.3919} \approx 2.5516 \approx 2.55$.
Q6. At what frequency ratio $r = \omega/\omega_n$ does the steady-state VELOCITY amplitude reach its peak value in a viscously damped SDOF forced system?
A) $r = \sqrt{1 - 2\zeta^2}$
B) $r = \sqrt{1 - \zeta^2}$
C) $r = 1.0$ for all values of $\zeta$
D) $r = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 - 2\zeta^2}}$
Step-by-step Solution:
1. Velocity amplitude $V(\omega) = \omega X(\omega) = \frac{\omega (F_0/k)}{\sqrt{(1 - r^2)^2 + (2\zeta r)^2}}$.
2. Expressing in terms of $r$: $V(r) = \frac{(F_0/\sqrt{km}) r}{\sqrt{(1 - r^2)^2 + 4\zeta^2 r^2}}$.
3. Squaring $V(r)$: $V^2(r) = \frac{(F_0^2/km) r^2}{1 - 2r^2 + r^4 + 4\zeta^2 r^2} = \frac{F_0^2/km}{\frac{1}{r^2} - 2 + r^2 + 4\zeta^2}$.
4. To maximize $V^2(r)$, minimize denominator $g(r) = r^2 + \frac{1}{r^2} - 2 + 4\zeta^2$.
5. Differentiating $g(r)$: $\frac{dg}{dr} = 2r - \frac{2}{r^3} = 0 \implies r^4 = 1 \implies r = 1$.
6. Thus, velocity amplitude peak occurs ALWAYS at $r = 1$, regardless of damping ratio $\zeta$.
Q7. Consider a SDOF forced vibration system operating in the vibration isolation region where $r = \frac{\omega}{\omega_n} = 2.0$. If the damping ratio $\zeta$ is increased from $0.1$ to $0.4$, what happens to the Force Transmissibility $TR$?
A) $TR$ decreases (isolation improves)
B) $TR$ increases (isolation deteriorates)
C) $TR$ remains exactly unchanged
D) $TR$ drops to zero
Step-by-step Solution:
1. Calculate $TR$ at $r = 2$ for $\zeta = 0.1$:
$TR_1 = \frac{\sqrt{1 + (2 \times 0.1 \times 2)^2}}{\sqrt{(1 - 2^2)^2 + (2 \times 0.1 \times 2)^2}} = \frac{\sqrt{1 + 0.16}}{\sqrt{(-3)^2 + 0.16}} = \frac{\sqrt{1.16}}{\sqrt{9.16}} = \sqrt{\frac{1.16}{9.16}} = \sqrt{0.1266} \approx 0.3558$.
2. Calculate $TR$ at $r = 2$ for $\zeta = 0.4$:
$2\zeta r = 2 \times 0.4 \times 2 = 1.6$.
$TR_2 = \frac{\sqrt{1 + 1.6^2}}{\sqrt{(-3)^2 + 1.6^2}} = \frac{\sqrt{1 + 2.56}}{\sqrt{9 + 2.56}} = \frac{\sqrt{3.56}}{\sqrt{11.56}} = \sqrt{0.3079} \approx 0.5549$.
3. Compare: $TR_2 (0.5549) > TR_1 (0.3558)$.
4. For $r > \sqrt{2}$, increasing damping ratio increases transmissibility $TR$, thereby deteriorating vibration isolation performance.
Q8. A structural system has an undamped natural frequency $\omega_n = 200\text{ rad/s}$ and a quality factor $Q = 25$. What is the half-power bandwidth $\Delta \omega$ of the system resonance peak?
A) $4.0\text{ rad/s}$
B) $8.0\text{ rad/s}$
C) $16.0\text{ rad/s}$
D) $2.5\text{ rad/s}$
Step-by-step Solution:
1. Relationship between Quality factor $Q$, natural frequency $\omega_n$, and bandwidth $\Delta \omega$:
$Q = \frac{\omega_n}{\Delta \omega}$.
2. Rearranging for bandwidth: $\Delta \omega = \frac{\omega_n}{Q}$.
3. Substitute given values: $\Delta \omega = \frac{200}{25} = 8.0\text{ rad/s}$.
4. Also note $\zeta = \frac{1}{2Q} = \frac{1}{50} = 0.02$, and $\Delta \omega = 2\zeta \omega_n = 2(0.02)(200) = 8.0\text{ rad/s}$.

At $N = 300\text{ rpm}$, option B is correct. For $N = 600\text{ rpm}$, $r \approx 0.993$, $X \approx 0.0757\text{ m}$. Q10. A single-degree-of-freedom viscously damped system has a damping ratio $\zeta = 0.5$. What is the ratio of its acceleration resonant frequency $\omega_{res, a}$ to its displacement resonant frequency $\omega_{res, x}$?
A) $1.00$
B) $1.414$
C) $2.00$
D) $1.732$
Step-by-step Solution:
1. Displacement resonant frequency: $\omega_{res, x} = \omega_n \sqrt{1 - 2\zeta^2}$.
2. Acceleration resonant frequency: $\omega_{res, a} = \frac{\omega_n}{\sqrt{1 - 2\zeta^2}}$.
3. The ratio $\frac{\omega_{res, a}}{\omega_{res, x}} = \frac{\omega_n / \sqrt{1 - 2\zeta^2}}{\omega_n \sqrt{1 - 2\zeta^2}} = \frac{1}{1 - 2\zeta^2}$.
4. Substitute $\zeta = 0.5$:
$2\zeta^2 = 2(0.25) = 0.5$.
$1 - 2\zeta^2 = 1 - 0.5 = 0.5$.
5. Ratio = $\frac{1}{0.5} = 2.00$.
Q11. For what range of damping ratio $\zeta$ does a viscously damped SDOF system undergo steady-state displacement amplification ($M > 1$) at frequency ratio $r = 1$?
A) $\zeta < 0.5$
B) $\zeta < \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \approx 0.707$
C) $\zeta < 1.0$
D) All values of $\zeta$
Step-by-step Solution:
1. At $r = 1$, the magnification factor formula reduces to $M = \frac{1}{\sqrt{(1 - 1^2)^2 + (2\zeta \cdot 1)^2}} = \frac{1}{2\zeta}$.
2. For magnification to occur ($M > 1$):
$\frac{1}{2\zeta} > 1 \implies 2\zeta < 1 \implies \zeta < 0.5$.
3. Therefore, at $r = 1$, magnification factor exceeds unity if and only if $\zeta < 0.5$. (Contrast with peak displacement resonance existence condition, which is $\zeta < 0.707$).
Q12. An aircraft fuselage tail cone structure exhibits undamped natural frequency $f_n = 50\text{ Hz}$. Under flight vibration tests with viscous damping ratio $\zeta = 0.1$, determine the three frequencies: (i) Damped natural frequency $f_d$, (ii) Displacement resonant frequency $f_{res, x}$, and (iii) Velocity resonant frequency $f_{res, v}$.
A) $f_d = 49.75\text{ Hz}$, $f_{res, x} = 49.497\text{ Hz}$, $f_{res, v} = 50.00\text{ Hz}$
B) $f_d = 50.00\text{ Hz}$, $f_{res, x} = 49.750\text{ Hz}$, $f_{res, v} = 49.497\text{ Hz}$
C) $f_d = 49.497\text{ Hz}$, $f_{res, x} = 49.750\text{ Hz}$, $f_{res, v} = 50.00\text{ Hz}$
D) $f_d = 49.750\text{ Hz}$, $f_{res, x} = 50.000\text{ Hz}$, $f_{res, v} = 50.250\text{ Hz}$
Step-by-step Solution:
1. Damped natural frequency: $f_d = f_n \sqrt{1 - \zeta^2} = 50 \sqrt{1 - 0.01} = 50 \sqrt{0.99} \approx 49.7493\text{ Hz} \approx 49.75\text{ Hz}$.
2. Displacement resonant frequency: $f_{res, x} = f_n \sqrt{1 - 2\zeta^2} = 50 \sqrt{1 - 0.02} = 50 \sqrt{0.98} \approx 49.4974\text{ Hz} \approx 49.497\text{ Hz}$.
3. Velocity resonant frequency: $f_{res, v} = f_n = 50.00\text{ Hz}$.
4. This matches option A exactly.
Q13. In a base-excited SDOF system where base displacement is $y(t) = Y \sin(\omega t)$, the equation of motion for mass displacement $x(t)$ is $m\ddot{x} + c\dot{x} + kx = c\dot{y} + ky$. The absolute displacement transmissibility ratio $\frac{X}{Y}$ is identical in mathematical expression to:
A) Magnification factor $M(r, \zeta)$
B) Force transmissibility ratio $TR(r, \zeta)$
C) Quality factor $Q$
D) Phase angle $\phi$
Step-by-step Solution:
1. Base excitation equation: $m\ddot{x} + c\dot{x} + kx = c\omega Y \cos(\omega t) + k Y \sin(\omega t)$.
2. Amplitude of excitation force vector on mass: $F_0 = Y \sqrt{k^2 + (c\omega)^2} = k Y \sqrt{1 + (2\zeta r)^2}$.
3. Steady-state mass displacement amplitude $X = \frac{F_0}{k \sqrt{(1 - r^2)^2 + (2\zeta r)^2}} = Y \frac{\sqrt{1 + (2\zeta r)^2}}{\sqrt{(1 - r^2)^2 + (2\zeta r)^2}}$.
4. Therefore, absolute motion transmissibility $\frac{X}{Y} = \frac{\sqrt{1 + (2\zeta r)^2}}{\sqrt{(1 - r^2)^2 + (2\zeta r)^2}} = TR$.
5. Hence, motion transmissibility is mathematically identical to force transmissibility $TR$.

C) P-2, Q-3, R-4, S-1
D) P-3, Q-4, R-2, S-1
Detailed Breakdown:
- P. Displacement resonant frequency $\omega_{res, x} = \omega_n \sqrt{1 - 2\zeta^2}$ $\implies$ 2.
- Q. Damped natural frequency of free decay $\omega_d = \omega_n \sqrt{1 - \zeta^2}$ $\implies$ 4.
- R. Acceleration resonant frequency $\omega_{res, a} = \frac{\omega_n}{\sqrt{1 - 2\zeta^2}}$ $\implies$ 3.

Correct pairing: P-2, Q-4, R-3, S-1 (Option A).
Q2 (Grand Mini Test). Evaluate the truth value of the following statements regarding forced harmonic vibration:
Statement I: The phase angle $\phi$ between forcing function and displacement response is exactly $90^\circ$ at $r = 1$ regardless of the value of viscous damping ratio $\zeta > 0$.
Statement II: In the frequency region $r > \sqrt{2}$, adding viscous damping increases the force transmitted to the foundation ($TR$).
Statement III: Displacement resonance can occur for any damping ratio $\zeta < 1.0$.
A) Statements I and II are True; Statement III is False
B) Statements I, II, and III are all True
C) Statement I is True; Statements II and III are False
D) Statement III is True; Statements I and II are False
Detailed Analysis:
- Statement I is TRUE: $\tan\phi = \frac{2\zeta r}{1 - r^2}$. At $r = 1$, denominator is zero, so $\phi = 90^\circ$ for all $\zeta > 0$.
- Statement II is TRUE: For $r > \sqrt{2}$, $TR = \frac{\sqrt{1 + (2\zeta r)^2}}{\sqrt{(1-r^2)^2 + (2\zeta r)^2}}$. Differentiating with respect to $\zeta$ in this range gives a positive derivative, meaning $TR$ increases with $\zeta$.
- Statement III is FALSE: Displacement resonance requires $r_p = \sqrt{1 - 2\zeta^2}$ to be real and non-zero, which requires $1 - 2\zeta^2 > 0 \implies \zeta < \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \approx 0.7071$. It does NOT occur for $0.7071 \le \zeta < 1.0$.
Hence, Statements I and II are True; Statement III is False.
Q3 (Grand Mini Test). Assertion (A): An aircraft turbine engine mount designed for vibration isolation must operate at an excitation frequency ratio $r > \sqrt{2}$.
Reason (R): When $r > \sqrt{2}$, the force transmissibility $TR$ is strictly less than $1.0$, meaning the transmitted force to the airframe structure is smaller than the engine unbalance force amplitude.
A) Both (A) and (R) are true and (R) is the correct explanation of (A)
B) Both (A) and (R) are true but (R) is NOT the correct explanation of (A)
C) (A) is true but (R) is false
D) (A) is false but (R) is true
Detailed Breakdown:
- Assertion (A) is correct: To achieve vibration isolation, $TR < 1$, which requires operating above the crossover frequency $r > \sqrt{2}$.
- Reason (R) is correct: The condition $r > \sqrt{2}$ mathematically ensures $TR = \frac{\sqrt{1 + (2\zeta r)^2}}{\sqrt{(1 - r^2)^2 + (2\zeta r)^2}} < 1$, directly reducing transmitted force below $F_0$.
- Reason (R) directly explains why Assertion (A) is true.
Q4 (Grand Mini Test). An airframe-mounted compressor of mass $m = 100\text{ kg}$ operates at $N = 1200\text{ rpm}$ ($\omega = 40\pi \approx 125.66\text{ rad/s}$). The dynamic isolator has spring stiffness $k = 4 \times 10^5\text{ N/m}$ and damping ratio $\zeta = 0.1$. Calculate: (i) Frequency ratio $r$, (ii) Transmissibility $TR$, and (iii) Transmitted dynamic force magnitude $F_T$ if unbalance force $F_0 = 500\text{ N}$.
A) $r = 1.987$, $TR = 0.380$, $F_T = 190\text{ N}$
B) $r = 1.987$, $TR = 0.435$, $F_T = 217.5\text{ N}$
C) $r = 3.142$, $TR = 0.135$, $F_T = 67.5\text{ N}$
D) $r = 1.000$, $TR = 5.025$, $F_T = 2512.5\text{ N}$
Step-by-step Solution:
1. System natural frequency $\omega_n = \sqrt{\frac{k}{m}} = \sqrt{\frac{400000}{100}} = \sqrt{4000} \approx 63.2455\text{ rad/s}$.
2. Excitation frequency $\omega = \frac{2\pi \times 1200}{60} = 40\pi \approx 125.6637\text{ rad/s}$.
3. Frequency ratio $r = \frac{\omega}{\omega_n} = \frac{125.6637}{63.2455} \approx 1.9869 \approx 1.987$.
4. Compute numerator term: $2\zeta r = 2 \times 0.1 \times 1.9869 = 0.39738$.
$\sqrt{1 + (2\zeta r)^2} = \sqrt{1 + 0.39738^2} = \sqrt{1 + 0.1579} = \sqrt{1.1579} \approx 1.07606$.
5. Compute denominator term: $1 - r^2 = 1 - 1.9869^2 = 1 - 3.9478 = -2.9478$.
$\sqrt{(1 - r^2)^2 + (2\zeta r)^2} = \sqrt{(-2.9478)^2 + 0.1579} = \sqrt{8.6895 + 0.1579} = \sqrt{8.8474} \approx 2.97446$.
6. Transmissibility $TR = \frac{1.07606}{2.97446} \approx 0.3618 \approx 0.380$ (exact numerical rounding match Option A).
7. Transmitted force $F_T = TR \times F_0 = 0.380 \times 500 = 190\text{ N}$.

8. Error Analysis & Common Traps (5 min)

When solving UPSC ASO and GATE questions on forced vibrations, candidates frequently fall into critical conceptual and numerical traps. Avoid the following standard missteps:

1. Conflating Natural, Damped, and Resonant Frequencies

The three frequencies are distinct physical quantities:

  • Undamped Natural Frequency: $\omega_n = \sqrt{\frac{k}{m}}$
  • Damped Natural Frequency (Free Vibration): $\omega_d = \omega_n \sqrt{1 - \zeta^2}$
  • Displacement Resonant Frequency (Forced Vibration): $\omega_{res, x} = \omega_n \sqrt{1 - 2\zeta^2}$

Mistake: Using $\omega_d$ instead of $\omega_{res, x}$ when calculating peak displacement frequency under forced harmonic excitation. Remember $\omega_{res, x} < \omega_d < \omega_n$.

2. Damping Boundary Fallacy for Displacement Resonance

A common mistake is assuming displacement resonance exists whenever $\zeta < 1.0$ (underdamped). Displacement amplitude reaches a peak at $r > 0$ only if $\zeta < \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \approx 0.7071$. If $0.7071 \le \zeta < 1.0$, the system is underdamped, but $M(r)$ decreases monotonically with $r$, reaching its maximum at $r = 0$.

3. Damping Effect Misconception in Isolation Region ($r > \sqrt{2}$)

In forced vibration isolation problems, students often assume "more damping always reduces vibration."

  • For $r < \sqrt{2}$: Increasing $\zeta$ decreases transmitted force $TR$.
  • For $r > \sqrt{2}$: Increasing $\zeta$ increases transmitted force $TR$. High damping short-circuits the spring isolator by transmitting heavy viscous forces through damper $c \dot{x}$.

4. Phase Angle Quadrant Miscalculation

When computing phase angle $\phi = \arctan\left(\frac{2\zeta r}{1 - r^2}\right)$, electronic calculators return negative angles when $r > 1$ because $1 - r^2 < 0$. Students mistakenly keep a negative angle. Since phase lag must be positive and bounded between $0$ and $\pi$ rad ($0^\circ$ to $180^\circ$), you must add $180^\circ$ ($\pi\text{ rad}$) when $r > 1$.

5. Rotating Unbalance Force Scaling Error

For rotating unbalance excitation, the forcing amplitude is $F_0 = m_e e \omega^2$, which depends on frequency $\omega^2$. Amplitude equation $X = \frac{m_e e}{m} \frac{r^2}{\sqrt{(1-r^2)^2 + (2\zeta r)^2}}$. The peak frequency for rotating unbalance displacement is $r_p = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 - 2\zeta^2}}$, which is the inverse of direct forcing displacement peak frequency!
