Surface tension & capillarity

Textbook-grade UPSC Air Safety Officer deep-dive: Comprehensive theoretical foundations, analytical derivations, engineering reference data, DGCA/ICAO airworthiness integration, 14 practice MCQs, and timed mini-test.

How This Topic Is Useful for Air Safety Officers & Aircraft Operations
DGCA CAR & ICAO Mandate

In aviation operations and UPSC ASO assessments, this parameter is critical across 4 core operational pillars:

  • 1. Aircraft Systems & Flight Deck Monitoring: Fuel quantity gauging systems and static port pressure sensing rely on accurate surface tension and capillary meniscus correction to prevent altimeter and fuel reserve computation errors.
  • 2. Flight Safety & Dispatch Envelopes: Incorrect fuel density and surface tension assumptions during refueling can cause fuel sloshing anomalies and incorrect center-of-gravity calculations.
  • 3. DGCA CAR & ICAO Airworthiness Compliance: DGCA CAR Section 2 and ICAO Annex 8 mandate rigorous fuel system and hydraulic fluid contamination checks where interfacial tension is a key indicator.
  • 4. Air Safety Investigation & Incident Prevention: During engine flameout investigations, ASO examiners audit fuel nozzle atomization quality and filter clogging driven by fuel surface tension variations.

Pillar 1: Formal Technical Definition & Nomenclature

Surface tension is the tensile force acting per unit length along the surface of a liquid, arising from unbalanced molecular cohesive forces, governed by $\sigma = \frac{F}{L}$. Capillarity is the phenomenon of liquid rising or falling in a narrow conduit due to the combined effects of surface tension and contact angle.
  • Physical Meaning: Molecules in the bulk liquid experience symmetric intermolecular attractive forces, whereas surface molecules experience a net inward cohesive pull.
  • Governing Law: Young-Laplace equation governs the pressure difference across a curved liquid interface: $\Delta p = \frac{2\sigma}{R}$.
  • Continuum Boundary: Valid down to micro-channels where molecular mean free path remains negligible compared to characteristic flow dimensions.

Nomenclature & Symbols

  • $\sigma$ = Surface tension coefficient ($N/m$ or $J/m^2$)
  • $\theta$ = Contact angle between liquid-solid interface (Degrees or Radians)
  • $r$ = Radius of capillary tube or droplet curvature ($m$)
  • $\Delta p$ = Pressure difference across interface ($Pa$)
  • $\rho$ = Density of liquid ($kg/m^3$)
  • $g$ = Acceleration due to gravity ($m/s^2$)
  • $h$ = Capillary rise or depression height ($m$)

Pillar 2: Microscopic Physics & Continuum Mechanics

1. Molecular Kinetic State: Liquid molecules experience continuous thermal collisions and short-range intermolecular cohesive forces pulling them equally in all directions within the bulk.
2. Continuum Forces & Gradients: Surface molecules lack neighboring molecules above them, resulting in a net inward force vector that minimizes total surface area.
3. Aerodynamic Consequence: Surface tension dictates fuel droplet atomization in gas turbine combustors, meniscus formation in static pressure ports, and liquid film rupture limits.

Pillar 3: Mathematical Derivations & Governing Laws

Step 1: Governing Relation

Equilibrate the vertical upward surface tension force with the downward weight of the liquid column in a capillary tube.

$$2\pi r \sigma \cos(\theta) = \pi r^2 h \rho g$$
Step 2: Boundary / Thermodynamic Conditions

Substitute the tube radius $r$ in terms of diameter $d$ and isolate the capillary height $h$ as the dependent variable.

$$h = \frac{4\sigma \cos(\theta)}{\rho g d}$$
Step 3: Integration & Substitution

For pure water in a clean glass tube, contact angle $\theta \approx 0^\circ$, simplifying the cosine term to unity.

$$h_{\text{water}} = \frac{4\sigma}{\rho g d}$$
Step 4: Final Working Law

General working equation for capillary rise or depression accounting for arbitrary contact angles.

$$h = \frac{2\sigma \cos(\theta)}{\rho g r}$$

Pillar 4: Dimensional Analysis, SI/CGS Units & Conversion Factors

  • Fundamental Dimensions: $[M^1 L^0 T^{-2}]$ derived from force per unit length $[MLT^{-2}] / [L]$.
  • SI Unit: Newton per meter ($N/m$) or Joules per square meter ($J/m^2$).
  • CGS Unit: Dyne per centimeter ($dyn/cm$), where $1\text{ dyn/cm} = 10^{-3}\text{ N/m}$.
  • Aeronautical & Imperial Equivalents: Pound-force per inch ($lbf/in$), where $1\text{ lbf/in} = 175.126\text{ N/m}$.

  • Regulatory Standards (DGCA CAR & ICAO): DGCA CAR Section 2, Series 'F' and ICAO Annex 8 mandate strict inspection of fuel tanks, vent systems, and hydraulic fluid properties.

Pillar 7: High-Frequency UPSC Examiner Traps & Misconceptions

Trap 1: Contact Angle Sign Convention

❌ Examiner Trap: Assuming wetting liquids have a contact angle greater than 90 degrees, causing incorrect capillary depression calculations.

✅ UPSC Truth: Wetting liquids (like water on clean glass) have $\theta < 90^\circ$ resulting in capillary rise, while non-wetting liquids (like mercury) have $\theta > 90^\circ$ resulting in capillary depression.

Trap 2: Tube Diameter vs Radius Confusion

❌ Examiner Trap: Using the tube diameter $d$ directly in the Young-Laplace capillary formula without converting it to radius $r = d/2$.

✅ UPSC Truth: The standard radius formula is $h = \frac{2\sigma \cos(\theta)}{\rho g r}$. If diameter is used, the numerator becomes $4\sigma \cos(\theta)$.

Trap 3: Temperature Independence Fallacy

❌ Examiner Trap: Treating surface tension as a constant regardless of operating altitude and ambient temperature changes.

✅ UPSC Truth: Surface tension decreases linearly as temperature increases and drops to zero at the critical temperature point.

Trap 4: Dimension and Unit Mismatch

❌ Examiner Trap: Mixing CGS units ($dyn/cm$) with SI units ($kg/m^3$, $m/s^2$) during numerical evaluation.

✅ UPSC Truth: Always convert $dyn/cm$ to $N/m$ by multiplying by $10^{-3}$ before computing pressures or heights.

Pillar 8: Step-by-Step Worked Aircraft Numerical Problem

Realistic Aircraft Scenario

A clean glass capillary tube of internal diameter $1.5\text{ mm}$ is inserted vertically into a container of Jet A-1 kerosene at $15^\circ C$. Calculate the capillary height depression or rise given that kerosene has a surface tension of $0.0276\text{ N/m}$, a density of $804\text{ kg/m}^3$, and a contact angle of $26^\circ$.

Given Flight Parameters:
Diameter $d = 1.5\text{ mm} = 1.5 \times 10^{-3}\text{ m}$; Radius $r = 0.75 \times 10^{-3}\text{ m}$; $\sigma = 0.0276\text{ N/m}$; $\rho = 804\text{ kg/m}^3$; $\theta = 26^\circ$; $g = 9.81\text{ m/s}^2$.
Governing Formula:
$$h = \frac{2\sigma \cos(\theta)}{\rho g r}$$
Step-by-Step Analytical Solution:
Step 1: Compute the cosine of the contact angle: $\cos(26^\circ) = 0.8987$. Step 2: Substitute the given parameters into the capillary formula: $h = \frac{2 \times 0.0276 \times 0.8987}{804 \times 9.81 \times 0.75 \times 10^{-3}}$. Step 3: Evaluate the numerator ($0.04961$) and denominator ($5.9189$) to yield $h = 0.00838\text{ m}$.
Final Answer: Capillary rise $h = 8.38\text{ mm}$
Air Safety Officer Operational Takeaway:

Understanding micro-channel capillary rise ensures fuel filters and micro-vent lines do not trap air locks or cause pressure differentials that starve gas turbine combustors during high-altitude climbs.

Question 1 • conceptual
What is the primary physical cause of surface tension in liquids?
A Unbalanced intermolecular cohesive forces acting on surface molecules
B Gravitational attraction between liquid and container walls
C External atmospheric pressure pressing down on the liquid surface
D Viscous shear stress generated by thermal molecular motion
Solution & Technical Rationale:
Surface molecules experience net inward cohesive forces because they lack neighboring molecules above them, creating a tensile skin effect.
Question 2 • statement
Consider the following statements regarding capillarity: 1. Capillary rise is inversely proportional to the radius of the tube. 2. Non-wetting liquids experience capillary depression. Which of the statements given above is/are correct?
A 1 only
B 2 only
C Both 1 and 2
D Neither 1 nor 2
Solution & Technical Rationale:
Both statements are correct. Capillary height $h = \frac{2\sigma \cos(\theta)}{\rho g r}$ shows inverse proportionality to $r$, and non-wetting liquids ($\theta > 90^\circ$) give negative $h$ (depression).
Question 3 • numerical
Calculate the excess pressure inside a spherical water droplet of radius $2\text{ mm}$ given surface tension $\sigma = 0.073\text{ N/m}$.
A 36.5 Pa
B 73.0 Pa
C 146.0 Pa
D 292.0 Pa
Solution & Technical Rationale:
For a spherical droplet, excess pressure $\Delta p = \frac{2\sigma}{r} = \frac{2 \times 0.073}{2 \times 10^{-3}} = 73.0\text{ Pa}.
Question 4 • assertion-reasoning
Assertion (A): Surface tension of water decreases with an increase in temperature. Reason (R): Increased thermal energy weakens intermolecular cohesive forces.
A Both A and R are true and R is the correct explanation of A
B Both A and R are true but R is not the correct explanation of A
C A is true but R is false
D A is false but R is true
Solution & Technical Rationale:
Higher thermal agitation increases molecular spacing, directly reducing cohesive attraction and thus lowering surface tension.
Question 5 • conceptual
What happens to the capillary rise of water in a glass tube if the tube radius is halved?
A It is halved
B It remains unchanged
C It is doubled
D It quadruples
Solution & Technical Rationale:
Since capillary rise $h$ is inversely proportional to radius $r$, halving $r$ doubles the capillary rise height.
Question 6 • statement
Which of the following units are dimensionally equivalent to surface tension? 1. $N/m$ 2. $J/m^2$ 3. $kg/s^2$
A 1 and 2 only
B 2 and 3 only
C 1 and 3 only
D 1, 2 and 3
Solution & Technical Rationale:
All three are valid: $N/m$ (Force/Length), $J/m^2$ (Energy/Area), and $kg/s^2$ (from dimensions $[MT^{-2}]$).
Question 7 • numerical
Find the excess pressure inside a cylindrical soap bubble of radius $3\text{ mm}$ with surface tension $0.025\text{ N/m}$. (Note: soap bubble has two free surfaces).
A 16.67 Pa
B 33.33 Pa
C 50.00 Pa
D 100.00 Pa
Solution & Technical Rationale:
A soap bubble has two surfaces, so excess pressure $\Delta p = \frac{4\sigma}{r} = \frac{4 \times 0.025}{3 \times 10^{-3}} = 33.33\text{ Pa}.
Question 8 • conceptual
Why does mercury form an inverted meniscus (capillary depression) in a glass tube?
A Cohesion between mercury molecules is weaker than adhesion to glass
B Adhesion between mercury and glass is weaker than cohesion between mercury molecules
C Atmospheric pressure is zero inside the capillary tube
D Mercury has infinite density compared to water
Solution & Technical Rationale:
Mercury is non-wetting because cohesive forces exceed adhesive forces with glass, yielding a contact angle greater than 90 degrees.
Question 9 • statement
Consider statement A: Surface tension acts tangential to the liquid surface. Consider statement B: Capillarity is negligible in large diameter pipes ($d > 50\text{ mm}$).
A Both statements are correct
B Both statements are incorrect
C Statement A is correct, B is incorrect
D Statement A is incorrect, B is correct
Solution & Technical Rationale:
Surface tension forces act tangentially along the interface. Capillarity varies inversely with diameter, making it negligible in large pipes.
Question 10 • assertion-reasoning
Assertion (A): Raindrops acquire a nearly spherical shape. Reason (R): Surface tension minimizes the surface area of a liquid volume for a given mass.
A Both A and R are true and R is the correct explanation of A
B Both A and R are true but R is not the correct explanation of A
C A is true but R is false
D A is false but R is true
Solution & Technical Rationale:
Minimum surface area configuration for a given volume is a sphere, which is enforced by inward surface tension forces.
Question 11 • numerical
A capillary tube of radius $0.5\text{ mm}$ is dipped in water ($\sigma = 0.0728\text{ N/m}$, $\theta = 0^\circ$). What is the capillary rise?
A 14.86 mm
B 29.72 mm
C 59.44 mm
D 74.30 mm
Solution & Technical Rationale:
$h = \frac{2\sigma}{\rho g r} = \frac{2 \times 0.0728}{998 \times 9.81 \times 0.5 \times 10^{-3}} = 0.02972\text{ m} = 29.72\text{ mm}.
Question 12 • conceptual
Which aviation fluid possesses the lowest surface tension among standard hydrocarbons?
A Water
B Skydrol LD-4
C Avgas 100LL
D SAE 50 Engine Oil
Solution & Technical Rationale:
Avgas 100LL has a surface tension around $0.0185\text{ N/m}$, significantly lower than water or heavy hydraulic oils.

Question 2 of 10 +3 / -1
Excess pressure inside a liquid droplet is given by:
A $\frac{\sigma}{r}$
B $\frac{2\sigma}{r}$
C $\frac{4\sigma}{r}$
D $\frac{8\sigma}{r}$
Rationale:
A liquid droplet has only one free surface, so excess pressure is $\Delta p = \frac{2\sigma}{r}.
Question 3 of 10 +3 / -1
Capillary depression occurs when the contact angle is:
A Zero
B Exactly 90 degrees
C Less than 90 degrees
D Greater than 90 degrees
Rationale:
When $\theta > 90^\circ$ (non-wetting), $\cos(\theta)$ is negative, resulting in capillary depression ($h < 0$).
Question 4 of 10 +3 / -1
If surface tension is expressed in $J/m^2$, what does it physically represent?
A Kinetic energy per unit volume
B Potential energy stored per unit surface area
C Viscous dissipation rate
D Pressure work done per unit mass
Rationale:
$J/m^2$ represents work done or potential energy stored per unit increase in surface area.
Question 5 of 10 +3 / -1
What is the excess pressure inside an air bubble formed inside a liquid mass?
A $\frac{\sigma}{r}$
B $\frac{2\sigma}{r}$
C $\frac{4\sigma}{r}$
D $\frac{3\sigma}{r}$
Rationale:
An interior bubble in a liquid mass has only one liquid-gas interface, hence $\Delta p = \frac{2\sigma}{r}.
Question 6 of 10 +3 / -1
In aviation fuel systems, high surface tension coupled with fine micro-filtration can lead to:
A Accelerated fuel vaporization
B Water coalescence and filter restriction if coalescers fail
C Immediate turbine overspeed
D Infinite hydraulic pressure surges
Rationale:
Surface tension properties dictate water droplet coalescence in fuel filter-water separators.
Question 7 of 10 +3 / -1
The capillary height $h$ is independent of which of the following parameters?
A Acceleration due to gravity ($g$)
B Density of liquid ($\rho$)
C Atmospheric pressure acting on the free surface
D Contact angle ($\theta$)
Rationale:
Capillary rise depends on surface tension, tube radius, density, contact angle, and gravity, but is independent of ambient barometric pressure acting equally on both liquid surfaces.
Question 8 of 10 +3 / -1
A soap bubble of radius $r$ has a surface tension $\sigma$. The total surface energy of the bubble is:
A $4\pi r^2 \sigma$
B $8\pi r^2 \sigma$
C $2\pi r^2 \sigma$
D $16\pi r^2 \sigma$
Rationale:
A soap bubble has two surfaces (inner and outer), total area = $2 \times 4\pi r^2 = 8\pi r^2$. Total energy = Area $\times \sigma = 8\pi r^2 \sigma$.
Question 9 of 10 +3 / -1
How does an increase in water temperature affect its capillary rise in a glass tube?
A Capillary rise increases
B Capillary rise decreases
C Capillary rise remains unchanged
D Capillary rise drops to zero instantly
Rationale:
Surface tension $\sigma$ decreases with rising temperature, which directly reduces capillary height $h$.

Which DGCA regulatory document governs aircraft fuel system airworthiness and contamination standards?
A DGCA CAR Section 2, Series 'F'

DGCA CAR Section 2 covers airworthiness and type certification standards including fuel systems.
