Speed of sound, Mach number

Fluid Mechanics Machinery Compressible Flow & Compressors Phase 1: Foundations 90-120 min Master Class

1. Understand (From Absolute Zero, 5–10 min)

Welcome, future Air Safety Officers and Aerospace Engineers. On Fluid Mechanics Machinery, we dive into the fundamental pillars of compressible gas dynamics: the Speed of Sound and the Mach Number. Imagine whispering across a quiet room versus shouting near a roaring jet engine. Sound waves are nothing more than infinitesimally small pressure waves (compression and expansion pulses) traveling through a medium. The speed at which these pressure disturbances propagate through a fluid is known as the speed of sound ($a$).

Now, what happens when an aircraft moves through the air? If the aircraft travels slower than the sound waves it emits, the air ahead of it is "warned" of its approach, allowing the streamlines to smoothly part. This is the realm of Subsonic Aerodynamics ($M < 1$). However, when the aircraft travels faster than the sound waves it creates—meaning it outruns its own acoustic footprint—the pressure disturbances pile up, coalesce, and form a violent, abrupt discontinuity known as a Shock Wave. This is the realm of Supersonic Aerodynamics ($M > 1$).

The Mach number ($M$), named after the Austrian physicist and philosopher Ernst Mach, is the dimensionless ratio of the local flow velocity ($V$) to the local speed of sound ($a$):

$$ M = Va $$

In standard UPSC terminology and aerospace engineering practice, Mach number dictates the entire mathematical classification of fluid flow regimes:

  • Incompressible Flow: $M < 0.3$ (Density changes are negligible, $Δ ρρ < 5%$).
  • Subsonic Flow: $0.3 ≤ M < 1.0$ (Compressibility effects matter; subsonic locally everywhere).
  • Transonic Flow: $0.8 ≤ M ≤ 1.2$ (Mixed flow regions; both subsonic and supersonic patches coexist on the aerodynamic body).
  • Supersonic Flow: $1.0 < M ≤ 5.0$ (Entire flow field is supersonic; governed by oblique and normal shock waves).
  • Hypersonic Flow: $M > 5.0$ (High-temperature gas dynamics, chemical dissociation, viscous interaction, and blunt shock layers dominate).

2. Learn the Concept (Complete Theoretical Foundation & Derivations, 20–25 min)

Let us build the complete theoretical foundation from absolute scratch. We begin by deriving the speed of sound from first principles of fluid mechanics and thermodynamics, moving through isentropic relations, normal/oblique shock jump equations (Rankine-Hugoniot), and concluding with internal duct flows governed by friction (Fanno flow) and heat transfer (Rayleigh flow).

1. Derivation of the Speed of Sound ($a$)

Consider a 1D compressible fluid flowing through a constant-area duct, or equivalently, an acoustic wave propagating through a stationary fluid. Let us analyze a moving normal compression wave (sound wave) of infinitesimal strength. By shifting to a reference frame moving with the wave, the wave is stationary, and the fluid enters the wave at velocity $V = a$ and exits at $a - du$.

Applying the 1D continuity equation: $$ ρ A = (ρ + dρ)(A + dA) \implies ρ a = (ρ + dρ)(a - du) $$ Expanding and neglecting second-order infinitesimally small terms ($dρ · du$): $$ ρ a = ρ a - ρ du + a dρ \implies ρ du = a dρ \implies du = aρ dρ $$

Next, applying the 1D momentum equation (neglecting friction): $$ pA - (p + dp)A = (ρ A)((a - du) - a) \implies -dp = ρ a (-du) \implies dp = ρ a du $$ Substitute $du = aρ dρ$ into the momentum equation: $$ dp = ρ a (aρ dρ) = a^2 dρ \implies a^2 = (dpdρ)process $$

Sound wave propagation occurs so rapidly that heat transfer to the surroundings is negligible, and due to the infinitesimal pressure jump, the acoustic wave process is reversible. Hence, sound wave propagation is strictly an isentropic process (adiabatic and reversible). Therefore, the derivative must be taken at constant entropy ($s$): $$ a = √(∂ p∂ ρ)_s $$

For an ideal gas, the isentropic relation is $p ρ-γ = constant$ (where $γ = C_pC_v$ is the ratio of specific heats, equal to $1.4$ for air). Differentiating this yields: $$ (∂ p∂ ρ)_s = γ pρ $$ Using the ideal gas law $p = ρ R T$ (where $R$ is the specific gas constant): $$ a = √γ R T $$ This is the foundational equation for the speed of sound in an ideal gas. It proves that the speed of sound depends only on the thermodynamic temperature ($T$) of the gas and its specific heat ratio ($γ$). It is entirely independent of pressure or density changes at constant temperature!

2. Isentropic Stagnation Relations

When a flowing gas is brought to a complete rest isentropically, its kinetic energy is converted entirely into enthalpy, raising its temperature and pressure to stagnation (total) values ($T_0, p_0, ρ_0$). Using the energy equation and the definition of Mach number, we derive the critical isentropic temperature, pressure, and density ratios:

$$ T_0T = 1 + γ - 12 M^2 $$ $$ p_0p = (1 + γ - 12 M^2)γγ - 1 $$ $$ ρ_0ρ = (1 + γ - 12 M^2)1γ - 1 $$

When the flow reaches sonic velocity ($M = 1$), it achieves critical conditions denoted by an asterisk ($^*$): $$ T^*T_0 = 2γ + 1, \quad p^*p_0 = (2γ + 1)γγ - 1, \quad ρ^*ρ_0 = (2γ + 1)1γ - 1 $$ For air ($γ = 1.4$), $T^*T_0 = 22.4 = 0.8333$ and $p^*p_0 = (0.8333)3.5 = 0.5283$.

3. Rankine-Hugoniot Normal Shock Relations

A normal shock wave is an extremely thin (on the order of micrometers) irreversible compression wave perpendicular to the flow. Across a normal shock, total temperature remains constant ($T01 = T02$), but total pressure drops severely ($p02 < p01$), and entropy increases ($Δ s > 0$).

The fundamental normal shock relations linking downstream Mach number ($M_2$) to upstream Mach number ($M_1$) are given by:

$$ M_2^2 = 2 + (γ - 1)M_1^22γ M_1^2 - (γ - 1) $$

The Rankine-Hugoniot pressure jump equation relating static pressures across the shock is: $$ p_2p_1 = 1 + 2γγ + 1(M_1^2 - 1) = 2γ M_1^2 - (γ - 1)γ + 1 $$

The density and velocity jump relations across a normal shock are inversely related: $$ ρ_2ρ_1 = V_1V_2 = (γ + 1)M_1^22 + (γ - 1)M_1^2 $$ Note that for any valid shock ($M_1 > 1$), the downstream Mach number is always subsonic ($M_2 < 1$), static pressure increases ($p_2 > p_1$), density increases ($ρ_2 > ρ_1$), and velocity decreases ($V_2 < V_1$). The maximum possible density ratio across a normal shock in air ($γ = 1.4$) as $M_1 \to ∞$ is $γ + 1γ - 1 = 2.40.4 = 6$.

4. Fanno Flow and Rayleigh Flow Choking Lines

Fanno Flow: Flow in a constant-area duct with friction and adiabatic conditions. Friction increases entropy, driving the Mach number toward $M = 1$ (choked flow) regardless of whether the initial flow is subsonic or supersonic. The locus of all states satisfying mass and momentum with friction is the Fanno line, which reaches maximum entropy at $M = 1$.

Rayleigh Flow: Frictionless flow in a constant-area duct with heat transfer. Heat addition drives the Mach number toward $M = 1$ (thermal choking), while heat rejection drives subsonic flow away from $M=1$ (toward lower Mach numbers). The maximum entropy point on the Rayleigh curve also occurs exactly at $M = 1$ for subsonic heat addition, but interestingly, maximum temperature occurs at $M = 1√γ$ (which is $M = 0.845$ for air).

3. Memorize Important Facts (10 min)

  • Core Formula 1 (Speed of Sound): $a = √γ R T$ (Depends exclusively on temperature $T$ for ideal gases).
  • Core Formula 2 (Mach Number): $M = Va$ (Ratio of local flow speed to local speed of sound).
  • Isentropic Stagnation Temperature Ratio: $T_0T = 1 + γ - 12 M^2$.
  • Isentropic Stagnation Pressure Ratio: $p_0p = (1 + γ - 12 M^2)γγ - 1$.
  • Critical Sonic Pressure Ratio (Air, $γ=1.4$): $p^*p_0 = 0.5283$.
  • Critical Sonic Temperature Ratio (Air, $γ=1.4$): $T^*T_0 = 0.8333$.
  • Normal Shock Downstream Mach Number: $M_2^2 = 2 + (γ - 1)M_1^22γ M_1^2 - (γ - 1)$.
  • Normal Shock Static Pressure Jump: $p_2p_1 = 2γ M_1^2 - (γ - 1)γ + 1$.
  • Maximum Normal Shock Density Ratio (Air, $M_1 \to ∞$): $ρ_2ρ_1 = γ + 1γ - 1 = 6$.
  • Fanno & Rayleigh Choking Condition: Flow chokes when local Mach number reaches $M = 1$ (Maximum Entropy point).

4. Aviation Application (5–10 min)

Understanding the speed of sound and Mach number is critical in aircraft design, supersonic flight, and propulsion systems:

  • Supersonic Inlets & Diffusers (Air-Breathing Engines): In supersonic aircraft like fighter jets or high-speed interceptors, incoming air at $M > 2$ must be slowed down to subsonic speeds ($M < 0.3$) before entering the turbojet/turbofan combustion chamber. This is achieved using a system of oblique shocks followed by a normal shock in a converging-diverging supersonic diffuser (e.g., inlet centerbodies or variable-ramp intakes).
  • Sonic Booms & Shock Cones: When an aircraft exceeds Mach 1, pressure disturbances cannot propagate ahead of the aircraft. Instead, they accumulate along an acoustic Mach wave cone whose semi-vertex angle ($μ$) is given by $μ = \arcsin(1M)$. The sudden arrival of this intense pressure wave at the ground produces a loud double-bang "sonic boom."
  • Rayleigh Flow in Ramjets / Scramjets: Combustion inside a ramjet or scramjet engine is modeled as Rayleigh flow (heat addition in a constant-area duct). If heat addition is excessive, thermal choking ($M=1$) occurs inside the combustor, leading to inlet unstart and catastrophic thrust loss.
  • Fanno Flow in Tailpipes & Fuel Lines: High-speed exhaust gases traveling through long convergent-divergent nozzles or tailpipes experience internal viscous boundary-layer friction, modeled by Fanno flow, which limits the maximum mass flow rate through the engine.

5. UPSC ASO Focus (10 min)

  • Frequently Tested UPSC ASO / GATE Facts:
    • $γ$ for air is strictly taken as $1.4$ unless specified otherwise ($R = 287 J/kg·K$).
    • Total temperature $T_0$ remains constant across a normal shock wave ($T01 = T02$), whereas total pressure $p_0$ always drops ($p02 < p01$).
    • The maximum density ratio across a normal shock in air is $6$, regardless of how high the upstream Mach number goes.
  • Conceptual Traps:
    • Trap 1: Assuming speed of sound depends on pressure. Remember $a = √γ R T$ depends only on temperature $T$.
    • Trap 2: Confusing stagnation temperature with static temperature across shocks. Energy conservation dictates $T_0$ is constant across a stationary shock, but static temperature $T$ jumps significantly.
    • Trap 3: Believing that heat addition in subsonic Rayleigh flow increases Mach number. In reality, heat addition in subsonic Rayleigh flow drives the Mach number upward toward $M=1$, while heat addition in supersonic Rayleigh flow drives the Mach number downward toward $M=1$.

8. Error Analysis & Common Traps (5 min)

Students preparing for the UPSC ASO and advanced aerospace exams frequently commit several recurring calculation and conceptual errors:

  1. Confusing Static and Stagnation Quantities Across Shocks: Many candidates mistakenly assume that static temperature $T$ remains constant across a normal shock wave. Energy conservation dictates that stagnation temperature ($T01 = T02$) is constant, but static temperature jumps dramatically.
  2. Misapplying Speed of Sound Formulas: Trying to use $a = √γ R T$ with gauge pressure instead of absolute temperature, or forgetting to convert Celsius to Kelvin ($T(K) = T(^° C) + 273.15$).
  3. Assuming Supersonic Flow Accelerates in Converging Ducts: Forgetting the area-velocity relation $dAA = (M^2 - 1)dVV$. In supersonic flow ($M > 1$), converging areas cause deceleration, not acceleration.
  4. Fanno and Rayleigh Choking Misunderstandings: Assuming choking in Fanno flow can exceed $M=1$. Friction and heat addition drive flows strictly toward $M=1$, beyond which supersonic branches are physically disconnected unless a separate nozzle geometry is employed.
