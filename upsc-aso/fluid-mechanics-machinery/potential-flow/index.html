Subtopic 1 of 7 ID: FM-POT-01

Velocity Potential & Stream Function

1. Formal Technical Definition

A velocity potential ($\phi$) is a scalar function whose spatial derivative yields the velocity components in an irrotational flow field ($\vec{V} = \nabla \phi$). A stream function ($\psi$) is a scalar function in two-dimensional flow whose lines of constant value represent streamlines, satisfying continuity identically for both compressible and incompressible planar flows.

2. Basic Concept & Physical Mechanism

At the continuum level, fluid elements in a potential flow undergo translation and deformation but zero net angular velocity (rotation vector $\vec{\omega} = \frac{1}{2} \nabla \times \vec{V} = 0$). The velocity potential exists if and only if the flow is irrotational (Kelvin's Circulation Theorem). The stream function exists due to the mass conservation principle (2D continuity equation), eliminating one spatial dimension by guaranteeing that mass flux between any two streamlines is constant.

3. Mathematical Derivation & Equations

For 2D Cartesian incompressible flow, velocity components $u$ (x-direction) and $v$ (y-direction) are defined via velocity potential $\phi(x,y)$:

$$u = \frac{\partial \phi}{\partial x}, \quad v = \frac{\partial \phi}{\partial y}$$

Irrotationality condition ($\zeta_z = \frac{\partial v}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial u}{\partial y} = 0$) substituted with $\phi$ yields Laplace's equation:

$$\frac{\partial^2 \phi}{\partial x^2} + \frac{\partial^2 \phi}{\partial y^2} = 0 \implies \nabla^2 \phi = 0$$

The 2D continuity equation for incompressible flow ($\frac{\partial u}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial v}{\partial y} = 0$) is identically satisfied by defining stream function $\psi(x,y)$ such that:

$$u = \frac{\partial \psi}{\partial y}, \quad v = -\frac{\partial \psi}{\partial x}$$

Cauchy-Riemann relations link $\phi$ and $\psi$ in orthogonal potential flow:

$$\frac{\partial \phi}{\partial x} = \frac{\partial \psi}{\partial y}, \quad \frac{\partial \phi}{\partial y} = -\frac{\partial \psi}{\partial x}$$

Fundamental Dimensions

Velocity Potential $[\phi] = [L^2 T^{-1}]$

Stream Function $[\psi] = [L^2 T^{-1}]$ (Volume flow rate per unit span)

SI & CGS Units

SI: $\text{m}^2/\text{s}$

CGS: $\text{cm}^2/\text{s}$

Conversion: $1 \text{ m}^2/\text{s} = 10^4 \text{ cm}^2/\text{s}$

6. Aircraft Application & Air Safety Officer Connection

Potential flow calculations are utilized in preliminary aircraft design (panel methods) to determine pressure distributions over supercritical wings. Under DGCA CAR Section 2 (Airworthiness), structural integrity assessments during certification must verify that localized flow acceleration does not induce premature boundary layer separation leading to stall. In accident investigation, CFD potential flow models help reconstruct wake turbulence trajectories and pitot-static probe calibration errors caused by local flow angularity.

7. UPSC Traps & Common Exam Pitfalls

Trap: UPSC examiners frequently state that velocity potential $\phi$ exists for *any* fluid flow.
Correction: Velocity potential exists only if the flow is irrotational ($\nabla \times \vec{V} = 0$). Similarly, stream function $\psi$ exists for 2D flows regardless of rotation, but the Laplace equation ($\nabla^2 \psi = 0$) holds only for irrotational incompressible flows.

8. Step-by-Step Worked Numerical

Problem: A uniform freestream flow of airspeed $U_\infty = 50 \text{ m/s}$ flows parallel to the x-axis. Find the velocity potential $\phi(x,y)$, stream function $\psi(x,y)$, and calculate the velocity vector at coordinate $(2\text{ m}, 3\text{ m})$.

Given: $U = 50 \text{ m/s}, V = 0, \text{Point } (x,y) = (2, 3)$

Step 1: Integrate $u = \frac{\partial \phi}{\partial x} = U_\infty \implies \phi = U_\infty x + C_1(y)$

Step 2: Differentiate w.r.t $y$: $\frac{\partial \phi}{\partial y} = \frac{d C_1}{d y} = V = 0 \implies \phi(x,y) = 50x$

Step 3: Stream function: $u = \frac{\partial \psi}{\partial y} = 50 \implies \psi = 50y + C_2(x)$, and $-\frac{\partial \psi}{\partial x} = v = 0 \implies \psi(x,y) = 50y$

Step 4: Velocity at $(2,3)$: $\vec{V} = u\hat{i} + v\hat{j} = 50\hat{i} + 0\hat{j} \text{ m/s}$

Aviation Operational Takeaway: Uniform flow represents undisturbed cruise flight far upstream of an aircraft wing, providing the baseline reference dynamic pressure ($q_\infty = \frac{1}{2}\rho U_\infty^2$).

Subtopic 2 of 7 ID: FM-POT-02

Equipotential Lines & Orthogonality

1. Formal Technical Definition

An equipotential line is a curve along which the velocity potential $\phi(x,y) = \text{constant}$. In a 2D potential flow field, equipotential lines and streamlines form an orthogonal grid (flow net) intersecting everywhere at right angles ($90^\circ$).

2. Basic Concept & Physical Mechanism

Fluid particles flow along streamlines (lines of constant $\psi$). The gradient of the velocity potential $\nabla \phi$ points in the direction of maximum rate of change of $\phi$, which is precisely the velocity vector $\vec{V}$. Because dot product $\nabla \phi \cdot \nabla \psi = 0$, the tangents to equipotential lines and streamlines are perpendicular, forming a curvilinear square mesh used historically for graphical fluid dynamic solutions.

3. Mathematical Derivation & Equations

The slope of an equipotential line ($m_\phi$) and the slope of a streamline ($m_\psi$) are given by:

$$m_\phi = \left(\frac{dy}{dx}\right)_{\phi = \text{const}} = -\frac{\partial \phi / \partial x}{\partial \phi / \partial y} = -\frac{u}{v}$$
$$m_\psi = \left(\frac{dy}{dx}\right)_{\psi = \text{const}} = -\frac{\partial \psi / \partial x}{\partial \psi / \partial y} = \frac{v}{u}$$

The product of their slopes confirms orthogonality:

$$m_\phi \cdot m_\psi = \left(-\frac{u}{v}\right) \left(\frac{v}{u}\right) = -1 \implies \theta = 90^\circ$$

Fundamental Dimensions

Slope: Dimensionless $[M^0 L^0 T^0]$

Potential Increment $\Delta \phi$: $[L^2 T^{-1}]$

SI & CGS Units

SI: Radians or degrees for angle; $\text{m}^2/\text{s}$ for potential difference.

CGS: $\text{cm}^2/\text{s}$

5. Engineering Reference Data Table

6. Aircraft Application & Air Safety Officer Connection

Flow nets generated by equipotential and stream lines are critical in designing convergent-divergent aircraft engine inlet cowls and supersonic wind tunnel contraction cones. Misalignment of flow or non-orthogonal shock interactions can cause boundary layer separation inside the intake, resulting in compressor stall and engine flameout—a primary hazard investigated under DGCA incident reporting guidelines.

7. UPSC Traps & Common Exam Pitfalls

Trap: Confusing the intersection angle of streamlines with equipotential lines.
Correction: Streamlines and equipotential lines intersect at orthogonal ($90^\circ$) angles everywhere except at stagnation points where velocity is zero and the potential gradient is undefined.

8. Step-by-Step Worked Numerical

Problem: Given the velocity potential $\phi = 3xy$, find the equation of the equipotential line passing through point $(1, 2)$ and determine the orthogonal streamline equation.

Step 1: Evaluate constant at $(1, 2)$: $\phi_0 = 3(1)(2) = 6$. Equipotential line equation: $3xy = 6 \implies y = \frac{2}{x}$.

Step 2: Velocity components: $u = \frac{\partial \phi}{\partial x} = 3y$, $v = \frac{\partial \phi}{\partial y} = 3x$.

Step 3: Stream function via Cauchy-Riemann: $\frac{\partial \psi}{\partial y} = u = 3y \implies \psi = \frac{3}{2}y^2 + f(x)$. Also $-\frac{\partial \psi}{\partial x} = v = 3x \implies \psi = \frac{3}{2}(y^2 - x^2) + C$.

Step 4: Streamline passing through $(1, 2)$: $\psi_0 = \frac{3}{2}(2^2 - 1^2) = \frac{3}{2}(3) = 4.5 \implies y^2 - x^2 = 3$.

Aviation Operational Takeaway: Orthogonal flow nets allow Air Safety Officers to map pressure gradients around blunt aircraft bodies during mishap trajectory analysis.

Subtopic 3 of 7 ID: FM-POT-03

Source & Sink

1. Formal Technical Definition

A line source is a elementary 2D potential flow singularity from which fluid radiates equally in all directions at a constant volumetric rate $m$ ($\text{m}^2/\text{s}$). A sink is an identical singularity where fluid converges radially inward ($m < 0$).

2. Basic Concept & Physical Mechanism

At the continuum level, a source represents a mathematical point creation of mass, violating continuity solely at the origin ($r = 0$). Fluid particles stream radially outward with velocity inversely proportional to radial distance ($V_r \propto 1/r$). This mimics the exhaust plume expansion or fuel tank venting dynamics analyzed in aircraft auxiliary power unit (APU) compartments.

3. Mathematical Derivation & Equations

In polar coordinates $(r, \theta)$, the velocity potential and stream function for a source of strength $m > 0$ are:

$$\phi = \frac{m}{2\pi} \ln r, \quad \psi = \frac{m}{2\pi} \theta$$

Radial and tangential velocity components:

$$V_r = \frac{\partial \phi}{\partial r} = \frac{m}{2\pi r}, \quad V_\theta = \frac{1}{r}\frac{\partial \phi}{\partial \theta} = 0$$

Fundamental Dimensions

Source Strength $[m] = [L^2 T^{-1}]$

Velocity Potential $[\phi] = [L^2 T^{-1}]$

SI & CGS Units

SI: $\text{m}^2/\text{s}$ (Volume flow per unit depth)

CGS: $\text{cm}^2/\text{s}$

5. Engineering Reference Data Table

Aviation System Typical Flow Rate ($m$) Potential Flow Analogy
Cabin Air Outflow Valve (Boeing 737) $\sim 2.5 \text{ m}^3/\text{s}$ per meter span Equivalent 2D Sink singularity
Jet Engine Intake (Idle Thrust) $\sim 150 \text{ kg/s}$ airflow induction Three-dimensional sink flow model

6. Aircraft Application & Air Safety Officer Connection

Source and sink singularities are superimposed to model complex aerodynamic shapes like Rankine ovals and flow around cylinders. In air safety investigations, engine intake ingestion hazards (foreign object damage - FOD) rely on sink flow velocity decay calculations to establish safety perimeters on the ramp during ground operations (DGCA CAR Series 'O').

7. UPSC Traps & Common Exam Pitfalls

Trap: Assuming that a source creates infinite velocity and infinite pressure at the origin ($r = 0$).
Correction: Mathematically, $V_r \to \infty$ as $r \to 0$, but physically, potential flow breaks down near singularities due to viscous boundary layer effects and cavitation.

8. Step-by-Step Worked Numerical

Problem: A line source of strength $m = 10\pi \text{ m}^2/\text{s}$ is located at the origin. Calculate the radial velocity and pressure drop from $r_1 = 2\text{ m}$ to $r_2 = 5\text{ m}$ in standard air ($\rho = 1.225 \text{ kg/m}^3$).

Given: $m = 10\pi, \rho = 1.225, r_1 = 2, r_2 = 5$

Step 1: Velocity at $r_1$: $V_{r1} = \frac{m}{2\pi r_1} = \frac{10\pi}{2\pi(2)} = 2.5 \text{ m/s}$.

Step 2: Velocity at $r_2$: $V_{r2} = \frac{10\pi}{2\pi(5)} = 1.0 \text{ m/s}$.

Step 3: Bernoulli equation between $r_1$ and $r_2$: $P_1 + \frac{1}{2}\rho V_{r1}^2 = P_2 + \frac{1}{2}\rho V_{r2}^2$.

Step 4: $P_2 - P_1 = \frac{1}{2}(1.225)(2.5^2 - 1.0^2) = 0.6125(6.25 - 1.0) = 3.216 \text{ Pa}$.

Aviation Operational Takeaway: Pressure recovery in expanding ducts (diffusers) follows similar deceleration principles.

Subtopic 4 of 7 ID: FM-POT-04

Doublet (Source-Sink Pair)

1. Formal Technical Definition

A doublet is the limiting flow field formed by placing an equal source and sink of strength $m$ at a vanishingly small separation distance $ds$, such that the product $\mu = m \cdot ds$ (doublet strength) remains constant.

2. Basic Concept & Physical Mechanism

The doublet is a fundamental building block in potential flow. When superimposed with a uniform parallel stream, it generates the exact flow field of an impermeable circular cylinder (or sphere in 3D). Physically, it represents a localized dipole disturbance that deflects oncoming streamlines symmetrically around a solid body without inducing circulation.

3. Mathematical Derivation & Equations

Combining source at $(-\epsilon, 0)$ and sink at $(\epsilon, 0)$ and taking the limit as $\epsilon \to 0$:

$$\phi = \frac{\mu \cos\theta}{2\pi r}, \quad \psi = -\frac{\mu \sin\theta}{2\pi r}$$

Velocity components in polar coordinates:

$$V_r = -\frac{\mu \cos\theta}{2\pi r^2}, \quad V_\theta = -\frac{\mu \sin\theta}{2\pi r^2}$$

Fundamental Dimensions

Doublet Strength $[\mu] = [L^3 T^{-1}]$

Velocity Potential $[\phi] = [L^2 T^{-1}]$

SI & CGS Units

SI: $\text{m}^3/\text{s}$

CGS: $\text{cm}^3/\text{s}$

5. Engineering Reference Data Table

Aviation Geometry Equivalent Singularity Key Design Parameter
Circular Pitot-Static Probe Head Uniform Stream + Doublet Stagnation pressure port location ($\theta = 0^\circ$)
Fuselage Cross-Section (Circular) 2D Doublet model Hoop stress & pressure distribution estimation

6. Aircraft Application & Air Safety Officer Connection

Pitot-static probes mounted on aircraft fuselages rely on doublet flow physics to measure total and static pressures. An Air Safety Officer investigating pitot-static icing or blockage incidents (e.g., Air France Flight 447 contributing factors) must understand how flow angularity and local pressure coefficients $C_p = 1 - 4\sin^2\theta$ deviate during yawed flight.

7. UPSC Traps & Common Exam Pitfalls

Trap: Confusing the dimensions of source strength ($m$: $\text{m}^2/\text{s}$) with doublet strength ($\mu$: $\text{m}^3/\text{s}$).
Correction: A doublet incorporates a length scale from the separation distance $ds$, boosting the dimensions from $L^2 T^{-1}$ to $L^3 T^{-1}$.

8. Step-by-Step Worked Numerical

Problem: A doublet of strength $\mu = 20\pi \text{ m}^3/\text{s}$ is placed in a flow field. Calculate the radial and tangential velocity at $r = 2\text{ m}, \theta = 30^\circ$.

Given: $\mu = 20\pi, r = 2, \theta = 30^\circ$ ($\cos 30^\circ = \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}, \sin 30^\circ = 0.5$)

Step 1: Radial velocity: $V_r = -\frac{\mu \cos\theta}{2\pi r^2} = -\frac{20\pi (\sqrt{3}/2)}{2\pi (2^2)} = -\frac{10\pi\sqrt{3}}{8\pi} = -1.25\sqrt{3} \approx -2.165 \text{ m/s}$.

Step 2: Tangential velocity: $V_\theta = -\frac{\mu \sin\theta}{2\pi r^2} = -\frac{20\pi (0.5)}{2\pi (4)} = -\frac{5\pi}{8\pi} = -0.625 \text{ m/s}$.

Aviation Operational Takeaway: Negative radial velocity indicates flow moving inward toward the doublet axis, simulating flow deceleration on the windward side of a cylinder.

Subtopic 5 of 7 ID: FM-POT-05

Free & Forced Vortex

1. Formal Technical Definition

A free (irrotational) vortex is a potential flow where fluid particles rotate without net angular momentum ($\nabla \times \vec{V} = 0$, $V_\theta r = \text{const}$). A forced (rotational) vortex is a solid-body rotation flow where fluid rotates uniformly with angular velocity $\omega$ ($V_\theta = \omega r$).

2. Basic Concept & Physical Mechanism

In a free vortex, velocity varies inversely with radius ($V_\theta \propto 1/r$), creating an intense low-pressure core (e.g., wingtip vortices, tornado cores). In a forced vortex, viscosity dominates, forcing the fluid to rotate like a solid body ($V_\theta \propto r$), characteristic of centrifugal pump impellers or swirling fuel in settling tanks.

3. Mathematical Derivation & Equations

For a Free Vortex (Circulation $\Gamma$):

$$\phi = \frac{\Gamma}{2\pi}\theta, \quad \psi = -\frac{\Gamma}{2\pi}\ln r, \quad V_\theta = \frac{\Gamma}{2\pi r}$$

For a Forced Vortex (Angular velocity $\omega$):

$$V_\theta = \omega r, \quad \zeta_z = \frac{1}{r}\frac{d}{dr}(r V_\theta) = 2\omega$$

Fundamental Dimensions

Circulation $[\Gamma] = [L^2 T^{-1}]$

Angular Velocity $[\omega] = [T^{-1}]$

SI & CGS Units

SI: $\Gamma$ in $\text{m}^2/\text{s}$; $\omega$ in $\text{rad/s}$

CGS: $\text{cm}^2/\text{s}$, $\text{rad/s}$

5. Engineering Reference Data Table

Aircraft wake turbulence behind heavy category aircraft

Vortex Type Velocity Profile ($V_\theta$) Aviation Application
Free Vortex $\propto 1/r$
Forced Vortex $\propto r$ Centrifugal compressor impellers in turbofan engines

6. Aircraft Application & Air Safety Officer Connection

Wake turbulence generated by wingtip free vortices poses severe hazards to following aircraft during approach and landing (ICAO wake turbulence separation minima). Air Safety Officers analyze vortex decay rates and ground roll encounters under DGCA Safety Circulars. Conversely, forced vortex dynamics govern centrifugal compressor operation in auxiliary power units (APUs).

7. UPSC Traps & Common Exam Pitfalls

Trap: Believing a free vortex has zero vorticity everywhere.
Correction: A free vortex is irrotational ($\nabla \times \vec{V} = 0$) everywhere except at the origin ($r = 0$) where vorticity is a Dirac delta function.

8. Step-by-Step Worked Numerical

Problem: A free vortex has a circulation $\Gamma = 40\pi \text{ m}^2/\text{s}$. Calculate the tangential velocity and pressure drop relative to ambient at $r = 4\text{ m}$ ($\rho = 1.225 \text{ kg/m}^3$).

Given: $\Gamma = 40\pi, r = 4, \rho = 1.225$

Step 1: Tangential velocity: $V_\theta = \frac{\Gamma}{2\pi r} = \frac{40\pi}{2\pi (4)} = 5 \text{ m/s}$.

Step 2: Bernoulli equation: $P_\infty + \frac{1}{2}\rho V_\infty^2 = P + \frac{1}{2}\rho V_\theta^2$. Assuming $V_\infty \approx 0$ far away, $P_\infty - P = \frac{1}{2}\rho V_\theta^2$.

Step 3: $\Delta P = \frac{1}{2} (1.225) (5^2) = 0.6125 (25) = 15.3125 \text{ Pa}$.

Aviation Operational Takeaway: Low-pressure cores in wingtip vortices can cause moisture condensation (contrails) and severe rolling moments on trailing aircraft.

Subtopic 6 of 7 ID: FM-POT-06

Circulation & Superposition Principle

1. Formal Technical Definition

Circulation ($\Gamma$) is the line integral of tangential velocity around a closed contour ($\Gamma = \oint \vec{V} \cdot d\vec{s}$). The Principle of Superposition states that because Laplace's equation is linear, any linear combination of elementary potential flows is also a valid potential flow solution.

2. Basic Concept & Physical Mechanism

Superposition allows aerodynamicists to build complex flow fields (like lifting cylinders and Joukowski airfoils) by adding together uniform streams, sources, sinks, doublets, and vortices. Circulation around an airfoil generates lift via the Kutta-Joukowski Theorem, linking fluid angular momentum directly to aerodynamic force generation.

3. Mathematical Derivation & Equations

By Stokes' Theorem, circulation relates to vorticity surface integral:

$$\Gamma = \oint_C \vec{V} \cdot d\vec{s} = \iint_S (\nabla \times \vec{V}) \cdot d\vec{A}$$

Superposed potential flow (Uniform stream + Doublet + Vortex):

$$\phi = U_\infty \left(r + \frac{R^2}{r}\right)\cos\theta + \frac{\Gamma}{2\pi}\theta$$

Fundamental Dimensions

Circulation $[\Gamma] = [L^2 T^{-1}]$

Lift per unit span $[L'] = [M L^{-1} T^{-2}]$

SI & CGS Units

SI: $\text{m}^2/\text{s}$ (Circulation); $\text{N/m}$ (Lift per span)

CGS: $\text{cm}^2/\text{s}$

5. Engineering Reference Data Table

Aircraft Parameter Typical Flight Value Superposition Component
Transport Wing Lift ($L$) $\sim 2.5 \times 10^6 \text{ N}$ (Boeing 777 MTOW) Uniform stream + Bound Vortex ($\Gamma$)
Stall Angle of Attack $\sim 15^\circ - 18^\circ$ Kutta condition breakdown point

6. Aircraft Application & Air Safety Officer Connection

The superposition of a vortex onto cylinder flow yields the Magnus effect and the Kutta-Joukowski lift theorem ($L' = \rho_\infty U_\infty \Gamma$). Under DGCA Airworthiness requirements (CAR 25), structural flight loads depend directly on spanwise circulation distribution ($\Gamma(y)$) calculated via Prandtl's lifting-line theory during certification.

7. UPSC Traps & Common Exam Pitfalls

Trap: Assuming superposition applies to nonlinear Navier-Stokes equations.
Correction: Superposition is valid only for linear differential equations like Laplace's equation ($\nabla^2 \phi = 0$). It fails in compressible or viscous boundary layer flows.

8. Step-by-Step Worked Numerical

Problem: A transport aircraft wing generates a circulation $\Gamma = 50 \text{ m}^2/\text{s}$ while flying at $U_\infty = 100 \text{ m/s}$ at sea level ($\rho = 1.225 \text{ kg/m}^3$). Calculate the lift force per unit span using the Kutta-Joukowski theorem.

Given: $\Gamma = 50, U_\infty = 100, \rho = 1.225$

Formula: $L' = \rho_\infty U_\infty \Gamma$

Calculation: $L' = (1.225 \text{ kg/m}^3)(100 \text{ m/s})(50 \text{ m}^2/\text{s}) = 6125 \text{ N/m}$

Aviation Operational Takeaway: Every meter of a lifting wing must sustain upward aerodynamic shear forces proportional to local circulation.

Subtopic 7 of 7 ID: FM-POT-07

Flow Around a Cylinder (with & without Circulation)

1. Formal Technical Definition

Flow around a circular cylinder is synthesized by superimposing a uniform stream, a doublet, and (optionally) a vortex. When circulation $\Gamma$ is added, stagnation points shift symmetrically or asynchronously, producing aerodynamic lift (Kutta-Joukowski theorem) and asymmetric pressure distributions.

2. Basic Concept & Physical Mechanism

Without circulation, flow around a cylinder exhibits D'Alembert's Paradox: zero net aerodynamic drag because front and rear pressure distributions are perfectly symmetric. Adding circulation shifts stagnation points downward, creating unequal upper and lower surface velocities and generating net lift without viscosity.

3. Mathematical Derivation & Equations

Superposition of uniform flow, doublet ($\mu = U_\infty R^2$), and vortex ($\Gamma$):

$$V_r = U_\infty \left(1 - \frac{R^2}{r^2}\right)\cos\theta, \quad V_\theta = -U_\infty \left(1 + \frac{R^2}{r^2}\right)\sin\theta + \frac{\Gamma}{2\pi r}$$

Surface velocity ($r = R$):

$$V_{\theta, r=R} = -2U_\infty \sin\theta + \frac{\Gamma}{2\pi R}$$

Fundamental Dimensions

Radius $[R] = [L]$

Pressure Coefficient $[C_p] = [M^0 L^0 T^0]$

SI & CGS Units

SI: Meters ($\text{m}$) for radius; dimensionless for $C_p$.

CGS: $\text{cm}$

5. Engineering Reference Data Table

Cylinder Flow Condition Stagnation Angles ($\theta_{stag}$) Net Lift & Drag
Without Circulation ($\Gamma = 0$) $\theta = 0^\circ, 180^\circ$ Lift = 0, Drag = 0 (D'Alembert)
With Circulation ($\Gamma < 4\pi U_\infty R$) Asymmetric on lower surface Lift $= \rho U_\infty \Gamma$, Drag = 0

6. Aircraft Application & Air Safety Officer Connection

Cylinder potential flow models simulate flow around circular fuselage sections, landing gear struts, and radar domes (radomes). Air Safety Officers investigating structural fatigue failures in landing gear struts must evaluate vortex-induced vibrations (Karman vortex shedding) which can trigger catastrophic resonant oscillations under specific airspeed ranges.

7. UPSC Traps & Common Exam Pitfalls

Trap: UPSC questions often ask if potential flow around a cylinder predicts zero aerodynamic drag in real flight.
Correction: Potential flow predicts zero drag (D'Alembert's Paradox) due to inviscid and irrotational assumptions. Real aircraft experience pressure drag and skin friction drag due to viscous boundary layer separation.

8. Step-by-Step Worked Numerical

Problem: A circular cylinder of radius $R = 1\text{ m}$ is placed in a uniform stream $U_\infty = 40 \text{ m/s}$ with circulation $\Gamma = 20\pi \text{ m}^2/\text{s}$. Calculate the surface velocity at the crown ($\theta = 90^\circ$).

Given: $R = 1, U_\infty = 40, \Gamma = 20\pi, \theta = 90^\circ$ ($\sin 90^\circ = 1$)

Formula: $V_{\theta, r=R} = -2U_\infty \sin\theta + \frac{\Gamma}{2\pi R}$

Calculation: $V_{\theta} = -2(40)(1) + \frac{20\pi}{2\pi (1)} = -80 + 10 = -70 \text{ m/s}$

Aviation Operational Takeaway: High negative velocity at the cylinder crown creates a low-pressure suction peak, analogous to suction over an airfoil upper surface.

Aviation Case Studies & DGCA Compliance

Case Study 1

American Airlines Flight 587 (Wake Turbulence Encounter)

On November 12, 2001, an Airbus A300-600 crashed in Queens, New York, after encountering intense wake turbulence (free vortex system) shed by a preceding Boeing 747-400. The aggressive rudder pedal inputs by the First Officer in response to the rolling vortex caused structural failure of the vertical stabilizer.

Fluid Mechanics Link: Free vortex velocity decay ($\Gamma / 2\pi r$) and induced rolling moments.

DGCA / ICAO Action: Strict adherence to wake turbulence radar separation minima and pilot upset recovery training mandated under CAR Section 8.

Case Study 2

Air France Flight 447 (Pitot-Static Icing & Flow Malfunction)

On June 1, 2009, an Airbus A330 crashed into the Atlantic Ocean after pitot-static probes iced over, causing erroneous airspeed indications. The blockage altered the local pressure distribution around the probe head (doublet and uniform stream interaction), resulting in autopilot disconnect and improper flight crew stall recovery.

Fluid Mechanics Link: Local pressure coefficient deviations ($C_p$) on blunt bodies during sensor icing.

DGCA / EASA Action: Mandatory replacement of pitot probes with heated, un-blockable multi-wash designs and enhanced upset recovery training.

UPSC Interview Corner: Board Model Answers

Q: If potential flow predicts zero drag on a cylinder (D'Alembert's Paradox), why do real aircraft wings experience drag? ▼

Model Answer: D'Alembert's Paradox arises because potential flow assumes fluid is inviscid ($\mu = 0$) and irrotational. In reality, viscous boundary layers form along solid surfaces. Near the rear of a blunt body, adverse pressure gradients cause boundary layer separation, creating a wide low-pressure wake that generates significant pressure (form) drag, alongside skin friction drag.
