Unit quantities

Textbook-grade UPSC Air Safety Officer deep-dive: Comprehensive theoretical foundations, analytical derivations, engineering reference data, DGCA/ICAO airworthiness integration, 14 practice MCQs, and timed mini-test.

How This Topic Is Useful for Air Safety Officers & Aircraft Operations
DGCA CAR & ICAO Mandate

In aviation operations and UPSC ASO assessments, this parameter is critical across 4 core operational pillars:

  • 1. Aircraft Systems & Flight Deck Monitoring: Engine performance monitoring systems and FADEC use corrected/unit parameters to evaluate turbine health across varying flight altitudes.
  • 2. Flight Safety & Dispatch Envelopes: Dynamic similarity models ensure engine surge margins and pump cavitation limits are respected throughout the aircraft operating envelope.
  • 3. DGCA CAR & ICAO Airworthiness Compliance: DGCA CAR Section 2 and ICAO Annex 8 mandate strict adherence to certified engine operating limits and scaling laws during overhaul testing.
  • 4. Air Safety Investigation & Incident Prevention: Air Safety Officers analyze engine performance data logs using unit quantities to detect compressor degradation or uncommanded thrust loss.

Pillar 1: Formal Technical Definition & Nomenclature

Unit quantities are scaling parameters derived from dynamic similarity laws used to predict turbine performance under variable head, speed, and discharge conditions ($N_{11} = \frac{N D}{\sqrt{H}}$ and $Q_{11} = \frac{Q}{D^2 \sqrt{H}}$).
  • Physical Meaning: They represent the actual operating parameters (speed, discharge, power) of a hydraulic turbine scaled to a hypothetical unit head of 1 meter.
  • Governing Law: Derived from non-dimensional specific speed and turbomachinery similarity laws based on geometric and kinematic similitude.
  • Continuum Boundary: Valid for incompressible fluid machinery operating within single-phase liquid regimes where cavitation inception is monitored.

Nomenclature & Symbols

  • $N_{11}$ = Unit speed (rpm)
  • $Q_{11}$ = Unit discharge ($m^3/s$)
  • $P_{11}$ = Unit power ($kW$)
  • $N$ = Actual rotational speed ($rpm$)
  • $H$ = Effective working head ($m$)
  • $D$ = Rotor/runner diameter ($m$)
  • $Q$ = Actual volumetric discharge ($m^3/s$)
  • $P$ = Developed mechanical power ($kW$)

Pillar 2: Microscopic Physics & Continuum Mechanics

1. Geometrical Similitude: Scaled homologous hydraulic turbines maintain exact dimensional ratios across all flow passages and blade angles.
2. Kinematic Similitude: Velocity triangles at inlet and outlet remain geometrically similar, keeping flow incidence angles constant.
3. Dynamic Similitude: Forces acting on fluid particles (inertia, pressure, gravity) scale proportionally, satisfying Froude and Reynolds similitude criteria.
4. Head Scaling: Variable operational heads are normalized to a standard 1-meter reference head to collapse performance curves into single characteristic master curves.

Pillar 3: Mathematical Derivations & Governing Laws

Step 1: Governing Relation

Discharge proportionality to velocity and area.

$$Q \propto A \cdot V \propto D^2 \sqrt{H}$$
Step 2: Boundary / Thermodynamic Conditions

Tangential velocity proportionality to runner speed and diameter.

$$V \propto N \cdot D \propto \sqrt{H}$$
Step 3: Integration & Substitution

Defining proportionality constants for unit discharge and unit speed.

$$Q_{11} = \frac{Q}{D^2 \sqrt{H}} \quad \text{and} \quad N_{11} = \frac{N \cdot D}{\sqrt{H}}$$
Step 4: Final Working Law

Unit power relation derived from discharge and head.

$$P_{11} = \frac{P}{D^2 H^{3/2}}$$

Pillar 4: Dimensional Analysis, SI/CGS Units & Conversion Factors

  • Fundamental Dimensions: Unit speed has dimensions of $[L^{-1/2} T^{-1} \cdot \text{Length}]$, unit discharge has $[L^{1/2} T^{-1}]$, and unit power has $[M L^2 T^{-3}]$.
  • SI Unit: Unit speed in $rpm\cdot m/\sqrt{m}$, unit discharge in $m^3/s$ per unit area-head, unit power in $kW/m^{2.5}$.
  • CGS Unit: Centimeter-gram-second equivalents scaled by exact conversion factors.
  • Aeronautical & Imperial Equivalents: Imperial units use feet for head ($ft$) and inches/feet for diameter ($ft$), converting discharge to $ft^3/s$ ($cfs$).

Pillar 7: High-Frequency UPSC Examiner Traps & Misconceptions

Trap 1: Diameter Omission in Unit Speed

❌ Examiner Trap: Assuming unit speed $N_{11}$ is independent of runner diameter $D$.

✅ UPSC Truth: Unit speed is defined as $N_{11} = \frac{ND}{\sqrt{H}}$, which explicitly includes the characteristic diameter $D$.

Trap 2: Dimensionless Confusion

❌ Examiner Trap: Treating unit quantities as strictly dimensionless numbers.

✅ UPSC Truth: Unit quantities retain dimensions (e.g., $rpm$, $m^3/s$) because they are scaled to unit head, unlike specific speed $N_s$.

Trap 3: Power Scaling Exponent

❌ Examiner Trap: Using $P_{11} = \frac{P}{D^2 H^{1/2}}$ for unit power.

✅ UPSC Truth: Correct unit power exponent for head is $H^{3/2}$, derived from $P \propto Q \cdot H \propto D^2 H^{3/2}$.

Trap 4: Compressible vs Incompressible Fluid Law

❌ Examiner Trap: Applying hydraulic turbine unit quantity formulas directly to high-pressure gas turbines without Mach number corrections.

✅ UPSC Truth: Gas turbines require corrected parameters accounting for temperature ratio ($\theta$) and pressure ratio ($\delta$).

Pillar 8: Step-by-Step Worked Aircraft Numerical Problem

Realistic Aircraft Scenario

A hydraulic turbine with a runner diameter of $2.0\text{ m}$ operates under an effective head of $25\text{ m}$ at a rotational speed of $300\text{ rpm}$, discharging $10\text{ m}^3/\text{s}$. Calculate the unit speed, unit discharge, and unit power if the overall efficiency is $85\%$.

Given Flight Parameters:
Diameter ($D$) = $2.0\text{ m}$; Head ($H$) = $25\text{ m}$; Speed ($N$) = $300\text{ rpm}$; Discharge ($Q$) = $10\text{ m}^3/\text{s}$; Efficiency ($\eta$) = $0.85$; Specific weight of water ($\gamma$) = $9810\text{ N/m}^3$.
Governing Formula:
$$N_{11} = \frac{N D}{\sqrt{H}}, \quad Q_{11} = \frac{Q}{D^2 \sqrt{H}}, \quad P = \eta \gamma Q H, \quad P_{11} = \frac{P}{D^2 H^{3/2}}$$
Step-by-Step Analytical Solution:
Step 1: Calculate unit speed $N_{11} = \frac{300 \times 2.0}{\sqrt{25}} = \frac{600}{5} = 120\text{ rpm}$. Step 2: Calculate unit discharge $Q_{11} = \frac{10}{(2.0)^2 \sqrt{25}} = \frac{10}{4 \times 5} = 0.5\text{ m}^3/\text{s}$. Step 3: Calculate actual power $P = 0.85 \times 9810 \times 10 \times 25 = 2,084,625\text{ W} = 2084.63\text{ kW}$, then unit power $P_{11} = \frac{2084.63}{2^2 \times (25)^{1.5}} = \frac{2084.63}{4 \times 125} = 4.17\text{ kW}$.
Final Answer: $N_{11} = 120\text{ rpm}, \quad Q_{11} = 0.5\text{ m}^3/\text{s}, \quad P_{11} = 4.17\text{ kW}$
Air Safety Officer Operational Takeaway:

Accurate calculation of unit quantities ensures that turbine performance maps during bench testing correlate correctly with in-service operational limits, preventing catastrophic overspeed or cavitation.

Question 1 • conceptual
What is the primary physical purpose of defining unit quantities in turbomachinery?
A To predict turbine performance under any head and speed based on tests conducted at a unit head of 1 meter
B To eliminate the effect of fluid viscosity in high-speed centrifugal compressors
C To convert compressible gas dynamics equations into incompressible forms
D To measure exact mechanical friction losses in turbine bearings
Solution & Technical Rationale:
Unit quantities normalize performance parameters to a unit head of 1 meter, enabling prediction of machine behavior under varying operational heads.
Question 2 • conceptual
Which of the following expressions correctly represents unit speed ($N_{11}$)?
A $\frac{N \sqrt{H}}{D}$
B $\frac{ND}{\sqrt{H}}$
C $\frac{\sqrt{H}}{ND}$
D $\frac{ND}{H^2}$
Solution & Technical Rationale:
Unit speed is derived from tangential velocity scaling and is defined as $N_{11} = \frac{ND}{\sqrt{H}}$.
Question 3 • conceptual
The unit discharge ($Q_{11}$) of a turbine is proportional to which of the following terms?
A $\frac{Q}{D^2 \sqrt{H}}$
B $\frac{Q \sqrt{H}}{D}$
C $\frac{Q}{D \sqrt{H}}$
D $\frac{Q \cdot H}{D^2}$
Solution & Technical Rationale:
From continuity and velocity triangles, discharge scales as $Q \propto D^2 \sqrt{H}$, making unit discharge $Q_{11} = \frac{Q}{D^2 \sqrt{H}}$.
Question 4 • statement-based
Consider the following statements regarding unit quantities: 1. Unit quantities are dimensionless numbers. 2. Unit speed depends on the runner diameter. Which of the statements given above is/are correct?
A 1 only
B 2 only
C Both 1 and 2
D Neither 1 nor 2
Solution & Technical Rationale:
Statement 1 is incorrect because unit quantities retain dimensions (e.g., rpm, m^3/s). Statement 2 is correct because $N_{11} = \frac{ND}{\sqrt{H}}$ explicitly depends on $D$.
Question 5 • numerical
A turbine runner of diameter $1.5\text{ m}$ runs at $400\text{ rpm}$ under a head of $16\text{ m}$. What is its unit speed?
A $150\text{ rpm}$
B $125\text{ rpm}$
C $75\text{ rpm}$
D $300\text{ rpm}$
Solution & Technical Rationale:
Using $N_{11} = \frac{ND}{\sqrt{H}} = \frac{400 \times 1.5}{\sqrt{16}} = \frac{600}{4} = 150\text{ rpm}$.
Question 6 • assertion-reasoning
Assertion (A): Unit power varies inversely with $H^{3/2}$. Reason (R): Power developed by a turbine is proportional to $Q \times H$.
A Both (A) and (R) are true and (R) is the correct explanation of (A)
B Both (A) and (R) are true but (R) is NOT the correct explanation of (A)
C (A) is true but (R) is false
D (A) is false but (R) is true
Solution & Technical Rationale:
Unit power is $P_{11} = \frac{P}{D^2 H^{3/2}}$, meaning $P_{11}$ varies directly (not inversely) with power and inversely with $H^{3/2}$. Thus Assertion is false, but Reason is true.
Question 7 • conceptual
What is the exponent of head ($H$) in the denominator of the unit power formula ($P_{11}$)?
A $0.5$
B $1.0$
C $1.5$
D $2.0$
Solution & Technical Rationale:
Unit power is $P_{11} = \frac{P}{D^2 H^{3/2}}$, so the head exponent in the denominator is $1.5$.
Question 8 • statement-based
Which parameters remain constant for a given turbine operating under varying heads according to unit quantity laws? 1. Unit speed $N_{11}$ 2. Actual speed $N$ 3. Unit discharge $Q_{11}$ Select the correct answer:
A 1 and 3 only
B 1 and 2 only
C 2 and 3 only
D 1, 2 and 3
Solution & Technical Rationale:
For geometrically similar operation at constant efficiency/gate opening, unit speed $N_{11}$ and unit discharge $Q_{11}$ remain constant while actual speed and discharge vary with $\sqrt{H}$.
Question 9 • numerical
If a turbine discharges $20\text{ m}^3/\text{s}$ under a head of $9\text{ m}$ with a diameter of $2\text{ m}$, what is the unit discharge?
A $1.11\text{ m}^3/\text{s}$
B $1.67\text{ m}^3/\text{s}$
C $3.33\text{ m}^3/\text{s}$
D $5.00\text{ m}^3/\text{s}$
Solution & Technical Rationale:
Using $Q_{11} = \frac{Q}{D^2 \sqrt{H}} = \frac{20}{2^2 \sqrt{9}} = \frac{20}{4 \times 3} = \frac{20}{12} = 1.67\text{ m}^3/\text{s}$.
Question 10 • conceptual
Why are unit quantities particularly valuable in airworthiness and turbine testing laboratories?
A They allow full-scale performance prediction using small-scale model tests in test rigs
B They eliminate the need for measuring fluid density
C They make Reynolds number scaling completely irrelevant
D They convert turbulent flow into laminar flow inside test flumes
Solution & Technical Rationale:
Unit quantities and similarity laws allow engineers to predict full-scale prototype performance from model testing under controlled laboratory conditions.
Question 11 • assertion-reasoning
Assertion (A): Geometric similarity is a prerequisite for applying unit quantity scaling laws. Reason (R): Homologous turbines have identical blade angles and proportional flow passage dimensions.
A Both (A) and (R) are true and (R) is the correct explanation of (A)
B Both (A) and (R) are true but (R) is NOT the correct explanation of (A)
C (A) is true but (R) is false
D (A) is false but (R) is true
Solution & Technical Rationale:
Geometric similarity ensures that velocity triangles and flow incidence angles remain identical between model and prototype, validating unit quantity scaling.
