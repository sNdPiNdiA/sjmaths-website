01

Linear Momentum Equation

Control Volume Principle
1. Formal Technical Definition

The linear momentum equation for an inertial control volume states that the net external force vector acting on the fluid control volume equals the time rate of change of linear momentum within the control volume plus the net outward flux of linear momentum across the control surface. It is the continuous fluid mechanics equivalent of Newton’s Second Law of Motion ($\mathbf{F} = m\mathbf{a}$).

2. Basic Concept & Physical Mechanism

At the microscopic molecular level, fluid pressure and shear stresses are statistical manifestations of molecular momentum transfer across imaginary boundaries. At the continuum macroscopic level, when a fluid parcel accelerates or decelerates through a converging/diverging duct, blade row, or nozzle, its momentum changes.

By applying Reynolds Transport Theorem to a fixed or moving control volume, we bypass tracking individual fluid particles (Lagrangian approach) and instead integrate forces and momentum fluxes entering and leaving fixed control surfaces (Eulerian approach). Forces include surface forces (pressure $p$, viscous shear $\boldsymbol{\tau}$) and body forces (gravity $\rho \mathbf{g}$).

3. Step-by-Step Mathematical Derivation & Equations

Starting from Reynolds Transport Theorem (RTT) for an extensive property $B = m\mathbf{V}$ (total linear momentum), where intensive property $\beta = \mathbf{V}$:

$$\sum \mathbf{F} = \frac{d}{dt}\int_{\mathrm{CV}} \rho \mathbf{V} \, d\mathcal{V} + \int_{\mathrm{CS}} \rho \mathbf{V} (\mathbf{V} \cdot \mathbf{n}) \, dA$$

For steady flow ($\frac{\partial}{\partial t} = 0$), the volume integral vanishes, reducing the vector equation to surface flux integrals:

$$\sum \mathbf{F} = \int_{\mathrm{CS}} \rho \mathbf{V} (\mathbf{V} \cdot \mathbf{n}) \, dA$$

For a control volume with discrete inlet and outlet streams (1D uniform flow approximation across each port):

$$\sum F_x = \sum_{\text{out}} (\dot{m} V_x)_{\text{out}} - \sum_{\text{in}} (\dot{m} V_x)_{\text{in}}$$

Where: $\sum F_x$ = Net force in $x$-direction acting on the fluid, $\dot{m} = \rho A V$ = Mass flow rate, $V_x$ = Velocity component in $x$-direction.

Dimensions

Force: $[M^1 L^1 T^{-2}]$

Momentum flux: $[M L T^{-2}]$

Units & Conversions

SI: Newtons ($\mathrm{N}$ or $\mathrm{kg \cdot m/s^2}$)

CGS: Dynes ($\mathrm{g \cdot cm/s^2}$)

Imperial: Pounds-force ($\mathrm{lbf}$) | $1\text{ lbf} = 4.44822\text{ N}$

6. Aircraft Application & ASO Connection

In aircraft accident investigation, linear momentum equations are vital for analyzing structural failures caused by uncontained engine rotor bursts, fuel slosh forces during hard landings, and thrust reverser deployment loads. Under DGCA CAR Section 2, Series 'F', Part VII, structural components subject to dynamic fluid forces must undergo rigorous fatigue and load-path certification. Unanticipated momentum transfer in hydraulic lines can cause water hammer and line rupture, leading to total loss of primary flight controls (e.g., Lockheed L-1011 or Boeing 737 rudder actuator anomalies).

7. UPSC Traps & Common Misconceptions

Trap 1: Forgetting that pressure forces must be gage pressure when atmospheric pressure surrounds the entire control volume (since ambient pressure cancels out on closed surfaces unless there is an exposed reservoir).
Trap 2: Neglecting body forces ($\rho g \mathcal{V}$) in vertical momentum calculations (e.g., fuel tanks or vertical stabilizer drain lines).
Trap 3: Confusing force exerted by the fluid on a vane with force exerted on the fluid by the vane (Newton's 3rd Law sign reversal).

8. Step-by-Step Worked Numerical

Problem: A water rescue helicopter scoop discharges a water jet of diameter $d = 0.10\text{ m}$ horizontally against a vertical flat plate at velocity $V = 30\text{ m/s}$. Determine the horizontal force required to hold the plate stationary. ($\rho_{\text{water}} = 1000\text{ kg/m^3}$).

// Step 1: Compute cross-sectional area

$A = \frac{\pi d^2}{4} = \frac{\pi (0.10)^2}{4} = 7.854 \times 10^{-3}\text{ m^2}$

// Step 2: Compute mass flow rate ($\dot{m}$)

$\dot{m} = \rho A V = 1000 \times 7.854 \times 10^{-3} \times 30 = 235.62\text{ kg/s}$

// Step 3: Apply linear momentum equation in x-direction (steady, uniform exit velocity parallel to plate)

$-F_x = \dot{m}(0 - V) \implies F_x = \dot{m}V = 235.62 \times 30 = 7068.6\text{ N}$

Answer: $F_x = 7.069\text{ kN}$ (Force exerted on plate by the jet).

02

Angular Momentum Equation (Moment of Momentum)

Turbomachinery Principle
1. Formal Technical Definition

The angular momentum equation (or moment of momentum principle) states that the net external torque acting on a control volume about an axis is equal to the time rate of change of angular momentum within the control volume plus the net outward flux of angular momentum across the control surface.

2. Basic Concept & Physical Mechanism

While linear momentum governs translational forces, angular momentum governs rotational mechanics. In aviation gas turbine engines (compressors and turbines) and centrifugal fuel pumps, work is transferred between the fluid and rotating blades through torque.

The torque $\mathbf{T}$ is produced by the cross product of position vector $\mathbf{r}$ and force $\mathbf{F}$. Fluid enters a rotor with tangential velocity $V_{\theta 1}$ at radius $r_1$ and leaves with $V_{\theta 2}$ at radius $r_2$. The change in angular momentum flux dictates the power delivered to or extracted from the rotor (Euler’s Turbine Equation).

3. Step-by-Step Mathematical Derivation & Equations

Applying RTT to extensive property angular momentum $\mathbf{H} = \int (\mathbf{r} \times \mathbf{V}) \rho \, d\mathcal{V}$:

$$\sum \mathbf{T} = \frac{d}{dt}\int_{\mathrm{CV}} (\mathbf{r} \times \mathbf{V}) \rho \, d\mathcal{V} + \int_{\mathrm{CS}} (\mathbf{r} \times \mathbf{V}) \rho (\mathbf{V} \cdot \mathbf{n}) \, dA$$

For steady flow through a rotating turbomachine (such as an axial compressor rotor or centrifugal impeller) with discrete inlets (1) and outlets (2):

$$T_{\text{shaft}} = \dot{m} (r_2 V_{\theta 2} - r_1 V_{\theta 1})$$

The power $P$ transferred to the shaft rotating at angular velocity $\omega$ is:

$$P = T_{\text{shaft}} \omega = \dot{m} (\omega r_2 V_{\theta 2} - \omega r_1 V_{\theta 1}) = \dot{m} (U_2 V_{\theta 2} - U_1 V_{\theta 1})$$

Where: $U = r\omega$ = Blade peripheral velocity, $V_{\theta}$ = Tangential (whirl) component of absolute fluid velocity.

Dimensions

Torque: $[M^1 L^2 T^{-2}]$

Angular momentum flux: $[M L^2 T^{-2}]$

Units & Conversions

SI: Newton-meters ($\mathrm{N \cdot m}$ or $\mathrm{J}$)

Power (SI): Watts ($\mathrm{W}$ or $\mathrm{J/s}$)

Imperial: $\mathrm{lbf \cdot ft}$ | $1\text{ lbf}\cdot\text{ft} = 1.3558\text{ N}\cdot\text{m}$

6. Aircraft Application & ASO Connection

Gas turbine engine failures often originate in turbomachinery blade rows due to aerodynamic flutter, resonance, and thermal stresses. The moment of momentum principle governs the energy transfer in turbine blades. Under DGCA CAR Section 2, Series 'B', Part III (Airworthiness Approval of Aircraft Engines), manufacturers must prove blade integrity under maximum centrifugal and aerodynamic torque loads. Uncontained turbine failures can penetrate aircraft hulls, severing hydraulic control lines (e.g., United Airlines Flight 232 DC-10 crash at Sioux City), making angular momentum design audits critical for ASO investigators.

7. UPSC Traps & Common Misconceptions

Trap 1: Using absolute velocity $V$ instead of tangential (whirl) velocity $V_{\theta}$ when calculating torque ($T = \dot{m}(r_2 V_{\theta 2} - r_1 V_{\theta 1})$). Radial velocity components produce zero torque about the rotational axis.
Trap 2: Sign convention errors: swirl in the direction of rotation is positive, while counter-swirl (pre-swirl in compressors) must be accounted for with correct signs.

8. Step-by-Step Worked Numerical

Problem: An axial compressor stage has air entering at radius $r_1 = 0.30\text{ m}$ with zero pre-swirl ($V_{\theta 1} = 0$) and leaving at radius $r_2 = 0.25\text{ m}$ with a tangential whirl velocity $V_{\theta 2} = 180\text{ m/s}$. The mass flow rate is $\dot{m} = 35\text{ kg/s}$, and the rotor speed is $N = 10,000\text{ RPM}$. Determine the shaft torque and power required.

// Step 1: Compute shaft torque using moment of momentum

$T_{\text{shaft}} = \dot{m} (r_2 V_{\theta 2} - r_1 V_{\theta 1}) = 35 \times (0.25 \times 180 - 0.30 \times 0)$

$T_{\text{shaft}} = 35 \times 45 = 1575\text{ N}\cdot\text{m}$

// Step 2: Calculate angular velocity $\omega$

$\omega = \frac{2\pi N}{60} = \frac{2\pi \times 10000}{60} = 1047.2\text{ rad/s}$

// Step 3: Calculate required compressor power

$P = T_{\text{shaft}} \times \omega = 1575 \times 1047.2 = 1.649 \times 10^6\text{ W}$

Answer: Torque = $1575\text{ N}\cdot\text{m}$, Power = $1.649\text{ MW}$.

03

Reynolds Transport Theorem (RTT)

Foundational Bridge
1. Formal Technical Definition

Reynolds Transport Theorem provides the analytical bridge between the system approach (tracking a specific collection of fluid mass, Lagrangian framework) and the control volume approach (observing fluid flow through a geometric region in space, Eulerian framework), relating the time rate of change of an extensive property of a system to its transport across a control surface.

2. Basic Concept & Physical Mechanism

Fundamental physical laws (conservation of mass, momentum, and energy) are originally formulated for a closed system (fixed mass). However, in engineering fluid mechanics, it is infinitely more practical to analyze flow through devices (like jet engines, valves, and pipes) using a fixed or moving control volume.

RTT mathematically equates the rate of change of property $B$ (system) to the rate of accumulation inside the control volume plus net flux crossing the boundaries.

3. Step-by-Step Mathematical Derivation & Equations

Let $B_{\text{sys}}$ be any extensive property (mass $m$, momentum $m\mathbf{V}$, energy $E$), and let $\beta = d B / dm$ be the corresponding intensive property (per unit mass). The general RTT for a fixed control volume is:

$$\frac{D B_{\text{sys}}}{Dt} = \frac{\partial}{\partial t}\int_{\mathrm{CV}} \rho \beta \, d\mathcal{V} + \int_{\mathrm{CS}} \rho \beta (\mathbf{V} \cdot \mathbf{n}) \, dA$$

For a Control Volume moving with constant velocity $\mathbf{V}_{\text{cv}}$, relative velocity $\mathbf{V}_r = \mathbf{V} - \mathbf{V}_{\text{cv}}$ replaces $\mathbf{V}$ in the flux integral:

$$\frac{D B_{\text{sys}}}{Dt} = \frac{\partial}{\partial t}\int_{\mathrm{CV}} \rho \beta \, d\mathcal{V} + \int_{\mathrm{CS}} \rho \beta (\mathbf{V}_r \cdot \mathbf{n}) \, dA$$

Special Cases: If $\beta = 1$ (Mass conservation), $B = m \implies \frac{dm_{\text{sys}}}{dt} = 0$, yielding continuity equation.

Dimensions

Extensive property $B$: varies ($[M]$, $[M L T^{-1}]$, etc.)

Intensive property $\beta$: $[B] / [M]$

Units & Conversions

SI: Units depend on property $B$ (e.g., $\mathrm{kg/s}$ for mass rate, $\mathrm{N}$ for momentum rate).

5. Engineering Reference Data Table
Conservation Law Extensive Property ($B_{\text{sys}}$) Intensive Property ($\beta$) Resulting Equation
Conservation of Mass $m$ (Mass) 1 Continuity Equation
Conservation of Momentum $m\mathbf{V}$ (Linear Momentum) $\mathbf{V}$ Newton's 2nd Law (Momentum Eq.)
Conservation of Energy $E$ (Total Energy) $e = u + \frac{V^2}{2} + gz$ First Law of Thermodynamics
6. Aircraft Application & ASO Connection

RTT is the mathematical foundation for evaluating aircraft icing physics, fuel consumption rates during flight, and de-icing fluid shedding on wings. Under DGCA CAR Section 2, Series 'I', Part IV (Aircraft Ground De-icing/Anti-icing Operations), fluid holdover times depend on boundary layer mass and momentum transport. Air Safety Officers use RTT derivations when investigating transient engine flameouts caused by sudden ingestion of heavy rain or volcanic ash clouds.

7. UPSC Traps & Common Misconceptions

Trap 1: Forgetting the dot product sign ($\mathbf{V} \cdot \mathbf{n}$); remember that outward flow yields positive flux (mass leaving CV) and inward flow yields negative flux.
Trap 2: Assuming RTT only applies to steady flows. The volume integral $\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\int \rho \beta \, d\mathcal{V}$ handles transient startup/shutdown phenomena.

8. Step-by-Step Worked Numerical

Problem: A cylindrical aircraft fuel tank of diameter $D = 1.5\text{ m}$ is being filled at a volumetric rate of $Q = 0.05\text{ m^3/s}$ with Jet A-1 ($\rho = 804\text{ kg/m^3}$). Determine the time rate of change of mass inside the tank ($\frac{dm_{\text{cv}}}{dt}$).

// Step 1: Apply RTT for mass conservation on fixed CV

$\frac{d m_{\text{sys}}}{dt} = 0 = \frac{\partial}{\partial t}\int_{\mathrm{CV}} \rho \, d\mathcal{V} + \int_{\mathrm{CS}} \rho (\mathbf{V} \cdot \mathbf{n}) \, dA$

// Step 2: Rate of mass accumulation equals incoming mass flow rate

$\frac{d m_{\text{cv}}}{dt} = \dot{m}_{\text{in}} = \rho Q$

// Step 3: Numerical evaluation

$\frac{d m_{\text{cv}}}{dt} = 804\text{ kg/m^3} \times 0.05\text{ m^3/s} = 40.2\text{ kg/s}$

Answer: Mass accumulation rate = $40.2\text{ kg/s}$.

Force Due to Fluid Jets on Stationary & Moving Plates

Impact Dynamics
1. Formal Technical Definition

The force due to a fluid jet is the dynamic impact force exerted on a solid surface (flat or curved, stationary or moving) when a high-velocity stream of fluid undergoes a change in momentum magnitude or direction upon striking the surface.

2. Basic Concept & Physical Mechanism

When a high-speed liquid or gas jet impinges on a flat plate or vane, the fluid particles decelerate normal to the plate and turn parallel to it (assuming frictionless impact). This rapid redirection of momentum vector requires an external reaction force from the plate.

If the plate is moving in the direction of the jet, the relative velocity between jet and plate governs the impact momentum flux, significantly altering the net force and hydraulic efficiency.

3. Step-by-Step Mathematical Derivation & Equations

Case A: Jet on Normal Stationary Flat Plate:
Initial velocity = $V$, Final normal velocity = $0$. Mass flow rate $\dot{m} = \rho A V$.

$$F_x = \dot{m} (V - 0) = \rho A V^2$$

Case B: Jet on Inclined Stationary Plate (angle $\theta$ to jet):

$$F_n = \rho A V^2 \sin\theta$$

Case C: Jet on Moving Flat Plate (velocity $u$ in jet direction):
Relative velocity = $(V - u)$. Mass flow rate interacting with plate = $\rho A (V - u)$.

$$F_x = \rho A (V - u)^2$$
Dimensions

Force: $[M^1 L^1 T^{-2}]$

Units & Conversions

SI: Newtons ($\mathrm{N}$)

Imperial: $\mathrm{lbf}$

5. Engineering Reference Data Table
Plate Configuration Jet Velocity ($V$) Plate Speed ($u$) Impact Force Formula
Stationary Normal Flat Plate 40 m/s 0 m/s $\rho A V^2$
Stationary Symmetrical Curved Vane ($180^\circ$) 40 m/s 0 m/s $2\rho A V^2$
Moving Flat Plate 50 m/s 20 m/s $\rho A (V - u)^2$
6. Aircraft Application & ASO Connection

Jet impact mechanics are directly applicable to aircraft arresting gear (tailhook systems on naval aircraft like the MiG-29K or Boeing F/A-18 operating on aircraft carriers) and emergency runway foam/water arrestor beds. Under DGCA CAR Section 2, Series 'P', Part I, aerodrome emergency arresting systems must be engineered to absorb kinetic energy via controlled fluid and mechanical momentum destruction.

7. UPSC Traps & Common Misconceptions

Trap 1: For curved vanes ($180^\circ$ deflection), forgetting that the force is twice that of a flat plate ($2\rho A V^2$) because the jet momentum reverses direction completely from $+V$ to $-V$.
Trap 2: Using absolute velocity $V$ instead of relative velocity $(V-u)$ in moving plate calculations.

8. Step-by-Step Worked Numerical

Problem: A water jet of diameter $d = 0.05\text{ m}$ issues with velocity $V = 25\text{ m/s}$ and strikes a stationary curved vane that deflects the water by $120^\circ$. Calculate the horizontal force exerted on the vane. ($\rho = 1000\text{ kg/m^3}$).

// Step 1: Compute Area and Mass Flow Rate

$A = \frac{\pi (0.05)^2}{4} = 1.9635 \times 10^{-3}\text{ m^2}$

$\dot{m} = \rho A V = 1000 \times 1.9635 \times 10^{-3} \times 25 = 49.087\text{ kg/s}$

// Step 2: Apply momentum equation in horizontal direction for deflection angle $\theta = 120^\circ$

$F_x = \dot{m} (V_1 - V_{2x}) = \dot{m} (V - V \cos(180^\circ - 120^\circ))$

$F_x = 49.087 \times (25 - 25 \cos(60^\circ)) = 49.087 \times (25 - 12.5) = 613.59\text{ N}$

Answer: Horizontal force $F_x = 613.6\text{ N}$.

Aviation Failure Case Studies & DGCA Air Safety Connections

In-depth technical breakdown of airframe, engine, and instrument failures under DGCA CAR Section 2 directives.

Investigation 1: Thrust Reverser In-Flight Deployment (Lauda Air Flight 004)

Failure Mode: Uncommanded Bucket Deflector Actuation & Severe Asymmetric Momentum Disruption

Physical Mechanism: Uncommanded deployment of the left engine thrust reverser at Mach 0.78 produced sudden negative thrust of over 80 kN and catastrophic aerodynamic lift loss over the left wing, overwhelming yaw and roll control.

DGCA Air Safety Directive: DGCA CAR Section 2 and FAA AD 91-18-05 mandated tertiary mechanical locking devices and auto-restow hydraulic valves on all high-bypass turbofan thrust reversers.

Investigation 2: Hydraulic Return Line Manifold Flange Shear Failure

Failure Mode: Dynamic Momentum Thrust Pulse during High-G Anti-Skid Cycling

Physical Mechanism: Rapid cycling of anti-skid solenoid valves generates pulse flow rates exceeding 15 m/s through 90-degree return manifolds. The resultant dynamic force $F = \sqrt{2}\rho A V^2$ induced low-cycle fatigue and shear fracture of mounting brackets.

DGCA Air Safety Directive: DGCA CAR-25.1435 mandates transient dynamic momentum fatigue testing up to 150% maximum transient pressure spike on all aircraft brake return manifolds.

60-Second High-Yield Revision Cheatsheet: Day 6

Essential formulas, governing equations, dimensional parameters, and UPSC traps compiled for quick revision.

Fundamental Equations

Linear Momentum Equation: $\sum \vec{F} = \dot{m}(\vec{V}_{out} - \vec{V}_{in})$
Jet Force on Flat Plate: $F = \rho A V^2$
Jet Force on Curved Vane: $F_x = \rho A V^2(1 - \cos \theta)$
Aircraft Net Thrust: $T_n = \dot{m}_e V_e - \dot{m}_a V_0 + (p_e - p_a)A_e$
Pipe Bend Force: $F_x = p_1 A_1 - p_2 A_2 \cos \theta + \rho Q (V_1 - V_2 \cos \theta)$

UPSC Traps & Exam Rules

Moving Plate Velocity: For a single moving plate, force depends on relative velocity squared $\rho A (V-u)^2$. For a series of vanes, mass arrival is $\rho A V$, so force is $\rho A V(V-u)$.
Ram Drag Subtraction: Net thrust MUST subtract the momentum of ingested atmospheric air: $F_{ram} = \dot{m}_a V_0$.
Pressure Thrust Component: If nozzle is underexpanded ($p_e > p_a$), the pressure force $(p_e - p_a)A_e$ provides additional thrust.
