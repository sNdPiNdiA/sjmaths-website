Similarity laws – geometric, kinematic, dynamic

Fluid Mechanics Machinery Fluid Mechanics & Machinery Phase 1: Foundations 90-120 min Master Class

1. Understand (From Absolute Zero, 5–10 min)

In aeronautical engineering and fluid dynamics, we are constantly faced with a fundamental challenge: How do we predict the performance of a full-scale aircraft (the prototype) using tests conducted on a smaller, cost-effective version (the model) in a wind tunnel? The answer lies in the elegant framework of Similarity Laws (also known as the theory of similitude).

Similitude is the study of predicting prototype behavior from model measurements. It is not enough for the model to simply "look like" the prototype. For the fluid flow around the model to behave in a manner that directly mirrors the flow around the prototype, we must satisfy three distinct levels of similarity, built sequentially upon one another:

  1. Geometric Similarity (Similarity of Shape):

    This is the most basic requirement. It dictates that the ratio of all corresponding linear dimensions between the model ($m$) and the prototype ($p$) must be a constant scale factor, $λ_L$ (or $L_r$).

    $$L_r = L_pL_m = b_pb_m = c_pc_m$$

    where $L$ is length, $b$ is wing span, and $c$ is chord length. Additionally, all corresponding angles must be identical ($α_p = α_m$).

  2. Kinematic Similarity (Similarity of Motion):

    This requires that the streamline patterns around the model and the prototype must be geometrically similar. Consequently, the velocity vectors at corresponding points in the flow fields must point in the identical direction, and their magnitudes must maintain a constant ratio, $λ_V$ (or $V_r$).

    $$V_r = V_pV_m = u_pu_m = w_pw_m$$

    Because acceleration is the time derivative of velocity, kinematic similarity also ensures a constant acceleration scale ratio ($λ_a$).

  3. Dynamic Similarity (Similarity of Forces):

    This is the ultimate level of similarity. It demands that the ratios of all corresponding forces acting on corresponding fluid particles and boundary surfaces must be equal. These forces include inertial forces, viscous forces, gravitational forces, pressure forces, surface tension forces, and elastic compressibility forces.

    $$(Finertial)_p(Finertial)_m = (Fviscous)_p(Fviscous)_m = (Fgravity)_p(Fgravity)_m = Constant$$

Physical Intuition: If a model is geometrically similar, and we scale the flow speed such that the balance of forces (viscous shear, pressure gradients, inertia) remains identical at every point, the fluid molecules will trace out identical, scaled trajectories. This guarantees that nondimensional aerodynamic coefficients, such as the lift coefficient ($C_L$) and drag coefficient ($C_D$), calculated from wind tunnel tests will apply directly to the real aircraft flying in the atmosphere.

Standard UPSC ASO Terminology & SI Units:

  • Prototype ($p$): The full-scale physical system (e.g., an actual Airbus A320).
  • Model ($m$): The scaled-down version used for testing (e.g., a 1:20 scale model).
  • Scale Ratio ($λ_i$ or $i_r$): The ratio of a prototype variable to a model variable (e.g., $L_r = L_p/L_m$, which has no units).
  • Viscous Force ($F_V$): $μ ( ∂ u∂ y ) A$ [Newtons, $N$].
  • Inertial Force ($F_I$): $ρ V^2 L^2$ [Newtons, $N$].

2. Learn the Concept (Complete Theoretical Foundation & Derivations, 20–25 min)

To mathematically formalize similarity laws, we must transition from physical intuition to rigorous mathematical proof. We do this in two ways: first, by non-dimensionalizing the governing equations of fluid motion (the Navier-Stokes equations), and second, via the classical Buckingham $\Pi$ Theorem of dimensional analysis.

1. Derivation of Similarity Parameters from the Navier-Stokes Equations

Consider an incompressible, viscous flow of a Newtonian fluid. The conservation of momentum is governed by the Navier-Stokes equations:

$$ ρ ( ∂ \vec{V}∂ t + (\vec{V} · \nabla)\vec{V} ) = -\nabla p + μ \nabla^2 \vec{V} + ρ \vec{g} $$

To non-dimensionalize this equation, we define characteristic reference scales for length ($L_c$), velocity ($V_∞$), density ($ρ_∞$), pressure ($p_∞$), and time ($t_c = L_c/V_∞$). We introduce the following dimensionless variables (denoted with an asterisk $*$):

$$ \vec{V}^* = \vec{V}V_∞, \quad \vec{x}^* = \vec{x}L_c, \quad t^* = t V_∞L_c, \quad p^* = p - p_∞ρ_∞ V_∞^2, \quad \nabla^* = L_c \nabla, \quad \nabla*2 = L_c^2 \nabla^2 $$

Substituting these dimensionless variables back into the momentum equation yields:

$$ ρ_∞ ( V_∞^2L_c ∂ \vec{V}^*∂ t^* + V_∞^2L_c (\vec{V}^* · \nabla^*)\vec{V}^* ) = -ρ_∞ V_∞^2L_c \nabla^* p^* + μ V_∞L_c^2 \nabla*2 \vec{V}^* + ρ_∞ \vec{g} $$

Divide the entire equation by the inertial scaling term $ρ_∞ V_∞^2L_c$:

$$ ∂ \vec{V}^*∂ t^* + (\vec{V}^* · \nabla^*)\vec{V}^* = -\nabla^* p^* + [ μρ_∞ V_∞ L_c ] \nabla*2 \vec{V}^* + [ g L_cV_∞^2 ] \vec{g}g $$

Analyzing the coefficients in brackets reveals the absolute foundation of dynamic similarity:

  1. The coefficient of the viscous diffusion term is the reciprocal of the Reynolds Number ($Re$): $$ Re = ρ_∞ V_∞ L_cμ = V_∞ L_cν $$
  2. The coefficient of the body force (gravitational) term is the reciprocal of the square of the Froude Number ($Fr$): $$ Fr = V_∞√g L_c $$

Crucial Conclusion: If two different flows (e.g., a scale model in a wind tunnel and a real aircraft in the sky) have identical geometric boundaries, and if their dimensionless parameters ($Re$ and $Fr$) are identical, the non-dimensional Navier-Stokes equations governing their motions are mathematically identical. Consequently, their non-dimensional solutions ($\vec{V}^*(x^*, y^*, z^*, t^*)$ and $p^*(x^*, y^*, z^*, t^*)$) must be identical. This is the mathematical proof of dynamic similarity!

2. Dimensional Analysis & the Buckingham $\Pi$ Theorem

When the governing differential equations are unknown or too complex, we rely on dimensional analysis. The Buckingham $\Pi$ Theorem states that if a physically meaningful equation involves $n$ dimensional variables containing $m$ fundamental dimensions (usually Mass $M$, Length $L$, Time $T$, and Temperature $θ$), the variables can be grouped into $k = n - m$ independent dimensionless parameters, denoted as $\Pi_1, \Pi_2, \dots, \Pi_k$.

Let's choose the drag force $D$ on an airfoil as our target variable. We hypothesize that $D$ depends on chord length $c$, fluid velocity $V$, fluid density $ρ$, fluid dynamic viscosity $μ$, and the speed of sound $a$ (compressibility effect):

$$ D = f(c, V, ρ, μ, a) \implies g(D, c, V, ρ, μ, a) = 0 $$

Here, $n = 6$ variables. The fundamental dimensions involved are $M$, $L$, and $T$ ($m = 3$). Thus, we expect $k = 6 - 3 = 3$ dimensionless $\Pi$ terms.

We select three repeating variables that span all fundamental dimensions and do not form a dimensionless group themselves: $ρ$, $V$, and $c$.

  • $\Pi_1 = ρ^a V^b c^c D \implies [M^0 L^0 T^0] = [ML-3]^a [LT-1]^b [L]^c [MLT-2]$

    Solving the linear system for mass, length, and time exponents yields $a = -1$, $b = -2$, $c = -2$.

    $$\Pi_1 = Dρ V^2 c^2 ∝ C_D \quad (Drag Coefficient)$$
  • $\Pi_2 = ρ^a V^b c^c μ \implies [M^0 L^0 T^0] = [ML-3]^a [LT-1]^b [L]^c [ML-1T-1]$

    Solving yields $a = -1$, $b = -1$, $c = -1$.

    $$\Pi_2 = μρ V c \implies 1\Pi_2 = ρ V cμ = Re \quad (Reynolds Number)$$
  • $\Pi_3 = ρ^a V^b c^c a \implies [M^0 L^0 T^0] = [ML-3]^a [LT-1]^b [L]^c [LT-1]$

    Solving yields $a = 0$, $b = -1$, $c = 0$.

    $$\Pi_3 = aV \implies 1\Pi_3 = Va = M \quad (Mach Number)$$

Thus, the dimensional functional relationship simplifies to a far more powerful nondimensional counterpart:

$$ C_D = φ(Re, M) $$

3. Boundary Layer Separation and Vorticity

Dynamic similarity is critical when predicting aerodynamic stall, which is triggered by boundary layer separation. In a viscous flow over a curved airfoil, fluid particles close to the solid wall are slowed down by viscous shear stresses. As the flow travels past the point of maximum thickness, it experiences an adverse pressure gradient ($∂ p / ∂ x > 0$).

The fluid velocity within the boundary layer eventually drops to zero, and the flow reverses direction. The point of separation is mathematically defined by the condition where the velocity gradient at the wall vanishes:

$$ \left. ∂ u∂ y |y=0 = 0 $$

Because the local viscous shear stress is $τ_w = μ ( ∂ u∂ y )y=0$, the separation point is characterized by zero wall shear stress ($τ_w = 0$). Matching the Reynolds number $Re$ between model and prototype ensures that the boundary layer growth, transition from laminar to turbulent flow, and the exact physical location of this separation point are matched precisely.

4. Potential Flow Idealizations and Superpositions

In regions outside the boundary layer, the fluid can often be assumed to be inviscid ($μ = 0$) and irrotational ($\nabla × \vec{V} = 0$). Under these conditions, the flow is governed by Laplace's equation for velocity potential ($\nabla^2 φ = 0$). We can model complex flow fields around aerodynamic shapes using the superposition of elementary potential flows:

  • Uniform Flow: $φ = U_∞ x$
  • Line Source (strength $\Lambda$): $φ = \Lambda2π \ln r$
  • Line Doublet (strength $\kappa$): $φ = \kappa \cosθr$
  • Point Vortex (strength $\Gamma$): $ψ = \Gamma2π \ln r$

For example, the superposition of a uniform flow and a doublet yields the inviscid flow around a circular cylinder. For potential flows, geometric similarity is the only absolute requirement for kinematic similarity, as viscous forces are neglected ($Re \to ∞$).

3. Memorize Important Facts (10 min)

To excel in UPSC ASO and GATE examinations, you must commit these fundamental formulas and dimensionless ratios to memory:

  • Reynolds Number ($Re$): Ratios of inertial to viscous forces. $$ Re = Inertial ForceViscous Force = ρ V Lμ = V Lν \quad [Dimensionless] $$
  • Froude Number ($Fr$): Ratios of inertial to gravitational forces (crucial for surface-wave resistance on ships/seaplanes). $$ Fr = Inertial ForceGravitational Force = V√g L \quad [Dimensionless] $$
  • Mach Number ($M$): Ratios of inertial to compressibility forces. $$ M = Flow VelocitySpeed of Sound = Va = V√γ R T \quad [Dimensionless] $$
  • Weber Number ($We$): Ratios of inertial to surface tension forces (critical for spray atomization, fuel droplets). $$ We = Inertial ForceSurface Tension Force = ρ V^2 Lσ \quad [Dimensionless] $$
  • Strouhal Number ($St$): Dimensionless frequency representing unsteady, oscillating flow fields (e.g., vortex shedding, wing flutter). $$ St = f LV \quad [Dimensionless] $$
  • Boundary Layer Separation Condition: $$ τ_w = \left. μ ∂ u∂ y |y=0 = 0 \quad (Separation onset) $$
  • Euler Number ($Eu$): Ratios of pressure forces to inertial forces. $$ Eu = Δ pρ V^2 \quad [Dimensionless] $$

4. Aviation Application (5–10 min)

Aviation & Aircraft Performance Applications

Similarity laws form the operational foundation of aviation research, wind tunnel testing, and computational fluid dynamics (CFD).

1. Wind Tunnel Testing & Scaling Conflicts

To test a 1:10 scale model of an aircraft designed to fly at $V_p = 100 m/s$ in standard atmospheric air, we must ideally match both the Reynolds number ($Re$) and the Mach number ($M$) to ensure dynamic similarity:

$$ Re_m = Re_p \implies ρ_m V_m L_mμ_m = ρ_p V_p L_pμ_p $$ $$ M_m = M_p \implies V_ma_m = V_pa_p $$

If we use the same working fluid (air at standard conditions, meaning $ρ, μ$, and $a$ are constant):

  • Mach matching demands: $V_m = V_p = 100 m/s$.
  • Reynolds matching demands: $V_m = V_p ( L_pL_m ) = 100 × 10 = 1000 m/s$.

This creates an impossible conflict! If we run the wind tunnel at $1000 m/s$ (Mach 3), we violate Mach matching and introduce massive shock waves not present in the subsonic prototype flow. If we run it at $100 m/s$, our Reynolds number is 10 times too small, meaning the boundary layer on the model will be thick, laminar, and separate far too early, giving an artificially high drag ($C_D$) and lower stall angle of attack.

Aviation Solutions:

  1. Variable Density / Pressurized Wind Tunnels: Increase the pressure in the wind tunnel (which increases density $ρ_m$) by a factor of 10. This allows us to match $Re$ at $V_m = 100 m/s$ because $ρ_m = 10 ρ_p$.
  2. Cryogenic Wind Tunnels: Lowering the temperature of the gaseous nitrogen working fluid reduces viscosity ($μ_m$) and increases density ($ρ_m$), dramatically increasing $Re_m$ without requiring transonic speeds.
  3. Boundary Layer Tripping: If $Re$ matching is impossible, engineers place grit or "trip strips" near the model's leading edge to force transition to a turbulent boundary layer, mimicking the high-$Re$ prototype boundary layer characteristics.

2. Hydrodynamics of Seaplanes & Froude-Reynolds Scaling

For seaplanes taking off from water, both gravitational forces (wave resistance, Froude scaling) and viscous forces (skin friction, Reynolds scaling) are significant. Since the model must operate in water, matching both $Re$ and $Fr$ requires:

$$ V_r = √L_r \quad (Froude Matching) $$ $$ V_r = 1L_r \quad (Reynolds Matching assuming constant viscosity) $$

This is mathematically impossible unless $L_r = 1$ (full-scale testing). Instead, engineers run model tests matching the Froude number to capture wave-making physics, and then analytically correct the drag values using flat-plate skin friction formulas (such as the ITTC correlation line) to account for the Reynolds mismatch.

5. UPSC ASO Focus (10 min)

UPSC ASO Key Facts & Analytical Traps

  • The "Scale Effect" Trap: UPSC loves to ask why scale effects occur. Scale effects are discrepancies that arise when perfect dynamic similarity cannot be achieved because we cannot match all relevant dimensionless numbers ($Re$, $Fr$, $M$) simultaneously.
  • Discharge ($Q$) Scaling under Froude Law: $$ Q_r = A_r V_r = L_r^2 · √L_r = L_r2.5 $$ For a 1:100 scale model of a spillway or seaplane hull, the discharge ratio is $1002.5 = 100,000$. A very common numerical trap is using $Q_r = L_r^3$ (which is only true if velocity scales linearly with length, violating Froude!).
  • Power ($P$) Scaling under Froude Law: $$ P = Force × Velocity = (ρ L^3 g) × √g L ∝ L3.5 $$ $$ P_r = L_r3.5 $$
  • Power ($P$) Scaling under Reynolds Law: $$ P = Force × Velocity = (τ A) × V = ( μ VL L^2 ) V = μ L V^2 $$ $$ P_r = μ_r L_r V_r^2 $$
  • Transition Criterion: For boundary layers over flat plates, the transition from laminar to turbulent flow occurs at a critical Reynolds number of $Rex,crit ≈ 5 × 10^5$.
  • Doublet Strength and Cylinder Radius: To model a cylinder of radius $R$ in a uniform flow $U_∞$, the strength of the doublet $\kappa$ must be exactly: $$ \kappa = U_∞ R^2 $$

8. Error Analysis & Common Traps (5 min)

Common Student Mistakes & Conceptual Traps

  • Mistake 1: The "Discharge Scaling" Fallacy

    Many students instinctively scale discharge as $Q_r = L_r^3$, assuming it's a volume-like quantity per unit time. However, under Froude similarity, velocity scales as $V_r = √L_r$. Therefore, discharge must scale as:

    $$ Q_r = A_r V_r = L_r^2 · L_r0.5 = L_r2.5 $$

    Using $L_r^3$ instead of $L_r2.5$ in spillway problems yields massive calculation errors.

  • Mistake 2: Failing to Match repeating variable criteria in Buckingham Pi

    Students often choose repeating variables that can themselves form a dimensionless group (e.g., choosing $V$, $g$, and $L$, which can be combined to form the Froude number $V/√gL$). This violates the fundamental rule of the Buckingham Pi theorem and leads to circular, unsolvable algebra.

  • Mistake 3: Neglecting scale limits of potential flow superpositions

    Assuming that a doublet-and-source potential flow model perfectly represents boundary layer separation. Potential flow cannot model flow separation because viscosity is entirely neglected ($μ = 0 \implies Re \to ∞$). Separation requires viscous-inviscid interaction and an adverse pressure gradient.

  • Mistake 4: Incorrect Strouhal Number scaling with RPM

    For rotating machinery, students often confuse frequency $f$ [Hz] with rotational speed $N$ [rpm or rad/s]. Remember that $f = N/60$ when converting rpm to Hz before substituting into $St = f L / V$.
