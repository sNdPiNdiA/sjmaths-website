Hagen-Poiseuille flow in pipes

Fluid Mechanics Machinery Fluid Mechanics & Machinery Phase 1: Foundations 90-120 min Master Class

1. Understand (From Absolute Zero, 5–10 min)

Imagine pushing a highly viscous fluid, like honey or heavy engine oil, through a thin straw. You have to push remarkably hard to get a steady stream flowing. If you look closely, the fluid touching the inner surface of the straw is completely stationary, while the fluid right along the central axis of the straw moves the fastest. This specific type of flow—characterized by steady, laminar, and viscous motion of an incompressible fluid through a straight, circular pipe of constant cross-section—is known as Hagen-Poiseuille flow (often simply called Poiseuille flow).

Named after German civil engineer Gotthilf Hagen (who conducted experiments in 1839) and French physician Jean Léonard Marie Poiseuille (who independently derived it in 1840 to study blood flow through capillaries), this flow represents one of the few exact analytical solutions to the formidable Navier-Stokes equations.

Physical Intuition & Core Concepts

At the microscopic level, the fluid can be envisioned as a series of infinitely thin concentric cylindrical shells sliding past one another. The outermost shell adheres perfectly to the pipe wall due to the attractive molecular forces between the fluid and the solid boundary. This physical reality is known as the no-slip boundary condition. As we move radially inward toward the pipe centerline, each successive fluid shell exerts a dragging shear force on its neighbor, slowing it down. This molecular friction is governed by the fluid's dynamic viscosity ($μ$).

Because of this shear interaction, the velocity distribution across the pipe cross-section is not uniform; instead, it forms a symmetric, parabolic profile. The maximum velocity is reached precisely at the central axis, while the velocity drops to zero at the pipe walls. To drive this viscous flow against the continuous drag of internal shear forces, a continuous drop in pressure must exist along the length of the pipe. This spatial pressure drop is the driving pressure gradient ($-dpdx$).

Standard UPSC Terminology & Units

  • Volumetric Flow Rate ($Q$): The volume of fluid passing through a given cross-section per unit time. Measured in cubic meters per second ($m^3/s$).
  • Dynamic Viscosity ($μ$): A measure of a fluid's internal resistance to gradual deformation by shear or tensile stress. Measured in Pascal-seconds ($Pa·s$), equivalent to $kg/(m·s)$, or Poise ($P$), where $1 Pa·s = 10 Poise$.
  • Kinematic Viscosity ($ν$): The ratio of dynamic viscosity to fluid density ($ν = μρ$). Measured in square meters per second ($m^2/s$) or Stokes ($St$), where $1 St = 10-4 m^2/s$.
  • Wall Shear Stress ($τ_w$): The drag force per unit area exerted by the fluid on the pipe wall, pointing in the direction of the flow. Measured in Newtons per square meter ($N/m^2$ or $Pa$).
  • Pressure Gradient ($dpdx$): The rate of change of pressure with respect to distance along the pipe axis. Measured in Pascals per meter ($Pa/m$).
  • Reynolds Number ($Re$): A dimensionless parameter representing the ratio of inertial forces to viscous forces: $$ Re = ρ Vavg Dμ $$ For pipe flow, the transition from laminar to turbulent flow typically begins at a critical Reynolds number of $Recrit ≈ 2300$. Hagen-Poiseuille equations are strictly valid only for laminar flow regimes ($Re < 2300$).

2. Learn the Concept (Complete Theoretical Foundation & Derivations, 20–25 min)

To establish the complete mathematical foundation of Hagen-Poiseuille flow, we begin with the fundamental conservation laws of fluid mechanics applied to an incompressible, Newtonian fluid flowing through a horizontal, rigid, circular pipe of constant radius $R$ (and diameter $D = 2R$).

1. Fundamental Physical Assumptions

To yield an exact analytical solution, we enforce the following physical constraints:

  1. Steady State: The flow properties at any spatial coordinate do not change with time, meaning $∂∂ t = 0$ for all variables.
  2. Incompressible Flow: The fluid density $ρ$ remains constant throughout the field, meaning $\nabla · \vec{\mathbf{v}} = 0$.
  3. Laminar Flow: The fluid flows in parallel, concentric layers without lateral mixing ($Re < 2300$).
  4. Fully Developed Flow: The velocity profile does not change along the direction of flow ($x$). Thus, the axial velocity $u$ is a function only of the radial coordinate $r$: $u = u(r)$, and $∂ u∂ x = 0$.
  5. Axisymmetric Flow: The flow properties are symmetric about the central longitudinal axis, which means there is no variation in the azimuthal direction ($∂∂ θ = 0$), and the azimuthal velocity component is zero ($v_θ = 0$).
  6. Parallel Flow: The radial velocity component is zero ($v_r = 0$).

2. The Governing Conservation Equations

We analyze the flow in cylindrical coordinates $(r, θ, x)$, where $r$ is the radial distance from the centerline, $θ$ is the azimuthal angle, and $x$ is the axial coordinate along the pipe length.

A. Conservation of Mass (The Continuity Equation)

In cylindrical coordinates, the continuity equation for an incompressible fluid is written as:

$$ 1r∂∂ r(r v_r) + 1r∂ v_θ∂ θ + ∂ u∂ x = 0 $$

Applying the assumptions of axisymmetric flow ($v_θ = 0$) and parallel/fully developed flow ($∂ u∂ x = 0$), the continuity equation reduces directly to:

$$ 1r∂∂ r(r v_r) = 0 \implies r v_r = C $$

Since the radial velocity must be zero at the solid pipe wall ($v_r = 0$ at $r = R$) due to the impermeability condition, the constant $C$ must be zero. Consequently, $v_r = 0$ everywhere across the pipe cross-section.

B. Conservation of Momentum (Navier-Stokes Equations)

Now, let us examine the Navier-Stokes momentum equations in cylindrical coordinates. Under our established conditions ($v_r = 0$, $v_θ = 0$, $∂∂ t = 0$, $∂∂ θ = 0$, and $∂ u∂ x = 0$), we evaluate the three directional components:

Radial ($r$-direction) Momentum:

$$ 0 = -∂ p∂ r + ρ g_r $$

Assuming gravity forces are negligible or acting only in the vertical direction (which can be incorporated into a modified piezometric pressure), we find:

$$ ∂ p∂ r = 0 $$

This tells us that pressure is uniform across any given pipe cross-section; pressure depends solely on the axial coordinate $x$: $p = p(x)$.

Axial ($x$-direction) Momentum:

$$ ρ ( ∂ u∂ t + v_r ∂ u∂ r + v_θr ∂ u∂ θ + u ∂ u∂ x ) = -∂ p∂ x + μ [ 1r∂∂ r(r ∂ u∂ r) + 1r^2∂^2 u∂ θ^2 + ∂^2 u∂ x^2 ] + ρ g_x $$

Substituting all our kinematic simplifications ($∂ u∂ t = 0$, $v_r = 0$, $v_θ = 0$, $∂ u∂ x = 0$, $∂^2 u∂ θ^2 = 0$, $∂^2 u∂ x^2 = 0$) and neglecting axial gravity effects ($g_x = 0$), we obtain the clean, governing ordinary differential equation for Hagen-Poiseuille flow:

$$ 1rddr(r dudr) = 1μdpdx $$

3. Step-by-Step Derivation of the Velocity Field

To solve the governing differential equation, we first analyze its mathematical structure. The left-hand side, $1rddr(r dudr)$, is a function only of the radial coordinate $r$. The right-hand side, $1μdpdx$, is a function only of the axial coordinate $x$ (since pressure only varies along $x$).

For a function of $r$ to be identically equal to a function of $x$ for all values of $r$ and $x$, both sides must equal a constant. Let this constant pressure gradient be defined as:

$$ dpdx = constant = -Δ PL $$

where $Δ P = P_1 - P_2$ is the pressure drop over a length $L$ of the pipe. The negative sign represents a physical drop in pressure along the direction of flow. Substituting this constant pressure gradient, our equation becomes:

$$ ddr(r dudr) = rμ(dpdx) $$

We integrate this ordinary differential equation with respect to $r$:

$$ r dudr = r^22μ(dpdx) + C_1 $$

Dividing both sides by $r$ gives the expression for the shear strain rate:

$$ dudr = r2μ(dpdx) + C_1r $$

To determine the integration constant $C_1$, we look at the physical centerline of the pipe, $r = 0$. At this central axis, the term $C_1r$ would tend to infinity. Because the velocity gradient must remain physically finite at the centerline of a physical pipe, we must set:

$$ C_1 = 0 $$

This leaves us with:

$$ dudr = r2μ(dpdx) $$

Now, we integrate a second time with respect to $r$:

$$ u(r) = r^24μ(dpdx) + C_2 $$

To determine the second integration constant $C_2$, we apply the physical no-slip boundary condition at the solid pipe wall, which states that the fluid velocity is zero at $r = R$:

$$ u(R) = 0 \implies R^24μ(dpdx) + C_2 = 0 \implies C_2 = -R^24μ(dpdx) $$

Substituting $C_2$ back into our equation yields the exact analytical velocity distribution:

$$ u(r) = -14μ(dpdx) (R^2 - r^2) $$

By expressing the pressure gradient in terms of pressure drop over length $L$ ($dpdx = -Δ PL$), we arrive at the classic parabolic velocity equation:

$$ u(r) = Δ P4μ L ( R^2 - r^2 ) = umax [ 1 - (rR)^2 ] $$

4. Derived Kinematic and Dynamic Variables

A. Maximum Velocity ($umax$)

The maximum flow velocity occurs along the pipe centerline ($r = 0$):

$$ umax = Δ P R^24μ L = Δ P D^216μ L $$

B. Average (Mean) Velocity ($Vavg$)

The average velocity is defined by integrating the velocity profile over the entire cross-sectional area $A = π R^2$ and dividing by the area:

$$ Vavg = 1π R^2 ∫0R u(r) · (2π r \, dr) = 2R^2 ∫0R umax [ 1 - (rR)^2 ] r \, dr $$

Let us evaluate the integral:

$$ ∫0R ( r - r^3R^2 ) dr = [ r^22 - r^44R^2 ]0R = R^22 - R^24 = R^24 $$

Substituting this back into the average velocity expression:

$$ Vavg = 2R^2 · umax · R^24 = 12 umax $$

This reveals a key mathematical property of Hagen-Poiseuille flow: the average velocity is exactly half of the maximum velocity.

$$ Vavg = Δ P R^28μ L = Δ P D^232μ L $$

C. Volumetric Flow Rate ($Q$) (Poiseuille's Law)

Multiplying the average velocity by the cross-sectional area yields the total volumetric flow rate:

$$ Q = Vavg · A = (Δ P D^232μ L) · (π D^24) = π Δ P D^4128μ L = π Δ P R^48μ L $$

This is the celebrated Hagen-Poiseuille Law. Notice that the volumetric flow rate is inversely proportional to dynamic viscosity and the pipe length, but is directly proportional to the fourth power of the pipe radius ($Q ∝ R^4$). A doubling of the pipe radius results in a sixteenfold ($2^4 = 16$) increase in flow rate for the same pressure drop.

D. Shear Stress Distribution ($τ(r)$) and Wall Shear Stress ($τ_w$)

According to Newton's law of viscosity, the shear stress in a pipe flow is given by:

$$ τ = -μ dudr $$

The negative sign accounts for the coordinate convention (where $r$ is measured outward from the center, so velocity decreases as $r$ increases). Taking the derivative of our velocity profile with respect to $r$:

$$ dudr = ddr [ Δ P4μ L (R^2 - r^2) ] = -Δ P r2μ L $$

Substituting this derivative back into Newton's law of viscosity gives:

$$ τ(r) = -μ ( -Δ P r2μ L ) = Δ P2L r $$

This shows that the shear stress is zero at the centerline ($r=0$) and increases linearly to its maximum value at the solid wall ($r=R$):

$$ τ_w = Δ P R2L = Δ P D4L $$

E. Darcy Friction Factor ($f$) and Head Loss ($h_f$)

By equating the wall shear force to the pressure drop force acting on a fluid element of length $L$ and diameter $D$:

$$ Δ P · (π D^24) = τ_w · (π D L) \implies Δ P = 4 τ_w LD $$

The head loss ($h_f$) due to friction is defined as:

$$ h_f = Δ Pρ g = 32 μ L Vavgρ g D^2 $$

Comparing this with the empirical Darcy-Weisbach equation:

$$ h_f = f LD Vavg^22g $$

We solve for the Darcy friction factor ($f$):

$$ f LD Vavg^22g = 32 μ L Vavgρ g D^2 \implies f = 64 μρ Vavg D = 64Re $$

This confirms that for laminar flow, the Darcy friction factor depends solely on the Reynolds number:

$$ f = 64Re \quad (Darcy friction factor) $$

Note that the Fanning friction factor ($C_f$), often preferred in chemical and aerodynamic disciplines, is defined as $C_f = τ_w12ρ Vavg^2 = f4 = 16Re$.

3. Memorize Important Facts (10 min)

  • Core Velocity Profile & Maximum Value: $u(r) = umax[1 - (rR)^2]$ where $umax = 2Vavg = Δ P R^24μ L$ (SI unit: $m/s$).
  • Hagen-Poiseuille Law (Discharge): $Q = π Δ P D^4128 μ L = π Δ P R^48 μ L$ (SI unit: $m^3/s$).
  • Wall Shear Stress: $τ_w = Δ P D4L = 8μ VavgD$ (SI unit: $N/m^2$ or $Pa$). Note that shear stress varies linearly across the pipe radius: $τ(r) = τ_w (rR)$.
  • Laminar Friction Factors: Darcy friction factor $f = 64Re$; Fanning friction factor $C_f = 16Re$.
  • Kinetic & Momentum Correction Factors: Kinetic energy correction factor $α = 2.0$; Momentum correction factor $β = 43 ≈ 1.33$ (for fully developed laminar flow).
  • Critical Reynolds Number for Pipe Flow: $Recrit = ρ V Dμ ≈ 2300$.

4. Aviation Application (5–10 min)

The principles of Hagen-Poiseuille flow are vital to aerospace systems, where precise fluid transport, instrument calibration, and boundary layer behavior are safety-critical. Key aviation applications include:

1. Aircraft Pitot-Static Systems and Pressure Transmission Lines

The airspeed indicator, altimeter, and vertical speed indicator (VSI) in aircraft rely on pressure measurements from the external Pitot tube and static ports. These pressures are transmitted to the instruments via long, narrow pneumatic lines. Since air is viscous, any rapid changes in flight speed or altitude require the air inside these lines to flow.

Because the lines are narrow, this flow behaves as a laminar Poiseuille flow. The viscous resistance within the tubing causes a slight delay in pressure equalization, known as instrument lag. Aeronautical engineers use the Hagen-Poiseuille relationship to design tubing systems that minimize this lag, ensuring real-time flight data remains accurate during high-rate climbs or descents.

2. Hydraulic Systems for Flight Control Actuators

Primary flight control surfaces (ailerons, elevators, and rudders) and landing gear systems on modern commercial and military aircraft are positioned by high-pressure hydraulic actuators. These systems transport hydraulic fluids through extensive piping networks at high pressures.

At low flight temperatures (such as $-50^\circC$ at cruising altitudes), the hydraulic fluid's dynamic viscosity ($μ$) increases significantly. According to the Hagen-Poiseuille equation, a higher viscosity results in a much larger pressure drop ($Δ P = 128 μ L Qπ D^4$) along the hydraulic lines. To prevent control responsiveness degradation or sluggish landing gear deployment, aerospace engineers must size hydraulic lines and select fluid formulations to handle these low-temperature viscous pressure losses.

3. Aircraft Engine Lubrication Channels

Gas turbine and piston aircraft engines rely on continuous oil circulation through narrow internal channels to lubricate and cool high-speed bearings. If the oil flow rate drops, bearings can overheat and seize, causing critical engine failure. Because these lubricating channels are narrow and the engine oil is highly viscous, the flow is laminar. Designers apply the Hagen-Poiseuille law to size oil pump capacities and internal galley dimensions, ensuring adequate oil flow ($Q ∝ D^4$) reaches critical bearings under all operating conditions.

5. UPSC ASO Focus (10 min)

  • The $Q ∝ D^4$ Scale Dependency: UPSC numerical questions frequently test how a change in pipe diameter affects the volumetric flow rate or the pressure drop. Memorize that for a constant pressure drop, $Q ∝ D^4$, and for a constant flow rate, $Δ P ∝ D-4$.
  • Darcy vs. Fanning Friction Factors: A classic exam trap is confusing the Darcy friction factor ($f = 64Re$) with the Fanning friction factor ($C_f = 16Re$). Always check which friction factor is being asked or provided. In mechanical/civil engineering questions, "friction factor" almost always refers to Darcy, whereas aerodynamicists sometimes use Fanning.
  • Velocity Profiling and Location Traps: Be prepared to find the radial position $r$ where the local velocity equals the average velocity. $$ u(r) = Vavg \implies umax[1 - (rR)^2] = 12umax \implies 1 - (rR)^2 = 0.5 \implies r = R√2 ≈ 0.707R $$ This is a very common conceptual test question.
  • Rotationality of the Flow Field: Although Hagen-Poiseuille flow is steady and parallel, it is highly rotational due to the strong viscous shear. The vorticity vector is non-zero everywhere except at the centerline ($r=0$): $$ ω_θ = (∂ v_r∂ x - ∂ u∂ r) = -∂ u∂ r = Δ P2μ Lr \neq 0 $$ Applying the standard irrotational Bernoulli equation between two points in this flow is fundamentally incorrect due to this strong viscous shear and energy dissipation.

6. Practice Questions (30 Exhaustive UPSC ASO MCQs)

Q1. In a fully developed laminar flow through a circular pipe of radius $R$, the local velocity at a radial distance $r = R2$ is what fraction of the maximum velocity?
A) $0.50$
B) $0.75$
C) $0.25$
D) $0.866$
The velocity distribution in a fully developed circular pipe flow is parabolic, given by: $$ u(r) = umax [ 1 - (rR)^2 ] $$ Substituting $r = R2$: $$ u(R2) = umax [ 1 - (R/2R)^2 ] = umax [ 1 - 14 ] = 0.75 \, umax $$ Thus, the local velocity is $75%$ (or $0.75$) of the maximum velocity.
Q2. If the diameter of a pipe carrying a viscous fluid under laminar conditions is halved while maintaining the same pressure drop and pipe length, the flow rate decreases by a factor of:
A) 2
B) 4
C) 8
D) 16
According to the Hagen-Poiseuille Law, the discharge is given by: $$ Q = π Δ P D^4128 μ L $$ Thus, flow rate $Q$ is proportional to the fourth power of the diameter ($Q ∝ D^4$). If the diameter is halved ($D_2 = D_1/2$): $$ Q_2 ∝ (D_12)^4 = D_1^416 \implies Q_2 = Q_116 $$ The flow rate decreases by a factor of 16.
Q3. At what radial distance from the centerline of a circular pipe of radius $R$ is the local velocity of a fully developed laminar flow exactly equal to its average velocity?
A) $0.500 \, R$
B) $0.707 \, R$
C) $0.866 \, R$
D) $0.333 \, R$
We know that for laminar flow in circular pipes, the average velocity $Vavg = 0.5 \, umax$. We set the local velocity equal to $Vavg$: $$ u(r) = Vavg \implies umax [ 1 - (rR)^2 ] = 12 umax $$ $$ 1 - (rR)^2 = 12 \implies (rR)^2 = 12 \implies r = R√2 ≈ 0.707 \, R $$
Q4. In a Hagen-Poiseuille flow system, the shear stress variation across the radial direction of the pipe is:
A) Parabolic, with maximum at the wall and zero at the center
B) Linear, with maximum at the wall and zero at the center
C) Constant across the entire cross section
D) Linear, with zero at the wall and maximum at the center
The shear stress distribution is derived as: $$ τ(r) = Δ P2L r $$ This is a linear function of radial coordinate $r$. At the centerline ($r=0$), $τ = 0$. At the wall ($r=R$), $τ = τ_w = Δ P R2L$ (maximum value).
Q5. An oil of dynamic viscosity $0.2 Pa·s$ flows through a $50 mm$ diameter pipe of length $100 m$. If the flow rate is $2 liters/sec$, what is the pressure drop over this length? (Assume fully developed laminar flow).
A) $130.4 kPa$
B) $260.8 kPa$
C) $521.6 kPa$
D) $65.2 kPa$
Given values: $μ = 0.2 Pa·s$, $D = 0.05 m$, $L = 100 m$, $Q = 2 liters/s = 0.002 m^3/s$. Using Poiseuille's formula for pressure drop: $$ Δ P = 128 μ L Qπ D^4 $$ $$ Δ P = 128 × 0.2 × 100 × 0.002π × (0.05)^4 $$ $$ Δ P = 5.12π × 6.25 × 10-6 = 5.121.9635 × 10-5 ≈ 260810 Pa ≈ 260.8 kPa $$
Q6. What is the value of the momentum correction factor ($β$) for fully developed laminar flow in a circular pipe?
A) $1.00$
B) $1.33$
C) $1.50$
D) $2.00$
The momentum correction factor is defined as: $$ β = 1A ∫_A (uVavg)^2 dA $$ For a circular pipe, $dA = 2π r dr$, and $u(r) = 2 Vavg [ 1 - (rR)^2 ]$. $$ β = 1π R^2 ∫_0^R 4 Vavg^2 [ 1 - (rR)^2 ]^2 2π r drVavg^2 = 8 ∫_0^1 (1-\eta^2)^2 \eta d\eta \quad (where \eta = r/R) $$ $$ β = 8 [ \eta^22 - 2\eta^44 + \eta^66 ]_0^1 = 8 [ 12 - 12 + 16 ] = 86 = 43 ≈ 1.33 $$
Q7. What is the value of the kinetic energy correction factor ($α$) for fully developed laminar flow in a circular pipe?
A) $1.33$
B) $1.50$
C) $2.00$
D) $1.00$
The kinetic energy correction factor is defined as: $$ α = 1A ∫_A (uVavg)^3 dA $$ Substituting $u(r) = 2 Vavg [1 - (r/R)^2]$ and $dA = 2π r dr$: $$ α = 1π R^2 ∫_0^R 8 Vavg^3 [ 1 - (rR)^2 ]^3 2π r drVavg^3 = 16 ∫_0^1 (1-\eta^2)^3 \eta d\eta \quad (where \eta = r/R) $$ Using substitution $w = 1-\eta^2$, $dw = -2\eta d\eta$: $$ α = 16 ∫_1^0 w^3 (-dw2) = 8 ∫_0^1 w^3 dw = 8 [ w^44 ]_0^1 = 84 = 2.0 $$
Q8. For steady laminar flow of a fluid in a circular pipe, how does the wall shear stress ($τ_w$) scale with the average flow velocity ($Vavg$)?
A) $τ_w ∝ Vavg^2$
B) $τ_w ∝ Vavg$
C) $τ_w ∝ Vavg1/2$
D) $τ_w$ is independent of $Vavg$
The wall shear stress is related to the pressure drop and velocity as: $$ τ_w = Δ P D4L $$ Since $Δ P = 32 μ L VavgD^2$, substituting this in gives: $$ τ_w = (32 μ L VavgD^2) D4L = 8 μ VavgD $$ Therefore, the wall shear stress is directly proportional to the average velocity ($τ_w ∝ Vavg$).
Q9. The Darcy friction factor ($f$) for fully developed laminar pipe flow is given by $f = 64Re$. The corresponding Fanning friction factor ($C_f$) is:
A) $16Re$
B) $32Re$
C) $8Re$
D) $4Re$
By definition, the Darcy friction factor ($f$) is four times the Fanning friction factor ($C_f$): $$ f = 4 C_f $$ Given $f = 64Re$: $$ C_f = f4 = 64/Re4 = 16Re $$
Q10. If the dynamic viscosity of a fluid flowing laminarly in a pipe is doubled while keeping the pressure gradient constant, the volume flow rate will:
A) Be doubled
B) Be halved
C) Remain unchanged
D) Decrease by a factor of 4
From the Hagen-Poiseuille Law: $$ Q = π R^4 Δ P8 μ L = π R^48μ ( -dpdx ) $$ Thus, flow rate $Q$ is inversely proportional to dynamic viscosity ($Q ∝ 1/μ$). If viscosity $μ$ is doubled, the flow rate is halved.
Q11. The pressure drop per unit length ($Δ P/L$) in a horizontal pipe of $10 cm$ diameter is $400 Pa/m$. If the fluid's dynamic viscosity is $0.1 Pa·s$, the maximum velocity of the flow is:
A) $2.5 m/s$
B) $5.0 m/s$
C) $10.0 m/s$
D) $1.25 m/s$
The maximum velocity of a laminar flow is: $$ umax = Δ P R^24μ L = 14μ (Δ PL) R^2 $$ Given: $D = 10 cm \implies R = 5 cm = 0.05 m$, $Δ PL = 400 Pa/m$, and $μ = 0.1 Pa·s$. $$ umax = 14 × 0.1 × 400 × (0.05)^2 $$ $$ umax = 400 × 0.00250.4 = 1.00.4 = 2.5 m/s $$
Q12. The power required to pump a viscous fluid through a pipe of length $L$ at a constant flow rate $Q$ under laminar flow conditions is proportional to:
A) $D-2$
B) $D-4$
C) $D4$
D) $D-1$
The power ($Ppower$) required to maintain the flow is given by: $$ Ppower = Q · Δ P $$ Under laminar flow conditions, the pressure drop is: $$ Δ P = 128 μ L Qπ D^4 $$ Substituting this into the power equation: $$ Ppower = Q · ( 128 μ L Qπ D^4 ) = 128 μ L Q^2π D^4 $$ Thus, for a constant flow rate $Q$, the power required is proportional to $D-4$.
Q13. In fully developed laminar pipe flow, the shear stress on the fluid at $r = R/3$ is what fraction of the wall shear stress ($τ_w$)?
A) $1/9$
B) $1/3$
C) $2/3$
D) $0$
The shear stress distribution is linear with radial coordinate $r$: $$ τ(r) = τ_w (rR) $$ Substituting $r = R/3$: $$ τ(R/3) = τ_w (R/3R) = 13 τ_w $$
Q14. What is the velocity gradient ($du/dr$) at the wall ($r = R$) of a circular pipe carrying a fully developed laminar flow with average velocity $Vavg$?
A) $-4 VavgR$
B) $-2 VavgR$
C) $-8 VavgR$
D) $0$
The velocity profile is: $$ u(r) = 2 Vavg [ 1 - (rR)^2 ] $$ Differentiating with respect to $r$: $$ dudr = 2 Vavg [ - 2rR^2 ] = -4 Vavg rR^2 $$ At the wall ($r = R$): $$ \left.dudr|r=R = -4 Vavg RR^2 = -4 VavgR $$
Q15. The entrance length ($L_e$) for a laminar flow of Reynolds number $Re$ in a pipe of diameter $D$ is approximately given by which empirical relation?
A) $0.05 · Re · D$
B) $4.4 · Re1/2 · D$
C) $0.35 · Re · D$
D) $10 · D$
For laminar flow through a circular pipe, the hydrodynamic entrance length ($L_e$), over which the boundary layer grows to fill the entire pipe and the flow becomes fully developed, is empirically given by: $$ L_eD ≈ 0.05 · Re \implies L_e ≈ 0.05 · Re · D $$
Q16. If a fluid flows through an inclined pipe of diameter $D$ and length $L$, the driving parameter for the Hagen-Poiseuille equation is modified by replacing the pressure drop $Δ P$ with the piezometric pressure drop $Δ P^*$. This term is defined as:
A) $Δ P + ρ g L \sin θ$
B) $Δ P - ρ g L \sin θ$
C) $Δ P ± ρ g L \sin θ$ (depending on flow direction)
D) $Δ P + ρ L \sin θ$
When a pipe is inclined at an angle $θ$ to the horizontal, gravity affects the flow. The active driving force is the change in piezometric pressure: $$ P^* = p + ρ g z $$ Therefore, the effective pressure drop driving the flow is: $$ Δ P^* = Δ p ± ρ g L \sinθ $$ The sign depends on whether the flow is uphill (gravity opposes flow, requiring more pressure) or downhill (gravity assists flow).
Q17. In a capillary-tube viscometer, a fluid of density $800 kg/m^3$ flows through a tube of length $1 m$ and diameter $2 mm$ under a head of $0.5 m$ of the same fluid. If the measured flow rate is $5 cm^3/s$, what is the dynamic viscosity of the fluid? (Take $g = 9.81 m/s^2$).
A) $0.0031 Pa·s$
B) $0.0123 Pa·s$
C) $0.0015 Pa·s$

8. Error Analysis & Common Traps (5 min)

To succeed in the UPSC ASO exam, candidates must identify and avoid several common conceptual and mathematical traps associated with Hagen-Poiseuille flow:

1. Confusing Pipe Radius ($R$) with Pipe Diameter ($D$) in Formulas

This is the most common source of calculation errors. The velocity distribution is defined in terms of radius $R$: $$ u(r) = umax[1 - (rR)^2] $$ However, volumetric discharge and pressure drop equations are often written using diameter $D$: $$ Q = π Δ P D^4128μ L \quad vs. \quad Q = π Δ P R^48μ L $$ Mixing up these terms can lead to errors by factors of $2$, $4$, $8$, or even $16$ ($2^4$). Always double-check which variable ($R$ or $D$) is requested or provided in the problem statement.

2. Misapplying the Standard Bernoulli Equation to Viscous Flow

The classical Bernoulli equation ($pρ g + V^22g + z = constant$) assumes **frictionless (inviscid), irrotational flow**. Because Hagen-Poiseuille flow is highly viscous, mechanical energy is continuously dissipated as heat. Applying the frictionless Bernoulli equation to calculate pressure drops in viscous pipe flow is incorrect and will yield wrong answers. Instead, you must use the modified Bernoulli equation with a head loss term:

$$ (p_1ρ g + α_1 V_1^22g + z_1) - h_f = (p_2ρ g + α_2 V_2^22g + z_2) $$

3. Forgetting the Temperature Dependence of Viscosity

Viscosity is highly temperature-dependent, but the relationship differs for liquids and gases:

  • For liquids (like hydraulic oil or fuel), viscosity **decreases** as temperature increases ($μ ∝ ea/T$). Heating a liquid reduces its resistance to flow, increasing the flow rate for a given pressure drop.
  • For gases (like air in pitot-static lines), viscosity **increases** as temperature increases ($μ ∝ √T$). Heating a gas increases its resistance to flow, reducing the flow rate for a given pressure drop.

4. Applying Hagen-Poiseuille Formulas to Turbulent Flow ($Re > 2300$)

Hagen-Poiseuille flow relations are exact analytical solutions derived strictly for **laminar flow**. In turbulent flow, momentum is transported by turbulent eddies rather than simple molecular shear. The velocity profile flattens significantly, and the pressure drop becomes proportional to $Vavg1.75$ or $Vavg^2$ rather than $Vavg$. Always calculate the Reynolds number first to verify that the flow is laminar ($Re < 2300$) before applying Poiseuille formulas.
