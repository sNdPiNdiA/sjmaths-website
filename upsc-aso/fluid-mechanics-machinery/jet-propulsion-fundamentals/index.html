Reynolds experiment & Reynolds number

Textbook-grade UPSC Air Safety Officer deep-dive: Comprehensive theoretical foundations, analytical derivations, engineering reference data, DGCA/ICAO airworthiness integration, 14 practice MCQs, and timed mini-test.

How This Topic Is Useful for Air Safety Officers & Aircraft Operations
DGCA CAR & ICAO Mandate

In aviation operations and UPSC ASO assessments, this parameter is critical across 4 core operational pillars:

  • 1. Aircraft Systems & Flight Deck Monitoring: Pitot-static probes and air data computers rely on predictable $Re$ corrections to prevent compressible flow calibration errors during high-altitude cruise.
  • 2. Flight Safety & Dispatch Envelopes: Incorrect fuel viscosity or icing contamination modifies $Re$ in fuel feed lines and control surface boundary layers, directly threatening stall margins.
  • 3. DGCA CAR & ICAO Airworthiness Compliance: DGCA CAR Section 2 Series 'X' Part VII mandates strict fluid cleanliness and viscosity verification to ensure laminar-to-turbulent transition thresholds are met.
  • 4. Air Safety Investigation & Incident Prevention: Air Safety Officers audit fuel nozzle $Re$ during engine flameout investigations to verify proper atomization and combustion stability.

Pillar 1: Formal Technical Definition & Nomenclature

The Reynolds number ($Re$) is a dimensionless parameter quantifying the ratio of inertial forces to viscous forces within a fluid flow regime: $Re = \frac{\rho v L}{\mu} = \frac{v L}{\nu}$.
  • Physical Meaning: It dictates whether an internal pipe flow or external boundary layer remains laminar or transitions to turbulence.
  • Governing Law: Derived by Osborne Reynolds in 1883 through dye-injection visualization in pipe flow experiments.
  • Continuum Boundary: Valid only when Knudsen number $Kn < 0.01$, ensuring the continuum fluid mechanics assumption holds.

Nomenclature & Symbols

  • $\rho$ = Fluid density ($\text{kg/m}^3$)
  • $v$ = Characteristic flow velocity ($\text{m/s}$)
  • $L$ = Characteristic length or hydraulic diameter ($\text{m}$)
  • $\mu$ = Dynamic viscosity of the fluid ($\text{Pa}\cdot\text{s}$ or $\text{kg}/(\text{m}\cdot\text{s})$)
  • $\nu$ = Kinematic viscosity of the fluid ($\text{m}^2/\text{s}$)
  • $Re$ = Reynolds number (Dimensionless)

Pillar 2: Microscopic Physics & Continuum Mechanics

1. Molecular Kinetic State: Fluid particles possess momentum transferred via molecular collisions and macroscopic convection, defining local shear stress.
2. Continuum Forces & Gradients: Inertial forces drive fluid parcels along streamlines, while viscous shear forces resist deformation due to molecular friction.
3. Aerodynamic Consequence: When $Re$ exceeds critical thresholds, inertial instabilities overpower viscous damping, triggering boundary layer transition and separation.

Pillar 3: Mathematical Derivations & Governing Laws

Step 1: Governing Relation

Inertial force scale derived from mass flow rate and velocity change.

$$F_{inertial} \propto \rho \cdot A \cdot v^2 \propto \rho \cdot L^2 \cdot v^2$$
Step 2: Boundary / Thermodynamic Conditions

Viscous force scale derived from Newton's law of viscosity and velocity gradient.

$$F_{viscous} \propto \tau \cdot A \propto \mu \left(\frac{v}{L}\right) L^2 \propto \mu \cdot v \cdot L$$
Step 3: Integration & Substitution

Form ratio of inertial force scale to viscous force scale.

$$Re = \frac{F_{inertial}}{F_{viscous}} = \frac{\rho \cdot L^2 \cdot v^2}{\mu \cdot v \cdot L}$$
Step 4: Final Working Law

Cancel common terms to yield standard engineering formulation.

$$Re = \frac{\rho v L}{\mu} = \frac{v L}{\nu}$$

Pillar 4: Dimensional Analysis, SI/CGS Units & Conversion Factors

  • Fundamental Dimensions: $[M^0 L^0 T^0]$ as all mass, length, and time dimensions cancel out completely in numerator and denominator.
  • SI Unit: Dimensionless scalar quantity with no primary unit or fundamental breakdown.
  • CGS Unit: Dimensionless scalar quantity identical to SI formulation.
  • Aeronautical & Imperial Equivalents: Evaluated using slugs/$\text{ft}^3$ for density, $\text{ft/s}$ for velocity, feet for length, and $\text{lb}\cdot\text{s}/\text{ft}^2$ for dynamic viscosity yielding identical dimensionless values.

Pillar 6: Aircraft Airworthiness, Systems & DGCA CAR / ICAO Compliance

  • Regulatory Standards (DGCA CAR & ICAO): ICAO Annex 8 Airworthiness of Aircraft and DGCA CAR Section 2 enforce rigorous testing of fluid lines where Reynolds number criteria govern pressure drop and clogging risks.
  • Critical Aircraft Systems: Hydraulic actuators and FADEC fuel metering units depend on stable laminar/turbulent flow regimes dictated by $Re$ to prevent control lag or cavitation.
  • Aviation Incident Case Study: Flight British Airways 009 encountered volcanic ash over Mount Galunggung, where altered boundary layer $Re$ and stagnation temperature disrupted pitot-static sensor readings.

Pillar 7: High-Frequency UPSC Examiner Traps & Misconceptions

Trap 1: Dimensioned Reynolds Number

❌ Examiner Trap: Assuming Reynolds number has units like $\text{m/s}$ or $\rho v l$.

✅ UPSC Truth: Reynolds number is strictly a dimensionless scaling parameter with no physical units.

Trap 2: Universal Transition Threshold

❌ Examiner Trap: Assuming pipe flow always transitions to turbulence at exactly $Re = 2300$ under all entrance conditions.

✅ UPSC Truth: The critical $Re$ of 2300 applies to smooth pipes; heavy disturbances can trigger turbulence at much lower $Re$, while ultra-clean setups reach $100,000$.

Trap 3: Kinematic vs Dynamic Viscosity Confusion

❌ Examiner Trap: Substituting dynamic viscosity $\mu$ where kinematic viscosity $\nu$ is required in the numerator formulation.

✅ UPSC Truth: Recall that $\nu = \mu / \rho$, so $Re = \frac{v L}{\nu}$, placing kinematic viscosity in the denominator.

Trap 4: Compressibility Neglect

❌ Examiner Trap: Using incompressible density formulations for high Mach number external aerodynamics without temperature corrections.

✅ UPSC Truth: Viscosity $\mu$ varies significantly with temperature via Sutherland's law, altering $Re$ in supersonic flight regimes.

Pillar 8: Step-by-Step Worked Aircraft Numerical Problem

Realistic Aircraft Scenario

An Airbus A320 wing section of chord length $L = 4.0 \text{ m}$ cruises at FL350 where ambient density $\rho = 0.380 \text{ kg/m}^3$, dynamic viscosity $\mu = 1.45 \times 10^{-5} \text{ Pa}\cdot\text{s}$, and true airspeed $v = 230 \text{ m/s}$. Calculate the Reynolds number.

Given Flight Parameters:
Chord length $L = 4.0 \text{ m}$; Density $\rho = 0.380 \text{ kg/m}^3$; Velocity $v = 230 \text{ m/s}$; Dynamic Viscosity $\mu = 1.45 \times 10^{-5} \text{ Pa}\cdot\text{s}$.
Governing Formula:
$$Re = \frac{\rho v L}{\mu}$$
Step-by-Step Analytical Solution:
Step 1: Compute numerator product of density, velocity, and chord length: $\rho \cdot v \cdot L = 0.380 \times 230 \times 4.0 = 349.6 \text{ kg/s}$. Step 2: Divide by dynamic viscosity: $\frac{349.6}{1.45 \times 10^{-5}} = 24,110,344$. Step 3: Express in standard scientific notation: $Re = 2.41 \times 10^7$.
Final Answer: $Re = 2.41 \times 10^7 (Turbulent boundary layer regime)$
Air Safety Officer Operational Takeaway:

High Reynolds numbers dictate fully turbulent boundary layers over commercial wings, requiring robust adverse pressure gradient management to prevent flow separation and stall.

Question 1 • conceptual
What physical ratio does the Reynolds number represent in fluid mechanics?
A Ratio of viscous forces to surface tension forces
B Ratio of inertial forces to viscous forces
C Ratio of pressure forces to gravity forces
D Ratio of convective acceleration to local acceleration
Solution & Technical Rationale:
The Reynolds number is defined as the ratio of inertial forces to viscous forces within a fluid flow.
Question 2 • conceptual
For internal pipe flow, what is the generally accepted lower critical Reynolds number for transition to turbulence in smooth circular pipes?
A 1000
B 2300
C 4000
D 10000
Solution & Technical Rationale:
In standard circular pipe flow, laminar flow typically persists up to a Reynolds number of approximately 2300, above which transition begins.
Question 3 • statement-based
Consider the following statements regarding the Reynolds number: 1. It is a dimensionless parameter. 2. It depends directly on dynamic viscosity in the numerator. 3. Kinematic viscosity appears in the denominator. Which of the statements given above are correct?
A 1 and 3 only
B 2 and 3 only
C 1, 2, and 3
D 1 only
Solution & Technical Rationale:
Statement 1 is correct (dimensionless). Statement 2 is false because dynamic viscosity is in the denominator ($\mu$). Statement 3 is correct because kinematic viscosity ($\nu$) is in the denominator ($Re = vL/\nu$).
Question 4 • numerical
Calculate the Reynolds number for water flowing at $2 \text{ m/s}$ through a pipe of diameter $0.05 \text{ m}$. Take kinematic viscosity $\nu = 1.0 \times 10^{-6} \text{ m}^2/\text{s}$.
A $5.0 \times 10^4$
B $1.0 \times 10^5$
C $2.0 \times 10^5$
D $5.0 \times 10^5$
Solution & Technical Rationale:
Using $Re = \frac{v D}{\nu} = \frac{2 \times 0.05}{1.0 \times 10^{-6}} = \frac{0.1}{1.0 \times 10^{-6}} = 1.0 \times 10^5$.
Question 5 • assertion-reasoning
Assertion (A): High-altitude aircraft experience lower Reynolds numbers than sea-level aircraft at identical true airspeeds. Reason (R): Air density decreases substantially with altitude while dynamic viscosity also decreases, but density reduction dominates the ratio.
A Both (A) and (R) are true, and (R) is the correct explanation of (A)
B Both (A) and (R) are true, but (R) is NOT the correct explanation of (A)
C (A) is true, but (R) is false
D (A) is false, but (R) is true
Solution & Technical Rationale:
As altitude increases, atmospheric density $\rho$ drops faster than dynamic viscosity $\mu$, causing a net reduction in Reynolds number for the same flight speed and chord.
Question 6 • conceptual
Which of the following dimensionless numbers is primarily used alongside the Reynolds number in high-speed compressible aerodynamic testing?
A Froude number
B Mach number
C Weber number
D Nusselt number
Solution & Technical Rationale:
Mach number ($M$) governs compressibility effects and is paired with Reynolds number ($Re$) in wind tunnel scaling for flight certification.
Question 7 • statement-based
Which statements are correct regarding dynamic and kinematic viscosity? 1. $\nu = \mu / \rho$. 2. Dynamic viscosity has units of $\text{Pa}\cdot\text{s}$. 3. Kinematic viscosity has units of $\text{m/s}$.
A 1 and 2 only
B 2 and 3 only
C 1, 2, and 3
D 1 only
Solution & Technical Rationale:
Statements 1 and 2 are correct. Statement 3 is false because kinematic viscosity units are $\text{m}^2/\text{s}$, not $\text{m/s}$.
Question 8 • numerical
Air with density $1.2 \text{ kg/m}^3$ and dynamic viscosity $1.8 \times 10^{-5} \text{ Pa}\cdot\text{s}$ flows past a flat plate of length $0.5 \text{ m}$ at $50 \text{ m/s}$. What is $Re$?
A $1.22 \times 10^5$
B $1.67 \times 10^6$
C $2.05 \times 10^6$
D $3.33 \times 10^6$
Solution & Technical Rationale:
$Re = \frac{1.2 \times 50 \times 0.5}{1.8 \times 10^{-5}} = \frac{30}{1.8 \times 10^{-5}} = 1.666 \times 10^6$.
Question 9 • conceptual
What happens to the skin friction drag coefficient ($C_f$) of a flat plate as the flow transitions from laminar to turbulent at higher Reynolds numbers?
A It drops to zero instantly
B It increases sharply
C It decreases compared to laminar flow initially but increases with $Re$ in turbulence due to higher momentum transfer
D It remains completely constant
Solution & Technical Rationale:
Transition causes an abrupt increase in local skin friction over laminar values, though turbulent boundary layers resist separation better.
Question 10 • statement-based
In the context of wind tunnel testing for aircraft scale models: 1. Geometric similarity is required. 2. Dynamic similarity requires matching the Reynolds number between model and full-scale aircraft. 3. Kinematic similarity requires matching flow velocity vectors.
A 1 and 2 only
B 2 and 3 only
C 1, 2, and 3
D 1 only
Solution & Technical Rationale:
All three statements define complete aerodynamic similitude requirements for wind tunnel scale model testing.
Question 11 • numerical
Fuel flows through an injector orifice of diameter $0.002 \text{ m}$ with velocity $10 \text{ m/s}$. If kinematic viscosity is $1.5 \times 10^{-6} \text{ m}^2/\text{s}$, find $Re$.
A 13333
B 20000
C 33333
D 66667
Solution & Technical Rationale:
$Re = \frac{10 \times 0.002}{1.5 \times 10^{-6}} = \frac{0.02}{1.5 \times 10^{-6}} = 13333.33$.
Question 12 • conceptual
Why is boundary layer tripping sometimes intentionally utilized on aircraft wings or wind tunnel models?
A To decrease lift coefficient
B To induce premature turbulent transition and prevent large laminar separation bubbles
C To increase dynamic viscosity
D To eliminate skin friction entirely
Solution & Technical Rationale:
Tripping devices force early transition to turbulence, energizing the boundary layer and preventing detrimental laminar separation at high angles of attack.

Question 1 of 10 +3 / -1
The Reynolds number is a ratio of:
A Viscous forces to pressure forces
B Inertial forces to viscous forces
C Gravity forces to inertial forces
D Surface tension forces to viscous forces
Rationale:
By definition, $Re = F_{inertial} / F_{viscous}$.
Question 2 of 10 +3 / -1
Which of the following parameters does NOT directly affect the Reynolds number?
A Fluid density
B Flow velocity
C Acceleration due to gravity
D Dynamic viscosity
Rationale:
Gravity ($g$) is absent from the Reynolds number formula ($Re = \rho v L / \mu$). Gravity governs Froude number.
Question 3 of 10 +3 / -1
What is the physical significance of a very low Reynolds number ($Re \ll 1$), also known as creeping flow?
A Inertial forces dominate completely
B Viscous forces dominate completely and convective acceleration is negligible
C Flow is fully turbulent
D Compressibility effects become critical
Rationale:
At $Re \ll 1$ (Stokes flow), viscous forces overwhelm inertial forces, simplifying the Navier-Stokes equations.
Question 4 of 10 +3 / -1
For a commercial jet aircraft cruising at Mach 0.8 at FL350, the external boundary layer flow over the wings is typically:
A Purely laminar
B Transitional and turbulent
C Inviscid
D Separated at the leading edge
Rationale:
High Reynolds numbers ($Re > 10^7$) ensure rapid transition from laminar to turbulent boundary layers over transport aircraft wings.
Question 5 of 10 +3 / -1
If the characteristic length of a flow system is doubled while keeping velocity and fluid properties constant, the Reynolds number will:
A Halve
B Remain unchanged
C Double
D Quadruple
Rationale:
Since $Re$ is directly proportional to characteristic length $L$, doubling $L$ doubles the Reynolds number.
Question 6 of 10 +3 / -1
Which DGCA regulatory document and standard governs airworthiness and fluid system safety design requirements?
A CAR Section 2 Airworthiness
B CAR Section 7 Flight Crew
C Aircraft Rules 1937 Rule 1
D ICAO Annex 1 Personnel Licensing
Rationale:
DGCA CAR Section 2 governs airworthiness standards, design compliance, and system safety for civil aircraft in India.
Question 7 of 10 +3 / -1
What is the dimension of the Reynolds number in the $[M L T]$ system?
A $[ML^{-1}T^{-1}]$
B $[M^0 L^0 T^0]$
C $[MLT^{-2}]$
D $[L^2 T^{-1}]$
Rationale:
Reynolds number is dimensionless, hence its dimensional matrix is $[M^0 L^0 T^0]$.
Question 8 of 10 +3 / -1
In pipe flow analysis, the friction factor ($f$) in the Moody diagram for turbulent flow depends on:
A Reynolds number and relative roughness ($e/D$)
B Reynolds number only
C Mach number only
D Froude number and Weber number
Rationale:
The Moody diagram dictates that turbulent friction factor is a function of both the Reynolds number and pipe relative roughness.
Question 9 of 10 +3 / -1
When scaling an aircraft model in a pressurized wind tunnel to achieve dynamic similarity, what must be matched?
A Reynolds number and Mach number
B Only temperature
C Only angle of attack
D Froude number only
Rationale:
Exact aerodynamic similitude requires matching both Reynolds number ($Re$) and Mach number ($M$) simultaneously.
Question 10 of 10 +3 / -1
Why does lubricating oil experience a dramatic change in Reynolds number inside an aircraft engine bearing when operating from cold-soak startup to hot cruise?
A Density changes by 500%
B Dynamic viscosity decreases exponentially with increasing temperature
C Characteristic length of the bearing increases threefold
D Shaft rotational speed becomes zero
Rationale:
Viscosity of liquid lubricants drops rapidly with temperature rise, increasing $Re$ and altering bearing lubrication regimes.
