01 Force on Submerged Plane Surfaces

1. Formal Technical Definition

The resultant hydrostatic force acting on any arbitrarily shaped plane surface submerged in a static, incompressible fluid is defined as the integral of pressure forces over the entire surface area, acting normal to the surface, whose magnitude equals the product of fluid pressure at the centroid and the total surface area.

2. Basic Concept & Physical Mechanism

At the continuum level, fluid molecules exert continuous momentum transfer against solid boundaries. Because pressure increases linearly with depth due to hydrostatic head ($p = \rho g h$), the elemental force $dF = p \, dA$ varies across the plane. For a submerged flat plate inclined at angle $\theta$ to the free surface, the pressure distribution forms a trapezoidal or triangular prism. The resultant force represents the vector sum of all these normal elementary forces.

3. Mathematical Derivation & Equations

Consider a submerged plane surface of arbitrary shape inclined at angle $\theta$ to the free liquid surface.

Let $y$ be the distance measured along the inclined plane from the free surface intersection ($O$), and $h = y \sin\theta$ be the vertical depth.

Elemental force: $dF = p \, dA = \rho g h \, dA = \rho g (y \sin\theta) dA$

Total resultant force $F_R$:
\(F_R = \int dF = \int \rho g y \sin\theta \, dA = \rho g \sin\theta \int y \, dA\)

Since $\int y \, dA = y_c A$ (where $y_c$ is the distance to the centroid $C$):
\(F_R = \rho g (y_c \sin\theta) A = \rho g h_c A = p_c A\)

Where: $\rho$ = fluid density, $g$ = acceleration due to gravity, $h_c$ = vertical depth of centroid, $A$ = total area, $p_c$ = pressure at the centroid.

6. Aircraft Application & DGCA ASO Connection

Consider a Boeing 777 main wing fuel tank during a 2.5G pull-up maneuver. The effective vertical acceleration becomes $a_{eff} = g + n_z g = 3.5g$. The hydrostatic force on the internal rib bulkheads increases proportionally, requiring rigorous finite element stress analysis under DGCA CAR Section 2 (Airworthiness) compliance rules.

7. UPSC Traps & Common Misconceptions

  • Trap 1: Confusing gauge pressure with absolute pressure when calculating resultant force. (Atmospheric pressure cancels out on closed vented tanks, but acts equally inside sealed units).
  • Trap 2: Assuming total force acts at the centroid. The resultant force acts below the centroid due to the non-linear pressure distribution!

8. Step-by-Step Worked Numerical

Problem: A rectangular inspection hatch of width $b = 1.2\text{ m}$ and depth $d = 1.8\text{ m}$ is submerged vertically in an aircraft fuel calibration tank containing Jet A-1 ($\rho = 804\text{ kg/m}^3$). The top edge of the hatch is flush with the fuel surface ($h_{top} = 0$). Calculate the total hydrostatic force on one side of the hatch. ($g = 9.81\text{ m/s}^2$).

Step 1 (Given): $\rho = 804$, $b = 1.2$, $d = 1.8$, $h_{top} = 0$, $A = b \times d = 2.16\text{ m}^2$.

Step 2 (Centroid Depth): $h_c = h_{top} + d/2 = 0 + 1.8/2 = 0.9\text{ m}$.

Step 3 (Formula): $F_R = \rho g h_c A$.

Step 4 (Calculation): $F_R = 804 \times 9.81 \times 0.9 \times 2.16 = 15,342.3\text{ N} = 15.34\text{ kN}$.

Operational Takeaway: The latch mechanisms securing the inspection panel must withstand over 15.3 kN of outward force, necessitating redundant locking pins per airworthiness directives.

02 Centre of Pressure ($h_{cp}$)

1. Formal Technical Definition

The centre of pressure is defined as the specific point on a submerged surface where the resultant hydrostatic force acts, such that the moment of this resultant force about any axis equals the moment of the distributed pressure forces about the same axis. It always lies below the geometric centroid of the submerged surface for any non-zero submerged depth.

2. Basic Concept & Physical Mechanism

Because hydrostatic pressure increases linearly with depth, the lower portions of a submerged plate experience higher forces than the upper portions. This skewed pressure distribution shifts the resultant force vector downward from the centroid $C$ toward the centre of pressure $P$. The vertical distance between them depends on the second moment of area (moment of inertia) and the depth of submersion.

3. Mathematical Derivation & Equations

Let $y_{cp}$ be the distance along the inclined plane from the free surface to the centre of pressure.

Taking moments about the free surface axis ($O-O$):
\(F_R \cdot y_{cp} = \int y \, dF = \int y (\rho g y \sin\theta) dA = \rho g \sin\theta \int y^2 \, dA\)

Recognizing $\int y^2 dA = I_o$ (moment of inertia about axis $O-O$):
By parallel axis theorem, $I_o = I_c + A y_c^2$, where $I_c$ is the moment of inertia about the centroidal axis.

Substituting $F_R = \rho g h_c A = \rho g y_c \sin\theta A$:
\(y_{cp} = \frac{I_c + A y_c^2}{A y_c} = y_c + \frac{I_c}{A y_c}\)

In terms of vertical depth $h_{cp} = y_{cp} \sin\theta$:
\(h_{cp} = h_c + \frac{I_c \sin^2\theta}{h_c A}\)

SI Units Meters (m)
CGS Units Centimeters (cm)

6. Aircraft Application & DGCA ASO Connection

Consider the hydraulic actuator reservoir or landing gear bay drainage doors on commercial transport aircraft. When fluid pressure acts on a rectangular access door, the hinge line must be aligned or evaluated relative to the centre of pressure $h_{cp}$. If the hinge is placed incorrectly relative to $h_{cp}$, the door will experience unexpected aerodynamic/hydrostatic jamming or sudden opening torque during pressurization cycles.

7. UPSC Traps & Common Misconceptions

  • Trap 1: Believing $h_{cp}$ depends on fluid density $\rho$ or gravity $g$. Notice that $\rho$ and $g$ cancel out in the ratio $\frac{I_c}{A y_c}$, making $h_{cp}$ strictly a function of geometry and depth!
  • Trap 2: As depth $h_c \to \infty$, $h_{cp} \to h_c$ (centre of pressure approaches the centroid for very deep submersions). UPSC trick questions often ask about limiting cases.

8. Step-by-Step Worked Numerical

Problem: A circular drainage hatch of diameter $D = 0.8\text{ m}$ is installed vertically on an aircraft fuselage bilge. The top edge of the hatch is submerged $1.0\text{ m}$ below the water line. Determine the depth of the centre of pressure $h_{cp}$.

Step 1 (Given): $D = 0.8\text{ m}$, depth of top edge $= 1.0\text{ m}$.

Step 2 (Centroid depth): $h_c = 1.0 + \frac{0.8}{2} = 1.4\text{ m}$.

Step 3 (Geometric properties): Area $A = \frac{\pi (0.8)^2}{4} = 0.50265\text{ m}^2$. Inertia $I_c = \frac{\pi (0.8)^4}{64} = 0.020106\text{ m}^4$.

Step 4 (Formula): $h_{cp} = h_c + \frac{I_c}{A h_c}$ (for vertical surface $\theta = 90^\circ$).

Step 5 (Calculation): $h_{cp} = 1.4 + \frac{0.020106}{0.50265 \times 1.4} = 1.4 + 0.02857 = 1.4286\text{ m}$.

Operational Takeaway: The centre of pressure is located $2.86\text{ cm}$ below the geometric centroid. Structural bolting torque calculations must account for this offset moment.

03 Hydrostatic Forces on Curved Surfaces

1. Formal Technical Definition

The resultant hydrostatic force on a curved submerged surface is determined by resolving the distributed normal pressure forces into orthogonal Cartesian components—specifically, a horizontal component equal to the force on the projected plane surface normal to that direction, and a vertical component equal to the weight of the fluid volume directly above the curved surface extending to the free surface.

2. Basic Concept & Physical Mechanism

Unlike plane surfaces where elemental forces are parallel, pressure forces on a curved surface act normal to the surface at every point, meaning their directional vectors constantly change. Direct vector integration is cumbersome. Instead, we isolate a fluid control volume bounded by the curved surface, its vertical and horizontal projections, and free surface planes, applying equilibrium equations.

3. Mathematical Derivation & Equations

Horizontal Component ($F_x$): Equals the hydrostatic force on the plane area formed by the projection of the curved surface onto a vertical plane normal to $x$.
\(F_x = \rho g h_{c,proj} A_{proj}\)

Vertical Component ($F_z$): Equals the weight of the actual or imaginary liquid column contained between the curved surface and the free liquid surface.
\(F_z = \rho g \forall\) (where $\forall$ is the enclosed volume of fluid)

Resultant Force ($F_R$):
\(F_R = \sqrt{F_x^2 + F_z^2}\)

Line of Action Angle ($\theta_r$):
\(\tan\theta_r = \frac{F_z}{F_x}\)

Fundamental Dimensions [M L T⁻²]
SI Units Newtons (N)
Engineering Units kilonewtons (kN), Metric tonnes-force

5. Reference Values Table (Cylindrical & Spherical Aviation Tanks)

Curved Geometry $F_x$ Formula $F_z$ Formula Aviation Application
Quarter-Cylinder (Radius $R$, width $b$) $\rho g (H + R/2) (R b)$ $\rho g b \left( H R + \frac{\pi R^2}{4} \right)$ Fuselage fuel tank bottom curved corners
Hemispherical Dome (Radius $R$) $\rho g (H + 4R/3\pi) (\pi R^2)$ $\rho g \left( \pi R^2 H + \frac{2}{3}\pi R^3 \right)$ Spherical liquid oxygen (LOX) converter tanks

6. Aircraft Application & DGCA ASO Connection

Liquid Oxygen (LOX) converters and spherical nitrogen pressurization spheres in military and transport aircraft operate under immense internal cryogenic and hydrostatic loads. Furthermore, seaplane hulls feature complex curved hydrodynamic lines that experience high vertical and horizontal slamming forces during water takeoff and landing. DGCA airworthiness certification requires proving structural margins against these curved surface force components.

7. UPSC Traps & Common Misconceptions

  • Trap 1: Assuming $F_z$ is only the weight of the fluid physically touching the plate. If the curved surface is concave upward beneath the fluid, the vertical force includes the entire imaginary fluid column above the curve extending up to the free surface level!
  • Trap 2: Trying to find a single point of application where all lines of action intersect. For general 3D curved surfaces, non-parallel normal forces do not intersect at a single point (they form a wrench).

8. Step-by-Step Worked Numerical

Problem: A cylindrical aircraft fuel pipe of radius $R = 0.5\text{ m}$ and length $L = 2.0\text{ m}$ is submerged horizontally with its top edge flush with the fuel surface ($\rho = 804\text{ kg/m}^3$). Calculate the magnitude of the horizontal hydrostatic force on one quadrant of the cylinder.

Step 1 (Given): $R = 0.5\text{ m}$, $L = 2.0\text{ m}$, $\rho = 804\text{ kg/m}^3$, top edge depth $= 0$.

Step 2 (Projected Area): The vertical projection of a quarter cylinder of radius $R$ and length $L$ is a rectangle of height $R$ and width $L$: $A_{proj} = 0.5 \times 2.0 = 1.0\text{ m}^2$.

Step 3 (Centroid of Projection): $h_{c,proj} = 0 + R/2 = 0.5 / 2 = 0.25\text{ m}$.

Step 4 (Formula): $F_x = \rho g h_{c,proj} A_{proj} = 804 \times 9.81 \times 0.25 \times 1.0$.

Step 5 (Calculation): $F_x = 1,972.1\text{ N} = 1.97\text{ kN}$.

Operational Takeaway: The horizontal bursting force on the curved pipe wall is $1.97\text{ kN}$, which must be balanced by hoop tensile stresses in the pipe material.

04 Buoyancy & Archimedes' Principle

1. Formal Technical Definition

Archimedes' principle states that any body wholly or partially immersed in a fluid is subjected to an upward buoyant force equal in magnitude to the weight of the fluid displaced by the body, and this buoyant force acts vertically upward through the centroid of the displaced volume (known as the centre of buoyancy).

2. Basic Concept & Physical Mechanism

Because pressure increases with depth ($p = \rho g h$), the bottom surface of an immersed body experiences higher upward pressure forces than the top surface experiences downward pressure forces. The net resultant of all vertical pressure forces integrated over the entire closed surface of the body yields the buoyant force $F_b = \rho_{fluid} g \forall_{submerged}$.

3. Mathematical Derivation & Equations

Consider a submerged body of volume $\forall$.

Upward force on bottom surface: $F_{bottom} = \int p_{bot} dA = \int \rho g (h_1 + z) dA$
Downward force on top surface: $F_{top} = \int p_{top} dA = \int \rho g h_1 dA$

Net buoyant force $F_b = F_{bottom} - F_{top} = \int \rho g z dA = \rho g \forall_{submerged}$

Where $\forall_{submerged}$ is the volume of fluid displaced.

Net vertical equilibrium force on a floating body in static equilibrium:
\(W_{body} = F_b = \rho_{fluid} g \forall_{displaced}\)

Fundamental Dimensions [M L T⁻²]
SI Units Newtons (N)
Engineering Units kilonewtons (kN), Kilograms-force (kgf)

Fluid Medium Density ($\rho$ at Standard Conditions) Buoyant Force per m³ of Displaced Volume Aircraft Relevance

6. Aircraft Application & DGCA ASO Connection

Consider floatplane pontoons or amphibious aircraft hull compartments. During water landings (ditching), the buoyant force must safely support the aircraft gross weight without causing nose-over pitching moments. Furthermore, magnetic or capacitive fuel quantity indicating systems use hollow cork or syntactic foam floats whose buoyancy calibration depends directly on Archimedes' principle in Jet A-1 fuel.

7. UPSC Traps & Common Misconceptions

  • Trap 1: Assuming buoyant force depends on the depth of immersion. Archimedes' principle states $F_b = \rho g \forall_{submerged}$; depth $h$ does not appear in the formula as long as the body is fully submerged!
  • Trap 2: Confusing the volume of the body with the volume of fluid displaced when the body is only partially floating.

8. Step-by-Step Worked Numerical

Problem: An amphibious aircraft float has a total volume of $5.0\text{ m}^3$ and weighs $12\text{ kN}$ in air. Calculate the fraction of its volume submerged when floating in freshwater ($\rho = 1000\text{ kg/m}^3$).

Step 1 (Given): $\forall_{total} = 5.0\text{ m}^3$, $W = 12\text{ kN} = 12,000\text{ N}$, $\rho = 1000\text{ kg/m}^3$, $g = 9.81\text{ m/s}^2$.

Step 2 (Equilibrium Condition): For a floating body, Upward Buoyant Force = Total Weight ($F_b = W$).

Step 3 (Formula): $\rho_{water} g \forall_{submerged} = W \implies 1000 \times 9.81 \times \forall_{submerged} = 12,000$.

Step 4 (Calculation): $\forall_{submerged} = \frac{12,000}{1000 \times 9.81} = 1.223\text{ m}^3$.

Step 5 (Fraction Submerged): $\frac{\forall_{submerged}}{\forall_{total}} = \frac{1.223}{5.0} = 0.2446$ (or $24.46\%$).

Operational Takeaway: The float operates at roughly 24.5% submergence under static load, leaving over 75% reserve buoyancy for dynamic wave crest impacts during takeoff.

05 Equilibrium of Floating Bodies

1. Formal Technical Definition

A floating body is in static equilibrium when the gravitational force acting downward through its centre of gravity ($G$) is exactly equal in magnitude and collinear with the buoyant force acting upward through its centre of buoyancy ($B$).

2. Basic Concept & Physical Mechanism

Unlike a fully submerged body where $G$ and $B$ can be at different vertical positions while maintaining equilibrium, a floating body's displacement volume changes as it tilts. For pure vertical equilibrium, $\sum F_z = 0 \implies W = FB$. For rotational equilibrium in calm fluid, the line of action of weight and buoyancy must pass through the same vertical axis.

3. Mathematical Derivation & Equations

Condition 1 (Vertical Equilibrium):
\(W = \rho_{fluid} g \forall_{displaced}\)

Condition 2 (Rotational Equilibrium):
The centre of gravity $G$ and centre of buoyancy $B$ must lie on the same vertical line when the body is upright.

Specific Gravity relation for uniform bodies:
\(\frac{h_{submerged}}{h_{total}} = \frac{\rho_{body}}{\rho_{fluid}}\)

Fundamental Dimensions [Dimensionless ratio for SG]
SI Units Newtons (W), kg/m³ ($\rho$)
CGS Units dynes, g/cm³

6. Aircraft Application & DGCA ASO Connection

During emergency sea ditching of commercial transport aircraft, the cabin and cargo hold floor compartments trap air, creating a massive floating composite body. Understanding floating equilibrium and waterline dynamics is vital for rescue coordination teams operating under DGCA Emergency Response Guidelines.

7. UPSC Traps & Common Misconceptions

  • Trap 1: Assuming centre of gravity ($G$) must always be below the centre of buoyancy ($B$) for a floating body to float upright. FALSE! For floating bodies, $G$ can be *above* $B$ as long as the metacentre $M$ is above $G$.

8. Step-by-Step Worked Numerical

Problem: A solid wooden spar used in seaplane wing strut fairing has a square cross-section ($0.2\text{ m} \times 0.2\text{ m}$) and a specific gravity $SG = 0.6$. Determine the depth of immersion when floating vertically in fresh water.

Step 1 (Given): $SG = 0.6$, $\rho_{body} = 600\text{ kg/m}^3$, $\rho_{water} = 1000\text{ kg/m}^3$, thickness $H = 0.2\text{ m}$.

Step 2 (Formula): $W = F_b \implies \rho_{body} g (A \times H) = \rho_{water} g (A \times h_{sub})$.

Step 3 (Simplification): $h_{sub} = H \times \left(\frac{\rho_{body}}{\rho_{water}}\right) = 0.2 \times 0.6 = 0.12\text{ m}$.

Operational Takeaway: The spar floats with $12\text{ cm}$ submerged and $8\text{ cm}$ exposed above the water line.
