Subtopic 1 of 6

The Continuity Equation (Conservation of Mass)

UPSC ASO Syllabus Ref: FM-01
1. Formal Technical Definition

The continuity equation is the mathematical statement of the principle of conservation of mass applied to a fluid flow field. For a control volume, the net rate of mass outflow must equal the time rate of decrease of mass within the control volume. In differential form, it is expressed as $\frac{\partial \rho}{\partial t} + \nabla \cdot (\rho \vec{V}) = 0$.

2. Basic Concept & Physical Mechanism

At the molecular level, fluid mass is neither created nor destroyed. In a continuum fluid parcel, if more molecules enter a fixed control volume than leave it over a time interval $\Delta t$, the density $\rho$ within the volume must rise (unsteady flow) or the boundaries must expand. For steady, incompressible flow ($\rho = \text{constant}$), the velocity vector field $\vec{V}$ is solenoidal ($\nabla \cdot \vec{V} = 0$), meaning streamlines bunch together where velocity increases and spread apart where velocity decreases.

3. Mathematical Derivation & Equations

Step-by-step differential derivation:

1. Consider a fixed infinitesimal control volume $dx \, dy \, dz$.

2. Mass flow rate entering through the left $y-z$ face: $\left( \rho u \right) dy dz$.

3. Mass flow rate leaving through the right face: $\left( \rho u + \frac{\partial(\rho u)}{\partial x} dx \right) dy dz$.

4. Net mass flux in x-direction: $\frac{\partial(\rho u)}{\partial x} dx dy dz$.

5. Summing fluxes in all three spatial directions along with the accumulation rate $\frac{\partial \rho}{\partial t} dx dy dz = 0$ yields:

$$\frac{\partial \rho}{\partial t} + \frac{\partial(\rho u)}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial(\rho v)}{\partial y} + \frac{\partial(\rho w)}{\partial z} = 0 \implies \frac{\partial \rho}{\partial t} + \nabla \cdot (\rho \vec{V}) = 0$$

6. For 1D steady incompressible pipe/duct flow ($A_1 V_1 = A_2 V_2$):

$$\dot{m} = \rho_1 A_1 V_1 = \rho_2 A_2 V_2 \implies A_1 V_1 = A_2 V_2 \quad (\text{if } \rho = \text{const})$$

Where: $\rho$ = fluid density, $u, v, w$ = velocity components in Cartesian coordinates, $A$ = cross-sectional area, $V$ = mean flow velocity.

4. Dimensions & Units
  • Mass Flow Rate ($\dot{m}$): $[M \, T^{-1}]$, SI: $\text{kg/s}$, CGS: $\text{g/s}$
  • Volumetric Flow Rate ($Q$): $[L^3 \, T^{-1}]$, SI: $\text{m}^3/\text{s}$, Imperial: $\text{CFM}$
  • Velocity ($V$): $[L \, T^{-1}]$, SI: $\text{m/s}$
  • Conversion: $1 \text{ m}^3/\text{s} = 2118.88 \text{ CFM}$

6. Aircraft Application & Air Safety Officer Connection

The continuity equation governs fuel distribution manifolds in aircraft wing tanks, hydraulic actuator flow rates in Fly-By-Wire flight control surfaces, and gas turbine compressor duct sizing. Under DGCA CAR Section 2 (Airworthiness), fuel feed systems must maintain continuous mass flow to prevent engine flameout during high-G maneuvers. If a fuel line constricts due to icing or mechanical failure ($A_2 < A_1$), local velocity $V_2$ must spike according to $A_1 V_1 = A_2 V_2$, causing severe localized pressure drops and potential vapor locking.

7. UPSC Traps & Common Exam Pitfalls

Trap: UPSC examiners frequently state that "density is constant, hence continuity is $V_1 = V_2$." This is false! Continuity requires $A_1 V_1 = A_2 V_2$; velocity changes inversely with area even if density is constant. Another trap involves applying incompressible continuity ($\nabla \cdot \vec{V} = 0$) to high-speed gas turbine flows where $Ma > 0.3$, ignoring significant density variations.

8. Step-by-Step Worked Numerical

Problem: A commercial transport aircraft's engine hydraulic supply pipe has an inner diameter of $D_1 = 0.05 \text{ m}$ at section 1, where hydraulic fluid (Skydrol LD-4, $\rho = 1025 \text{ kg/m}^3$) flows at $V_1 = 4.0 \text{ m/s}$. At section 2, the pipe constricts to $D_2 = 0.025 \text{ m}$. Calculate the mass flow rate and velocity at section 2.

Given: $D_1 = 0.05\text{ m}$, $V_1 = 4.0\text{ m/s}$, $D_2 = 0.025\text{ m}$, $\rho = 1025\text{ kg/m}^3$

Area 1: $A_1 = \frac{\pi}{4}(0.05)^2 = 1.9635 \times 10^{-3} \text{ m}^2$

Mass Flow Rate ($\dot{m}$): $\dot{m} = \rho A_1 V_1 = 1025 \times 1.9635 \times 10^{-3} \times 4.0 = 8.046 \text{ kg/s}$

Velocity 2 ($V_2$): $A_1 V_1 = A_2 V_2 \implies V_2 = V_1 \left(\frac{D_1}{D_2}\right)^2 = 4.0 \times \left(\frac{0.05}{0.025}\right)^2 = 4.0 \times 4 = 16.0 \text{ m/s}$

Operational Takeaway: Halving the diameter quadruples the velocity, increasing dynamic pressure head and raising local frictional shear stress by a factor of 16 in turbulent pipe flow.

Subtopic 2 of 6

Euler Equation of Motion

UPSC ASO Syllabus Ref: FM-02
1. Formal Technical Definition

Euler's equation of motion is the statement of Newton's Second Law of Motion ($F = ma$) applied to an inviscid, compressible or incompressible fluid parcel moving along a streamline or in a general continuum field. It relates fluid acceleration exclusively to pressure gradient forces and body forces (such as gravity), explicitly neglecting viscous shear stresses.

2. Basic Concept & Physical Mechanism

In a real fluid, molecules experience intermolecular collisions and momentum exchange (viscosity). However, in high-Reynolds-number external flows (like airflow over aircraft wings outside boundary layers) or inviscid core regions, viscous forces are vanishingly small compared to pressure and inertial forces. Newton's second law dictates that a fluid parcel accelerates if and only if there is a net unbalanced pressure force or body force acting on it.

3. Mathematical Derivation & Equations

Step-by-step derivation along a streamline:

1. Consider a fluid element of cross-sectional area $dA$ and length $ds$ along a streamline.

2. Forces acting on the element in the direction of flow $s$:

  • Pressure force: $p dA - \left(p + \frac{\partial p}{\partial s} ds\right) dA = -\frac{\partial p}{\partial s} ds dA$
  • Gravity body force component: $-\rho g dA ds \sin\theta = -\rho g dA ds \frac{dz}{ds}$

3. Applying Newton's Second Law ($F_s = m \cdot a_s$):

$$-\frac{\partial p}{\partial s} ds dA - \rho g ds dA \frac{dz}{ds} = (\rho ds dA) a_s$$

4. Since acceleration $a_s = \frac{V \partial V}{\partial s} + \frac{\partial V}{\partial t}$ (steady flow $\frac{\partial V}{\partial t} = 0$), dividing by $\rho ds dA$ yields Euler's equation along a streamline:

$$\frac{1}{\rho} \frac{\partial p}{\partial s} + g \frac{dz}{ds} + V \frac{\partial V}{\partial s} = 0 \quad \text{or} \quad \frac{dp}{\rho} + g dz + V dV = 0$$

Where: $p$ = static pressure, $\rho$ = density, $g$ = gravitational acceleration, $z$ = geodetic elevation, $V$ = flow velocity, $s$ = streamline coordinate.

4. Dimensions & Units
  • Each Term Units: $\text{m}^2/\text{s}^2$ (Energy per unit mass) or $\text{N/kg}$
  • Pressure Term ($dp/\rho$): $[L^2 \, T^{-2}]$, SI: $\text{J/kg} = \text{m}^2/\text{s}^2$
  • Gravity Term ($g dz$): $[L^2 \, T^{-2}]$, SI: $\text{m}^2/\text{s}^2$
  • Kinetic Term ($V dV$): $[L^2 \, T^{-2}]$, SI: $\text{m}^2/\text{s}^2$

6. Aircraft Application & Air Safety Officer Connection

Euler's equation is the theoretical bedrock of potential flow theory used in Computational Fluid Dynamics (CFD) for aircraft airfoil design. In air safety investigations, understanding Euler's equation explains why pressure drops precipitously over the upper camber of a wing ($V$ increases $\implies p$ decreases). Under DGCA airworthiness advisory circulars, high-altitude stall warnings depend on pressure taps governed by Euler's pressure-velocity coupling.

7. UPSC Traps & Common Exam Pitfalls

Trap: UPSC often asks whether Euler's equation accounts for friction. Never! Euler's equation explicitly assumes zero viscosity ($\mu = 0$). Equations that include friction are the Navier-Stokes equations. Another trap is assuming Euler's equation can only be integrated when density is constant; it can be integrated for compressible barotropic flows where $p = f(\rho)$.

8. Step-by-Step Worked Numerical

Problem: Along a horizontal streamline in an inviscid wind tunnel test section, air velocity increases from $V_1 = 50 \text{ m/s}$ to $V_2 = 150 \text{ m/s}$. Given air density $\rho = 1.225 \text{ kg/m}^3$, determine the static pressure drop $\Delta p = p_1 - p_2$ using Euler's equation.

Given: $V_1 = 50\text{ m/s}$, $V_2 = 150\text{ m/s}$, $\rho = 1.225\text{ kg/m}^3$, $dz = 0$ (horizontal)

Euler Integration: $\frac{dp}{\rho} + V dV = 0 \implies \frac{p_2 - p_1}{\rho} + \frac{V_2^2 - V_1^2}{2} = 0$

Pressure Drop Calculation: $p_1 - p_2 = \frac{1}{2} \rho (V_2^2 - V_1^2)$

Substitution: $p_1 - p_2 = 0.5 \times 1.225 \times (150^2 - 50^2) = 0.6125 \times (22500 - 2500) = 0.6125 \times 20000 = 12,250 \text{ Pa}$

Operational Takeaway: A static pressure drop of $12.25 \text{ kPa}$ occurs due to flow acceleration, illustrating why aircraft cabin or spoiler seals experience strong suction forces.

The Bernoulli Equation

UPSC ASO Syllabus Ref: FM-03
1. Formal Technical Definition

The Bernoulli equation is the integral of Euler's equation of motion along a streamline for steady, inviscid, incompressible, and irrotational flow. It states that the sum of static pressure head, dynamic pressure head, and potential elevation head remains constant along any streamline: $p + \frac{1}{2}\rho V^2 + \rho gz = \text{constant}$.

2. Basic Concept & Physical Mechanism

Bernoulli's principle is a manifestation of the conservation of mechanical energy for a fluid parcel. As a fluid parcel accelerates (e.g., entering a constriction or flowing over a cambered airfoil), its kinetic energy per unit volume ($\frac{1}{2}\rho V^2$) increases. Because no external work or heat is added, this kinetic energy gain must be offset by an exact reduction in flow work (static pressure $p$) or potential energy ($\rho gz$).

3. Mathematical Derivation & Equations

Step-by-step integration of Euler's equation:

1. Start with Euler's equation along a streamline for steady flow:

$\frac{dp}{\rho} + g dz + V dV = 0$

2. Assume constant density ($\rho = \text{constant}$, incompressible flow):

$\int \frac{dp}{\rho} + \int g dz + \int V dV = \text{constant}$

3. Integrating yields the classic Bernoulli equation:

$$\frac{p}{\rho} + g z + \frac{V^2}{2} = \text{constant} \quad \implies \quad p_1 + \frac{1}{2}\rho V_1^2 + \rho g z_1 = p_2 + \frac{1}{2}\rho V_2^2 + \rho g z_2$$

4. Expressed as hydraulic head (meters of fluid column):

$$\frac{p}{\gamma} + z + \frac{V^2}{2g} = H = \text{constant}$$

Where: $\gamma = \rho g$ (specific weight), $H$ = total head, $p/\gamma$ = pressure head, $z$ = elevation head, $V^2/(2g)$ = velocity head.

4. Dimensions & Units
  • Pressure Form: $\text{Pa} = \text{N/m}^2 = \text{kg}\cdot\text{m}^{-1}\cdot\text{s}^{-2}$
  • Head Form: Meters of fluid column ($\text{m}$)
  • Energy per unit mass: $\text{J/kg} = \text{m}^2/\text{s}^2$
  • Standard Atmospheric Pressure ($P_0$): $101,325 \text{ Pa} = 760 \text{ mmHg} = 1013.25 \text{ hPa}$

6. Aircraft Application & Air Safety Officer Connection

Bernoulli's equation is fundamental to aerodynamics, explaining lift generation via pressure differentials across cambered airfoils and airspeed measurement via pitot-static systems. Under DGCA CAR Section 2, flight instruments must undergo rigorous pitot-static leak checks. If pitot tubes become blocked by ice or insects, erroneous Bernoulli pressure differentials lead to catastrophic airspeed misreadings, directly implicated in accidents such as Air France Flight 447.

7. UPSC Traps & Common Exam Pitfalls

Trap: UPSC examiners frequently try to trap candidates by applying Bernoulli's equation across streamlines or inside boundary layers where rotationality (vorticity) and viscous shear dissipation are significant. Bernoulli's equation is strictly valid along a single streamline (or across streamlines ONLY if the flow is irrotational / potential flow).

8. Step-by-Step Worked Numerical

Problem: A training aircraft flies at an altitude where air density is $\rho = 1.0 \text{ kg/m}^3$. A pitot-static probe measures a stagnation pressure $P_t = 104,325 \text{ Pa}$ and static pressure $P_s = 101,325 \text{ Pa}$. Calculate the true airspeed ($V$) of the aircraft.

Given: $\rho = 1.0\text{ kg/m}^3$, $P_t = 104325\text{ Pa}$, $P_s = 101325\text{ Pa}$

Bernoulli Stagnation Relation: $P_t = P_s + \frac{1}{2}\rho V^2$

Dynamic Pressure ($q$): $q = P_t - P_s = 104325 - 101325 = 3000 \text{ Pa}$

Velocity Calculation: $V = \sqrt{\frac{2(P_t - P_s)}{\rho}} = \sqrt{\frac{2 \times 3000}{1.0}} = \sqrt{6000} = 77.46 \text{ m/s}$

Operational Takeaway: True airspeed is $77.46 \text{ m/s}$ (approx. $150.7 \text{ knots}$), which is critical for dispatch reliability and stall avoidance.

Assumptions & Limitations of Bernoulli's Equation

UPSC ASO Syllabus Ref: FM-04
1. Formal Technical Definition

The limitations of Bernoulli's equation define the exact physical boundaries where the underlying assumptions (steady, inviscid, incompressible, irrotational, no shaft work, no heat transfer) break down in real fluid engineering systems, requiring generalized energy equations with head loss terms ($\text{Darcy-Weisbach}$ or modified Bernoulli with friction).

2. Basic Concept & Physical Mechanism

Real fluids possess viscosity ($\mu > 0$), experience thermal conduction, undergo turbulent mixing, and pass through pumps, turbines, or shock waves. When viscous shear stresses act on a fluid parcel, mechanical energy is irreversibly converted into internal thermal energy (dissipation/friction head loss, $h_f$). If Bernoulli's equation is applied blindly across boundary layers, hydraulic pipe fittings, or supersonic shock waves, calculated pressures will exhibit massive errors because mechanical energy is not conserved.

3. Mathematical Derivation & Equations

The Modified Bernoulli Equation (Real Fluids with Friction & Pumps):

1. Starting from the First Law of Thermodynamics (Open System Energy Equation) for steady flow:

$$\frac{p_1}{\gamma} + \alpha_1 \frac{V_1^2}{2g} + z_1 + h_p = \frac{p_2}{\gamma} + \alpha_2 \frac{V_2^2}{2g} + z_2 + h_t + h_L$$

2. Where $h_L$ is total head loss (major losses $f \frac{L}{D}\frac{V^2}{2g}$ plus minor losses $\sum K \frac{V^2}{2g}$).

3. For compressible gas flow ($Ma > 0.3$), density changes must be accounted for using isentropic relations:

$$\frac{k}{k-1}\frac{p_1}{\rho_1} + \frac{V_1^2}{2} = \frac{k}{k-1}\frac{p_2}{\rho_2} + \frac{V_2^2}{2}$$

Where: $h_p$ = pump head added, $h_t$ = turbine head extracted, $h_L$ = head loss, $\alpha$ = kinetic energy correction factor ($1.0$ for turbulent, $2.0$ for laminar).

4. Dimensions & Units
  • Head Loss ($h_L$): Meters ($\text{m}$) or Joules per kilogram ($\text{J/kg}$)
  • Darcy Friction Factor ($f$): Dimensionless
  • Mach Number ($Ma$): Dimensionless $[M^0 L^0 T^0]$
  • Compressibility Threshold: $Ma = 0.3$ ($\Delta\rho/\rho \approx 5\%$)

6. Aircraft Application & Air Safety Officer Connection

In aircraft fuel transfer systems and environmental control systems (ECS), ignoring pipe friction and minor losses (valves, bends) results in starved engine fuel feeds or inadequate cabin pressurization. In high-speed transonic flight ($Ma \approx 0.8 - 1.2$), shock waves violate Bernoulli's assumption of isentropic/inviscid continuity, causing abrupt total pressure recovery loss—a critical factor evaluated during DGCA type certification flight tests.

7. UPSC Traps & Common Exam Pitfalls

Trap: UPSC interviewers and objective paper setters frequently ask: "Can Bernoulli's equation be applied across a hydraulic jump or shock wave?" Absolute negative! Shock waves and hydraulic jumps involve violent irreversible mechanical energy dissipation into heat; Bernoulli conservation fails completely. Momentum equations must be used instead.

8. Step-by-Step Worked Numerical

Problem: Aviation fuel (Jet A-1, $\rho = 804 \text{ kg/m}^3$) flows through a pipeline with a head loss of $h_L = 2.5 \text{ m}$. At section 1, $p_1 = 300 \text{ kPa}$, $V_1 = 2 \text{ m/s}$, $z_1 = 0 \text{ m}$. At section 2 (elevation $z_2 = 5 \text{ m}$), $V_2 = 6 \text{ m/s}$. Find static pressure $p_2$.

Given: $\rho = 804\text{ kg/m}^3$, $\gamma = 804 \times 9.81 = 7887.24\text{ N/m}^3$, $h_L = 2.5\text{ m}$, $p_1 = 300,000\text{ Pa}$

Energy Equation: $\frac{p_1}{\gamma} + \frac{V_1^2}{2g} + z_1 = \frac{p_2}{\gamma} + \frac{V_2^2}{2g} + z_2 + h_L$

Head Terms: $\frac{300000}{7887.24} + \frac{2^2}{2(9.81)} + 0 = \frac{p_2}{7887.24} + \frac{6^2}{2(9.81)} + 5 + 2.5$

Numerical Values: $38.036 + 0.204 = \frac{p_2}{7887.24} + 1.835 + 7.5 \implies 38.240 = \frac{p_2}{7887.24} + 9.335$

Solving for $p_2$: $\frac{p_2}{7887.24} = 28.905 \implies p_2 = 28.905 \times 7887.24 = 227,955 \text{ Pa} = 227.96 \text{ kPa}$

Operational Takeaway: Friction and elevation drop the pressure from $300 \text{ kPa}$ to $227.96 \text{ kPa}$, which must be factored into aircraft fuel boost pump sizing.

The Venturimeter (Internal Flow Measurement)

UPSC ASO Syllabus Ref: FM-05
1. Formal Technical Definition

A venturimeter is a precision differential pressure device used to measure internal volumetric or mass flow rates in pipes and conduits. It consists of a converging inlet cone, a cylindrical throat, and a diverging recovery cone that minimizes permanent pressure loss through gradual diffuser expansion.

2. Basic Concept & Physical Mechanism

When fluid flows from a wide pipe cross-section ($A_1$) into a constricted throat ($A_2$), the continuity equation forces the velocity to increase ($V_2 > V_1$). According to Bernoulli's principle, this velocity spike causes a localized drop in static pressure ($p_1 - p_2$). By measuring this differential pressure using a manometer or pressure transducer, the flow rate can be determined with high accuracy and minimal permanent head loss due to the smooth diverging recovery cone.

3. Mathematical Derivation & Equations

Derivation of Venturimeter Flow Rate Formula:

1. Apply Bernoulli between inlet (1) and throat (2) assuming horizontal flow ($z_1 = z_2$):

$$\frac{p_1}{\gamma} + \frac{V_1^2}{2g} = \frac{p_2}{\gamma} + \frac{V_2^2}{2g} \implies \frac{p_1 - p_2}{\gamma} = \frac{V_2^2 - V_1^2}{2g}$$

2. Apply continuity equation: $A_1 V_1 = A_2 V_2 \implies V_1 = V_2 \left(\frac{A_2}{A_1}\right)$

3. Substituting $V_1$ into the pressure differential equation and solving for $V_2$:

$$V_2 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 - (A_2/A_1)^2}} \sqrt{2g \left(\frac{p_1 - p_2}{\gamma}\right)}$$

4. Ideal volumetric flow rate ($Q_{ideal} = A_2 V_2$). Real actual flow rate incorporates discharge coefficient $C_d$:

$$Q_{actual} = C_d \frac{A_1 A_2}{\sqrt{A_1^2 - A_2^2}} \sqrt{2gh} \quad \text{where } h = \frac{p_1 - p_2}{\gamma}$$

Where: $C_d$ = coefficient of discharge (typically $0.95 - 0.98$ for venturimeters), $A_1, A_2$ = inlet and throat areas.

4. Dimensions & Units
  • Volumetric Flow ($Q$): $[L^3 T^{-1}]$, SI: $\text{m}^3/\text{s}$ or $\text{Liters/min}$
  • Discharge Coefficient ($C_d$):* Dimensionless (ratio of actual to ideal flow)
  • Pressure Head ($h$): Meters of fluid column ($\text{m}$)

Venturi tubes are utilized in legacy aircraft vacuum systems to drive gyroscopic flight instruments (attitude and heading indicators) and in carbureted aircraft engines to meter fuel-air mixtures. Under DGCA airworthiness standards, instrument vacuum pumps and venturi suction sources must maintain specified suction limits ($4.5 - 5.0 \text{ in. 7. UPSC Traps & Common Exam Pitfalls

Trap: UPSC numerical questions often test whether candidates use throat-to-inlet area ratios correctly. A frequent error is mixing up diameter ratios ($d_2/d_1$) with area ratios in the denominator term $\sqrt{1 - (d_2/d_1)^4}$. Remember that area scales with diameter squared, so $(A_2/A_1)^2 = (d_2/d_1)^4$.

8. Step-by-Step Worked Numerical

Problem: A venturimeter with inlet diameter $D_1 = 0.2 \text{ m}$ and throat diameter $D_2 = 0.1 \text{ m}$ is used to measure water flow ($Q$). The differential U-tube mercury manometer connected to the inlet and throat reads a mercury deflection of $\Delta h = 0.25 \text{ m}$. Given mercury specific gravity $SG = 13.6$ and water $SG = 1.0$, take $C_d = 0.98$. Calculate the actual flow rate $Q$.

Given: $D_1 = 0.2\text{ m}$, $D_2 = 0.1\text{ m}$, $\Delta h = 0.25\text{ m}$ (mercury), $C_d = 0.98$, $S_m = 13.6$, $S_w = 1.0$

Equivalent Water Head ($h$): $h = \Delta h \left(\frac{S_m}{S_w} - 1\right) = 0.25 \left(13.6 - 1\right) = 0.25 \times 12.6 = 3.15 \text{ m of water}$

Areas: $A_1 = \frac{\pi}{4}(0.2)^2 = 0.031416 \text{ m}^2$, $A_2 = \frac{\pi}{4}(0.1)^2 = 0.007854 \text{ m}^2$

Flow Calculation: $Q = 0.98 \times \frac{0.031416 \times 0.007854}{\sqrt{0.031416^2 - 0.007854^2}} \sqrt{2 \times 9.81 \times 3.15} = 0.0617 \text{ m}^3/\text{s} \text{ (61.7 L/s)}$

Investigation 1: Air France 447 Pitot Probe Icing & Stagnation Pressure Collapse

Failure Mode: Ice Crystal Accretion inside Pitot Tube Drain Holes

Physical Mechanism: High-altitude convective cloud ice crystals clogged the Pitot tubes. With the stagnation port blocked while the static port remained open, the airspeed indicator began acting as an altimeter, presenting erratic readings and triggering autopilot disconnect and fatal stall.

DGCA Air Safety Directive: DGCA CAR Section 2 Series I and EASA AD 2009-0195 mandated replacement of Thales pitot probes with Goodrich heated models exhibiting higher ice-crystal liquid water content (LWC) resistance.

Investigation 2: Venturi-Type Carburetor Vaporization Icing & Engine Failure

Failure Mode: Bernoulli Pressure Drop Combined with Fuel Evaporative Cooling

Physical Mechanism: Air accelerating through the carburetor venturi experiences a static pressure drop: $\Delta p = \frac{1}{2}\rho (V_{throat}^2 - V_1^2)$, causing an adiabatic temperature drop amplified by latent heat of fuel vaporization. This causes moisture to freeze on throttle plates even at $+20^\circ\text{C}$ ambient temperatures.

DGCA Air Safety Directive: DGCA CAR-23.1093 requires carburetor pre-heat systems supplying a minimum temperature rise of $50^\circ\text{C}$ to melt throat ice formations.

60-Second High-Yield Revision Cheatsheet: Day 5

Essential formulas, governing equations, dimensional parameters, and UPSC traps compiled for quick revision.

Fundamental Equations

Euler's Equation: $\frac{dp}{\rho} + V dV + g dz = 0$
Bernoulli's Equation: $\frac{p}{\rho g} + \frac{V^2}{2g} + z = H = \text{constant}$
Pitot Airspeed: $V = \sqrt{\frac{2(p_0 - p_s)}{\rho}}$
Venturimeter Flow: $Q = C_d \frac{A_1 A_2}{\sqrt{A_1^2 - A_2^2}} \sqrt{2gh}$
Kinetic Energy Correction: $\alpha = \frac{1}{A} \int \left(\frac{u}{\bar{V}}\right)^3 dA$

UPSC Traps & Exam Rules

Bernoulli across Streamlines: Bernoulli constant differs across streamlines in rotational flow. It is constant everywhere ONLY if flow is irrotational.
Kinetic Energy Factor: In laminar flow, actual kinetic energy head is $\alpha \frac{V^2}{2g} = 2.0 \frac{V^2}{2g}$, NOT just $V^2/2g$.
Stagnation Pressure Definition: Stagnation pressure includes both static pressure AND dynamic pressure: $p_0 = p + \frac{1}{2}\rho V^2$.
