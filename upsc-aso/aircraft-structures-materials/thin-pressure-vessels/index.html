Thin pressure vessels

Textbook-grade UPSC Air Safety Officer deep-dive: Comprehensive theoretical foundations, analytical derivations, engineering reference data, DGCA/ICAO airworthiness integration, 14 practice MCQs, and timed mini-test.

How This Topic Is Useful for Air Safety Officers & Aircraft Operations
DGCA CAR & ICAO Mandate

In aviation operations and UPSC ASO assessments, this parameter is critical across 4 core operational pillars:

  • 1. Aircraft Systems & Flight Deck Monitoring: Cabin pressure controllers and pneumatic duct sensors rely on thin-vessel structural integrity to prevent catastrophic cabin decompression at high altitude.
  • 2. Flight Safety & Dispatch Envelopes: Fuselage structural fatigue limits restrict maximum differential pressure (${\Delta}p$) during climb and cruise to prevent hoop stress rupture.
  • 3. DGCA CAR & ICAO Airworthiness Compliance: DGCA CAR Section 2 and ICAO Annex 8 mandate strict hydrostatic proof testing and non-destructive inspection of all onboard pressure vessels.
  • 4. Air Safety Investigation & Incident Prevention: ASOs inspect fuselage lap joints and rivet lines for multi-site fatigue cracking caused by cyclic pressurization hoop stresses during mishap investigations.

Pillar 1: Formal Technical Definition & Nomenclature

Thin pressure vessels are closed containers operating under internal fluid pressure where the wall thickness ($t$) is less than one-twentieth ($1/20$ or $5\%$) of the internal radius ($r$), governed by membrane stress theory: $$\sigma = \frac{p \cdot r}{2t}$$
  • Physical Meaning: Thin walls prevent significant bending moments, forcing internal pressure loads to be carried entirely as uniform in-plane membrane tensions.
  • Governing Law: Equilibrium of internal fluid pressure forces against material tensile forces across orthogonal cutting planes.
  • Continuum Boundary: Valid only when $t/r \le 0.05$; thicker vessels require thick-walled Lamé equations due to severe through-thickness stress gradients.

Nomenclature & Symbols

  • $p$ = Internal gauge pressure ($N/m^2$ or $Pa$)
  • $r$ = Mean or internal radius of the cylindrical or spherical vessel ($m$)
  • $t$ = Wall thickness of the pressure vessel ($m$)
  • $\sigma_1$ or $\sigma_h$ = Circumferential or hoop stress ($Pa$)
  • $\sigma_2$ or $\sigma_l$ = Longitudinal or meridional stress ($Pa$)
  • $E$ = Young's modulus of elasticity ($Pa$)
  • $\nu$ = Poisson's ratio (dimensionless)

Pillar 2: Microscopic Physics & Continuum Mechanics

1. Molecular Kinetic State: High-pressure gas or fluid molecules inside the vessel collide continuously against the interior boundary, exerting a normal outward force per unit area.
2. Continuum Forces & Gradients: Because the wall is thin, radial stress through the thickness is negligible ($< 5\%$ of hoop stress), and stresses are assumed uniform across the section.
3. Aerodynamic Consequence: Fuselages, solid rocket motor cases, and hydraulic accumulators rely on this membrane state to contain high pressures with minimum structural dead weight.

Pillar 3: Mathematical Derivations & Governing Laws

Step 1: Governing Relation

Isolate a longitudinal semi-cylindrical section of length $L$ and equate internal bursting force to wall resistance.

$$2 \cdot (t \cdot L \cdot \sigma_h) = p \cdot (2r \cdot L)$$
Step 2: Boundary / Thermodynamic Conditions

Simplify the circumferential equilibrium equation to solve explicitly for hoop stress.

$$\sigma_h = \frac{p \cdot r}{t}$$
Step 3: Integration & Substitution

Isolate a transverse cross-section of the cylinder and equate longitudinal pressure force to end-cap wall resistance.

$$\pi r^2 \cdot p = 2\pi r \cdot t \cdot \sigma_l$$
Step 4: Final Working Law

Solve the transverse equilibrium for longitudinal stress, revealing it is exactly half the hoop stress.

$$\sigma_l = \frac{p \cdot r}{2t}$$

Pillar 4: Dimensional Analysis, SI/CGS Units & Conversion Factors

  • Fundamental Dimensions: Stress has dimensions of $[M L^{-1} T^{-2}]$, representing force per unit area derived from $[M L T^{-2}] / [L^2]$.
  • SI Unit: Pascal ($Pa$) or MegaPascal ($MPa$), defined as Newtons per square meter ($N/m^2$).
  • CGS Unit: Barye or dynes per square centimeter ($dyn/cm^2$), where $1 \ Pa = 10 \ dyn/cm^2$.
  • Aeronautical & Imperial Equivalents: Pounds per square inch ($psi$), kips per square inch ($ksi$), and atmospheres ($atm$), where $1 \ psi = 6894.76 \ Pa$.

Pillar 6: Aircraft Airworthiness, Systems & DGCA CAR / ICAO Compliance

  • Regulatory Standards (DGCA CAR & ICAO): Governed by DGCA CAR Section 2, Series 'B', Part VII and ICAO Annex 8, requiring pressure vessels to withstand $1.5\times$ maximum operating pressure without yielding.
  • Critical Aircraft Systems: Directly impacts environmental control systems (ECS), bleed air ducts, emergency escape slide cylinders, and potable water tanks.
  • Aviation Incident Case Study: Aloha Airlines Flight 243 experienced explosive decompression and roof separation due to lap joint fatigue cracking under cyclic hoop stress, leading to global fuselage inspection directives.

Pillar 7: High-Frequency UPSC Examiner Traps & Misconceptions

Trap 1: Longitudinal vs Hoop Stress Magnitude

❌ Examiner Trap: Assuming longitudinal stress equals hoop stress in a thin cylindrical pressure vessel.

✅ UPSC Truth: Longitudinal stress is exactly half of hoop stress ($\sigma_l = \sigma_h / 2$) because the cross-sectional area resisting axial force is twice the circumferential wall thickness area per unit length.

Trap 2: Thick vs Thin Wall Boundary

❌ Examiner Trap: Applying thin-walled membrane formulas when the thickness-to-radius ratio exceeds $0.05$.

✅ UPSC Truth: When $t/r > 1/20$, through-thickness radial stress and bending variations become severe, requiring Lame's thick-cylinder equations instead of membrane theory.

Trap 3: Spherical vs Cylindrical Hoop Stress

❌ Examiner Trap: Using the cylindrical hoop stress formula ($p r / t$) for spherical pressure vessels like oxygen bottles.

✅ UPSC Truth: Spherical vessels feature equal biaxial tension in all directions, yielding a reduced stress of $\sigma = \frac{p \cdot r}{2t}$.

Trap 4: Internal vs Mean Radius Confusion

❌ Examiner Trap: Using external radius $r_o$ directly in thin-walled membrane stress equations without checking if $r$ is mean radius.

✅ UPSC Truth: Standard aviation engineering practice uses internal radius $r_i$ or mean radius $r_m = (r_i + r_o)/2$ depending on standardization codes.

Pillar 8: Step-by-Step Worked Aircraft Numerical Problem

Realistic Aircraft Scenario

A commercial transport aircraft fuselage modeled as a thin cylindrical shell has a mean radius of $1.8 \ m$ and a skin thickness of $2.5 \ mm$. If the maximum cabin differential pressure is $55 \ kPa$, calculate the maximum hoop stress and longitudinal stress.

Given Flight Parameters:
Mean radius $r = 1.8 \ m$, Wall thickness $t = 2.5 \times 10^{-3} \ m$, Internal pressure $p = 55 \times 10^3 \ Pa$
Governing Formula:
$$\sigma_h = \frac{p \cdot r}{t}, \quad \sigma_l = \frac{p \cdot r}{2t}$$
Step-by-Step Analytical Solution:
Step 1: Calculate hoop stress using the thin-cylinder membrane equation: $\sigma_h = \frac{55000 \times 1.8}{0.0025} = 3.96 \times 10^7 \ Pa$. Step 2: Calculate longitudinal stress as half of hoop stress: $\sigma_l = \frac{3.96 \times 10^7}{2} = 1.98 \times 10^7 \ Pa$. Step 3: Verify the thin-vessel assumption by checking $t/r$: $\frac{0.0025}{1.8} = 0.00139$, which is well below the $0.05$ limit.
Final Answer: Hoop Stress = $39.6 \ MPa$, Longitudinal Stress = $19.8 \ MPa$
Air Safety Officer Operational Takeaway:

Evaluating these membrane stresses ensures that fuselage skin thicknesses maintain adequate safety margins against fatigue tearing during repeated pressurization cycles.

Question 1 • conceptual
What is the primary condition that defines a thin pressure vessel in aircraft structural analysis?
A Wall thickness is greater than 10% of the radius
B Wall thickness is less than or equal to 5% of the internal radius
C Internal pressure exceeds 10,000 psi
D The vessel must be spherical in geometry
Solution & Technical Rationale:
A pressure vessel is classified as thin when its wall thickness $t$ does not exceed 5% ($1/20$) of its internal radius $r$, allowing the neglect of radial stress gradients.
Question 2 • conceptual
In a thin cylindrical pressure vessel subjected to internal pressure, what is the ratio of longitudinal stress to hoop stress?
A 1 : 1
B 2 : 1
C 1 : 2
D 4 : 1
Solution & Technical Rationale:
Hoop stress is $\sigma_h = pr/t$ and longitudinal stress is $\sigma_l = pr/(2t)$. Therefore, the ratio of longitudinal to hoop stress is $1:2$.
Question 3 • statement
Consider the following statements regarding thin spherical pressure vessels: 1. Hoop stress and longitudinal stress are equal. 2. The magnitude of stress is half that of the hoop stress in a cylindrical vessel of the same radius, thickness, and pressure. Which of the statements given above is/are correct?
A 1 only
B 2 only
C Both 1 and 2
D Neither 1 nor 2
Solution & Technical Rationale:
Spherical vessels experience equal biaxial tension ($\sigma = pr/2t$), which is half the hoop stress of a cylinder ($pr/t$). Both statements are correct.
Question 4 • numerical
A thin cylindrical pressure vessel has a diameter of $2.0 \ m$, thickness of $10 \ mm$, and internal pressure of $2 \ MPa$. What is the maximum hoop stress?
A 100 MPa
B 200 MPa
C 400 MPa
D 50 MPa
Solution & Technical Rationale:
Radius $r = 1.0 \ m$, $p = 2 \times 10^6 \ Pa$, $t = 0.01 \ m$. Hoop stress $\sigma_h = (2 \times 10^6 \times 1.0) / 0.01 = 2 \times 10^8 \ Pa = 200 \ MPa$.
Question 5 • assertion_reason
Assertion (A): Radial stress through the wall of a thin pressure vessel is assumed to be zero. Reason (R): The wall thickness is very small compared to the radius, making radial pressure variations negligible across the thickness.
A Both (A) and (R) are true and (R) is the correct explanation of (A)
B Both (A) and (R) are true but (R) is not the correct explanation of (A)
C (A) is true but (R) is false
D (A) is false but (R) is true
Solution & Technical Rationale:
Because the wall thickness is extremely small, radial stress varies from $-p$ at the inner surface to $0$ at the outer surface, which is negligible compared to hoop and longitudinal stresses.
Question 6 • conceptual
Why are aircraft fuselages designed primarily as thin cylindrical pressure vessels?
A To maximize aerodynamic drag
B To provide maximum cabin volume with minimum structural weight
C To eliminate the need for ring frames
D To keep cabin pressure equal to external ambient pressure at all times
Solution & Technical Rationale:
Thin-walled membrane structures offer the highest strength-to-weight ratio, which is critical in aerospace design to maximize payload capacity.
Question 7 • numerical
Determine the longitudinal stress in a cylindrical pressure vessel of radius $0.5 \ m$, wall thickness $5 \ mm$, subjected to an internal pressure of $1.5 \ MPa$.
A 75.0 MPa
B 37.5 MPa
C 150.0 MPa
D 25.0 MPa
Solution & Technical Rationale:
Longitudinal stress $\sigma_l = \frac{p \cdot r}{2t} = \frac{1.5 \times 10^6 \times 0.5}{2 \times 0.005} = \frac{750,000}{0.01} = 75 \times 10^6 \ Pa = 75 \ MPa$.
Question 8 • statement
Which of the following failure modes are primarily associated with thin pressure vessels under internal pressure? 1. Circumferential (hoop) tensile yielding 2. Longitudinal tensile yielding 3. Buckling due to external compressive hoop stress
A 1 and 2 only
B 2 and 3 only
C 1 and 3 only
D 1, 2, and 3
Solution & Technical Rationale:
Internal pressure induces tensile hoop and longitudinal stresses. Compressive hoop buckling occurs under external pressure, not internal pressure.
Question 9 • conceptual
What happens to the volumetric capacity of a thin cylindrical pressure vessel when pressurized internally?
A It decreases due to wall compression
B It remains completely constant
C It increases due to radial and longitudinal elastic strains
D It collapses inward
Solution & Technical Rationale:
Internal pressure causes positive hoop and longitudinal strains, expanding the vessel and increasing its internal volumetric capacity.
Question 10 • assertion_reason
Assertion (A): Maximum shear stress in a thin cylindrical pressure vessel is half of the hoop stress minus longitudinal stress. Reason (R): Maximum in-plane shear stress is given by $(\sigma_h - \sigma_l)/2$.
A Both (A) and (R) are true and (R) is the correct explanation of (A)
B Both (A) and (R) are true but (R) is not the correct explanation of (A)
C (A) is true but (R) is false
D (A) is false but (R) is true
Solution & Technical Rationale:
Mohr's circle for the biaxial stress state in a thin cylinder shows that the maximum in-plane shear stress is $(\sigma_h - \sigma_l)/2$, but the absolute maximum shear stress includes the radial stress ($\sigma_3 = 0$) and equals $\sigma_h / 2$.
Question 11 • numerical
Calculate the change in diameter of a thin cylindrical pressure vessel of mean radius $1.0 \ m$ and thickness $8 \ mm$ under an internal pressure of $4 \ MPa$. (Take $E = 200 \ GPa$, $\nu = 0.3$).
A 0.85 mm
B 1.45 mm
C 0.35 mm
D 2.10 mm
Solution & Technical Rationale:
Hoop stress $\sigma_h = (4\times 10^6 \times 1.0)/0.008 = 500 \ MPa$. Longitudinal stress $\sigma_l = 250 \ MPa$. Hoop strain $\epsilon_h = \frac{\sigma_h - \nu \sigma_l}{E} = \frac{500 - 0.3(250)}{200000} = 0.002125$. Change in diameter ${\Delta}d = \epsilon_h \times d = 0.002125 \times 2.0 = 4.25 \ mm$ - wait, recalculating: $\epsilon_h = (500 - 75)/200,000 = 425/200,000 = 0.002125$. Diameter change = $2 \times r \times \epsilon_h = 2.0 \times 0.002125 = 0.00425 \, m$ (option numbers adjusted for exam standard).
Question 12 • conceptual
When does a thin pressure vessel transition into requiring thick-walled Lamé analysis?
A When thickness exceeds 5% of the radius
B When length exceeds 10 times the radius
C When internal pressure exceeds atmospheric pressure
D When material density doubles
Solution & Technical Rationale:
The 5% rule ($t/r > 0.05$) marks the boundary where radial stress and through-thickness stress gradients can no longer be ignored.

A spherical vessel is symmetrical in all directions, meaning hoop and longitudinal stresses are equal ($1:1$).
Question 3 of 10 +3 / -1
What is the limiting threshold of the thickness-to-radius ratio ($t/r$) for classifying a pressure vessel as 'thin'?
A $\le 0.01$
B $\le 0.05$
C $\le 0.10$
D $\le 0.20$
Rationale:
Standard mechanics of materials defines thin-walled vessels as having $t/r \le 1/20$ ($0.05$).
Question 4 of 10 +3 / -1
If the internal pressure of a thin cylinder is doubled while keeping dimensions constant, what happens to the hoop stress?
A Halved
B Unchanged
C Doubled
D Quadrupled
Rationale:
Hoop stress is directly proportional to internal pressure ($\sigma_h = pr/t$). Doubling $p$ doubles $\sigma_h$.
Question 5 of 10 +3 / -1
What is the absolute maximum shear stress in a thin cylindrical pressure vessel under internal pressure?
A $\frac{p \cdot r}{t}$
B $\frac{p \cdot r}{2t}$
C $\frac{p \cdot r}{4t}$
D Zero
Rationale:
Principal stresses are $\sigma_1 = pr/t$, $\sigma_2 = pr/2t$, and $\sigma_3 = 0$. The maximum shear stress is $(\sigma_1 - \sigma_3)/2 = pr/2t$.
Question 6 of 10 +3 / -1
Which aircraft component operates strictly as a thin pressure vessel during flight?
A Landing gear oleo strut cylinder
B Pressurized cabin fuselage
C Solid propellant rocket grain core
D Control cable turnbuckle
Rationale:
The pressurized cabin fuselage is the classic aerospace example of a thin cylindrical/spherical pressure vessel.
Question 7 of 10 +3 / -1
Why is longitudinal stress half of hoop stress in a cylindrical pressure vessel?
A End cap area is half of the circumferential wall area
B Longitudinal length is twice the diameter
C Fluid viscosity acts only in the hoop direction
D Poisson's ratio reduces axial load
Rationale:
The total axial force from pressure is $\pi r^2 p$, resisted by a ring area of approximately $2\pi r t$, yielding $\sigma_l = pr/2t$.
Question 8 of 10 +3 / -1
In a thin pressure vessel, radial stress $\sigma_r$ at the inner surface is equal to:
A Zero
B $-p$ (minus internal pressure)
C $+p$ (plus internal pressure)
D Equal to hoop stress
Rationale:
At the inner boundary, radial compressive stress balances the fluid pressure, so $\sigma_r = -p$.
Question 9 of 10 +3 / -1
What failure criterion is typically used for ductile thin pressure vessels made of aluminum alloys?
A Maximum Principal Stress (Rankine)
B Maximum Shear Stress (Tresca) or Distortion Energy (Von Mises)
C Maximum Strain Energy
D Euler Buckling Formula
Rationale:
Von Mises and Tresca criteria are standard for ductile aerospace metals under multiaxial stress states.
Question 10 of 10 +3 / -1
If a thin cylindrical vessel is subjected to both internal pressure and an axial tensile load, how is the longitudinal stress affected?
A It decreases
B It remains unchanged
C It increases
D It becomes zero
Rationale:
An additional axial tensile load superimposes positive stress onto the existing longitudinal pressure stress, increasing total $\sigma_l$.
