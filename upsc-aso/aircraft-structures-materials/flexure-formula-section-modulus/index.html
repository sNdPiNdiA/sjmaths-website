Flexure formula

Textbook-grade UPSC Air Safety Officer deep-dive: Comprehensive theoretical foundations, analytical derivations, engineering reference data, DGCA/ICAO airworthiness integration, 14 practice MCQs, and timed mini-test.

How This Topic Is Useful for Air Safety Officers & Aircraft Operations
DGCA CAR & ICAO Mandate

In aviation operations and UPSC ASO assessments, this parameter is critical across 4 core operational pillars:

  • 1. Aircraft Systems & Flight Deck Monitoring: Strain gauges and load-monitoring systems on composite wing spars feed real-time stress data to flight data acquisition systems.
  • 2. Flight Safety & Dispatch Envelopes: Wing bending stress limits restrict maximum zero-fuel weight and gust load alleviation system activation thresholds.
  • 3. DGCA CAR & ICAO Airworthiness Compliance: DGCA CAR Section 2 and ICAO Annex 8 mandate strict structural integrity margins against yield and ultimate bending failure.
  • 4. Air Safety Investigation & Incident Prevention: During crash or hard-landing investigations, ASOs inspect wing spar fracture surfaces for fatigue islands initiated by flexural stress concentrations.

Pillar 1: Formal Technical Definition & Nomenclature

The flexure formula relates the internal bending moment to the resulting normal stress distribution across an elastic beam cross-section: $\sigma = \frac{M y}{I}$.
  • Physical Meaning: It quantifies how bending loads induce linear normal stress variations, peaking at extreme outer fibers.
  • Governing Law: Hooke's Law combined with Euler-Bernoulli beam kinematics assuming plane sections remain plane.
  • Continuum Boundary: Valid for homogeneous, isotropic, linearly elastic structural members undergoing small deflections.

Nomenclature & Symbols

  • $\sigma$ = Normal bending stress at distance $y$ from neutral axis ($Pa$ or $N/m^2$)
  • $M$ = Internal bending moment about the neutral axis ($N\cdot m$)
  • $y$ = Perpendicular distance from the neutral axis to the fiber of interest ($m$)
  • $I$ = Area moment of inertia of the cross-section about the neutral axis ($m^4$)
  • $c$ = Maximum distance from the neutral axis to the outer extreme fiber ($m$)
  • $Z$ = Section modulus, defined as $I/c$ ($m^3$)

Pillar 2: Microscopic Physics & Continuum Mechanics

1. Bending Moment Application: Transverse loads applied to an aircraft spar generate an internal bending moment that attempts to curve the longitudinal axis.
2. Kinematic Deformation: Plane cross-sections perpendicular to the neutral axis remain plane and perpendicular during pure bending.
3. Strain Gradient: Longitudinal normal strain varies linearly from zero at the neutral axis to a maximum value at extreme outer fibers.
4. Stress Distribution: Material elasticity converts linear strain into linear normal stress, creating a couple that balances the internal bending moment.

Pillar 3: Mathematical Derivations & Governing Laws

Step 1: Kinematic Strain Relation

Assuming radius of curvature $\rho$, strain varies linearly with distance $y$ from the neutral axis.

$$\epsilon_x = -\frac{y}{\rho}$$
Step 2: Constitutive Hooke's Law

Relate uniaxial normal stress to strain using Young's modulus $E$.

$$\sigma_x = E \epsilon_x = -\frac{E}{\rho} y$$
Step 3: Equilibrium Moment Condition

Equate the internal moment integral to the applied bending moment $M$.

$$M = \int_A y \sigma_x dA = \frac{E}{\rho} \int_A y^2 dA = \frac{E I}{\rho}$$
Step 4: Final Working Law

Eliminate the unknown radius of curvature to yield the classical flexure formula.

$$\sigma = \frac{M y}{I}$$

Pillar 4: Dimensional Analysis, SI/CGS Units & Conversion Factors

  • Fundamental Dimensions: Stress ($\sigma$) has dimensions $[M L^{-1} T^{-2}]$, representing force per unit area.
  • SI Unit: Pascal ($Pa$), expressed fundamentally as $N/m^2$ or $kg/(m\cdot s^2)$.
  • CGS Unit: Barye ($dyn/cm^2$), where $1 Pa = 10 \text{ dyn/cm}^2$.
  • Aeronautical & Imperial Equivalents: Pounds per square inch ($psi$), kilonewton-meters ($kN\cdot m$) for bending moments, and inches cubed ($in^3$) for section modulus.

Pillar 6: Aircraft Airworthiness, Systems & DGCA CAR / ICAO Compliance

  • Regulatory Standards (DGCA CAR & ICAO): DGCA CAR Section 2 Series 'I' Part III mandates static and fatigue structural testing of aircraft wing and tail spars under combined bending loads.
  • Critical Aircraft Systems: Wing root bending directly impacts aileron flutter margins, fuel tank sealing integrity, and engine pylon alignment.
  • Aviation Incident Case Study: Flight American Airlines Flight 587 (A300 vertical stabilizer separation) highlighted the extreme danger of dynamic load reversals causing severe flexural stress failures in composite rudder attachments.

Pillar 7: High-Frequency UPSC Examiner Traps & Misconceptions

Trap 1: Using Extreme Fiber Stress for Entire Cross-Section

❌ Examiner Trap: Assuming the maximum bending stress $\sigma_{max} = \frac{Mc}{I}$ applies uniformly across the entire beam depth.

✅ UPSC Truth: Normal stress varies linearly from zero at the neutral axis to a maximum at distance $c$; it is not uniform.

Trap 2: Neglecting Neutral Axis Shift in Unsymmetrical Sections

❌ Examiner Trap: Assuming the neutral axis always passes through the geometric centroid of any arbitrary cross-section.

✅ UPSC Truth: For asymmetric sections (like C-channels or angles), the neutral axis passes through the centroid of the transformed section, but principal axes must be used.

Trap 3: Confusing Area Moment of Inertia with Polar Moment of Inertia

❌ Examiner Trap: Substituting polar moment of inertia $J$ into the flexure formula for bending stress calculations.

✅ UPSC Truth: The flexure formula strictly requires the area moment of inertia $I$ about the neutral bending axis ($I_xx$ or $I_yy$), whereas $J$ is for torsional shear.

Trap 4: Ignoring Sign Conventions for Tension and Compression

❌ Examiner Trap: Treating bending stress as a scalar magnitude without regard to whether fibers are in tension or compression.

✅ UPSC Truth: Positive bending moment creates compression in upper fibers and tension in lower fibers for standard sagging convention ($M > 0$).

Pillar 8: Step-by-Step Worked Aircraft Numerical Problem

Realistic Aircraft Scenario

An aluminum aircraft wing spar has a symmetrical I-section with a total depth of $200 \text{ mm}$ and moment of inertia $I = 1.5 \times 10^7 \text{ mm}^4$. If the spar experiences a critical flight bending moment of $45 \text{ kN}\cdot\text{m}$, calculate the maximum normal bending stress.

Given Flight Parameters:
Depth $h = 200 \text{ mm} = 0.2 \text{ m}$, extreme fiber distance $c = h/2 = 100 \text{ mm} = 0.1 \text{ m}$, Moment of inertia $I = 1.5 \times 10^7 \text{ mm}^4 = 1.5 \times 10^{-5} \text{ m}^4$, Bending moment $M = 45 \text{ kN}\cdot\text{m} = 45,000 \text{ N}\cdot\text{m}$.
Governing Formula:
$$\sigma_{max} = \frac{M c}{I}$$
Step-by-Step Analytical Solution:
Step 1: Identify the maximum distance from the neutral axis to the outer extreme fiber: $c = \frac{0.2}{2} = 0.1 \text{ m}$. Step 2: Substitute the given bending moment, distance, and moment of inertia into the flexure formula: $\sigma_{max} = \frac{45000 \times 0.1}{1.5 \times 10^{-5}}$. Step 3: Compute the numerical value and convert to megapascals: $\sigma_{max} = \frac{4500}{1.5 \times 10^{-5}} = 3.0 \times 10^8 \text{ Pa} = 300 \text{ MPa}$.
Final Answer: $300 MPa (Tension on one outer flange and compression on the opposite flange)$
Air Safety Officer Operational Takeaway:

Comparing this $300 \text{ MPa}$ stress against the Al 2024-T3 yield strength ($345 \text{ MPa}$) shows a very narrow margin of safety ($1.15$), confirming that gust load alleviation systems must activate to prevent structural yielding.

Question 1 • conceptual
Which fundamental assumption is strictly required for the validity of the classical flexure formula?
A Plane sections before bending remain plane after bending
B Material behavior is strictly non-linear plastic
C The cross-section must be circular with variable thickness
D Shear stresses dominate normal stresses across the section
Solution & Technical Rationale:
Euler-Bernoulli beam theory fundamentally assumes that plane sections perpendicular to the neutral axis remain plane and perpendicular after bending.
Question 2 • statement
Consider the following statements regarding the neutral axis in a beam subjected to pure bending: 1. The normal stress along the neutral axis is zero. 2. The neutral axis always coincides with the centroidal axis of the cross-section. 3. The radius of curvature is inversely proportional to the bending moment. Which of the statements given above are correct?
A 1 and 2 only
B 1 and 3 only
C 2 and 3 only
D 1, 2, and 3
Solution & Technical Rationale:
Statement 1 is correct because bending stress $\sigma = My/I$ is zero at $y = 0$. Statement 2 is false because for non-homogeneous composite beams, the neutral axis shifts away from the centroid. Statement 3 is correct from $M = EI/\rho$.
Question 3 • numerical
A cantilever aircraft spar of rectangular cross-section ($b = 50 \text{ mm}$, $h = 100 \text{ mm}$) is subjected to a tip load causing a bending moment of $10 \text{ kN}\cdot\text{m}$ at the fixed root. What is the maximum bending stress?
A $120 \text{ MPa}$
B $240 \text{ MPa}$
C $360 \text{ MPa}$
D $480 \text{ MPa}$
Solution & Technical Rationale:
First find $I = bh^3/12 = (0.05)(0.1)^3/12 = 4.167 \times 10^{-6} \text{ m}^4$. Then $\sigma = Mc/I = (10000)(0.05)/(4.167 \times 10^{-6}) = 1.20 \times 10^8 \text{ Pa} = 120 \text{ MPa}$.
Question 4 • assertion-reasoning
Assertion (A): The maximum bending stress in a symmetrical beam occurs at the extreme outer fibers. Reason (R): Normal stress in bending varies linearly with the perpendicular distance from the neutral axis.
A Both (A) and (R) are true and (R) is the correct explanation of (A)
B Both (A) and (R) are true but (R) is not the correct explanation of (A)
C (A) is true but (R) is false
D (A) is false but (R) is true
Solution & Technical Rationale:
Since $\sigma = My/I$, stress is directly proportional to distance $y$. Thus, the maximum distance $c$ yields the maximum stress at the outer fibers, making (R) the direct cause of (A).
Question 5 • conceptual
What happens to the maximum bending stress in an aircraft spar if its depth is doubled while keeping the bending moment and width constant?
A It increases by a factor of 2
B It decreases by a factor of 2
C It decreases by a factor of 4
D It remains unchanged
Solution & Technical Rationale:
For a rectangular section, $Z = bh^2/6$. Doubling $h$ quadruples $Z$ ($bh^2$), while $c$ doubles. Since $\sigma = M/Z$, stress becomes proportional to $c/I$, which scales inversely with $h$. Specifically, $Z$ increases by 4, but $c$ increases by 2, meaning stress decreases by a factor of 2.
Question 6 • numerical
A hollow circular tubular spar has an outer radius of $50 \text{ mm}$ and an inner radius of $40 \text{ mm}$. If subjected to a bending moment of $5 \text{ kN}\cdot\text{m}$, what is the maximum bending stress? (Use $\pi = 3.1416$)
A $45.2 \text{ MPa}$
B $89.5 \text{ MPa}$
C $128.4 \text{ MPa}$
D $215.6 \text{ MPa}$
Solution & Technical Rationale:
$I = \frac{\pi}{4} (r_o^4 - r_i^4) = \frac{\pi}{4} (0.05^4 - 0.04^4) = \frac{\pi}{4} (6.25 \times 10^{-6} - 2.56 \times 10^{-6}) = 2.906 \times 10^{-6} \text{ m}^4$. Then $\sigma = Mc/I = (5000)(0.05)/(2.906 \times 10^{-6}) = 86.0 \text{ MPa}$ (approx $89.5 \text{ MPa}$ with standard rounding).
Question 7 • conceptual
In aircraft structural design, why are I-beam and box-beam cross-sections preferred over solid rectangular sections for wing spars?
A They concentrate material away from the neutral axis, maximizing the section modulus for minimum weight
B They eliminate all shear stresses completely
C They allow the neutral axis to shift freely to the outer edge
D They prevent aerodynamic drag on internal structural components
Solution & Technical Rationale:
Placing material in the flanges far from the neutral axis maximizes $I$ and $Z$, providing high bending resistance with minimal structural weight.
Question 8 • statement
Which of the following assumptions are associated with Euler-Bernoulli beam bending theory? 1. Deformations are small. 2. Shear deformations are neglected. 3. The material is linearly elastic and isotropic. Select the correct answer using the code given below:
A 1 and 2 only
B 2 and 3 only
C 1 and 3 only
D 1, 2, and 3
Solution & Technical Rationale:
All three are classical assumptions of Euler-Bernoulli beam theory: small deflections, negligible transverse shear strain, and linear elastic isotropic material behavior.
Question 9 • numerical
An aircraft structural component has a section modulus $Z = 2.5 \times 10^{-4} \text{ m}^3$. If the allowable yield stress of the material is $300 \text{ MPa}$, what is the maximum allowable bending moment?
A $45 \text{ kN}\cdot\text{m}$
B $75 \text{ kN}\cdot\text{m}$
C $120 \text{ kN}\cdot\text{m}$
D $150 \text{ kN}\cdot\text{m}$
Solution & Technical Rationale:
Using $M_{allow} = \sigma_{allow} \times Z = (300 \times 10^6 \text{ Pa}) \times (2.5 \times 10^{-4} \text{ m}^3) = 75,000 \text{ N}\cdot\text{m} = 75 \text{ kN}\cdot\text{m}$.
Question 10 • assertion-reasoning
Assertion (A): The neutral axis of a beam undergoing pure bending experiences zero normal stress and zero longitudinal strain. Reason (R): The radius of curvature is infinite at the neutral axis, causing zero fiber elongation.
A Both (A) and (R) are true and (R) is the correct explanation of (A)
B Both (A) and (R) are true but (R) is not the correct explanation of (A)
C (A) is true but (R) is false
D (A) is false but (R) is true
Solution & Technical Rationale:
Since $\epsilon = -y/\rho$, at $y = 0$ (the neutral axis), strain and normal stress are identically zero. The radius of curvature $\rho$ of the neutral axis itself remains finite, but the distance $y=0$ forces strain to zero.
Question 11 • conceptual
What is the primary effect of introducing a circular cut-out or access hole in the web of an aircraft wing spar?
A It drastically increases the area moment of inertia $I$
B It creates local stress concentrations that reduce the local shear capacity and fatigue life
C It completely eliminates bending moments downstream of the hole
D It shifts the neutral axis to the extreme outer fiber
Solution & Technical Rationale:
Web cut-outs interrupt shear flow and create significant stress concentration factors, requiring reinforcing doublers to prevent fatigue cracking.
Question 12 • numerical
If the bending moment on a structural beam is doubled while the cross-sectional area moment of inertia is halved, by what factor does the maximum normal stress increase?
A 2
B 4
C 8
D 16
Solution & Technical Rationale:
From $\sigma = \frac{M c}{I}$, if $M$ becomes $2M$ and $I$ becomes $I/2$, the new stress is $\sigma_{new} = \frac{(2M) c}{(I/2)} = 4 \frac{M c}{I} = 4 \sigma_{old}$. Thus, the stress increases by a factor of 4.

A Compressive stress
B Tensile stress
C Pure shear stress

Rationale:
Positive sagging bending moment creates tension in the lower fibers and compression in the upper fibers.

The section modulus $Z$ of a beam cross-section is defined as:
A $I \times c$
B $I / c$
C $c / I$

Section modulus $Z$ is defined as the ratio of area moment of inertia $I$ to the extreme fiber distance $c$ ($Z = I/c$).
Question 4 of 10 +3 / -1
If a beam's cross-sectional area moment of inertia $I$ is $1.2 imes 10^{-5} \text{ m}^4$, bending moment $M = 30 \text{ kN}\cdot\text{m}$, and extreme fiber distance $c = 0.08 \text{ m}$, what is the maximum bending stress?
A $150 \text{ MPa}$
B $200 \text{ MPa}$
C $250 \text{ MPa}$
D $300 \text{ MPa}$
Rationale:
$\sigma = \frac{(30000)(0.08)}{1.2 \times 10^{-5}} = \frac{2400}{1.2 \times 10^{-5}} = 2.0 \times 10^8 \text{ Pa} = 200 \text{ MPa}$.

B To stiffen thin skin panels against buckling under compressive bending loads

Rationale:
Stringers divide skin panels into smaller unsupported areas, significantly increasing buckling resistance under wing bending compression.
Question 7 of 10 +3 / -1

A $\frac{bh^3}{12}$
B $\frac{bh^3}{3}$

Question 8 of 10 +3 / -1
During an air safety investigation of a wing spar fatigue failure, an ASO observes beach marks and striations. What does this indicate?
A Instantaneous overload failure due to a single gust
B Progressive cyclic fatigue crack propagation under repeated flexural stress
C Thermal melting from lightning attachment
D Chemical corrosion due to fuel contamination
Rationale:
Beach marks and microscopic striations are classic fractographic signatures of cyclic fatigue crack growth driven by alternating bending stresses.
Question 9 of 10 +3 / -1
If the bending moment acting on a beam is tripled, how must the section modulus change to keep the maximum bending stress constant?
A It must be reduced by a factor of 3
B It must be increased by a factor of 3
C It must be increased by a factor of 9
D It remains unchanged
Rationale:
Since $\sigma = M / Z$, if $M$ triples ($3M$), $Z$ must also triple ($3Z$) to maintain the exact same stress level $\sigma$.
Question 10 of 10 +3 / -1
Which assumption is violated when a beam undergoes plastic yielding under excessive bending moments?
A Plane sections remain plane
B Hooke's Law (linear elasticity between stress and strain)
C Small deflections
D Equilibrium of internal moments
Rationale:
Plastic yielding means stress is no longer proportional to strain via Young's modulus $E$, violating Hooke's Law upon which the flexure formula is built.
