1 Torsion Equation

Subtopic 1 of 7

1. Formal Technical Definition

The torsion equation establishes the mathematical relationship between applied twisting moment (torque), the resulting shear stress distribution across a cross-section, the angle of twist along the longitudinal axis, and the geometric and material properties of the shaft. For isotropic, linear-elastic members of circular cross-section, it is expressed as: $$\frac{T}{J} = \frac{\tau}{r} = \frac{G \theta}{L}$$

2. Basic Concept & Physical Mechanism

When a torque $T$ is applied to a structural member, planes perpendicular to the longitudinal axis rotate relative to one another. At the continuum level, fluid or solid parcels undergo pure shear deformation. Cross-sections of circular shafts remain plane and unwarped during twist (Bernoulli-Navier hypothesis for circular sections), while radial lines remain straight. The internal resistance is provided by complementary shear stresses acting tangential to the radius, varying linearly from zero at the neutral torsional axis (center) to a maximum at the outer fiber ($r = R$).

3. Mathematical Derivation & Equations

Consider a shaft of length $L$, radius $R$, subjected to twist angle $\theta$. The shear strain $\gamma$ at radius $r$ is: $$\gamma = \frac{r \theta}{L}$$ Using Hooke's Law in shear ($\tau = G \gamma$), the shear stress at radius $r$ is: $$\tau(r) = \frac{G \theta r}{L} \implies \frac{\tau}{r} = \frac{G \theta}{L}$$ To relate internal stress to external torque $T$, integrate the moment of elemental shear forces over the cross-sectional area $A$: $$T = \int_A \tau r \, dA = \int_A \left(\frac{G \theta r}{L}\right) r \, dA = \frac{G \theta}{L} \int_A r^2 \, dA$$ Recognizing the polar moment of inertia $J = \int_A r^2 \, dA$, we obtain: $$T = \frac{G \theta J}{L} \implies \frac{T}{J} = \frac{G \theta}{L}$$ Combining both yields the complete torsion equation: $\frac{T}{J} = \frac{\tau}{r} = \frac{G \theta}{L}$.

6. Aircraft Application & DGCA Air Safety Connection

Application: Aircraft control cables, torque tubes operating ailerons/flaps, helicopter tail rotor drive shafts, and engine accessory drive shafts all rely on the torsion equation for sizing.

DGCA Compliance: Under DGCA CAR Section 2, Airworthiness Series 'L', strict torque limits and inspection intervals are mandated for flight control torque tubes. Fatigue cracking induced by cyclic torsional loads in helicopter tail rotor drive shafts has led to fatal accidents (e.g., Eurocopter EC135 tail rotor drive failure investigations). Air Safety Officers inspect these components for fretting, torsional permanent set, and angular misalignment during maintenance audits.

7. UPSC Traps & Common Misconceptions

Trap 1: Assuming non-circular cross-sections (like rectangular or open channels) follow the standard polar moment of inertia $J = \frac{\pi}{32}(D^4 - d^4)$ formula. Correction: Non-circular sections warp under torsion; shear stress is maximum at the boundary closest to the center, not at the extreme outer fiber, and Prandtl's stress function or membrane analogy must be used.
Trap 2: Confusing angle of twist $\theta$ (radians) with rate of twist $\frac{d\theta}{dx}$ ($\text{rad/m}$).

8. Step-by-Step Worked Numerical

Problem: A solid steel torque tube operating a Boeing 737 flap actuation mechanism has an outer diameter $D = 50 \text{ mm}$ and length $L = 1.5 \text{ m}$. It transmits a torque $T = 1.2 \text{ kN}\cdot\text{m}$. If the shear modulus of steel is $G = 80 \text{ GPa}$, calculate: (a) Maximum shear stress $\tau_{max}$, and (b) Total angle of twist $\theta$.

Given: $D = 0.05 \text{ m} \implies R = 0.025 \text{ m}$, $L = 1.5 \text{ m}$, $T = 1200 \text{ N}\cdot\text{m}$, $G = 80 \times 10^9 \text{ Pa}$.

Step 1: Polar Moment of Inertia ($J$):
$$J = \frac{\pi}{32} D^4 = \frac{\pi}{32} (0.05)^4 = \frac{\pi}{32} (6.25 \times 10^{-6}) = 6.1359 \times 10^{-7} \text{ m}^4$$

Step 2: Maximum Shear Stress ($\tau_{max}$):
$$\tau_{max} = \frac{T R}{J} = \frac{1200 \times 0.025}{6.1359 \times 10^{-7}} = \frac{30}{6.1359 \times 10^{-7}} = 4.889 \times 10^7 \text{ Pa} = 48.89 \text{ MPa}$$

Step 3: Angle of Twist ($\theta$):
$$\theta = \frac{T L}{G J} = \frac{1200 \times 1.5}{(80 \times 10^9)(6.1359 \times 10^{-7})} = \frac{1800}{49087.2} = 0.03666 \text{ rad}$$ $$\theta_{\text{deg}} = 0.03666 \times \frac{180}{\pi} = 2.10^\circ$$

Operational Takeaway: The maximum shear stress ($48.89 \text{ MPa}$) is well below the yield strength of high-grade steel ($\approx 800 \text{ MPa}$), ensuring an ample safety margin for actuation cycles.

2 Circular Shafts & Tubes (Solid vs. Hollow)

Subtopic 2 of 7

1. Formal Technical Definition

Circular shafts and tubes are axisymmetric structural elements designed to transmit torsional loads. The polar moment of inertia $J$ for a solid circular shaft of diameter $D$ is $J = \frac{\pi D^4}{32}$, while for a thin-walled or thick-walled hollow circular tube with outer diameter $D_o$ and inner diameter $D_i$ (or outer radius $R$ and thickness $t$), it is $J = \frac{\pi}{32}(D_o^4 - D_i^4) \approx \frac{\pi}{2} R^3 t$.

2. Basic Concept & Physical Mechanism

In solid shafts, material near the neutral axis ($r \approx 0$) experiences very low shear stress and contributes negligibly to torsional resistance while adding dead weight. In aerospace applications, weight minimization is paramount. Hollow circular tubes place the material at the maximum possible radial distance from the neutral axis, maximizing structural efficiency (strength-to-weight ratio).

3. Mathematical Derivation & Equations

For a hollow circular shaft with outer radius $R_o$ and inner radius $R_i$: $$J = \int_{R_i}^{R_o} r^2 (2\pi r) dr = 2\pi \left[ \frac{r^4}{4} \right]_{R_i}^{R_o} = \frac{\pi}{2} (R_o^4 - R_i^4) = \frac{\pi}{32} (D_o^4 - D_i^4)$$ Letting $R_o = R$ and $R_i = R - t$ where $t \ll R$: $$R^4 - (R-t)^4 = R^4 - (R^4 - 4R^3 t + 6R^2 t^2 - \dots) \approx 4 R^3 t$$ Thus, for thin-walled circular tubes: $$J \approx \frac{\pi}{2} (4 R^3 t) = 2\pi R^3 t = 2 \pi R^3 t$$ The torsional section modulus $Z_p$ for hollow tubes is: $$Z_p = \frac{J}{R_o} = \frac{\pi}{16} \frac{D_o^4 - D_i^4}{D_o}$$

6. Aircraft Application & DGCA Air Safety Connection

Application: Thin-walled hollow circular tubes are extensively used in aircraft fuselages, landing gear torque links, and control linkages because they offer high torsional stiffness with minimum mass.

DGCA Compliance: Thin-walled circular tubes are highly susceptible to torsional buckling (wrinkling) if wall thickness $t$ is too small relative to radius $R$. DGCA CAR Series 'M' mandates non-destructive testing (NDT) such as eddy current and ultrasonic testing on critical landing gear torque links to detect subsurface micro-cracks before catastrophic torsional instability occurs.

7. UPSC Traps & Common Misconceptions

Trap: Assuming that substituting a hollow tube for a solid shaft of the same cross-sectional area maintains equal torsional strength. Correction: A hollow tube of the same cross-sectional area has a significantly larger outer diameter, yielding a higher polar moment of inertia $J$ and greater torsional strength and stiffness than the solid shaft.

8. Step-by-Step Worked Numerical

Problem: Compare a solid steel shaft of diameter $D = 60 \text{ mm}$ with a hollow steel tube of outer diameter $D_o = 70 \text{ mm}$ and inner diameter $D_i = 50 \text{ mm}$. Both are subjected to the same torque $T = 4 \text{ kN}\cdot\text{m}$. Find the maximum shear stress in each and determine the percentage weight saving of the hollow tube.

Solid Shaft: $D = 0.06 \text{ m} \implies R = 0.03 \text{ m}$.
$$J_{\text{solid}} = \frac{\pi}{32} (0.06)^4 = 1.2723 \times 10^{-6} \text{ m}^4$$ $$\tau_{\text{solid}} = \frac{T R}{J_{\text{solid}}} = \frac{4000 \times 0.03}{1.2723 \times 10^{-6}} = 94.32 \text{ MPa}$$

Hollow Tube: $D_o = 0.07 \text{ m}$, $D_i = 0.05 \text{ m}$.
$$J_{\text{hollow}} = \frac{\pi}{32} (0.07^4 - 0.05^4) = \frac{\pi}{32} (2.401 \times 10^{-5} - 6.25 \times 10^{-6}) = 1.7429 \times 10^{-6} \text{ m}^4$$ $$\tau_{\text{hollow}} = \frac{T R_o}{J_{\text{hollow}}} = \frac{4000 \times 0.035}{1.7429 \times 10^{-6}} = 80.33 \text{ MPa}$$

Cross-sectional Area (Weight Comparison):
$A_{\text{solid}} = \frac{\pi}{4}(0.06)^2 = 2.827 \times 10^{-3} \text{ m}^2$
$A_{\text{hollow}} = \frac{\pi}{4}(0.07^2 - 0.05^2) = 1.885 \times 10^{-3} \text{ m}^2$
$$\text{Weight Saving} = \frac{2.827 - 1.885}{2.827} \times 100 = 33.3\%$$

Operational Takeaway: The hollow tube carries the torque with a 14.8% lower maximum shear stress while achieving a 33.3% reduction in structural weight—a vital trade-off in aircraft design.

3 Open & Closed Thin-Walled Sections

Subtopic 3 of 7

1. Formal Technical Definition

Thin-walled sections are structural profiles where the wall thickness $t$ is small compared to overall cross-sectional dimensions ($t/L \le 0.1$). They are classified into closed sections (e.g., circular tubes, rectangular box beams, single/multi-cell wing torsions boxes) which form continuous loops, and open sections (e.g., I-beams, channel sections, Z-sections, angle sections) which feature unclosed boundaries.

2. Basic Concept & Physical Mechanism

The torsional behavior of closed and open sections differs fundamentally. A closed section resists torsion by generating a continuous circulating shear flow $q$ around the perimeter, enclosing a large area $A_m$. This provides extremely high torsional rigidity. Conversely, an open section cannot sustain a continuous circulating shear flow; shear stresses flow up one side and down the other, reversing direction across the thickness. Consequently, open sections have virtually negligible torsional resistance (torsional stiffness is proportional to $t^3$ rather than $t$).

3. Mathematical Derivation & Equations

For an open section composed of $n$ thin rectangular strips of length $s_i$ and thickness $t_i$, the torsional constant $J$ (Saint-Venant torsion constant) is: $$J = \frac{1}{3} \sum_{i=1}^{n} s_i t_i^3$$ Because $t_i$ is small, $t_i^3$ is extremely small. For a closed thin-walled section (Bredt's theory): $$J = \frac{4 A_m^2}{\oint \frac{ds}{t}}$$ Where $A_m$ is the area enclosed by the centerline of the thin wall. Comparing $J_{\text{open}}$ ($O(t^3)$) with $J_{\text{closed}}$ ($O(Rt^3)$ or $O(R^3t)$), closed sections are orders of magnitude stiffer in torsion.

6. Aircraft Application & DGCA Air Safety Connection

Application: Modern aircraft wings are designed as closed multi-cell box beams (torsion boxes) formed by front and rear spars, upper and lower skin panels, and stringers. Fuselages are closed semi-monocoque shells. Open sections (stringers, frames) are used internally for local stiffening.

DGCA Compliance: Wing torsion boxes must maintain structural continuity. In-service corrosion or fatigue cracking of skin panels can convert a closed box into a structurally open or compromised section, drastically reducing torsional rigidity and triggering catastrophic aeroelastic flutter. DGCA CAR Section 2 mandates rigorous ultrasonic and dye-penetrant inspections of wing skin splices and rivet lines.

7. UPSC Traps & Common Misconceptions

Trap: Assuming that adding a small longitudinal slit to a closed circular tube has a negligible effect on its torsional strength. Correction: Slitting a closed tube converts it into an open section, causing its torsional stiffness to plummet by several orders of magnitude (proportional to $t^3$ instead of $R^3 t$).

8. Step-by-Step Worked Numerical

Problem: Compare the torsional constants $J$ of two aluminium profiles of equal wall thickness $t = 2 \text{ mm}$: (a) An open rectangular channel of dimensions $100 \text{ mm} \times 50 \text{ mm}$, and (b) A closed rectangular tube of the same outer dimensions $100 \text{ mm} \times 50 \text{ mm}$.

(a) Open Channel: Lengths of segments: $s_1 = 100 \text{ mm}$, $s_2 = 50 \text{ mm}$, $s_3 = 50 \text{ mm}$ (Total $s = 200 \text{ mm}$).
$$J_{\text{open}} = \frac{1}{3} \sum s_i t^3 = \frac{1}{3} (100 + 50 + 50) (2)^3 = \frac{1}{3} (200)(8) = 533.33 \text{ mm}^4 = 5.33 \times 10^{-10} \text{ m}^4$$

(b) Closed Rectangular Tube: Enclosed centerline dimensions: $a = 100 - 2 = 98 \text{ mm}$, $b = 50 - 2 = 48 \text{ mm}$.
Enclosed area $A_m = a \times b = 98 \times 48 = 4704 \text{ mm}^2 = 4.704 \times 10^{-3} \text{ m}^2$.
Perimeter $\oint ds = 2(98 + 48) = 292 \text{ mm}$.
$$J_{\text{closed}} = \frac{4 A_m^2}{\oint \frac{ds}{t}} = \frac{4 (4704)^2}{\frac{292}{2}} = \frac{88,501,248}{146} = 606,172 \text{ mm}^4 = 6.06 \times 10^{-7} \text{ m}^4$$

Operational Takeaway: $J_{\text{closed}} / J_{\text{open}} = 606,172 / 533.33 \approx 1,136$. The closed section is over 1,100 times stiffer in torsion than the open channel of identical wall thickness and outer dimensions!

4 Shear Flow ($q$)

Subtopic 4 of 7

1. Formal Technical Definition

Shear flow ($q$) is defined as the product of the shear stress $\tau$ and the section wall thickness $t$ at any point along a thin-walled cross-section: $$q = \tau \cdot t$$ It represents the shear force transmitted per unit length along the perimeter of a thin-walled structural member.

2. Basic Concept & Physical Mechanism

In thin-walled structures, determining shear stress directly across a minute wall thickness $t$ is impractical. By grouping $\tau$ and $t$ into a single parameter $q$, analysis is greatly simplified. In a closed single-cell section subjected to pure torsion, equilibrium dictates that the shear flow $q$ is strictly constant around the entire perimeter ($q = \text{constant}$). This means where the wall thickness $t$ is smaller, the shear stress $\tau = q/t$ is higher.

3. Mathematical Derivation & Equations

Consider an elemental segment of a thin-walled tube of length $\Delta x$ and perimeter arc length $\Delta s$. Force equilibrium in the longitudinal direction ($x$) requires: $$\frac{\partial q}{\partial s} = 0 \implies q = \text{constant}$$ The resultant torque $T$ produced by the shear flow around the reference origin is obtained by integrating the moment of elemental shear forces: $$T = \oint q r_\perp ds = q \oint r_\perp ds$$ Since $\oint r_\perp ds = 2 A_m$ (twice the enclosed area), we obtain: $$T = 2 q A_m \implies q = \frac{T}{2 A_m}$$ Thus, shear stress at any point with wall thickness $t$ is: $$\tau = \frac{q}{t} = \frac{T}{2 A_m t}$$

6. Aircraft Application & DGCA Air Safety Connection

Application: Shear flow calculations are the bedrock of semi-monocoque aircraft design. They determine fastener pitch (rivet spacing) along skin-stringer joints in wings and fuselages.

DGCA Compliance: Rivet shear failure occurs if the shear flow $q$ exceeds the allowable fastener shear load divided by pitch ($q_{allow} = P_{rivet} / p$). DGCA airworthiness inspectors verify rivet pitch calculations during major structural repairs (SRM - Structural Repair Manual compliance) to prevent skin-panel joint separation during high-g flight maneuvers.

7. UPSC Traps & Common Misconceptions

Trap: Assuming shear flow $q$ varies around the perimeter of a closed single-cell section under pure torsion. Correction: For a single-cell closed section under pure torsion, static equilibrium dictates that shear flow $q$ is constant everywhere around the contour, regardless of variations in wall thickness $t$ or geometry.

8. Step-by-Step Worked Numerical

Problem: A thin-walled single-cell rectangular box beam has dimensions $a = 600 \text{ mm}$ and $b = 300 \text{ mm}$ (centerline dimensions). It is subjected to a pure torsional moment $T = 25 \text{ kN}\cdot\text{m}$. The wall thickness on the top and bottom is $t_1 = 2.5 \text{ mm}$, and on the webs (sides) is $t_2 = 4.0 \text{ mm}$. Calculate: (a) Shear flow $q$, and (b) Shear stress in the webs and flanges.

Step 1: Enclosed Area ($A_m$):
$$A_m = a \times b = 0.6 \text{ m} \times 0.3 \text{ m} = 0.18 \text{ m}^2$$

Step 2: Shear Flow ($q$):
$$q = \frac{T}{2 A_m} = \frac{25,000 \text{ N}\cdot\text{m}}{2 \times 0.18 \text{ m}^2} = \frac{25,000}{0.36} = 69,444.4 \text{ N/m} = 69.44 \text{ N/mm}$$

Step 3: Shear Stresses ($\tau$):
- Flanges (Top/Bottom, $t_1 = 2.5 \text{ mm} = 0.0025 \text{ m}$):
$$\tau_{\text{flange}} = \frac{q}{t_1} = \frac{69,444.4}{0.0025} = 2.778 \times 10^7 \text{ Pa} = 27.78 \text{ MPa}$$
- Webs (Sides, $t_2 = 4.0 \text{ mm} = 0.004 \text{ m}$):
$$\tau_{\text{web}} = \frac{q}{t_2} = \frac{69,444.4}{0.004} = 1.736 \times 10^7 \text{ Pa} = 17.36 \text{ MPa}$$

Operational Takeaway: Thinner walls experience higher shear stress for the exact same shear flow $q$. This explains why skin thinning for weight reduction must be carefully controlled near high-torsion root sections.

5 Torsional Rigidity ($GJ$ or $GI_t$)

Subtopic 5 of 7

1. Formal Technical Definition

Torsional rigidity (denoted as $GJ$ for circular sections or $GI_t$ for general sections) is the measure of a structural member's resistance to angular twist when subjected to an applied torque. It is the product of the material's shear modulus ($G$) and the cross-section's polar moment of inertia ($J$) or Saint-Venant torsion constant ($I_t$).

2. Basic Concept & Physical Mechanism

Torsional rigidity dictates the twist per unit length ($\frac{d\theta}{dx} = \frac{T}{GJ}$) of a wing, fuselage, or control rod. In aircraft design, insufficient torsional rigidity can lead to aileron reversal (where wing twist under aileron deflection counteracts aerodynamic rolling moments) or aeroelastic divergence.

3. Mathematical Derivation & Equations

From the torsion equation $\theta = \frac{T L}{GJ}$, the rate of twist per unit length $k$ is: $$k = \frac{d\theta}{dx} = \frac{T}{GJ}$$ For a multi-cell closed section (e.g., a two-cell wing box with cells 1 and 2), the twist rate of each cell must be identical due to structural compatibility: $$\frac{d\theta}{dx} = \frac{1}{2 A_1 G} \oint_1 \frac{q_1 - q_2}{t} ds = \frac{1}{2 A_2 G} \oint_2 \frac{q_2 - q_1}{t} ds$$ This compatibility equation allows solving for redundant shear flows in multi-cell aircraft wings.

DGCA Compliance: DGCA CAR Section 2 and FAA FAR Part 25.629 require rigorous flutter clearance flight tests. If structural modifications (e.g., installing heavy wingtip missile rails or larger winglets) alter the wing's torsional rigidity without re-evaluating flutter boundaries, catastrophic structural divergence can occur, as witnessed in historical flutter accidents.

7. UPSC Traps & Common Misconceptions

Trap: Confusing flexural rigidity ($EI$) with torsional rigidity ($GJ$). Correction: $EI$ governs out-of-plane bending under transverse loads, whereas $GJ$ governs angular twist under twisting moments. They involve entirely different geometric section properties ($I$ vs $J$).

8. Step-by-Step Worked Numerical

Problem: A transport aircraft wing modeled as a cantilever box beam has a length $L = 12 \text{ m}$ and constant torsional rigidity $GJ = 2.5 \times 10^8 \text{ N}\cdot\text{m}^2$. It experiences an aerodynamic pitching moment resulting in a distributed torque equivalent to a total tip torque $T = 150 \text{ kN}\cdot\text{m}$. Calculate: (a) Rate of twist $k$, and (b) Maximum angle of twist $\theta$ at the wingtip.

Given: $L = 12 \text{ m}$, $GJ = 2.5 \times 10^8 \text{ N}\cdot\text{m}^2$, $T = 150,000 \text{ N}\cdot\text{m}$.

Step 1: Rate of Twist ($k$):
$$k = \frac{T}{GJ} = \frac{150,000}{2.5 \times 10^8} = 0.0006 \text{ rad/m}$$

Step 2: Total Tip Angle of Twist ($\theta$):
$$\theta = k \times L = 0.0006 \times 12 = 0.0072 \text{ rad}$$ $$\theta_{\text{deg}} = 0.0072 \times \frac{180}{\pi} = 0.413^\circ$$

Operational Takeaway: A tip twist of $0.413^\circ$ is within acceptable aeroelastic limits, ensuring negligible loss in aileron effectiveness during high-speed cruise.

6 Shear Centre (Flexural Center)

Subtopic 6 of 7

1. Formal Technical Definition

The shear centre (or flexural centre) is that unique point on the cross-section of a structural member through which a transverse shear load must pass so that the member bends without any accompanying twist (torsion). If the transverse load does not pass through the shear centre, it induces both bending and torsion.

2. Basic Concept & Physical Mechanism

When a transverse shear force is applied to an unsymmetrical open thin-walled section (such as a channel or Z-section), the internal shear flows create a net twisting moment about the centroid. To prevent this parasitic twist, the external load line of action must be shifted away from the centroid to a specific point—the shear centre—where the moment of internal shear flows balances the applied load moment. For sections with two axes of symmetry (e.g., I-beams, circular tubes, square boxes), the shear centre coincides with the centroid.

3. Mathematical Derivation & Equations

Consider a thin-walled channel section under vertical shear force $V_y$. The shear flow $q$ in the flanges and web creates a moment about the web-flange junction. Equating the external moment ($V_y \times e$, where $e$ is the distance from the web to the shear centre) to the internal moment of shear forces: $$V_y \cdot e = \int q \cdot r_\perp ds \implies e = \frac{1}{V_y} \int q \cdot r_\perp ds$$ For a channel with flange width $b$ and web depth $h$: $$e = \frac{3b^2}{6b + h}$$

6. Aircraft Application & DGCA Air Safety Connection

Application: Aircraft wing spars, rib attachment points, and fuselage stringers must be aligned with or account for the shear centre. Aerodynamic lift forces act along the aerodynamic center; if the wing's elastic axis (shear centre line) does not coincide with the center of lift, every gust induces severe wing twist.

DGCA Compliance: Misalignment during structural repairs of wing attachment fittings can shift the load path away from the shear centre, introducing uncalculated torsional fatigue loads. Air Safety Officers scrutinize modification approvals (STCs) to ensure shear center offset moments are fully accounted for in stress reports.

7. UPSC Traps & Common Misconceptions

Trap 1: Believing the shear centre always coincides with the centroid. Correction: The shear centre coincides with the centroid only if the cross-section possesses at least two axes of symmetry. For asymmetric or singly-symmetric sections (like C-channels or half-tubes), the shear centre lies outside the solid material entirely!
Trap 2: Confusing neutral axis (bending) with shear centre (torsion-free shear).

8. Step-by-Step Worked Numerical

Problem: A thin-walled channel section (C-section) has flange width $b = 100 \text{ mm}$ and web depth $h = 200 \text{ mm}$ (uniform wall thickness $t = 3 \text{ mm}$). Calculate the shear centre offset distance $e$ from the centerline of the web.

Given: $b = 0.1 \text{ m}$, $h = 0.2 \text{ m}$.
Formula for shear centre offset $e$ of a channel section: $$e = \frac{3 b^2}{6b + h}$$

Calculation:
$$3 b^2 = 3 (0.1)^2 = 3 (0.01) = 0.03$$ $$6b + h = 6(0.1) + 0.2 = 0.6 + 0.2 = 0.8$$ $$e = \frac{0.03}{0.8} = 0.0375 \text{ m} = 37.5 \text{ mm}$$

Operational Takeaway: The shear centre lies $37.5 \text{ mm}$ outside the web on the axis of symmetry. Any vertical shear force applied through the web must be offset by $37.5 \text{ mm}$ to prevent twisting of the structural channel.

7 Bredt-Batho Concept (Single & Multi-Cell Closed Sections)

Subtopic 7 of 7

1. Formal Technical Definition

The Bredt-Batho theory (or Bredt's formulas) provides the analytical framework for calculating shear flow, shear stress, and torsional stiffness in single-cell and multi-cell closed thin-walled tubes subjected to torsion. It establishes that torque is balanced by the product of twice the enclosed area and the constant shear flow.

2. Basic Concept & Physical Mechanism

Aircraft wings are rarely single-cell tubes; modern wings feature multiple internal spars creating multi-cell closed boxes (e.g., fuel tank cells, leading-edge cells, main spar cells). The Bredt-Batho theory extends single-cell shear flow analysis to multi-cell structures by applying twist compatibility across shared internal webs. Each cell has its own shear flow ($q_1, q_2, \dots$), and shared webs carry the algebraic difference in shear flow ($q_i - q_j$).

3. Mathematical Derivation & Equations

For a single-cell tube: $$q = \frac{T}{2 A_m}, \quad \theta = \frac{T L}{4 A_m^2 G} \oint \frac{ds}{t}$$ For a two-cell tube subjected to total torque $T$: $$T = 2 A_1 q_1 + 2 A_2 q_2$$ Using the rate of twist compatibility ($\frac{d\theta_1}{dx} = \frac{d\theta_2}{dx}$): $$\frac{1}{2 A_1 G} \left( \oint_1 \frac{q}{t} ds - \int_{\text{shared}} \frac{q_2}{t} ds \right) = \frac{1}{2 A_2 G} \left( \oint_2 \frac{q}{t} ds - \int_{\text{shared}} \frac{q_1}{t} ds \right)$$ Solving this simultaneous equation yields the redundant shear flows $q_1$ and $q_2$.

7. UPSC Traps & Common Misconceptions

Trap: Assuming shear flow in the shared web of a multi-cell tube is simply $q_1 + q_2$. Correction: The shear flow in the shared internal web is the algebraic difference of the adjacent cell shear flows ($q_1 - q_2$ or $q_2 - q_1$), depending on the assumed circulation direction.

8. Step-by-Step Worked Numerical

Problem: A single-cell thin-walled closed box beam has a rectangular cross-section of centerline dimensions $a = 800 \text{ mm}$ and $b = 400 \text{ mm}$. Uniform wall thickness $t = 3.0 \text{ mm}$. It is subjected to a torsional moment $T = 40 \text{ kN}\cdot\text{m}$. If shear modulus $G = 27 \text{ GPa}$, calculate: (a) Shear flow $q$, (b) Maximum shear stress $\tau$, and (c) Rate of twist $\frac{d\theta}{dx}$.

Step 1: Enclosed Area ($A_m$):
$$A_m = 0.8 \times 0.4 = 0.32 \text{ m}^2$$

Step 2: Shear Flow ($q$):
$$q = \frac{T}{2 A_m} = \frac{40,000}{2 \times 0.32} = \frac{40,000}{0.64} = 62,500 \text{ N/m} = 62.5 \text{ N/mm}$$

Step 3: Maximum Shear Stress ($\tau$):
$$\tau = \frac{q}{t} = \frac{62,500}{0.003} = 2.083 \times 10^7 \text{ Pa} = 20.83 \text{ MPa}$$

Step 4: Rate of Twist ($\frac{d\theta}{dx}$):
Perimeter $\oint ds = 2(800 + 400) = 2400 \text{ mm} = 2.4 \text{ m}$.
$$\frac{d\theta}{dx} = \frac{T}{4 A_m^2 G} \oint \frac{ds}{t} = \frac{40,000}{4 (0.32)^2 (27 \times 10^9)} \left( \frac{2.4}{0.003} \right)$$ $$\oint \frac{ds}{t} = \frac{2.4 \text{ m}}{0.003 \text{ m}} = 800$$ $$\frac{d\theta}{dx} = \frac{40,000 \times 800}{4 (0.1024)(27 \times 10^9)} = \frac{32,000,000}{1.10592 \times 10^{10}} = 0.00289 \text{ rad/m}$$

Operational Takeaway: Bredt's formulas yield a clean, highly accurate estimate of twist rate and shear stress for closed aircraft box structures without requiring complex 3D finite element meshes.

Aviation Case Studies & DGCA Air Safety Connections

Real-world air crash investigations and airworthiness directives demonstrating why structural torsion, shear flow compliance, and shear centre alignment are vital for Air Safety Officers.

Case Study 1: Helicopter Tail Rotor Drive Shaft Torsional Fatigue Failure

Incident Overview: Multiple accidents involving light twin-engine helicopters operating in offshore roles revealed sudden loss of directional control due to tail rotor drive shaft failure. Investigation by air safety authorities discovered that torsional fatigue cracks initiated at spline interfaces and propagated rapidly through thin-walled tubular drive shafts operating at high rotational speeds.

Air Safety Officer Analysis & DGCA Compliance

Tail rotor drive shafts are thin-walled circular tubes subjected to combined high-frequency torsional oscillations and steady transmission torque. Under DGCA CAR Section 2, Airworthiness Series 'L', operators must enforce mandatory vibration monitoring (HUMS - Health and Usage Monitoring Systems) and fluorescent penetrant inspections at tight hourly intervals. Air Safety Officers auditing maintenance organizations must verify that dynamic balancing and torsional run-out checks are strictly logged.

Case Study 2: Wing Torsion Box Corrosion & Aileron Flutter (Transatlantic Transport)

Incident Overview: A mid-size transport aircraft experienced severe high-speed wing flutter during descent, resulting in structural deformation of the outboard aileron control bracket and localized skin wrinkling. Post-flight investigation revealed severe exfoliation corrosion inside the lower wing skin torsion box, which reduced effective wall thickness and converted a robust closed box into a structurally degraded section.

Air Safety Officer Analysis & DGCA Compliance

Corrosion thinning directly impacts shear flow capacity ($q = \tau \cdot t$). When wall thickness $t$ decreases, shear stress $\tau$ spikes dramatically under identical torsional moments. DGCA CAR Series 'M' mandates strict corrosion prevention and control programs (CPCP). Air Safety Officers must ensure that structural repair manuals (SRM) are followed precisely when patching skin panels so that torsional rigidity and shear centre location are not inadvertently altered.

High-Yield Revision Summary (Last-Minute UPSC ASO Cheat Sheet)

Core equations, definitions, and principles condensed for rapid revision before the UPSC Air Safety Officer examination.

Core Equations

  • Torsion Equation: $\frac{T}{J} = \frac{\tau}{r} = \frac{G \theta}{L}$
  • Polar Moment (Solid): $J = \frac{\pi D^4}{32}$
  • Polar Moment (Hollow): $J = \frac{\pi}{32}(D_o^4 - D_i^4) \approx 2\pi R^3 t$
  • Shear Flow (Closed): $q = \tau \cdot t = \frac{T}{2 A_m}$
  • Torsional Rigidity: $GJ$ (or $GI_t$)
  • Open Section Torsion Constant: $J = \frac{1}{3}\sum s_i t_i^3$
  • Closed Section Torsion Constant: $J = \frac{4 A_m^2}{\oint \frac{ds}{t}}$

Key Conceptual Rules

  • Closed vs. Open: Closed sections resist torsion via continuous shear flow ($O(Rt^3)$); open sections have negligible torsional stiffness ($O(t^3)$).
  • Shear Centre: Transverse load line of action must pass through the shear centre to cause pure bending without twist.
  • Symmetry: If a section has 2 axes of symmetry, shear centre coincides with the centroid.
  • Shared Webs: In multi-cell tubes, shared internal webs carry the algebraic difference in shear flow ($q_1 - q_2$).
