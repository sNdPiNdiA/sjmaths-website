Subtopic 1 of 5

Shear Force Diagram (SFD) & Bending Moment Diagram (BMD)

1. Formal Technical Definition

The Shear Force ($V$) at any cross-section of a beam is the algebraic sum of all transverse forces acting to the left or right of that section. The Bending Moment ($M$) at any cross-section is the algebraic sum of the moments about the centroidal axis of all forces acting to the left or right of that section. SFD and BMD are continuous or piecewise continuous graphical representations of $V(x)$ and $M(x)$ along the longitudinal coordinate $x$ of a beam.

2. Basic Concept & Physical Mechanism

At the continuum level, when an external transverse load (such as aerodynamic lift on a wing or passenger weight on a cabin floor beam) is applied, internal forces must develop across every infinitesimal cross-sectional area to maintain static equilibrium.

The internal resultant force vector resolved normal to the cross-section is the shear force $V$, tending to slide adjacent material planes past one another. The internal resultant couple is the bending moment $M$, which induces differential longitudinal stretching (tension) on one side of the neutral axis and compression on the other. Equilibrium of an infinitesimal beam segment of length $dx$ establishes the fundamental differential relationships: $\frac{dV}{dx} = -q(x)$ (where $q(x)$ is distributed load) and $\frac{dM}{dx} = V(x)$.

3. Mathematical Derivation & Governing Equations

Consider an equilibrium slice of a beam of length $dx$ subjected to distributed load $q(x)$ pointing downwards:

$$\sum F_y = 0 \implies V - q(x)dx - (V + dV) = 0 \implies \frac{dV}{dx} = -q(x)$$

$$\sum M_o = 0 \implies M + Vdx - q(x)dx\left(\frac{dx}{2}\right) - (M + dM) = 0 \implies \frac{dM}{dx} = V(x)$$

$$\text{Integrating between points } A \text{ and } B: \quad V_B - V_A = -\int_{x_A}^{x_B} q(x)dx, \quad M_B - M_A = \int_{x_A}^{x_B} V(x)dx$$

Variables Defined: $V$ = Shear force ($N$), $M$ = Bending moment ($Nm$), $q(x)$ = Distributed transverse load per unit length ($N/m$), $x$ = Longitudinal position along beam axis ($m$).

6. Aircraft Application & DGCA Air Safety Connection

In aircraft structural airworthiness (DGCA CAR Section 2, Series 'L'), accurate SFD and BMD construction is vital for establishing static test programs. Wings generate aerodynamic lift distributed elliptically or trapezoidally along the span. This creates maximum bending moment at the wing root (fuselage attachment fitting), making the root spar joint the most critical fatigue and static failure zone.

Air Safety Implication: Failure to account for fuel-ogive relief (storing heavy Jet A-1 fuel in outboard or inboard tanks that counter-act lift bending moments) can lead to severe over-stressing during high-g maneuvers or turbulence penetration.

7. UPSC Traps & Common Misconceptions

  • Trap: Confusing the sign convention of bending moments. Sagging moment (tension on bottom) is universally positive; hogging moment (tension on top) is negative. UPSC often reverses concentrated loads to test sign convention alertness.
  • Trap: Assuming shear force is zero where bending moment is maximum. Mathematically, $\frac{dM}{dx} = V$. Therefore, max/min bending moment occurs where shear force is zero or passes through zero (points of inflection / local extrema).

8. Step-by-Step Worked Numerical

Problem: A cantilever wing spar of span $L = 10\text{ m}$ is subjected to a uniformly distributed aerodynamic lift load $q = 15\text{ kN/m}$. Calculate the maximum shear force and maximum bending moment.

Given: $L = 10\text{ m}$, $q = 15,000\text{ N/m}$ (upwards, taken positive for magnitude)

Step 1 (Shear Force at Root, $x = 0$):

$V(x) = \int_{x}^{L} q \, dx = q(L - x) \implies V(0) = 15,000 \times 10 = 150,000\text{ N} = 150\text{ kN}$.

Step 2 (Bending Moment at Root, $x = 0$):

$M(x) = -\int_{0}^{x} V(x) dx \implies M(0) = -\frac{qL^2}{2} = -\frac{15,000 \times (10)^2}{2} = -750,000\text{ N}\cdot\text{m} = -750\text{ kN}\cdot\text{m}$.

Operational Interpretation: The wing attachment bulkhead must withstand a staggering 750 kN·m of root bending moment under 1g flight, which scales up to 1,875 kN·m at ultimate limit load ($n = 2.5 \times 1.5$ safety factor).

Subtopic 2 of 5

The Flexure Formula & Pure Bending

1. Formal Technical Definition

The Flexure Formula relates the normal bending stress $\sigma_x$ at any point in a beam cross-section to the applied bending moment $M$, the area moment of inertia $I$, and the perpendicular distance $y$ from the neutral axis: $$\sigma_x = -\frac{My}{I} \quad \text{or} \quad \sigma_x = \frac{My}{I}$$ depending on sign convention. It is derived under the Euler-Bernoulli beam assumptions of plane sections remaining plane and normal to the neutral axis after deformation.

2. Basic Concept & Physical Mechanism

When a pure bending moment is applied to a beam, longitudinal metallic or composite fibers on the convex side experience tensile strain (atoms pulled apart against interatomic bond forces), while fibers on the concave side experience compressive strain (atoms pushed closer together).

Between these regions lies a surface of zero strain—the neutral surface. Assuming linear elastic behavior governed by Hooke's Law ($\sigma = E\epsilon$), the stress is strictly proportional to the distance from the neutral axis. The resisting moment generated by this internal stress distribution must exactly balance the applied external bending moment.

3. Mathematical Derivation & Governing Equations

Consider a beam segment bent into a circular arc of radius of curvature $\rho$. The longitudinal strain $\varepsilon_x$ at distance $y$ from the neutral axis is:

$$\varepsilon_x = \frac{ds' - ds}{ds} = \frac{(\rho - y)d\theta - \rho d\theta}{\rho d\theta} = -\frac{y}{\rho}$$

$$\text{By Hooke's Law } (\sigma_x = E \varepsilon_x): \quad \sigma_x = -\frac{Ey}{\rho}$$

$$\text{Enforcing internal moment equilibrium } M = \int_A (-\sigma_x) y \, dA = \int_A \frac{Ey^2}{\rho} dA = \frac{E}{\rho}\int_A y^2 dA = \frac{EI}{\rho}$$

$$\text{Eliminating } \rho \text{ yields the Flexure Formula:} \quad \sigma_x = -\frac{My}{I}$$

Variables Defined: $\sigma_x$ = Normal bending stress ($Pa$), $M$ = Bending moment ($N\cdot m$), $y$ = Distance from neutral axis ($m$), $I$ = Area moment of inertia about neutral axis ($m^4$), $E$ = Young's modulus ($Pa$), $\rho$ = Radius of curvature ($m$).

6. Aircraft Application & DGCA Air Safety Connection

The flexure formula $\sigma = My/I$ is the cornerstone for sizing commercial aircraft wing spar caps. Because bending stress increases linearly with distance $y$ from the neutral axis, aircraft designers place the majority of structural mass (spar caps, stringers) as far from the neutral axis as possible—hence the I-beam or box-beam cross-section geometry.

Air Safety Implication: Corrosion pitting on lower wing skin surfaces (tension side) acts as a severe stress concentration factor ($K_t$), lowering the fatigue threshold and leading to catastrophic in-flight fatigue crack propagation (e.g., Aloha Airlines Flight 243 type structural degradation).

7. UPSC Traps & Common Misconceptions

  • Trap: Applying the flexure formula to non-homogeneous beams (e.g., carbon-fiber reinforced composites bonded to aluminum honeycombs) without transforming cross-sectional areas using the modular ratio $n = E_2 / E_1$.
  • Trap: Assuming the neutral axis always passes through the geometric centroid of an asymmetric cross-section. For asymmetric or composite sections, the neutral axis coincides with the centroid of the transformed section, not the geometric center.

8. Step-by-Step Worked Numerical

Problem: A rectangular aluminum spar has a width $b = 50\text{ mm}$ and height $h = 150\text{ mm}$. It is subjected to a bending moment $M = 45\text{ kN}\cdot\text{m}$. Calculate the maximum tensile bending stress in the outer fiber.

Given: $b = 0.05\text{ m}$, $h = 0.15\text{ m}$, $M = 45,000\text{ N}\cdot\text{m}$

Step 1 (Calculate Moment of Inertia $I$ for rectangular section):

$I = \frac{bh^3}{12} = \frac{0.05 \times (0.15)^3}{12} = \frac{0.05 \times 0.003375}{12} = 1.40625 \times 10^{-5}\text{ m}^4$.

Step 2 (Determine max distance $y_{max}$):

$y_{max} = \frac{h}{2} = \frac{0.15}{2} = 0.075\text{ m}$.

Step 3 (Calculate maximum bending stress $\sigma_{max}$):

$\sigma_{max} = \frac{M y_{max}}{I} = \frac{45,000 \times 0.075}{1.40625 \times 10^{-5}} = \frac{3,375}{1.40625 \times 10^{-5}} = 240,000,000\text{ Pa} = 240\text{ MPa}$.

Operational Interpretation: A bending stress of 240 MPa is safely below the 345 MPa yield strength of 2024-T3 aluminum, providing an initial static safety margin of $\frac{345}{240} = 1.438$, though additional fatigue and ultimate factors must be satisfied.

Subtopic 3 of 5

Neutral Axis & Section Modulus ($Z$)

1. Formal Technical Definition

The Neutral Axis (NA) is the locus of longitudinal points within a beam cross-section where normal bending stress is identically zero ($\sigma = 0$). The Section Modulus ($Z$ or $S$) is a geometric property of a cross-section defined as the ratio of the area moment of inertia to the maximum distance from the neutral axis: $$Z = \frac{I}{y_{max}}$$ It quantifies a section's bending resistance capacity.

2. Basic Concept & Physical Mechanism

From a static equilibrium standpoint, the net axial force resulting from pure bending must vanish ($\int \sigma dA = 0$). Since $\sigma = -Ey/\rho$, this condition simplifies to requiring that the first moment of area about the neutral axis is zero ($\int y dA = 0$).

This dictates that the neutral axis must pass precisely through the centroid of the cross-section (for homogeneous, linearly elastic materials). The section modulus $Z$ condenses $I$ and $y_{max}$ into a single index, allowing engineers to immediately compute the maximum bending stress via $\sigma_{max} = \frac{M}{Z}$.

3. Mathematical Derivations & Standard Formulas

Derivation of centroidal location for asymmetric cross-sections and standard section moduli:

$$\bar{y} = \frac{\sum y_i A_i}{\sum A_i} = \frac{\int y \, dA}{\int dA}$$

$$\text{Rectangular Section } (b \times h): \quad I = \frac{bh^3}{12}, \quad y_{max} = \frac{h}{2} \implies Z = \frac{bh^2}{6}$$

$$\text{Circular Solid Section } (radius \ r): \quad I = \frac{\pi r^4}{4}, \quad y_{max} = r \implies Z = \frac{\pi r^3}{4}$$

$$\text{Thin-walled Circular Tube}: \quad Z \approx \pi r^2 t \quad (\text{for } t \ll r)$$

Variables Defined: $\bar{y}$ = Centroidal height ($m$), $A_i$ = Elemental area ($m^2$), $b$ = Width ($m$), $h$ = Height ($m$), $r$ = Radius ($m$), $t$ = Wall thickness ($m$).

4. Dimensions & Units

6. Aircraft Application & DGCA Air Safety Connection

Fuselage bending moments induced by landing impact loads and pressurization cycles require optimized section moduli in stringers and frames. Maximizing $Z$ per unit weight ($Z/W$) is the primary objective in aerospace structural optimization.

Air Safety Implication: Chemical milling or improper mechanical repairs that shave off stringer flanges reduce $Z$ locally, creating high stress concentrations that violate type design specifications under DGCA CAR Section 2 airworthiness directives.

7. UPSC Traps & Common Misconceptions

  • Trap: Assuming $Z$ is symmetric for non-symmetric sections (like T-sections or L-sections). Asymmetric sections have two distinct section moduli ($Z_{top}$ and $Z_{bottom}$) because $y_{top} \neq y_{bottom}$.
  • Trap: Confusing polar moment of inertia ($J$) with area moment of inertia ($I$). $J$ is for torsion; $I$ is for bending.

8. Step-by-Step Worked Numerical

Problem: Determine the section modulus $Z$ of a hollow circular tube used as a tail boom, having outer diameter $d_o = 200\text{ mm}$ and wall thickness $t = 5\text{ mm}$ ($d_i = 190\text{ mm}$).

Given: $r_o = 0.1\text{ m}$, $r_i = 0.095\text{ m}$

Step 1 (Calculate Area Moment of Inertia $I$):

$I = \frac{\pi}{4}(r_o^4 - r_i^4) = \frac{\pi}{4}((0.1)^4 - (0.095)^4) = \frac{\pi}{4}(0.0001 - 0.00008145) = \frac{\pi}{4}(1.855 \times 10^{-5}) = 1.456 \times 10^{-5}\text{ m}^4$.

Step 2 (Calculate Section Modulus $Z$):

$Z = \frac{I}{r_o} = \frac{1.456 \times 10^{-5}}{0.1} = 1.456 \times 10^{-4}\text{ m}^3 = 145,600\text{ mm}^3$.

Operational Interpretation: Hollow tubular sections provide exceptional torsional and bending section moduli relative to their weight, making them ideal for helicopter tail booms and fuselage longerons.

Subtopic 4 of 5

Transverse Shear-Stress Distribution ($q = \tau t$)

1. Formal Technical Definition

The transverse shear stress $\tau$ distributed across a beam cross-section subjected to shear force $V$ is governed by the Jourawski Shear Stress Formula: $$\tau = \frac{V Q}{I t}$$ where $Q$ is the first moment of the cut-off area about the neutral axis, $I$ is the total moment of inertia, and $t$ is the local section thickness.

2. Basic Concept & Physical Mechanism

While the flexure formula accounts for normal stresses from bending moments, non-zero shear force $V(x)$ requires longitudinal variation in bending moments ($\frac{dM}{dx} = V$).

By examining equilibrium of an infinitesimal longitudinal block cut from a beam, unequal normal forces on opposing faces require complementary shear stresses acting horizontally and vertically (due to shear stress reciprocity: $\tau_{xy} = \tau_{yx}$). In a rectangular cross-section, shear stress is zero at the extreme outer fibers (where $Q = 0$) and reaches a parabolic maximum at the neutral axis.

3. Mathematical Derivations & Governing Equations

Deriving the Jourawski formula from longitudinal force equilibrium:

$$\sum F_x = 0 \implies \int_{A'} \frac{M_1 y}{I} dA - \int_{A'} \frac{M_2 y}{I} dA + \tau t \, dx = 0$$

$$\text{Since } M_2 - M_1 = dM \text{ and } \frac{dM}{dx} = V: \quad \tau t \, dx = \frac{dM}{I} \int_{A'} y \, dA \implies \tau = \frac{V}{I t} \int_{A'} y \, dA$$

$$\text{Defining } Q = \int_{A'} y \, dA \text{ (first moment of area):} \quad \tau = \frac{V Q}{I t}$$

$$\text{For rectangular cross-section:} \quad \tau_{max} = \frac{3V}{2A} = 1.5 \tau_{avg}$$

Variables Defined: $\tau$ = Shear stress ($Pa$), $V$ = Shear force ($N$), $Q$ = First moment of cut area ($m^3$), $I$ = Area moment of inertia ($m^4$), $t$ = Thickness ($m$), $A$ = Total cross-sectional area ($m^2$).

6. Aircraft Application & DGCA Air Safety Connection

In thin-walled aircraft wing spars and fuselage webs, shear forces are predominantly carried by the thin vertical web plates, while bending moments are carried by heavy cap flanges. The concept of shear flow ($q = \tau t$) is critical for sizing rivet spacing and fastener pitch joining skin panels to spar webs.

Air Safety Implication: Inadequate rivet shear pitch or thin-web buckling under high shear loads can lead to catastrophic shear web failure during pull-up maneuvers, directly investigated under DGCA airworthiness incident protocols.

7. UPSC Traps & Common Misconceptions

  • Trap: Assuming shear stress is uniformly distributed across a beam cross-section. In reality, shear stress is zero at extreme fibers and parabolic (or quadratic) in rectangular beams, peaking at the neutral axis.
  • Trap: Forgetting to update the thickness $t$ when evaluating shear stress in stepped or I-beam sections where web thickness differs from flange thickness.

8. Step-by-Step Worked Numerical

Problem: A rectangular wooden or composite spar has width $b = 80\text{ mm}$ and height $h = 200\text{ mm}$. If the applied vertical shear force is $V = 50\text{ kN}$, calculate the maximum shear stress at the neutral axis.

Given: $b = 0.08\text{ m}$, $h = 0.2\text{ m}$, $V = 50,000\text{ N}$

Step 1 (Calculate Area Moment of Inertia $I$):

$I = \frac{bh^3}{12} = \frac{0.08 \times (0.2)^3}{12} = \frac{0.08 \times 0.008}{12} = 5.333 \times 10^{-5}\text{ m}^4$.

Step 2 (Calculate First Moment of Area $Q$ above Neutral Axis):

$Q = \bar{y}' A' = \left(\frac{h}{4}\right)\left(\frac{bh}{2}\right) = \left(0.05\right)\left(0.08 \times 0.1\right) = 0.05 \times 0.008 = 4.0 \times 10^{-4}\text{ m}^3$.

Step 3 (Calculate Maximum Shear Stress $\tau_{max}$):

$\tau_{max} = \frac{V Q}{I t} = \frac{50,000 \times 4.0 \times 10^{-4}}{5.333 \times 10^{-5} \times 0.08} = \frac{20}{4.266 \times 10^{-6}} = 4,688,255\text{ Pa} \approx 4.69\text{ MPa}$.

Operational Interpretation: Using the shortcut formula $\tau_{max} = \frac{3V}{2A} = \frac{3(50,000)}{2(0.08 \times 0.2)} = \frac{150,000}{0.032} = 4.6875\text{ MPa}$, confirming exact agreement with Jourawski integration.

Subtopic 5 of 5

Deflection Fundamentals & Elastic Curve

1. Formal Technical Definition

The Deflection ($v(x)$) of a beam is the transverse displacement of its neutral axis from its undeformed position. The governing differential equation of the elastic curve, derived from Euler-Bernoulli beam theory, relates internal bending moments to curvature via: $$EI \frac{d^2v}{dx^2} = M(x)$$ Successive integration yields slope ($\theta = \frac{dv}{dx}$) and deflection ($v(x)$) subject to boundary conditions.

2. Basic Concept & Physical Mechanism

When loads act on an aircraft wing or fuselage, the entire structure bends like an elastic spring. The curvature $\kappa$ at any station along the beam is inversely proportional to the flexural rigidity ($EI$) and directly proportional to the bending moment $M(x)$.

For small deflections ($\frac{dv}{dx} \ll 1$), curvature is accurately approximated by the second derivative of the deflection curve ($\frac{d^2v}{dx^2}$). Maintaining strict deflection limits is essential in aviation to prevent aerodynamic control surface binding, wingtip strike against runways, or cabin door jamming under high-g flight.

3. Mathematical Derivations & Standard Formulas

Governing differential equations and standard cantilever/simply supported beam deflection formulas:

$$\text{Curvature relation:} \quad \frac{1}{\rho} \approx \frac{d^2v}{dx^2} = \frac{M(x)}{EI}$$

$$\text{Differentiating with respect to } x \text{ yields shear and load relations:} \quad EI \frac{d^3v}{dx^3} = V(x), \quad EI \frac{d^4v}{dx^4} = -q(x)$$

$$\text{Cantilever beam with end load } P: \quad v_{max} = \frac{PL^3}{3EI}, \quad \theta_{max} = \frac{PL^2}{2EI}$$

$$\text{Cantilever beam with UDL } q: \quad v_{max} = \frac{qL^4}{8EI}, \quad \theta_{max} = \frac{qL^3}{6EI}$$

Variables Defined: $v$ = Deflection ($m$), $\theta$ = Slope (radians), $E$ = Young's modulus ($Pa$), $I$ = Moment of inertia ($m^4$), $P$ = Point load ($N$), $L$ = Span length ($m$).

6. Aircraft Application & DGCA Air Safety Connection

Modern high-aspect-ratio composite wings (like the Boeing 787 or Airbus A350) undergo massive upward deflections (exceeding 3 meters at wingtips). Air safety certification under DGCA and FAA/EASA rules requires static ground bend tests where wings are loaded up to 150% of limit load (ultimate load) to verify that deflections do not cause structural failure or control surface jamming.

Air Safety Implication: Excessive wing flexibility can alter local angle of attack (aeroelastic aero-servo-elastic coupling/flutter), necessitating rigorous ground vibration testing (GVT) and flight flutter testing.

7. UPSC Traps & Common Misconceptions

  • Trap: Confusing the formula for a cantilever beam tip deflection ($\frac{PL^3}{3EI}$) with a simply supported beam center deflection ($\frac{PL^3}{48EI}$). UPSC frequently tests these boundary condition multipliers.
  • Trap: Assuming superposition cannot be applied to deflections. Because $EI \frac{d^2v}{dx^2} = M$ is a linear differential equation, linear superposition applies directly for combined loading cases.

8. Step-by-Step Worked Numerical

Problem: A composite cabin floor beam of length $L = 2.0\text{ m}$ is simply supported and subjected to a central concentrated seat load $P = 12\text{ kN}$. Given flexural rigidity $EI = 4.5 \times 10^4\text{ N}\cdot\text{m}^2$, calculate the maximum central deflection $v_{max}$.

Given: $L = 2.0\text{ m}$, $P = 12,000\text{ N}$, $EI = 45,000\text{ N}\cdot\text{m}^2$

Step 1 (Identify Simply Supported Central Load Deflection Formula):

$v_{max} = \frac{PL^3}{48EI}$.

Step 2 (Substitute values):

$v_{max} = \frac{12,000 \times (2.0)^3}{48 \times 45,000} = \frac{12,000 \times 8}{2,160,000} = \frac{96,000}{2,160,000} = 0.04444\text{ m} = 44.44\text{ mm}$.

Operational Interpretation: A central floor beam deflection of 44.4 mm under a 12 kN load must be checked against interior passenger compartment clearances to prevent cabin floor panel buckling or galley equipment jamming during emergency landing deceleration.

Aviation Case Studies & DGCA Air Safety Connections

Real-world structural failures analyzed through the lens of beam bending, shear stress concentrations, and airworthiness regulations (DGCA CAR Section 2 / FAA FAR Part 25).

Case Study 01

Aloha Airlines Flight 243 (Fuselage Bending & Lap Joint Shear Failure)

On April 28, 1988, a Boeing 737-200 experienced a major failure and decompression when a 35-meter section of the upper forward fuselage tore off during flight. The root cause was multi-site fatigue cracking along longitudinal fuselage lap joints, severely degrading the skin's ability to transmit hoop tension and shear stresses.

Beam & Stress Connection: Fuselage structures act as thin-walled hollow beams subjected to internal pressurization hoop stress and vertical bending moments from aerodynamic lift and landing loads. Lap joints carry high shear flow ($q = \tau t$). When cold-bond adhesive failed, load transferred entirely to rivets, creating intense local stress concentrations ($K_t$).

DGCA CAR Section 2 Impact: Prompted mandatory Aging Aircraft Safety Programs (AASP), enhanced NDT (Non-Destructive Testing) eddy current inspections for fuselage lap joints, and strict damage-tolerance analysis requirements for type certificate holders.

Case Study 02

Qantas Flight 32 (A380 Uncontained Engine Failure & Spar Web Shear Damage)

On November 4, 2010, an Airbus A380 experienced an uncontained turbine failure in engine #2. High-energy shrapnel severed numerous control lines, hydraulic pipes, and critically gouged the front spar web of the left wing box structure.

Beam & Stress Connection: The wing box front spar web is a primary shear-resisting element carrying $V(x)$. Shrapnel penetration severed local web thickness $t$, drastically reducing local shear resistance ($\tau = \frac{VQ}{It}$). The structure survived only because of redundant internal rib and stringer load paths (fail-safe design philosophy).

DGCA CAR Section 2 Impact: Reinforced stringent engine burst containment criteria and mandatory structural damage tolerance inspections for multi-engine widebody aircraft operating in Indian airspace.

Core Stress & Geometry Formulas

$$\sigma_x = -\frac{My}{I} = \frac{M}{Z} \quad (\text{Flexure Formula})$$
$$\tau = \frac{VQ}{It} \quad (\text{Jourawski Shear Stress})$$
$$Z = \frac{I}{y_{max}} \quad (\text{Section Modulus})$$

🎤 UPSC Interview Corner (Air Safety Officer Board Q&A)

Q: As an Air Safety Officer inspecting an older transport aircraft showing wing root skin wrinkles, what structural mechanics failure mode would you suspect?

Model Answer: Wing skin wrinkles near the wing root typically indicate shear web buckling or compressive yield buckling under high torsional and bending moments. When a thin-walled aluminum panel exceeds its critical buckling shear flow ($q_{crit}$), diagonal tension field action occurs. As an ASO, I would immediately ground the aircraft for detailed NDT eddy current inspection, check for excessive wing root bending moment fatigue cracks, and review maintenance logs against DGCA structural repair manuals (SRM).

Q: Why do commercial transport aircraft feature high-aspect-ratio flexible wings instead of rigid, thick box beams?

Model Answer: Flexible, high-aspect-ratio wings significantly reduce induced drag, improving fuel efficiency (crucial for airline economics and carbon emissions reduction). Furthermore, wing flexibility acts as a natural "gust alleviation" mechanism: when the aircraft encounters a sharp vertical gust, the wing flexes upward, spilling lift at the tips and reducing the peak root bending moment by up to 25-30% compared to a rigid wing.
