Torsion equation

Textbook-grade UPSC Air Safety Officer deep-dive: Comprehensive theoretical foundations, analytical derivations, engineering reference data, DGCA/ICAO airworthiness integration, 14 practice MCQs, and timed mini-test.

How This Topic Is Useful for Air Safety Officers & Aircraft Operations
DGCA CAR & ICAO Mandate

In aviation operations and UPSC ASO assessments, this parameter is critical across 4 core operational pillars:

  • 1. Aircraft Systems & Flight Deck Monitoring: Torque transducers in turboprop and helicopter drive systems rely directly on elastic twist calibration for cockpit power gauges.
  • 2. Flight Safety & Dispatch Envelopes: Exceeding maximum allowable torque limits during engine start or maneuvers causes permanent torsional deformation and structural failure.
  • 3. DGCA CAR & ICAO Airworthiness Compliance: DGCA CAR Section 2 and ICAO Annex 8 mandate strict inspection of torque tubes, control linkages, and drive shafts during C-checks.
  • 4. Air Safety Investigation & Incident Prevention: ASOs examine torsional failure surfaces (chevron patterns, 45-degree shear lips) during wreckage analysis to diagnose engine over-torque events.

Pillar 1: Formal Technical Definition & Nomenclature

The torsion equation relates internal torque to shear stress and angular twist in a circular cross-section shaft subjected to pure torsion, expressed as $\frac{T}{J} = \frac{\tau}{r} = \frac{G\theta}{L}$.
  • Physical Meaning: It defines the resistance of structural shafts against rotational twisting loads.
  • Governing Law: Hooke's Law in shear combined with geometric compatibility of twist.
  • Continuum Boundary: Assumes homogeneous, isotropic, linearly elastic materials undergoing small twists.

Nomenclature & Symbols

  • $T$ = Applied internal torque or twisting moment ($\text{N}\cdot\text{m}$)
  • $J$ = Polar moment of inertia of the cross-section ($\text{m}^4$)
  • $\tau$ = Shear stress at a specific radial distance ($\text{Pa}$ or $\text{N}/\text{m}^2$)
  • $r$ = Radial distance from the centroidal axis to the fiber ($\text{m}$)
  • $G$ = Shear modulus or modulus of rigidity ($\text{Pa}$)
  • $\theta$ = Angle of twist of the shaft over length $L$ (Radians)
  • $L$ = Length of the structural shaft section ($\text{m}$)

Pillar 2: Microscopic Physics & Continuum Mechanics

1. Applied Torsional Moment: External rotational loads are applied at structural interfaces like engine output pads or control linkages.
2. Internal Shear Stress Distribution: Resistance to rotation creates complementary shear stresses varying linearly from zero at the neutral axis to maximum at the outer fiber.
3. Angular Deformation: Cross-sections rotate relative to one another along the longitudinal axis without warping in circular geometries.
4. Strain Energy Storage: Elastic strain energy is stored within the twisted volume until yielding or ultimate shear failure occurs.

Pillar 3: Mathematical Derivations & Governing Laws

Step 1: Kinematic Twist Relation

Relating shear strain to the angle of twist per unit length.

$$\gamma = \frac{r\theta}{L}$$
Step 2: Constitutive Elastic Law

Applying Hooke's Law in shear to determine stress distribution.

$$\tau = G\gamma = \frac{G r \theta}{L}$$
Step 3: Equilibrium Torque Integral

Equating applied torque to the integral of internal resisting shear forces.

$$T = \int_A \tau r \, dA = \int_A \left(\frac{G\theta}{L} r\right) r \, dA = \frac{G\theta}{L} \int_A r^2 \, dA$$
Step 4: Final Working Law

Introducing polar moment of inertia $J = \int_A r^2 \, dA$ to yield the final torsion equation.

$$\frac{T}{J} = \frac{\tau}{r} = \frac{G\theta}{L}$$

Pillar 4: Dimensional Analysis, SI/CGS Units & Conversion Factors

  • Fundamental Dimensions: Torque ($[M L^2 T^{-2}]$), Shear Stress ($[M L^{-1} T^{-2}]$), Polar Moment ($[L^4]$), Modulus ($[M L^{-1} T^{-2}]$), Angle ($[1]$).
  • SI Unit: Torque in Newton-meters ($\text{N}\cdot\text{m}$), stress in Pascals ($\text{Pa}$), angle in radians.
  • CGS Unit: Torque in Dyne-centimeters ($\text{dyn}\cdot\text{cm}$), stress in Barye ($\text{dyn}/\text{cm}^2$).
  • Aeronautical & Imperial Equivalents: Torque in Pound-inches ($ ext{lb}\cdot\text{in}$) or Pound-feet ($ ext{lb}\cdot\text{ft}$); stress in psi; angle in degrees.

Pillar 6: Aircraft Airworthiness, Systems & DGCA CAR / ICAO Compliance

  • Regulatory Standards (DGCA CAR & ICAO): DGCA CAR Section 2, Series 'B', Part III mandates rigorous non-destructive testing (NDT) of critical drive shafts and control torque tubes.
  • Critical Aircraft Systems: Helicopter main rotor shafts, turboprop accessory gearboxes, and flight control actuation tubes depend heavily on torsion limits.
  • Aviation Incident Case Study: In the Robinson R66 main rotor drive shaft failure incident, torsional fatigue cracking initiated at internal spline notches due to cyclic over-torque. Post-incident mandates required enhanced magnetic particle inspection intervals.

Pillar 7: High-Frequency UPSC Examiner Traps & Misconceptions

Trap 1: Confusing Polar Moment ($J$) with Area Moment of Inertia ($I$)

❌ Examiner Trap: Using $I = \frac{\pi d^4}{64}$ in the torsion equation for circular shafts.

✅ UPSC Truth: Torsion requires the polar moment of inertia $J = \frac{\pi d^4}{32}$ for solid circular sections.

Trap 2: Applying the Torsion Equation to Non-Circular Sections

❌ Examiner Trap: Assuming $\tau = \frac{Tr}{J}$ works for rectangular, open, or thin-walled aircraft box beams.

✅ UPSC Truth: The classic torsion equation is strictly valid for circular cross-sections; non-circular sections warp and require Prandtl's stress function or specialized torsion constants.

Trap 3: Unit Mismatch in Angle of Twist Calculations

❌ Examiner Trap: Substituting angle of twist $\theta$ directly in degrees into the governing equation without conversion.

✅ UPSC Truth: The variable $\theta$ in the torsion formula is strictly dimensionless and must always be evaluated in radians.

Trap 4: Neglecting Hollow Shaft Inner Radius in Polar Moment

❌ Examiner Trap: Using $J = \frac{\pi d_o^4}{32}$ for a hollow torque tube without subtracting the inner diameter.

✅ UPSC Truth: For hollow shafts, $J = \frac{\pi (d_o^4 - d_i^4)}{32}$ where $d_o$ and $d_i$ are outer and inner diameters.

Pillar 8: Step-by-Step Worked Aircraft Numerical Problem

Realistic Aircraft Scenario

A solid steel helicopter tail rotor drive shaft of outer diameter $0.05\text{ m}$ and length $3.0\text{ m}$ transmits a torque of $1200\text{ N}\cdot\text{m}$. Given the shear modulus of steel is $80\text{ GPa}$, calculate the maximum shear stress and total angle of twist.

Given Flight Parameters:
Diameter $d = 0.05\text{ m}$, Radius $r = 0.025\text{ m}$, Length $L = 3.0\text{ m}$, Torque $T = 1200\text{ N}\cdot\text{m}$, Shear Modulus $G = 80 \times 10^9\text{ Pa}$.
Governing Formula:
$$\tau_{\max} = \frac{Tr}{J}, \quad \theta = \frac{TL}{GJ}, \quad J = \frac{\pi d^4}{32}$$
Step-by-Step Analytical Solution:
Step 1: Compute polar moment of inertia $J = \frac{\pi (0.05)^4}{32} = 6.1359 \times 10^{-7} \text{ m}^4$. Step 2: Calculate maximum shear stress $\tau_{\max} = \frac{1200 \times 0.025}{6.1359 \times 10^{-7}} = 4.889 \times 10^7 \text{ Pa} = 48.89 \text{ MPa}$. Step 3: Calculate angle of twist $\theta = \frac{1200 \times 3.0}{(80 \times 10^9)(6.1359 \times 10^{-7})} = 0.0733 \text{ radians} = 4.20^\circ$.
Final Answer: Maximum Shear Stress = $48.89\text{ MPa}$, Angle of Twist = $0.0733\text{ rad}$ ($4.20^\circ$).
Air Safety Officer Operational Takeaway:

Evaluating peak shear stress ensures the drive shaft operates safely below the yield limit of the alloy, preventing in-flight torsional fracture during high-power maneuvers.

Question 1 • conceptual
What is the primary assumption regarding cross-sections in the standard derivation of the torsion equation for circular shafts?
A Plane cross-sections remain plane and unwarped after twisting
B Cross-sections warp significantly due to axial stress gradients
C Cross-sections expand radially under applied torque
D Shear stress remains uniform across the entire radius
Solution & Technical Rationale:
For circular shafts, radial symmetry ensures that plane cross-sections remain plane and do not warp during pure torsion.
Question 2 • conceptual
How does shear stress vary radially across a solid circular shaft subjected to torsion?
A Parabolically, with maximum at the center
B Linearly, from zero at the center to maximum at the outer surface
C Uniformly across the entire cross-section
D Inversely proportional to the cube of the radius
Solution & Technical Rationale:
From $\tau = \frac{Tr}{J}$, shear stress is directly proportional to radial distance $r$, being zero at the neutral axis and maximum at $r = c$.
Question 3 • statement-based
Consider the following statements regarding the polar moment of inertia $J$ for circular shafts: 1. For a solid circular shaft, $J = \frac{\pi d^4}{32}$. 2. For a hollow circular shaft, $J = \frac{\pi (d_o^4 - d_i^4)}{32}$. 3. $J$ has the fundamental dimension of $[L^3]$. Which of the statements given above are correct?
A 1 and 2 only
B 2 and 3 only
C 1 and 3 only
D 1, 2 and 3
Solution & Technical Rationale:
Statements 1 and 2 are correct formulas. Statement 3 is false because $J = \int r^2 dA$ has dimensions of $[L^4]$, not $[L^3]$.
Question 4 • numerical
A solid steel shaft of diameter $0.1\text{ m}$ is subjected to a pure torque of $5\text{ kN}\cdot\text{m}$. What is the maximum shear stress induced in the shaft?
A $25.46 \text{ MPa}$
B $50.93 \text{ MPa}$
C $101.86 \text{ MPa}$
D $12.73 \text{ MPa}$
Solution & Technical Rationale:
Radius $r = 0.05\text{ m}$. $J = \frac{\pi (0.1)^4}{32} = 9.8175 \times 10^{-6}\text{ m}^4$. $\tau = \frac{5000 \times 0.05}{9.8175 \times 10^{-6}} = 2.546 \times 10^7\text{ Pa}$ ... wait, recomputing: $\tau = \frac{16 T}{\pi d^3} = \frac{16 \times 5000}{\pi (0.1)^3} = \frac{80000}{\pi \times 0.001} = 25.46 \text{ MPa}$? Let's check: $16 \times 5000 / (3.14159 imes 0.001) = 25,464,790 \text{ Pa} = 25.46 \text{ MPa}$. Option A is correct.
Question 5 • assertion-reasoning
Assertion (A): The classic torsion equation cannot be directly applied to thin-walled open structural sections like channels or angles. Reason (R): Open sections warp freely and experience large shear stresses due to primary torsion that cannot be modeled by polar moments of inertia.
A Both (A) and (R) are true and (R) is the correct explanation of (A)
B Both (A) and (R) are true but (R) is not the correct explanation of (A)
C (A) is true but (R) is false
D (A) is false but (R) is true
Solution & Technical Rationale:
Open sections have very low torsional rigidity because cross-sections warp freely, making the polar moment of inertia formula invalid.
Question 6 • conceptual
What is the torsional stiffness $k_t$ of a structural shaft defined as?
A Torque per unit angle of twist ($T/\theta$)
B Angle of twist per unit torque ($\theta/T$)
C Shear stress per unit radius
D Modulus of rigidity multiplied by polar moment
Solution & Technical Rationale:
Torsional stiffness is defined as the resisting torque required to produce a unit angle of twist, given by $k_t = \frac{GJ}{L}$.
Question 7 • numerical
If the diameter of a solid circular shaft is doubled while keeping the applied torque constant, what happens to the maximum shear stress?
A It increases by a factor of 2
B It decreases by a factor of 2
C It decreases by a factor of 8
D It remains unchanged
Solution & Technical Rationale:
Since $\tau = \frac{16T}{\pi d^3}$, doubling $d$ increases $d^3$ by a factor of 8, thereby reducing shear stress by a factor of 8.
Question 8 • statement-based
Which of the following material properties directly govern the angular twist of an aircraft torque tube under load? 1. Shear Modulus ($G$) 2. Young's Modulus ($E$) 3. Poisson's Ratio ($\nu$) Which of the statements given above are correct?
A 1 only
B 1 and 3 only
C 2 only
D 1, 2 and 3
Solution & Technical Rationale:
The angle of twist formula is $\theta = \frac{TL}{GJ}$, meaning only the shear modulus $G$ directly governs torsional twist.
Question 9 • conceptual
What type of fracture surface is typically observed in a brittle aircraft steel shaft failing purely under torsion?
A Flat transverse fracture perpendicular to the axis
B 45-degree helical fracture surface due to principal tensile stress
C Longitudinal splitting along the fiber lines
D Ductile cup-and-cone fracture
Solution & Technical Rationale:
Brittle materials fail in tension; pure torsion creates principal tensile stresses at 45 degrees to the shaft axis, resulting in a characteristic helical fracture.
Question 10 • numerical
A hollow circular shaft has an outer diameter of $100\text{ mm}$ and an inner diameter of $60\text{ mm}$. What is the ratio of inner diameter to outer diameter ($d_i/d_o$)?
A $0.5$
B $0.6$
C $0.8$
D $1.67$
Solution & Technical Rationale:
Ratio is $d_i / d_o = 60 / 100 = 0.6$.
Question 11 • conceptual
Why are hollow circular shafts preferred over solid shafts of equal cross-sectional area in aerospace structural design?
A They provide higher torsional strength and stiffness-to-weight ratio
B They are easier to manufacture and machine
C They eliminate all sources of shear stress
D They have zero polar moment of inertia
Solution & Technical Rationale:
Material located near the center contributes little to torsional resistance; hollowing out the center removes dead weight while maintaining high polar moment of inertia.
Question 12 • assertion-reasoning
Assertion (A): The maximum shear stress in a circular shaft under torsion occurs at the outer boundary. Reason (R): Shear stress varies linearly with the radial distance $r$ from the central axis, reaching its peak at $r = c$.
A Both (A) and (R) are true and (R) is the correct explanation of (A)
B Both (A) and (R) are true but (R) is not the correct explanation of (A)
C (A) is true but (R) is false
D (A) is false but (R) is true
Solution & Technical Rationale:
Equation $\tau = \frac{Tr}{J}$ directly establishes that shear stress is directly proportional to $r$, making the outer radius the point of maximum shear stress.

A $1600 \text{ N}\cdot\text{m}$
B $3200 \text{ N}\cdot\text{m}$
C $160 \text{ N}\cdot\text{m}$
D $800 \text{ N}\cdot\text{m}$
Rationale:
Using $T = \frac{GJ\theta}{L} = \frac{(80 \times 10^9)(1.0 \times 10^{-6})(0.04)}{2.0} = \frac{3200}{2} = 1600 / 10$? Wait: $80 \times 10^9 \times 10^{-6} = 80,000$. $80,000 \times 0.04 = 3200$. $3200 / 2.0 = 1600 \text{ N}\cdot\text{m}$. Option A is correct.

B $G = \frac{E}{1 - 2\nu}$
C $G = E(1 + \nu)$

Question 5 of 10 +3 / -1
If an aircraft control torque tube has its outer diameter increased by 10% while keeping wall thickness constant, what is the primary structural effect?
A Torsional rigidity increases significantly
B Torsional rigidity decreases due to increased weight
C Shear stress increases proportionately
D Shear modulus drops to zero
Rationale:
Increasing outer diameter increases the polar moment of inertia $J$ cubically/quartically, substantially enhancing torsional stiffness and reducing shear stress.
Question 6 of 10 +3 / -1
In structural dynamic analysis of aircraft drive trains, torsional vibration resonances are critical because:
A They cause compressive buckling of the cabin skin
B They induce high cyclic shear stress peaks leading to fatigue failure
C They increase aerodynamic lift drag
D They alter the aircraft center of gravity
Rationale:
Torsional resonance amplifies dynamic twist angles and cyclic shear stresses, rapidly inducing fatigue cracking in rotating shafts.
Question 7 of 10 +3 / -1
What does the term 'warping' refer to in the context of torsional mechanics?
A Out-of-plane displacement of cross-sectional planes during twisting
B Radial expansion of the shaft under centrifugal loads
C Bending deflection caused by lateral aerodynamic forces
D Permanent plastic elongation along the longitudinal axis
Rationale:
Warping is the out-of-plane displacement of cross-sectional planes, which occurs freely in non-circular sections but is zero for circular sections.
Question 8 of 10 +3 / -1
Calculate the polar moment of inertia $J$ for a solid circular shaft of diameter $40\text{ mm}$.
A $2.513 \times 10^{-7} \text{ m}^4$
B $5.026 \times 10^{-7} \text{ m}^4$
C $1.257 \times 10^{-7} \text{ m}^4$
D $2.010 \times 10^{-6} \text{ m}^4$
Rationale:
$d = 0.04\text{ m}$. $J = \frac{\pi (0.04)^4}{32} = \frac{3.14159 \times 2.56 \times 10^{-6}}{32} = 2.513 \times 10^{-7}\text{ m}^4$.

A DGCA CAR Section 2 - Airworthiness
B CAR Section 4 - Aerodromes

D CAR Section 6 - Flight Crew Training
Rationale:
DGCA CAR Section 2 outlines the airworthiness certification and recurring structural inspection requirements for aircraft systems.
Question 10 of 10 +3 / -1
When a ductile aircraft steel shaft fails under pure torsional overload, what is the characteristic orientation of the failure surface?
A Perpendicular (transverse) to the longitudinal axis due to maximum shear stress
B At 45 degrees due to maximum tensile stress
C Irregular jagged longitudinal split
D Radial shear plane
Rationale:
Ductile materials fail along planes of maximum shear stress, which for torsion in circular shafts lies on the transverse cross-sectional plane.
