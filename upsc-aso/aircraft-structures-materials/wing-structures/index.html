01

Spars: Primary Bending & Shear Load-Carrying Beams

Subtopic 1 of 7
1. Formal Technical Definition

A spar is the primary longitudinal structural member of a wing, extending spanwise from the wing root to the tip. It acts as the principal beam resisting vertical bending moments induced by lift and inertial forces, horizontal bending moments from drag and thrust, and vertical shear forces, while transferring these loads directly into the fuselage central wing box structure.

2. Basic Concept & Continuum Mechanics

At the continuum mechanics level, a spar functions as an idealized I-beam or box-beam under combined bending and transverse shear. When aerodynamic lift acts on the wing airfoil surface, it creates a distributed upward force. This force creates an increasing bending moment from tip to root, peaking at the root joint.

The spar caps (flanges) are placed at the maximum possible distance from the neutral axis to form a couple that resists the bending moment. The upper cap experiences compressive stress ($\sigma_c$), while the lower cap experiences tensile stress ($\sigma_t$). The spar web connects the caps and primarily resists the transverse shear force ($V$), experiencing principal diagonal tension and compression stresses governed by Mohr's circle.

3. Mathematical Derivation & Equations

From Euler-Bernoulli beam theory, the bending stress $\sigma$ at any vertical distance $y$ from the neutral axis is given by:

$$\sigma(y) = -\frac{M \cdot y}{I_z}$$

Where $M$ is the bending moment ($\text{N}\cdot\text{m}$), $y$ is the vertical distance from neutral axis ($\text{m}$), and $I_z$ is the area moment of inertia about the neutral axis ($\text{m}^4$).

For an idealized spar where caps carry all bending and webs carry all shear, the cross-sectional area of each cap $A_{cap}$ required to sustain bending moment $M$ at allowable stress $\sigma_{all}$ with spar height (web depth) $h$ is:

$$A_{cap} = \frac{M}{h \cdot \sigma_{all}}$$

The transverse shear stress $\tau$ in the spar web of thickness $t_w$ is governed by Jourawski's shear stress formula:

$$\tau = \frac{V \cdot Q}{I_z \cdot t_w}$$

Where $V$ is total shear force ($\text{N}$), $Q$ is the first moment of area above the point about the neutral axis ($\text{m}^3$), and $t_w$ is web thickness ($\text{m}$).

6. Aircraft Application & DGCA Air Safety Connection

In commercial transports (e.g., Airbus A320, Boeing 737), the main spar (front spar and rear spar) forms the rigid backbone of the wing box. Per DGCA CAR Section 2, Series 'I', Part III and FAR Part 25.571, wing spars are classified as Principal Structural Elements (PSEs) requiring damage-tolerant design and mandatory Non-Destructive Testing (NDT) Eddy Current/Ultrasonic inspections during Heavy Maintenance Visits (C/D Checks).

Accident Connection: Fatigue cracking in aluminum spar lower caps due to cyclic gust and landing loads caused the catastrophic in-flight breakup of a commercial turboprop, prompting mandatory DGCA Airworthiness Directives (ADs) requiring repetitive fluorescent penetrant inspections and carbon-fiber reinforcement doublers.

7. UPSC Traps & Common Exam Pitfalls

Trap 1: UPSC setters often state that "spar webs carry all bending moments while spar caps carry all shear forces." This is completely false. Spar caps carry bending moments (via tension-compression couples), whereas spar webs carry transverse shear forces and prevent buckling.

Trap 2: Assuming isotropic behavior for composite spars. Composite carbon-epoxy spars exhibit extreme orthotropy; lamina orientation (e.g., $0^\circ, \pm 45^\circ, 90^\circ$ plies) dictates shear and bending failure modes independently.

8. Step-by-Step Worked Numerical

Problem:

A cantilever wing spar of span length $L = 15\text{ m}$ is subjected to a uniform upward lift load $w = 4000\text{ N/m}$. The spar is modeled as an idealized I-beam with spar cap separation (depth) $h = 0.5\text{ m}$. Calculate the maximum bending moment at the root and the required cross-sectional area of the upper spar cap if the allowable tensile/compressive stress is $\sigma_{all} = 300\text{ MPa}$.

Step 1: Compute Maximum Bending Moment at Root ($x = 0$)

$$M_{max} = \frac{w \cdot L^2}{2} = \frac{4000 \times 15^2}{2} = \frac{4000 \times 225}{2} = 450,000\text{ N}\cdot\text{m} = 450\text{ kN}\cdot\text{m}$$

Step 2: Compute Required Spar Cap Area ($A_{cap}$)

$$A_{cap} = \frac{M_{max}}{h \cdot \sigma_{all}} = \frac{450,000\text{ N}\cdot\text{m}}{0.5\text{ m} \times (300 \times 10^6\text{ N/m}^2)} = \frac{450,000}{150,000,000} = 0.0030\text{ m}^2 = 3000\text{ mm}^2$$

Aviation Operational Interpretation:

A cross-sectional area of $3000\text{ mm}^2$ ($30\text{ cm}^2$) of high-grade aluminum 7075-T6 is required in each flange at the wing root joint to safely sustain a $2.5g$ limit maneuver load without plastic deformation or fatigue yielding.

Ribs & Stringers: Shape Maintenance & Skin Stabilization

Subtopic 2 of 7
1. Formal Technical Definition

Ribs are chordwise structural members that maintain the aerodynamic airfoil contour, distribute concentrated loads (such as engine mounts, landing gear trunnions, and control surface hinges) into the wing box, and prevent premature column or shear buckling of the skin-stringer panels. Stringers (or stiffeners) are spanwise longitudinal extrusions attached to the internal skin surface to stabilize thin metallic/composite skins against compressive buckling and carry axial bending loads.

2. Basic Concept & Continuum Mechanics

Thin sheet metal or composite wing skins possess negligible bending stiffness on their own and would wrinkle or buckle instantly under minor compressive or shear loads. By riveting or bonding longitudinal stringers at close pitch, the unbraced panel width is reduced from the distance between spars to the spacing between stringers, drastically increasing the critical buckling stress.

Ribs act as rigid transverse diaphragms that subdivide the wing box into discrete bays. They prevent cross-sectional ovalization (warping) under torsional twisting and transmit aerodynamic pressure loads from the skin panels into the main spars.

3. Mathematical Derivation & Equations

The critical buckling stress $\sigma_{cr}$ for a flat rectangular skin plate of width $b$, thickness $t$, simply supported along its edges under uniform compression, is given by Bryan's plate buckling formula:

$$\sigma_{cr} = \frac{k_c \cdot \pi^2 \cdot E}{12(1 - \nu^2)} \left( \frac{t}{b} \right)^2$$

Where $k_c$ is the plate buckling coefficient (function of aspect ratio and boundary conditions), $E$ is Young's modulus, $\nu$ is Poisson's ratio ($\approx 0.33$ for aluminum), $t$ is skin thickness, and $b$ is stringer spacing.

Effective width of skin acting with a stringer in compression (Von Karman effective width formula):

$$w_e = 1.9 \cdot t \sqrt{\frac{E}{\sigma_{max}}}$$

6. Aircraft Application & DGCA Air Safety Connection

Ribs are classified into normal ribs, compression ribs (which absorb chordwise drag loads), and fuel-tight ribs (baffling fuel tanks). Stringers run continuously through cutouts in the ribs. Under DGCA CAR Section 2, Airworthiness, loose or cracked rivets attaching stringers to wing skins are critical structural defects that must be investigated during routine checks using high-frequency eddy current (HFEC) testing.

Accident Connection: Failure of rib attachment clips led to localized skin wrinkling and progressive aerodynamic flutter in transport aircraft, resulting in stringent DGCA advisory circulars on fastener torque and structural integrity monitoring.

7. UPSC Traps & Common Exam Pitfalls

Trap 1: Confusing stringers with spars. Stringers are numerous longitudinal stiffeners designed primarily to stabilize skin against local buckling, whereas spars are massive primary vertical load-carrying beams.

Trap 2: Assuming ribs only maintain aerodynamic shape. Ribs also provide vital torsional rigidity and transfer concentrated landing gear reactions into the wing box.

8. Step-by-Step Worked Numerical

Problem:

An aluminum skin panel ($E = 73\text{ GPa}$, $\nu = 0.3$) is simply supported between stringers spaced at $b = 120\text{ mm}$. The skin thickness is $t = 1.5\text{ mm}$. Taking the plate buckling coefficient $k_c = 4.0$, calculate the critical compressive buckling stress $\sigma_{cr}$.

Step 1: Apply Bryan's Buckling Formula

$$\sigma_{cr} = \frac{4.0 \times \pi^2 \times (73 \times 10^9\text{ Pa})}{12(1 - 0.3^2)} \left( \frac{0.0015\text{ m}}{0.120\text{ m}} \right)^2$$

Step 2: Evaluate Numerical Terms

$$\frac{t}{b} = \frac{1.5}{120} = 0.0125 \implies \left(\frac{t}{b}\right)^2 = 0.00015625$$ $$\text{Denominator} = 12 \times (1 - 0.09) = 12 \times 0.91 = 10.92$$ $$\sigma_{cr} = \frac{2,879,793,000}{10.92} \times 0.00015625 = 41.17 \times 10^6\text{ N/m}^2 = 41.17\text{ MPa}$$

Aviation Operational Interpretation:

The skin panel will buckle at $41.17\text{ MPa}$ in compression if unbacked. Adding closely spaced stringers raises this threshold, allowing post-buckling strength utilization in modern metallic wing designs.

Skin & Torsion Box: Aerodynamic Contouring & Torsional Shear Flow

Subtopic 3 of 7
1. Formal Technical Definition

The wing skin forms the outer aerodynamic boundary layer surface of the wing, smoothing airflow and directly transmitting aerodynamic lift and drag pressures into internal structures. When combined with front and rear spars and upper/lower skin panels, it forms a closed tubular or multi-cell torsion box, which is structurally optimized to resist twisting (torsional) moments caused by aerodynamic center offsets from the elastic axis.

2. Basic Concept & Continuum Mechanics

Because the aerodynamic lift vector rarely acts precisely through the elastic axis of the wing cross-section, pitching moments are generated that tend to twist the wing. An open structural section (like a channel or angle) has abysmal torsional rigidity. However, closing the cross-section between the spars and skins creates a continuous torsion box.

Under torsional loading, internal shear stresses circulate around the closed perimeter in a continuous loop known as shear flow ($q$), governed by the Bredt-Batho formula for thin-walled closed tubes.

3. Mathematical Derivation & Equations

Bredt's First Theory for thin-walled single-cell closed tubes under pure torsion $T$:

$$q = \frac{T}{2 A_0}$$

Where $q$ is shear flow ($\text{N/m}$), $T$ is applied torque ($\text{N}\cdot\text{m}$), and $A_0$ is the enclosed area bounded by the median line of the cross-section ($\text{m}^2$).

The shear stress $\tau$ in a wall of thickness $t$ is:

$$\tau = \frac{q}{t} = \frac{T}{2 A_0 t}$$

Rate of twist per unit length $\frac{d\theta}{dx}$ (Bredt's Second Theory):

$$\frac{d\theta}{dx} = \frac{T}{4 A_0^2 G} \oint \frac{ds}{t}$$

Where $G$ is the shear modulus of elasticity and $\oint \frac{ds}{t}$ is the contour integral around the closed cell perimeter.

6. Aircraft Application & DGCA Air Safety Connection

The wing box torsion box is the primary load-bearing structure of modern jets. Per DGCA CAR Section 2, Airworthiness, skin panels must be meticulously inspected for lightning strike damage, disbonds, and corrosion pitting. A breach in the torsion box skin compromises both fuel containment and torsional stiffness, risking aeroelastic divergence or control-reversal flutter.

Accident Connection: Severe Hail and Bird Strike impact breaching leading-edge torsion skins caused asymmetric aerodynamic pitching moments and autopilot hardovers, reinforcing strict DGCA bird strike certification standards under FAR 25.631.

7. UPSC Traps & Common Exam Pitfalls

Trap 1: Assuming open sections can resist significant torsion. Open channels warp freely and have negligible torsional stiffness compared to closed torsion cells.

Trap 2: Confusing enclosed area $A_0$ with total cross-sectional area of metal. $A_0$ is the area bounded by the median centerline of the thin-walled closed cell.

8. Step-by-Step Worked Numerical

Problem:

A single-cell rectangular wing torsion box has a width $a = 1.2\text{ m}$ and height $b = 0.4\text{ m}$. The uniform skin thickness is $t = 2.0\text{ mm}$. If the applied torsional moment is $T = 60,000\text{ N}\cdot\text{m}$, calculate the shear flow $q$ and the maximum shear stress $\tau$ in the torsion box.

Step 1: Compute Enclosed Area $A_0$

$$A_0 = a \times b = 1.2\text{ m} \times 0.4\text{ m} = 0.48\text{ m}^2$$

Step 2: Calculate Shear Flow ($q$) using Bredt's Formula

$$q = \frac{T}{2 A_0} = \frac{60,000\text{ N}\cdot\text{m}}{2 \times 0.48\text{ m}^2} = \frac{60,000}{0.96} = 62,500\text{ N/m}$$

Step 3: Calculate Maximum Shear Stress ($\tau$)

$$\tau = \frac{q}{t} = \frac{62,500\text{ N/m}}{0.002\text{ m}} = 31,250,000\text{ Pa} = 31.25\text{ MPa}$$

Aviation Operational Interpretation:

The resulting shear stress of $31.25\text{ MPa}$ is well below the shear yield limit of aluminum 2024-T3 ($\approx 200\text{ MPa}$), ensuring adequate torsional safety margin during high-speed rolling maneuvers.

Multi-Spar Construction: Redundancy & Thin Profile Optimization

Subtopic 4 of 7
1. Formal Technical Definition

Multi-spar construction is a structural wing architecture utilizing three or more parallel spanwise webs (spars) distributed across the chord, dividing the wing cross-section into multiple internal cells. This design is extensively used in high-speed, thin-profile supersonic aircraft and heavy transports requiring exceptional structural redundancy and damage tolerance.

2. Basic Concept & Continuum Mechanics

In supersonic fighters or thin-airfoil wings, the vertical thickness ($h$) available for a traditional two-spar box beam is severely constrained. To achieve sufficient bending inertia ($I \propto h^2$), a single front and rear spar would require excessively thick webs.

By deploying 3 to 6 parallel webs, the bending and shear loads are shared across multiple vertical members. Furthermore, multi-spar architecture provides fail-safe structural redundancy: if a fatigue crack propagates through one web, adjacent spars redistribute the load, preventing catastrophic in-flight structural failure.

3. Mathematical Derivation & Equations

Total bending stiffness ($EI_{total}$) of a multi-spar wing box is the sum of individual moments of inertia of all $n$ spars and skin stringer panels about the neutral axis:

$$(EI)_{total} = \sum_{i=1}^{n} E_i I_i + \sum_{j} E_j A_j y_j^2$$

For a multi-cell torsion box under torque $T$, the twist angle equation involves interacting shear flows $q_k$ in each cell $k$ governed by multi-cell flexibility matrices:

$$\theta = \frac{1}{2 A_k G} \oint_k q \frac{ds}{t}$$

6. Aircraft Application & DGCA Air Safety Connection

Multi-spar construction is standard in military combat aircraft (e.g., Sukhoi Su-30MKI operated by the IAF) where high-g maneuver loads ($+9g / -3g$) and thin supersonic wing profiles demand exceptional load distribution. Per DGCA CAR Section 2, military-derived or civil transport multi-spar structures require rigorous NDT scanning of internal web attachment angles for stress corrosion cracking (SCC).

Accident Connection: Multi-spar redundancy prevented catastrophic wing separation during an uncontained engine failure that severed one spar web, validating the fail-safe design philosophy mandated by airworthiness authorities.

7. UPSC Traps & Common Exam Pitfalls

Trap 1: Assuming multi-spar wings always have more internal fuel capacity. In reality, multiple vertical webs segment the internal volume, often reducing available integral fuel tank capacity compared to a clean two-spar box.

Trap 2: Confusing multi-spar with multi-rib construction. Spars are spanwise load-bearing beams; ribs are chordwise contour stabilizers.

8. Step-by-Step Worked Numerical

Problem:

A multi-spar wing box contains 3 identical parallel spars, each contributing $I_{spar} = 1.2 \times 10^{-4}\text{ m}^4$ to the total bending inertia about the neutral axis. The skin-stringer panels contribute an additional $I_{skin} = 1.6 \times 10^{-4}\text{ m}^4$. Young's modulus for the aluminum alloy is $E = 72\text{ GPa}$. Calculate the total flexural rigidity $(EI)_{total}$ of the wing box section.

Step 1: Sum Total Area Moment of Inertia ($I_{total}$)

$$I_{total} = (3 \times I_{spar}) + I_{skin} = (3 \times 1.2 \times 10^{-4}) + (1.6 \times 10^{-4})$$ $$I_{total} = 3.6 \times 10^{-4} + 1.6 \times 10^{-4} = 5.2 \times 10^{-4}\text{ m}^4$$

Step 2: Compute Total Flexural Rigidity $(EI)_{total}$

$$(EI)_{total} = (72 \times 10^9\text{ N/m}^2) \times (5.2 \times 10^{-4}\text{ m}^4) = 37,440,000\text{ N}\cdot\text{m}^2 = 3.744 \times 10^7\text{ N}\cdot\text{m}^2$$

Aviation Operational Interpretation:

A flexural rigidity of $3.744 \times 10^7\text{ N}\cdot\text{m}^2$ ensures minimal wing tip deflection and high aeroelastic flutter margin under maximum dynamic pressure ($q_\infty$).

Stressed-Skin Structure: Monocoque & Semi-Monocoque Mechanics

Subtopic 5 of 7
1. Formal Technical Definition

A stressed-skin structure is an engineering design in which the outer skin of the aircraft absorbs a significant portion of the primary bending, torsional, and shear loads, rather than relying exclusively on an internal framework of heavy beams. Modern aircraft wings utilize semi-monocoque construction, where the skin is reinforced by internal stringers, frames, and spars to share structural loads efficiently.

2. Basic Concept & Continuum Mechanics

In early aviation (e.g., WWI biplanes), structural loads were carried entirely by wooden internal trusses and wire bracing, with fabric used solely to create an aerodynamic shape (non-stressed skin). As aircraft speeds and loads escalated, this approach proved heavy and inefficient.

In stressed-skin construction, the outer metallic or composite skin acts as the outer flanges of an integrated box beam. Because material placed farthest from the neutral axis provides the highest resistance to bending moments ($I = \int y^2 dA$), putting the heavy structural mass on the outer perimeter minimizes structural weight while maximizing strength.

3. Mathematical Derivation & Equations

Idealized semi-monocoque panel stress distribution. The normal stress $\sigma$ in any stringer $i$ carrying area $A_i$ at distance $y_i$ from the neutral axis under bending moment $M_z$ and axial load $P$ is:

$$\sigma_i = \frac{P}{A_{total}} + \frac{M_z \cdot y_i}{I_z}$$

Shear flow in the skin panels between boom elements (Batho-Shed shear flow equation for open/closed sections):

$$q_k = q_0 - \frac{V_y}{I_z} \sum_{j=1}^{k} (A_j \cdot y_j)$$

6. Aircraft Application & DGCA Air Safety Connection

All modern commercial and military aircraft utilize semi-monocoque stressed skin wings. Per DGCA CAR Section 2, Airworthiness, skin panels are subject to mandatory structural integrity checks for fatigue cracking around rivet lines and fuel fastener wet-seals. Rivet pop or loose fasteners indicate localized overloading of the stressed skin.

Accident Connection: Multiple-site damage (MSD) and widespread fatigue damage (WFD) in aging aircraft stressed skin panels led to explosive decompression accidents, prompting strict DGCA compliance with Supplemental Structural Inspection Documents (SSID).

7. UPSC Traps & Common Exam Pitfalls

Trap 1: Believing a pure monocoque structure has internal framing. A pure monocoque relies entirely on skin shell strength (like an eggshell), whereas semi-monocoque incorporates internal stringers, ribs, and spars.

Trap 2: Assuming lower wing skins experience compression. Lower wing skins experience high tensile stresses during positive $g$ flight maneuvers due to upward lift bending.

8. Step-by-Step Worked Numerical

Problem:

A stringer-stiffened lower wing skin panel is subjected to an axial tensile force of $P = 450\text{ kN}$ and a bending moment $M_z = 200\text{ kN}\cdot\text{m}$. A specific stringer boom has an area $A_i = 500\text{ mm}^2$, located at a distance $y_i = 0.25\text{ m}$ from the neutral axis. The total cross-sectional area of all booms is $A_{total} = 8000\text{ mm}^2$, and the total area moment of inertia is $I_z = 1.5 \times 10^{-3}\text{ m}^4$. Calculate the total normal stress in this stringer boom.

Step 1: Calculate Direct Axial Stress Component

$$\sigma_{axial} = \frac{P}{A_{total}} = \frac{450,000\text{ N}}{0.008\text{ m}^2} = 56,250,000\text{ Pa} = 56.25\text{ MPa}$$

Step 2: Calculate Bending Stress Component

$$\sigma_{bending} = \frac{M_z \cdot y_i}{I_z} = \frac{(200,000\text{ N}\cdot\text{m}) \times 0.25\text{ m}}{1.5 \times 10^{-3}\text{ m}^4} = \frac{50,000}{0.0015} = 33,333,333\text{ Pa} = 33.33\text{ MPa}$$

Step 3: Total Normal Stress

$$\sigma_{total} = \sigma_{axial} + \sigma_{bending} = 56.25 + 33.33 = 89.58\text{ MPa (Tension)}$$

Aviation Operational Interpretation:

The combined tensile stress of $89.58\text{ MPa}$ is safely below the yield strength of aluminium 2024-T3 ($345\text{ MPa}$), providing a positive safety margin under limit load conditions.

Wing Load Paths: Aerodynamic to Fuselage Transfer Mechanics

Subtopic 6 of 7
1. Formal Technical Definition

Wing load paths define the continuous, uninterrupted mechanical route by which aerodynamic forces (lift, drag, pitching moments) and inertial forces (fuel weight, engine masses) generated along the wing span are collected, channeled through internal structural members (skins, stringers, spars, ribs), and ultimately transferred into the fuselage central wing box attachment fittings.

2. Basic Concept & Continuum Mechanics

Tracing the load path is fundamental to aircraft structural airworthiness analysis. 1. Generation: Distributed air pressure acts on the upper and lower airfoil skin surfaces. 2. Collection: Skin panels transfer pressure into adjacent stringers and ribs. 3. Channelling: Ribs channel chordwise loads into front and rear spars; spar webs carry shear, and spar caps carry bending couples. 4. Concentration & Transfer: Spanwise bending moments and shear forces converge at the wing root, where massive titanium or steel forgings (wing attachment fittings, clevis pins, and lug joints) bolt directly into the fuselage center section frames.

3. Mathematical Derivation & Equations

Shear force $V(x)$ and bending moment $M(x)$ differential relationships along the spanwise coordinate $x$ (Euler-Bernoulli beam equilibrium):

$$\frac{dV(x)}{dx} = -w(x)$$ $$\frac{dM(x)}{dx} = V(x)$$

Pin/Lug bearing stress $\sigma_{br}$ at wing-to-fuselage attachment fittings under concentrated reaction force $P$ acting on pin diameter $d$ and plate thickness $t$:

$$\sigma_{br} = \frac{P}{d \cdot t}$$

6. Aircraft Application & DGCA Air Safety Connection

Wing attachment root fittings are among the most heavily stressed components on an aircraft. Per DGCA CAR Section 2, Series 'I', Part V, wing attachment pins and clevis bolts are mandatory life-limited items (LLI) subject to ultrasonic non-destructive testing and mandatory replacement intervals to prevent catastrophic in-flight structural separation.

Accident Connection: Failure of corroded wing root attachment bolts caused the fatal crash of a charter aircraft, leading to rigorous DGCA airworthiness directives mandating specialized borescope and ultrasonic inspections of hidden wing-to-fuselage attach fittings.

7. UPSC Traps & Common Exam Pitfalls

Trap 1: Assuming loads transfer instantaneously from skin to fuselage. Load transfer is progressive: skin $\rightarrow$ stringers $\rightarrow$ spars $\rightarrow$ root ribs $\rightarrow$ attachment fittings $\rightarrow$ fuselage frames.

Trap 2: Confusing bearing stress with shear stress in lug pins. Bearing stress acts across the projected contact area ($d \cdot t$), whereas shear stress acts across the cross-sectional area of the pin ($\pi d^2 / 4$).

8. Step-by-Step Worked Numerical

Problem:

A wing attachment lug joint transmits a concentrated limit reaction force $P = 180\text{ kN}$ into the fuselage center spar. The steel attachment pin diameter is $d = 25\text{ mm}$ and the lug plate thickness is $t = 30\text{ mm}$. Calculate the bearing stress $\sigma_{br}$ in the lug fitting.

Step 1: Compute Projected Bearing Area ($A_{br}$)

$$A_{br} = d \times t = 0.025\text{ m} \times 0.030\text{ m} = 0.00075\text{ m}^2 = 750\text{ mm}^2$$

Step 2: Calculate Bearing Stress ($\sigma_{br}$)

$$\sigma_{br} = \frac{P}{A_{br}} = \frac{180,000\text{ N}}{0.00075\text{ m}^2} = 240,000,000\text{ Pa} = 240\text{ MPa}$$

Aviation Operational Interpretation:

A bearing stress of $240\text{ MPa}$ is well below the allowable bearing yield of high-strength steel ($1100\text{ MPa}$), ensuring structural integrity under maximum limit loading.

Fuel Tank Integration: Wet Wings, Sealing & Structural Relief

Subtopic 7 of 7
1. Formal Technical Definition

Fuel tank integration in modern wings involves utilizing the internal sealed cavity of the wing box torsion box as an integral fuel tank ("wet wing"). This eliminates the weight penalty of standalone rubber bladder tanks while providing significant structural bending relief through distributed inertial fuel mass opposing aerodynamic lift forces.

2. Basic Concept & Continuum Mechanics

In a wet wing, the internal volume bounded by the front spar, rear spar, upper skin, lower skin, and rib baffles is sealed airtight and watertight using specialized fuel-resistant polysulfide sealants and wet-installed fasteners.

Bending Relief Mechanism: Aerodynamic lift creates an upward bending moment at the wing root. Because fuel mass is distributed across the span, the downward inertial weight of the fuel directly opposes the upward lift forces, substantially reducing the net bending moment at the wing root and allowing lighter spar caps to be used.

3. Mathematical Derivation & Equations

Net bending moment $M_{net}(x)$ at any spanwise station $x$ including distributed lift $w_{lift}(x)$ and distributed fuel inertial load $w_{fuel}(x)$ at load factor $n$:

$$M_{net}(x) = \int_{x}^{L} (\xi - x) \cdot [w_{lift}(\xi) - n \cdot w_{fuel}(\xi)] d\xi$$

Hydrostatic fuel pressure $p_h$ at depth $h$ under maneuver load factor $n_z$:솥

$$p_h = \rho_{fuel} \cdot g \cdot n_z \cdot h$$

6. Aircraft Application & DGCA Air Safety Connection

Integral fuel tanks store thousands of liters of Jet A-1 inside the wings of commercial transports. Per DGCA CAR Section 2, Airworthiness and ICAO Annex 8, wet wing structures require rigorous lightning strike protection (static dischargers, fastener conductivity) and fuel tank flammability reduction means (FTFRM) such as nitrogen generation systems (NGS) to prevent explosive ignition under lightning or fault-arc conditions.

Accident Connection: Fuel tank explosions caused by electrical short circuits in fuel quantity indicator probes prompted worldwide FAA and DGCA Airworthiness Directives mandating mandatory fuel air-inerting systems and fault-tolerant wiring separation.

7. UPSC Traps & Common Exam Pitfalls

Trap 1: Assuming fuel adds to the wing root bending moment. The exact opposite occurs: fuel weight acts downward while lift acts upward, providing vital bending relief that reduces root bending moments.

Trap 2: Confusing integral wet wing tanks with rubber bladder fuel cells. Wet wings use the sealed aircraft structural torsion box itself as the fuel container.

8. Step-by-Step Worked Numerical

Problem:

An integral wing fuel tank is filled with Jet A-1 fuel of density $\rho = 800\text{ kg/m}^3$. During a pull-up maneuver at a load factor $n_z = 3.0$, calculate the hydrostatic fuel pressure at the bottom of the fuel tank where the fuel depth is $h = 0.6\text{ m}$. Take gravitational acceleration $g = 9.81\text{ m/s}^2$.

Step 1: Apply Hydrostatic Pressure Formula with Load Factor

$$p_h = \rho \cdot (n_z \cdot g) \cdot h$$ $$p_h = 800\text{ kg/m}^3 \times (3.0 \times 9.81\text{ m/s}^2) \times 0.6\text{ m}$$

Step 2: Execute Calculation

$$n_z \cdot g = 29.43\text{ m/s}^2$$ $$p_h = 800 \times 29.43 \times 0.6 = 14,126.4\text{ Pa} = 14.13\text{ kPa}$$

Aviation Operational Interpretation:

The hydrostatic pressure of $14.13\text{ kPa}$ acts on the lower wing skin and fastener wet seals, requiring robust polysulfide sealant elasticity to prevent fuel leaks during high-$g$ maneuvers.

Case Study 01

Aloha Airlines Flight 243 (Fuselage/Wing Skin Fatigue)

On April 28, 1988, a Boeing 737 experienced explosive decompression and structural failure of the upper cabin crown skin due to widespread fatigue damage (WFD) and multiple-site damage (MSD) around cold-bonded rivet lines.

ASO Examination Takeaway:

Reinforced the absolute necessity of Supplemental Structural Inspection Documents (SSID), eddy current NDT rivet inspections, and aging aircraft structural airworthiness mandates under DGCA CAR Section 2.

Case Study 02

Trans World Airlines Flight 800 (Integral Fuel Tank Ignition)

On July 17, 1996, a Boeing 747 experienced an in-flight breakup off Long Island, New York, resulting from an explosion in the center wing fuel tank caused by an electrical arc from fuel quantity indication wiring.

ASO Examination Takeaway:

Triggered sweeping global airworthiness directives requiring fuel tank flammability reduction means (FTFRM), nitrogen inerting systems, and rigorous electrical separation in wet wing integral fuel tanks.

DGCA CAR Section 2 Compliance Summary for Air Safety Officers

As an Air Safety Officer, compliance with DGCA CAR Section 2 (Airworthiness) and CAR Series 'I' governs the approval, modification, and maintenance of aircraft wing structures. Key mandates include:

  • Mandatory Damage Tolerance (FAR 25.571 / CAR): All principal structural elements (spars, wing root fittings, torsion box skins) must be designed to withstand fatigue crack growth up to predictable inspection intervals.
  • Corrosion Prevention and Control Programs (CPCP): Wet wing integral fuel tanks require periodic microbial growth (fungal slime) inspection and biocide treatment to prevent pitting corrosion of lower wing skin panels.
  • Non-Destructive Testing (NDT): Mandatory fluorescent penetrant, ultrasonic, and eddy-current inspection schedules for wing attachment pins and spar cap fastener holes during C and D checks.

UPSC Interview Corner: Board Member Model Q&A

Q: "As an Air Safety Officer inspecting an aging transport aircraft wing, what specific structural anomalies would you look for along the spar lower cap and skin rivet lines?"

Model Answer: "Sir/Madam, in inspecting an aging transport wing, my primary focus would be on Multiple-Site Damage (MSD) and Widespread Fatigue Damage (WFD) along the lower spar caps and skin rivet lines. Because lower wing skins experience cyclic tensile stresses during positive $g$ flight, minute fatigue cracks initiate at rivet fastener holes where stress concentration factors ($K_t$) are highest.

I would mandate high-frequency eddy current (HFEC) NDT scans around fastener rows and inspect for fuel weeping, which indicates compromised polysulfide wet seals. Additionally, I would verify compliance with DGCA Supplemental Inspection Documents (SSID) and ensure no stress corrosion cracking (SCC) is present in aluminum-lithium or 7075-T6 structural joints."

Q: "Why are composite materials increasingly replacing aluminum alloys in modern wing torsion boxes, and what are the primary airworthiness challenges they introduce?"

Model Answer: "Carbon-fiber reinforced polymer (CFRP) composites offer superior specific strength ($F/ \rho$) and specific stiffness compared to aluminum, eliminating fatigue crack propagation and allowing seamless, highly contoured multi-spar wing box construction.

However, from an airworthiness perspective, composites introduce unique challenges: they are brittle (poor impact damage tolerance from hail or runway debris), susceptible to hidden internal delaminations requiring advanced ultrasonic phased-array NDT, and lack inherent electrical conductivity, demanding integrated copper mesh or foil layers for lightning strike protection under DGCA CAR Section 2."
