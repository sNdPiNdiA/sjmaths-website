Subtopic 1 of 8 DGCA CAR Section 2 — Airworthiness

1. Drilling & Reaming Engineering Practices

1. Formal Technical Definition

Drilling is a rotary material-removal machining process that utilizes a multi-point end-cutting tool (drill bit) to generate a cylindrical hole in solid metallic or composite structural components. Reaming is a precision secondary finishing operation performed with a multi-fluted rotary tool to enlarge an existing pre-drilled hole to exact dimensional tolerances, exceptional surface finish ($R_a \le 0.8\mu m$), and superior cylindrical geometry.

2. Basic Physical Concept & Molecular Mechanics

At the continuum and atomic level, drilling relies on high shear stress concentration at the chisel edge and cutting lips of the twist drill. As the tool advances, atomic bonds within the crystal lattice of the metal (e.g., Al 2024-T3 or Ti-6Al-4V) are forcibly severed via localized plastic shear deformation along the shear plane angle ($\phi$). The friction between chip flow and rake face generates extreme thermal energy ($T > 600^\circ C$). Reaming removes a minimal radial depth of cut ($0.1 - 0.5\text{ mm}$), relieving residual work-hardening stresses left by drilling and ensuring true circularity without eccentric chatter.

3. Mathematical Derivation & Equations

Cutting speed ($V_c$), spindle rotational speed ($N$), feed rate ($f$), and material removal rate ($MRR$) are governed by fundamental kinematics:

$$V_c = \frac{\pi \cdot D \cdot N}{1000} \quad [\text{m/min}]$$

Where: $D = \text{Drill diameter (mm)}, N = \text{Spindle speed (RPM)}$

$$MRR = \frac{\pi}{4} \cdot D^2 \cdot f_n \cdot N \quad [\text{mm}^3/\text{min}]$$

Where: $f_n = \text{Feed per revolution (mm/rev)}$

4. Dimensions & Units

Fundamental dimensions: Diameter $[L]$, Speed $[T^{-1}]$, Feed $[L]$, Force $[M L T^{-2}]$.

SI Units: $D$ in m, $V_c$ in m/s, $MRR$ in $\text{m}^3/\text{s}$
Practical Units: $D$ in mm, $V_c$ in m/min, Feed in mm/rev
Imperial: $D$ in inches, $V_c$ in SFPM (Surface Feet Per Minute)

5. Reference Values Table

Material Cutting Speed ($V_c$ m/min) Feed Rate ($f_n$ mm/rev) Reaming Allowance (mm)
Aluminium Alloy (2024-T3) 80 - 120 0.10 - 0.25 0.2 - 0.4
Titanium Alloy (Ti-6Al-4V) 15 - 30 0.05 - 0.15 0.3 - 0.5
Corrosion Resisting Steel (CRES 321) 20 - 45 0.08 - 0.20 0.25 - 0.40

6. Aircraft Application & DGCA Airworthiness Connection

Drilling and reaming are critical during aircraft spar assembly, wing skin riveting, and landing gear trunnion mounting. Improper drilling induces burrs, delamination in composite-metal stacks, or micro-cracks at hole edges acting as stress concentration zones ($K_t > 3.0$). Under DGCA CAR Section 2, Series 'L', Part VII, all structural hole preparation must utilize calibrated pneumatic drills with positive depth stops and mandatory reaming prior to interference-fit fastener installation to prevent fatigue crack initiation under cyclic cabin pressurization and gust loads.

7. UPSC Traps & Common Misconceptions

Trap: Assuming reaming removes bulk structural material.
Correction: Reaming is strictly a sizing and finishing operation; attempting to ream a large hole from solid stock causes tool breakage and severe bore scarring. Also, note that spindle speed calculation requires diameter in millimeters divided by 1000 when using meters per minute.

8. Step-by-Step Worked Numerical

Problem: Calculate the required spindle RPM ($N$) and Material Removal Rate ($MRR$) for drilling a $\varnothing 12.7\text{ mm}$ ($0.5\text{ inch}$) hole in an Aluminium 7075-T6 main spar boom, given a recommended cutting speed $V_c = 90\text{ m/min}$ and feed rate $f_n = 0.2\text{ mm/rev}$.

Step 1: Calculate Spindle RPM (N)

$$N = \frac{1000 \cdot V_c}{\pi \cdot D} = \frac{1000 \times 90}{\pi \times 12.7} = \frac{90000}{39.898} \approx 2255.7\text{ RPM}$$

Step 2: Calculate MRR

$$MRR = \frac{\pi}{4} \cdot D^2 \cdot f_n \cdot N = \frac{\pi}{4} \cdot (12.7)^2 \cdot 0.2 \cdot 2255.7$$

$$MRR = 0.7854 \times 161.29 \times 0.2 \times 2255.7 \approx 57,178.6\text{ mm}^3/\text{min} \approx 57.18\text{ cm}^3/\text{min}$$

Operational Takeaway: High metal removal rate requires adequate soluble oil coolant flow to prevent localized thermal softening of the aluminium spar.

Subtopic 2 of 8 DGCA CAR Section 2 — Overhaul & Repair

2. Grinding Engineering Practices

1. Formal Technical Definition

Grinding is an abrasive machining process that employs a bonded abrasive wheel rotating at high peripheral speeds ($30 - 50\text{ m/s}$) to remove microscopic chips from metallic or ceramic workpieces, achieving ultra-precise dimensional tolerances ($\pm 0.001\text{ mm}$) and mirror-like surface finishes ($R_a \le 0.2\mu m$).

2. Basic Physical Concept & Molecular Mechanics

Each abrasive grain (e.g., Aluminium Oxide $Al_2O_3$ or Silicon Carbide $SiC$) acts as an independent microscopic cutting edge with high negative rake angles ($-60^\circ$ to $-85^\circ$). High frictional rubbing dominates over shearing, converting mechanical energy almost entirely into thermal energy at the contact zone. Controlled cooling via high-pressure fluid jets prevents metallurgical phase transformations (such as untempered martensite or grinding burn) in heat-treated aircraft steels.

3. Mathematical Derivation & Equations

Specific energy and wheel speed relationships:

$$v_s = \frac{\pi \cdot D_s \cdot N_s}{60} \quad [\text{m/s}]$$

Where: $D_s = \text{Wheel diameter (m)}, N_s = \text{Wheel RPM}$

$$q_w = v_w \cdot d \quad [\text{mm}^2/\text{s}]$$

Where: $v_w = \text{Workpiece velocity}, d = \text{Depth of cut}$

4. Dimensions & Units

Dimensions: Velocity $[L T^{-1}]$, Frequency $[T^{-1}]$.

SI Units: $v_s$ in m/s, $D_s$ in m
CGS Units: cm/s
Imperial: SFPM (Surface Feet Per Minute)

5. Reference Values Table

Grinding Operation Abrasive Type Wheel Speed ($v_s$ m/s) Typical Surface Roughness ($R_a$)
Landing Gear Cylinder OD Grinding Aluminium Oxide ($Al_2O_3$) 30 - 35 0.2 - 0.4 $\mu m$
Turbine Blade Root Creep-Feed Grinding Cubic Boron Nitride (CBN) 60 - 120 0.1 - 0.2 $\mu m$
Brake Disc Surface Flattening Silicon Carbide ($SiC$) 25 - 30 0.8 - 1.6 $\mu m$

6. Aircraft Application & DGCA Airworthiness Connection

Grinding is extensively utilized in manufacturing and overhaul of aircraft engine turbine shafts, landing gear chrome-plated cylinders, and hydraulic spool valves. Grinding burns or tensile residual stresses can lead to catastrophic fatigue failure of landing gear under high impact landing loads. DGCA regulations mandate non-destructive testing (Nital etch inspection or fluorescent penetrant inspection) immediately following precision grinding of high-strength steel components.

7. UPSC Traps & Common Misconceptions

Trap: Assuming grinding is a roughing operation.
Correction: Grinding is a precision finishing process. High negative rake angles mean specific cutting energy is up to 10 times higher than conventional milling or turning.

8. Step-by-Step Worked Numerical

Problem: Determine the peripheral speed ($v_s$) in m/s of a cylindrical grinding wheel having diameter $D_s = 400\text{ mm}$ rotating at $N_s = 1800\text{ RPM}$.

Step 1: Apply Peripheral Speed Formula

$$v_s = \frac{\pi \cdot D_s \cdot N_s}{60}$$

Step 2: Substitute Values ($D_s = 0.4\text{ m}$)

$$v_s = \frac{\pi \times 0.4\text{ m} \times 1800\text{ RPM}}{60} = \frac{2261.95}{60} = 37.7\text{ m/s}$$

Operational Takeaway: $37.7\text{ m/s}$ is well within safe operating limits for vitrified bonded aluminium oxide wheels ($< 45\text{ m/s}$).

Subtopic 3 of 8 DGCA CAR Section 2 — Special Processes

3. Welding Engineering Practices

1. Formal Technical Definition

Welding is a permanent joining process that produces coalescence of materials by heating them to melting temperatures with or without the application of pressure, or by the application of pressure alone, with or without filler metal. In aerospace, fusion welding methods such as GTAW (TIG) and GMAW (MIG) predominate.

2. Basic Physical Concept & Molecular Mechanics

Fusion welding melts parent metal boundaries, permitting inter-diffusion of metal atoms across the interface. Upon solidification, a cast grain structure forms. The Heat Affected Zone (HAZ) experiences steep thermal gradients, causing microstructural phase changes (e.g., grain growth, carbide precipitation, or martensite formation) which drastically alter mechanical strength and corrosion resistance.

3. Mathematical Derivation & Equations

Heat input ($H$) calculation for arc welding:

$$H = \frac{\eta \cdot V \cdot I}{v} \quad [\text{kJ/mm}]$$

Where: $\eta = \text{Arc efficiency (0.6 - 0.8 for GTAW)}, V = \text{Voltage (V)}, I = \text{Current (A)}, v = \text{Travel speed (mm/s)}$

4. Dimensions & Units

Fundamental dimensions: Energy per length $[M L T^{-2}]$.

SI Units: J/m or kJ/mm
Electrical: Volts (V), Amperes (A)
Imperial: kJ/in

5. Reference Values Table

Welding Process Shielding Gas Typical Application Arc Efficiency ($\eta$)
GTAW (TIG) Argon / Helium Engine Exhaust ducts, Inconel/Titanium lines 0.60 - 0.70
GMAW (MIG) Argon + $CO_2$ Steel engine mounts, GSE structures 0.80 - 0.90
EBW (Electron Beam) Vacuum ($< 10^{-4}\text{ torr}$) Titanium fan rotors, gearbox housings 0.95

6. Aircraft Application & DGCA Airworthiness Connection

Welding is strictly controlled in aviation. While primary airframe structures (wing spars, fuselage frames) primarily use mechanical fasteners or bonding due to fatigue concerns, engine mounts, exhaust collector rings, and fluid lines rely heavily on TIG welding. Under DGCA CAR Section 2, all aerospace welders must hold valid certifications (ASME Sec IX or AWS D17.1), and 100% radiographic or fluorescent penetrant inspection is mandatory on safety-critical welds.

7. UPSC Traps & Common Misconceptions

Trap: Assuming all structural aluminium alloys (like 2024 or 7075) can be fusion welded.
Correction: High-strength heat-treatable aluminium alloys suffer severe hot cracking and loss of mechanical properties upon melting; they are considered "unweldable" by fusion means and must be riveted or bolted.

8. Step-by-Step Worked Numerical

Problem: Calculate the heat input per unit length ($H$) for a GTAW titanium tube weld operating at $V = 18\text{ V}$, $I = 150\text{ A}$, travel speed $v = 3\text{ mm/s}$, with an arc efficiency $\eta = 0.65$.

Step 1: Formula Setup

$$H = \frac{\eta \cdot V \cdot I}{v}$$

Step 2: Substitution & Calculation

$$H = \frac{0.65 \times 18\text{ V} \times 150\text{ A}}{3\text{ mm/s}} = \frac{1755}{3} = 585\text{ J/mm} = 0.585\text{ kJ/mm}$$

Operational Takeaway: Controlled low heat input prevents excessive grain growth in titanium weld zones.

Subtopic 4 of 8 DGCA CAR Section 2 — Tubing & Wiring

4. Brazing & Soldering Basics

1. Formal Technical Definition

Soldering is a metal-joining process where filler metal has a liquidus temperature below $450^\circ C$ ($840^\circ F$). Brazing is a joining process where filler metal has a liquidus temperature above $450^\circ C$ but below the solidus temperature of the base metals. Both processes rely on capillary action to distribute molten filler metal into closely fitted joint clearances ($0.03 - 0.1\text{ mm}$).

2. Basic Physical Concept & Molecular Mechanics

The fundamental physical mechanism is wetting and capillary attraction. Molten filler metal wets the solid base metal surface via intermolecular attraction (van der Waals forces and atomic inter-diffusion). The capillary height ($h$) or horizontal spread is governed by surface tension ($\gamma$), contact angle ($\theta$), and gap clearance ($d$).

3. Mathematical Derivation & Equations

Capillary pressure ($\Delta P$) driving filler metal into the joint:

$$\Delta P = \frac{2 \cdot \gamma \cdot \cos\theta}{d}$$

Where: $\gamma = \text{Surface tension of liquid filler}, \theta = \text{Contact angle}, d = \text{Joint clearance gap}$

4. Dimensions & Units

Dimensions: Pressure $[M L^{-1} T^{-2}]$, Surface Tension $[M T^{-2}]$.

SI Units: $\Delta P$ in Pa, $\gamma$ in N/m
Temperature: Celsius ($^\circ C$) or Kelvin ($K$)
Imperial: Fahrenheit ($^\circ F$), psi

5. Reference Values Table

Process Typical Filler Alloy Liquidus Temp Primary Aircraft Application
Soft Soldering Sn63/Pb37 (Tin-Lead) $183^\circ C$ Avionics PCB wiring, instrument terminals
Silver Brazing AWS BAg series (Silver-Copper-Zinc) $620 - 750^\circ C$ Oxygen lines, hydraulic fuel drain lines
Copper Brazing BCuP (Copper-Phosphorus) $710 - 800^\circ C$ Oil coolers, heat exchangers

6. Aircraft Application & DGCA Airworthiness Connection

Soldering is ubiquitous in electrical wiring interconnection systems (EWIS) under DGCA CAR Section 2 requirements, but soft solder is strictly prohibited for mechanical load-bearing or primary fuel/hydraulic lines due to low creep strength. Silver brazing is used for high-pressure oxygen and hydraulic tubing. Flux residues must be 100% removed post-brazing to prevent active corrosion.

7. UPSC Traps & Common Misconceptions

Trap: Confusing the temperature threshold between soldering and brazing.
Correction: The exact dividing line is $450^\circ C$ ($840^\circ F$). Below $450^\circ C$ is soldering; above $450^\circ C$ is brazing.

8. Step-by-Step Worked Numerical

Problem: Calculate the capillary driving pressure ($\Delta P$) for a silver brazing operation where filler surface tension $\gamma = 0.5\text{ N/m}$, contact angle $\theta = 0^\circ$ ($\cos 0 = 1$), and joint radial clearance $d = 0.05\text{ mm}$ ($5 \times 10^{-5}\text{ m}$).

Step 1: Formula

$$\Delta P = \frac{2 \cdot \gamma \cdot \cos\theta}{d}$$

Step 2: Substitution

$$\Delta P = \frac{2 \times 0.5 \times 1}{5 \times 10^{-5}} = \frac{1.0}{5 \times 10^{-5}} = 20,000\text{ Pa} = 20\text{ kPa}$$

Operational Takeaway: $20\text{ kPa}$ capillary suction easily draws molten brazing alloy vertically upward against gravity.

Subtopic 5 of 8 DGCA CAR Section 2 — Structures

5. Sheet-Metal Work & Riveting

1. Formal Technical Definition

Sheet-metal work involves cold-forming structural skins, ribs, and bulkheads from thin metallic sheets (e.g., Al 2024-T3) via shearing, bending, flanging, and stretching. Riveting is a permanent mechanical fastening method utilizing shear-resistant cylindrical pins (solid rivets like MS20470 universal head) driven to join overlapping sheets.

2. Basic Physical Concept & Molecular Mechanics

Sheet bending induces elastic-plastic deformation where outer fibers experience tensile stress exceeding yield strength ($\sigma_y$), resulting in permanent elongation, while inner fibers undergo compression. Riveting relies on plastic deformation of the rivet shank inside a tight-tolerance hole, causing radial expansion that locks the rivet in place and transfers shear loads between structural skins via friction and direct bearing stress.

3. Mathematical Derivation & Equations

Rivet shear strength ($P_s$) and plate bearing strength ($P_b$) failure modes:

$$P_s = n \cdot \left(\frac{\pi}{4} \cdot d^2\right) \cdot \tau_{ult} \quad [\text{N}]$$

$$P_b = t \cdot d \cdot \sigma_{br} \quad [\text{N}]$$

Where: $d = \text{Rivet dia}, t = \text{Sheet thickness}, \tau_{ult} = \text{Rivet shear ultimate stress}, \sigma_{br} = \text{Bearing stress}$$

4. Dimensions & Units

Dimensions: Force $[M L T^{-2}]$, Stress $[M L^{-1} T^{-2}]$.

SI Units: Newtons (N), MPa
Imperial: Pounds (lbs), psi
Dimensions in inches or mm

5. Reference Values Table

Rivet Material / Alloy Code Head Marking Shear Strength (MPa) Application
1100 (Type A - Plain) Plain (No mark) ~160 MPa Non-structural fairings, interior trim
2117 (Type AD - Field Rivet) Dimple / Teat ~260 MPa General airframe structural skin
2017 / 2024 (Type D / DD) Raised Dot / Double Dash ~380 MPa High-stress primary spar joints

6. Aircraft Application & DGCA Airworthiness Connection

Monocoque and semi-monocoque aircraft fuselages and wings rely almost entirely on aluminium sheet-metal assemblies joined by solid rivets. Under DGCA CAR Section 2, rivet pitch, edge distance ($min\ 2 \times d$), and countersinking depths must strictly adhere to FAA AC 43.13-1B standards. Improper driving resulting in "smile" shop heads or tilted rivets mandates immediate drill-out and replacement.

7. UPSC Traps & Common Misconceptions

Trap: Assuming rivets are designed to carry tensile loads perpendicular to sheets.
Correction: Rivets are structural shear fasteners; they possess very poor tensile strength and must never be loaded in direct tension.

8. Step-by-Step Worked Numerical

Problem: Calculate the ultimate single shear failure load ($P_s$) for a $\varnothing 4.8\text{ mm}$ ($3/16\text{ inch}$) MS20470AD-6 rivet made of 2117-T4 aluminium alloy having an ultimate shear strength $\tau_{ult} = 260\text{ MPa}$.

Step 1: Formula

$$P_s = \frac{\pi}{4} \cdot d^2 \cdot \tau_{ult}$$

Step 2: Calculation ($d = 0.0048\text{ m}, \tau_{ult} = 260 \times 10^6\text{ Pa}$)

$$P_s = 0.7854 \times (0.0048)^2 \times (260 \times 10^6) = 0.7854 \times 2.304 \times 10^{-5} \times 260 \times 10^6$$

$$P_s = 4.704 \times 4698.8 = 4,704\text{ N} \approx 4.7\text{ kN}$$

Operational Takeaway: Each 3/16" AD rivet safely transfers $4.7\text{ kN}$ of shear load in single shear.

Subtopic 6 of 8 DGCA CAR Section 2 — Advanced Materials

6. Composite Repair Basics

1. Formal Technical Definition

Composite repair involves restoring structural integrity and aerodynamic contour to damaged polymer-matrix composite (PMC) components (such as carbon-fiber-reinforced polymer CFRP or glass-fiber-reinforced polymer GFRP) using bonded scarf or stepped patch repairs cured under controlled temperature and vacuum pressure.

2. Basic Physical Concept & Molecular Mechanics

Unlike isotropic metals, anisotropic composites require load transfer through interlaminar shear across staggered ply steps or scarfs (typically a $30:1$ to $50:1$ slope ratio). Epoxies polymerize via cross-linking chemical reactions activated by thermal energy, forming a rigid three-dimensional network that bonds new prepreg plies to the parent structure.

3. Mathematical Derivation & Equations

Scarf repair overlap length ($L$) requirement based on parent laminate thickness ($t$) and adhesive shear strength ($\tau_a$):

$$L = \frac{t \cdot \sigma_{ult}}{2 \cdot \tau_a} \quad [\text{mm}]$$

Where: $t = \text{Laminate thickness}, \sigma_{ult} = \text{Ultimate tensile strength of composite}, \tau_a = \text{Allowable adhesive shear strength}$

4. Dimensions & Units

Dimensions: Length $[L]$, Stress $[M L^{-1} T^{-2}]$.

SI Units: mm, MPa
Imperial: inches, psi
Temperature in $^\circ C$ or $^\circ F$

5. Reference Values Table

Repair Parameter CFRP / Epoxy System GFRP / Polyester System
Standard Scarf Slope Ratio 30:1 to 50:1 20:1 to 30:1
Curing Temperature Range $120^\circ C - 177^\circ C$ $80^\circ C - 120^\circ C$
Required Vacuum Pressure $-85\text{ kPa}$ ($25\text{ inHg}$) $-70\text{ kPa}$ ($22\text{ inHg}$)

6. Aircraft Application & DGCA Airworthiness Connection

Modern aircraft (Boeing 787, Airbus A350) utilize carbon composites for over 50% of structural weight (fuselage barrels, wings, control surfaces). Lightning strikes, bird strikes, or ramp damage require bonded scarf repairs. Under DGCA CAR Section 2, composite repairs must follow SRM (Structural Repair Manual) guidelines with mandatory NDT ultrasonic C-scan inspection post-cure to verify absence of porosity or delamination.

7. UPSC Traps & Common Misconceptions

Trap: Assuming composite patch repairs can be bolted directly without regard to galvanic corrosion and fiber cutting.
Correction: Bolting composites causes high local bearing stress concentrations; bonded scarf joints are preferred to maintain smooth load transfer across continuous carbon fibers.

8. Step-by-Step Worked Numerical

Problem: Calculate the minimum scarf repair overlap length ($L$) for a CFRP skin of thickness $t = 4.0\text{ mm}$, ultimate tensile strength $\sigma_{ult} = 1200\text{ MPa}$, and allowable adhesive shear strength $\tau_a = 50\text{ MPa}$.

Step 1: Formula Setup

$$L = \frac{t \cdot \sigma_{ult}}{2 \cdot \tau_a}$$

Step 2: Substitution ($t = 4\text{ mm}, \sigma_{ult} = 1200\text{ MPa}, \tau_a = 50\text{ MPa}$)

$$L = \frac{4.0 \times 1200}{2 \times 50} = \frac{4800}{100} = 48\text{ mm}$$

Operational Takeaway: A minimum scarf overlap length of $48\text{ mm}$ (a $12:1$ slope per side) is required to prevent adhesive shear failure.

Subtopic 7 of 8 DGCA CAR Section 2 — Corrosion Control

7. Surface Treatment Practices

1. Formal Technical Definition

Surface treatment encompasses electrochemical, chemical, and organic coating processes applied to aircraft metallic substrates to enhance corrosion resistance, wear performance, and paint adhesion. Key processes include anodizing, chromate conversion coating (alodining), cadmium/zinc plating, and shot peening.

2. Basic Physical Concept & Molecular Mechanics

Anodizing converts aluminium surfaces into a controlled, thick porous aluminium oxide ($Al_2O_3$) barrier layer via electrolytic oxidation in chromic or sulfuric acid baths. Shot peening bombards metal surfaces with cast steel or ceramic shot, inducing compressive residual stresses in the outer surface layer ($0.2 - 0.5\text{ mm}$ depth), which effectively arrests fatigue crack propagation.

3. Mathematical Derytoin & Equations

Faraday's Law of Electrolysis for coating thickness/mass deposition ($m$):

$$m = \frac{I \cdot t \cdot M}{z \cdot F} \quad [\text{grams}]$$

Where: $I = \text{Current (A)}, t = \text{Time (s)}, M = \text{Molar mass}, z = \text{Valency}, F = \text{Faraday constant } (96485\text{ C/mol})$

4. Dimensions & Units

Dimensions: Mass $[M]$, Current $[I]$, Time $[T]$.

SI Units: grams (g), Coulombs (C)
Coating Thickness: Microns ($\mu m$) or mils ($0.001\text{ in}$)
Anodic layer: $2 - 25\mu m$

5. Reference Values Table

Surface Treatment Substrate Primary Purpose Standard Thickness
Chromic Acid Anodizing (MIL-A-8625) Aluminium Alloys Corrosion protection & paint base 2 - 5 $\mu m$
Cadmium Plating (QQ-P-416) High-strength Steel fasteners Sacrificial galvanic protection 8 - 12 $\mu m$
Shot Peening (AMS 2430) Landing gear & steel spars Fatigue strength enhancement Almen intensity $0.006A - 0.012A$

6. Aircraft Application & DGCA Airworthiness Connection

Surface treatments are the primary defense against atmospheric and marine corrosion. Under DGCA CAR Section 2, any structural repair that removes anodized or cadmium-plated layers requires immediate chemical conversion coating (Alodine) and primer application. Cadmium plating is strictly monitored due to toxicity, with strict environmental phase-out rules while retaining exemptions for critical aerospace fasteners.

7. UPSC Traps & Common Misconceptions

Trap: Confusing chromic acid anodizing with sulfuric acid anodizing.
Correction: Chromic anodizing yields a very thin layer ($2 - 5\mu m$) that does not significantly reduce fatigue strength, making it ideal for assembled aircraft parts; sulfuric anodizing yields thicker layers ($25\mu m$) used for wear resistance.

8. Step-by-Step Worked Numerical

Problem: Calculate the mass ($m$) of cadmium deposited on a steel bracket during electroplating when a current of $I = 5\text{ A}$ flows for $t = 1800\text{ seconds}$ ($30\text{ minutes}$). Cadmium atomic weight $M = 112.4\text{ g/mol}$, valency $z = 2$, Faraday constant $F = 96,485\text{ C/mol}$.

Step 1: Apply Faraday's Law

$$m = \frac{I \cdot t \cdot M}{z \cdot F}$$

Step 2: Substitute Values

$$m = \frac{5\text{ A} \times 1800\text{ s} \times 112.4\text{ g/mol}}{2 \times 96485\text{ C/mol}} = \frac{1,011,600}{192,970} \approx 5.24\text{ grams}$$

Operational Takeaway: $5.24\text{ grams}$ of sacrificial cadmium provides robust corrosion protection over the component surface area.

Subtopic 8 of 8 DGCA CAR Section 2 — Airworthiness & Safety

8. Corrosion Types & Prevention

1. Formal Technical Definition

Corrosion is the destructive electrochemical or chemical attack of a metallic aircraft material by its environment, resulting in material degradation, loss of cross-sectional area, and catastrophic structural failure. Major forms include Intergranular Corrosion (IGC), Galvanic Corrosion, Pitting, Exfoliation, and Stress Corrosion Cracking (SCC).

2. Basic Physical Concept & Molecular Mechanics

Electrochemical corrosion requires four elements: an anode (where oxidation/metal dissolution occurs: $M \rightarrow M^{n+} + ne^-$), a cathode (where reduction occurs), an electrolyte (moisture film with dissolved salts), and a metallic pathway connecting anode and cathode. Galvanic corrosion occurs when two dissimilar metals are electrically coupled in an electrolyte, with the more active (anodic) metal corroding preferentially.

3. Mathematical Derivation & Equations

Corrosion rate penetration ($CR$) governed by Faraday's law and current density ($i_{corr}$):

$$CR = \frac{K \cdot i_{corr} \cdot EW}{d} \quad [\text{mpy (mils per year)}]$$

Where: $K = \text{Constant }(0.129), i_{corr} = \text{Corrosion current density } (\mu\text{A/cm}^2), EW = \text{Equivalent weight}, d = \text{Density}$$

4. Dimensions & Units

Dimensions: Rate $[L T^{-1}]$, Potential $[V]$.

SI Units: mm/year or $\mu m$/year
Practical Units: mpy (mils per year)
Galvanic potential in millivolts (mV)

5. Reference Values Table

Corrosion Type Susceptible Alloy Visual / NDT Indication Prevention Method
Exfoliation Al 2024 / 7075 Extrusions Blistered paint, lifting metal flakes Proper heat treatment, cladding, sealing
Stress Corrosion Cracking (SCC) High-strength Steel & 7075-T6 Fine transgranular/intergranular cracks Stress relief, shot peening, coating
Galvanic Corrosion CFRP coupled to Aluminium White powdery pits around fasteners Fiberglass barrier plies, sealant

6. Aircraft Application & DGCA Airworthiness Connection

Corrosion is the single largest threat to aging aircraft airworthiness. Under DGCA CAR Section 2 and Mandatory Continuing Airworthiness Requirements (MCAR), airlines must execute rigorous Corrosion Prevention and Control Programs (CPCP). Classic case studies like Aloha Airlines Flight 243 (lap joint fatigue and corrosion) underscore why baseline corrosion inspections are mandated during every heavy C and D check.

7. UPSC Traps & Common Misconceptions

Trap: Believing stainless steel (CRES) never corrodes in aviation environments.
Correction: Stainless steel suffers from pitting and stress corrosion cracking in chloride-rich marine environments if not passivated properly.

8. Step-by-Step Worked Numerical

Problem: Calculate the corrosion penetration rate in mpy for an aluminium skin experiencing a uniform corrosion current density $i_{corr} = 15\text{ }\mu\text{A/cm}^2$. Equivalent weight of Al $EW = 9\text{ g/eq}$, density $d = 2.7\text{ g/cm}^3$, constant $K = 0.129\text{ mil}\cdot\text{cm}^2/\text{}(\mu\text{A}\cdot\text{year})$.

Step 1: Formula

$$CR = \frac{K \cdot i_{corr} \cdot EW}{d}$$

Step 2: Substitution

$$CR = \frac{0.129 \times 15 \times 9}{2.7} = \frac{17.415}{2.7} \approx 6.45\text{ mpy}$$

Operational Takeaway: A penetration rate of $6.45\text{ mils/year}$ ($0.16\text{ mm/year}$) requires prompt corrosion removal and repainting before structural margins are compromised.

Aviation Case Studies & DGCA Airworthiness Connections

Real-world aviation accidents linked directly to general engineering practices, improper repairs, and corrosion neglect.

1. Aloha Airlines Flight 243 (1988)

Failure Mode: Multiple Site Damage (MSD), Lap Joint Corrosion, Rivet Fatigue

A Boeing 737 experienced explosive decompression and structural failure of the upper forward fuselage due to severe exfoliation corrosion and cold-bond lap joint cracking originating from improper cold-working and moisture entrapment between bonded fuselage skins.

ASO Takeaway: Directly catalyzed global Airworthiness Directives (ADs) for aging aircraft inspection, non-destructive eddy current testing of rivet rows, and stringent CPCP compliance.

2. United Airlines Flight 232 (Sioux City, 1989)

Failure Mode: Inclusion in Titanium Billet, Fan Disk Fracture

An uncontained failure of the GE CF6-6 engine stage 1 fan disk occurred due to a hard alpha nitrogen inclusion introduced during vacuum arc remelting (VAR) of the titanium billet. The inclusion acted as a severe stress concentration site leading to fatigue crack growth.

ASO Takeaway: Revolutionized raw material melting inspection standards, ultrasonic inspection of rotating engine parts, and overhaul machining quality control.

Q: As an Air Safety Officer, how would you evaluate a non-OEM composite repair on a primary control surface?

Model Answer: "I would verify compliance with DGCA CAR Section 2 and the aircraft Manufacturer’s Structural Repair Manual (SRM). Specifically, I would inspect the repair documentation for correct scarf slope ratio (minimum 30:1), vacuum cure pressure records ($> -85\text{ kPa}$), glass transition temperature ($T_g$) verification via DSC, and mandatory post-cure ultrasonic C-scan NDT report to ensure zero internal delamination or moisture entrapment before releasing the aircraft to service."
