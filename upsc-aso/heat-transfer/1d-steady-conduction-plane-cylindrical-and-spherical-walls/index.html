1D steady conduction: plane, cylindrical & spherical walls

Textbook-grade UPSC Air Safety Officer deep-dive: Comprehensive theoretical foundations, analytical derivations, engineering reference data, DGCA/ICAO airworthiness integration, 14 practice MCQs, and timed mini-test.

How This Topic Is Useful for Air Safety Officers & Aircraft Operations
DGCA CAR & ICAO Mandate

In aviation operations and UPSC ASO assessments, this parameter is critical across 4 core operational pillars:

  • 1. Aircraft Systems & Flight Deck Monitoring: Engine bleed air temperature sensors and FADEC thermal monitoring units rely on accurate composite wall conduction models.
  • 2. Flight Safety & Dispatch Envelopes: Proper insulation design prevents fuselage skin freeze-thaw degradation and structural adhesive bond failure at FL410.
  • 3. DGCA CAR & ICAO Airworthiness Compliance: DGCA CAR Section 2 and ICAO Annex 8 mandate strict structural thermal endurance for firewalls and pressurized cabins.
  • 4. Air Safety Investigation & Incident Prevention: Investigators analyze thermal barrier coating spalling and bleed duct burn-through using cylindrical conduction equations during uncontained fire root-cause audits.

Pillar 1: Formal Technical Definition & Nomenclature

1D steady conduction governs heat transfer across plane, cylindrical, and spherical walls where temperature varies in a single spatial coordinate without temporal fluctuation ($\frac{\partial T}{\partial t} = 0$).
  • Physical Meaning: Thermal energy diffuses through solid barriers driven exclusively by temperature gradients, obeying Fourier's Law of Heat Conduction.
  • Governing Law: Expressed mathematically as $\dot{Q} = -kA \frac{dT}{dx}$ for plane geometries, accounting for area variation in radial systems.
  • Continuum Boundary: Valid only within continuous solid structural media where the Knudsen number remains well below $0.01$.

Nomenclature & Symbols

  • $\dot{Q}$ = Rate of heat transfer (W)
  • $k$ = Thermal conductivity (W/(m$\cdot$K))
  • $A$ = Heat transfer surface area (m$^2$)
  • $T$ = Temperature (K or $^\circ$C)
  • $r, x$ = Spatial coordinates (m)
  • $R_{th}$ = Thermal resistance (K/W)

Pillar 2: Microscopic Physics & Continuum Mechanics

1. Molecular Kinetic State: Thermal energy transfers through lattice vibration (phonons) in solid insulators and free electron migration in metallic structures.
2. Continuum Forces & Gradients: Kinetic energy exchanges occur continuously across microscopic lattice layers, establishing a linear or logarithmic macroscopic temperature gradient.
3. Aerodynamic Consequence: Heat rejection through engine cowlings, turbine blade cooling channels, and cabin insulation walls relies entirely on these conduction resistances.

Pillar 3: Mathematical Derivations & Governing Laws

Step 1: Governing Relation

Apply Fourier's law with conservation of energy for steady 1D flow.

$$\frac{d}{dx}\left( k A(x) \frac{dT}{dx} \right) = 0$$
Step 2: Plane Wall Integration

Integrate across thickness $L$ with constant area $A$ and conductivity $k$.

$$\dot{Q}_{plane} = \frac{T_1 - T_2}{\frac{L}{kA}}$$
Step 3: Cylindrical Wall Integration

Integrate across inner radius $r_1$ to outer radius $r_2$ with $A = 2\pi r L$.

$$\dot{Q}_{cyl} = \frac{2\pi L k (T_1 - T_2)}{\ln(r_2/r_1)}$$
Step 4: Spherical Wall Integration

Integrate across inner radius $r_1$ to outer radius $r_2$ with $A = 4\pi r^2$.

$$\dot{Q}_{sph} = \frac{4\pi k (T_1 - T_2)}{\left(\frac{1}{r_1} - \frac{1}{r_2}\right)}$$

Pillar 4: Dimensional Analysis, SI/CGS Units & Conversion Factors

  • Fundamental Dimensions: Thermal resistance has dimensions of $[\theta M^{-1} L^{-2} T^3]$, while heat transfer rate is $[M L^2 T^{-3}]$.
  • SI Unit: Heat transfer rate in Watts (W), thermal conductivity in W/(m$\cdot$K), and thermal resistance in Kelvin per Watt (K/W).
  • CGS Unit: Heat flow in cal/s, thermal conductivity in cal/(s$\cdot$cm$\cdot^\circ$C), and resistance in $^\circ$C$\cdot$s/cal.
  • Aeronautical & Imperial Equivalents: British Thermal Units per hour (BTU/hr) for aircraft environmental control systems and $^\circ$F$\cdot$hr/BTU for thermal resistance.

Pillar 7: High-Frequency UPSC Examiner Traps & Misconceptions

Trap 1: Using Plane Area for Cylindrical Walls

❌ Examiner Trap: Assuming arithmetic mean area can be substituted into Fourier's law for thick-walled aviation tubing.

✅ UPSC Truth: Cylindrical and spherical geometries strictly require logarithmic mean area or direct integration, yielding natural log terms in thermal resistance.

Trap 2: Neglecting Contact Resistance

❌ Examiner Trap: Assuming perfect thermal contact between aircraft skin panels and internal stiffeners or insulation blankets.

✅ UPSC Truth: Interfacial micro-gaps introduce thermal contact resistance ($R_{tc}$), which must be added in series to composite wall thermal circuits.

Trap 3: Temperature-Dependent Conductivity Ignored

❌ Examiner Trap: Treating thermal conductivity $k$ as constant across extreme temperature ranges from $-50^\circ\text{C}$ to $900^\circ\text{C}$.

✅ UPSC Truth: For high-temperature turbine casings, variation of $k(T)$ must be integrated across the spatial domain using an average or integral conductivity value.

Trap 4: Confusing Critical Radius of Insulation

❌ Examiner Trap: Assuming adding insulation to a cylindrical wire or small tube always decreases heat loss.

✅ UPSC Truth: For cylinders and spheres, adding insulation increases heat loss up to a critical radius $r_{cr} = k/h$ before convection resistance dominates.

Pillar 8: Step-by-Step Worked Aircraft Numerical Problem

Realistic Aircraft Scenario

A stainless steel engine bleed air pipe ($k = 15 \text{ W/(m}\cdot\text{K)$) has an inner radius of $r_1 = 0.05\text{ m}$ and outer radius $r_2 = 0.06\text{ m}$. The inner surface is exposed to hot air at $T_1 = 450^\circ\text{C}$ and the outer surface to ambient air at $T_2 = 50^\circ\text{C}$ with length $L = 2\text{ m}$. Calculate the steady rate of heat loss.

Given Flight Parameters:
k = 15 W/(m\cdot$K), r1 = 0.05 m, r2 = 0.06 m, T1 = 450 °C, T2 = 50 °C, L = 2 m
Governing Formula:
$$\dot{Q} = \frac{2\pi L k (T_1 - T_2)}{\ln(r_2 / r_1)}$$
Step-by-Step Analytical Solution:
Step 1: Compute temperature difference $\Delta T = 450 - 50 = 400\text{ K}$. Step 2: Compute logarithmic radius ratio $\ln(0.06 / 0.05) = \ln(1.2) = 0.1823$. Step 3: Substitute values into the cylindrical conduction equation: $\dot{Q} = \frac{2 \cdot \pi \cdot 2 \cdot 15 \cdot 400}{0.1823} = \frac{75398.2}{0.1823} = 413,594\text{ W}$.
Final Answer: $413.59 kW$
Air Safety Officer Operational Takeaway:

High heat rejection rates through uninsulated bleed ducts require proper fire-sleeve shielding to prevent ignition of adjacent hydraulic lines.

Question 1 • conceptual
What is the primary assumption governing 1D steady heat conduction in aircraft structural walls?
A Temperature varies in two spatial directions and changes with time
B Temperature varies in a single spatial coordinate and remains constant with time
C Thermal conductivity is infinite throughout the solid medium
D Heat transfer rate is zero across the boundaries
Solution & Technical Rationale:
1D steady conduction implies temperature is a function of only one spatial dimension and $\partial T / \partial t = 0$.
Question 2 • statement
Consider the following statements regarding thermal resistance: 1. Thermal resistance of a plane wall is directly proportional to its thickness. 2. Thermal resistance of a cylindrical wall depends logarithmically on radii. Which of the statements given above is/are correct?
A 1 only
B 2 only
C Both 1 and 2
D Neither 1 nor 2
Solution & Technical Rationale:
Both statements are correct. Plane wall resistance is $L/(kA)$, and cylindrical wall resistance is $\ln(r_2/r_1)/(2\pi L k)$.
Question 3 • numerical
Calculate the thermal resistance of an aluminum aircraft skin panel of thickness $2\text{ mm}$, area $0.5\text{ m}^2$, and conductivity $k = 200\text{ W/(m}\cdot\text{K)}$.
A $2.0 \times 10^{-5}\text{ K/W}$
B $5.0 \times 10^{-5}\text{ K/W}$
C $1.0 \times 10^{-4}\text{ K/W}$
D $2.0 \times 10^{-4}\text{ K/W}$
Solution & Technical Rationale:
$R_{th} = \frac{L}{kA} = \frac{0.002}{200 \times 0.5} = \frac{0.002}{100} = 2.0 \times 10^{-5}\text{ K/W}$.
Question 4 • assertion-reasoning
Assertion (A): Adding insulation to a very thin electrical wire always increases heat loss up to a certain critical radius. Reason (R): Conduction resistance of insulation increases while convection resistance decreases with outer radius.
A Both A and R are true and R is the correct explanation of A
B Both A and R are true but R is not the correct explanation of A
C A is true but R is false
D A is false but R is true
Solution & Technical Rationale:
The critical radius phenomenon occurs because adding insulation decreases convection resistance faster than it increases conduction resistance up to $r_{cr} = k/h$.
Question 5 • conceptual
What is the unit of thermal conductivity in SI units?
A W/m$^2\cdot$K
B W/(m$\cdot$K)
C J/(kg$\cdot$K)
D W$\cdot$m/K$^2$
Solution & Technical Rationale:
Thermal conductivity $k$ has SI units of Watts per meter Kelvin (W/(m$\cdot$K)).
Question 6 • conceptual
Which geometry yields a thermal resistance expression containing a natural logarithmic term?
A Plane wall
B Cylindrical wall
C Spherical wall
D Rectangular fin
Solution & Technical Rationale:
Integration of Fourier's law in cylindrical coordinates yields $\ln(r_2/r_1)$ in the thermal resistance denominator.
Question 7 • numerical
A spherical fuel tank of inner radius $1\text{ m}$ and outer radius $1.1\text{ m}$ has a thermal conductivity of $0.05\text{ W/(m}\cdot\text{K)}$. What is its conduction thermal resistance?
A $0.289\text{ K/W}$
B $0.573\text{ K/W}$
C $1.146\text{ K/W}$
D $2.292\text{ K/W}$
Solution & Technical Rationale:
Spherical resistance is $R_{th} = \frac{1}{4\pi k} \left(\frac{1}{r_1} - \frac{1}{r_2}\right) = \frac{1}{4\pi(0.05)} \left(1 - \frac{1}{1.1}\right) = \frac{1}{0.6283} (0.0909) = 0.1445 / 0.5 = 0.289\text{ K/W}$.
Question 8 • statement
Consider the following statements for composite plane walls in series: 1. The rate of heat transfer is identical through each wall layer. 2. Total thermal resistance is the sum of individual resistances. Which of the statements given above is/are correct?
A 1 only
B 2 only
C Both 1 and 2
D Neither 1 nor 2
Solution & Technical Rationale:
For series thermal circuits under steady state, heat flux is constant and resistances add up directly.
Question 9 • conceptual
In the context of aircraft fuselage insulation, what happens to the temperature profile across a multi-layer flat wall under steady state?
A It is parabolic within each solid layer
B It is linear within each homogeneous solid layer
C It is exponential across the entire composite wall
D It remains constant across all layers
Solution & Technical Rationale:
For 1D steady conduction with constant thermal conductivity, the temperature profile within each homogeneous layer is strictly linear.
Question 10 • conceptual
What does Fourier's law of heat conduction state regarding heat flux?
A Heat flux is proportional to temperature difference
B Heat flux is inversely proportional to thermal conductivity
C Heat flux is proportional to the temperature gradient and acts in the direction of increasing temperature
D Heat flux is proportional to the temperature gradient and acts in the direction of decreasing temperature
Solution & Technical Rationale:
Fourier's law includes a negative sign because heat flows down the temperature gradient from high to low temperature.
Question 11 • numerical
If the temperature drop across a plane wall of thermal resistance $0.02\text{ K/W}$ is $100\text{ K}$, what is the heat transfer rate?
A $2000\text{ W}$
B $5000\text{ W}$
C $10000\text{ W}$
D $50000\text{ W}$
Solution & Technical Rationale:
\dot{Q} = \frac{\Delta T}{R_{th}} = \frac{100}{0.02} = 5000\text{ W}.
Question 12 • assertion-reasoning
Assertion (A): Metals generally have much higher thermal conductivity than gases. Reason (R): Heat conduction in metals occurs primarily via free electron migration and lattice vibrations, whereas gases rely on molecular collisions.
A Both A and R are true and R is the correct explanation of A
B Both A and R are true but R is not the correct explanation of A
C A is true but R is false
D A is false but R is true
Solution & Technical Rationale:
Free electron transport in crystalline metallic lattices makes electronic thermal conductivity dominant and significantly higher than molecular diffusion in gases.

What is the thermal resistance formula for a cylindrical tube of length $L$, inner radius $r_1$, outer radius $r_2$, and thermal conductivity $k$?

Rationale:
Integrating Fourier's law in cylindrical coordinates yields $R_{th,cyl} = \frac{\ln(r_2/r_1)}{2\pi L k}$.
Question 2 of 10 +3 / -1
The critical radius of insulation for a sphere is given by which of the following expressions?
A $r_{cr} = k/h$
B $r_{cr} = 2k/h$
C $r_{cr} = h/k$
D $r_{cr} = 4k/h$
Rationale:
For spherical geometry, differentiation of total resistance with respect to radius yields $r_{cr} = 2k/h$ (whereas for cylinders it is $k/h$).
Question 3 of 10 +3 / -1
In a composite plane wall consisting of three layers in series, the overall thermal resistance is:
A The product of individual resistances
B The sum of individual resistances
C The harmonic mean of individual resistances
D The difference between outer and inner resistances
Rationale:
Thermal resistances in series add up directly, analogous to electrical resistors in series.
Question 4 of 10 +3 / -1
What is the governing differential equation for 1D steady heat conduction in a plane wall with constant thermal conductivity and no internal heat generation?
A $\frac{d^2T}{dx^2} = 0$
B $\frac{dT}{dx} = \text{constant}$
C $\frac{\partial^2T}{\partial x^2} = \frac{1}{\alpha} \frac{\partial T}{\partial t}$
D Both A and B
Rationale:
Since $\frac{d}{dx}(k \frac{dT}{dx}) = 0$, integration gives $\frac{dT}{dx} = C_1$ (constant gradient) and $\frac{d^2T}{dx^2} = 0$.
Question 5 of 10 +3 / -1
Calculate the heat transfer rate through a plane composite wall of area $2\text{ m}^2$ and total thermal resistance $0.5\text{ K/W}$ when the temperature difference across it is $150\text{ K}$.
A $75\text{ W}$
B $150\text{ W}$
C $300\text{ W}$
D $600\text{ W}$
Rationale:
\dot{Q} = \frac{\Delta T}{R_{th}} = \frac{150}{0.5} = 300\text{ W}.
Question 6 of 10 +3 / -1
Why is the temperature profile in a cylindrical wall logarithmic rather than linear?
A Because thermal conductivity varies with temperature
B Because the heat transfer area increases with radial distance ($A = 2\pi r L$)
C Because convection occurs inside the cylinder
D Because steady state assumes time-varying boundary conditions
Rationale:
Area expansion $A(r) = 2\pi r L$ in Fourier's law leads to integration of $dr/r$, producing a natural logarithm.
Question 7 of 10 +3 / -1
What is the SI unit of thermal resistance ($R_{th}$)?
A W/K
B K/W
C J/K
D m$^2\cdot$K/W
Rationale:
Thermal resistance is defined as $\Delta T / \dot{Q}$, giving units of Kelvin per Watt (K/W).
Question 8 of 10 +3 / -1
In aircraft bleed air ducting, contact resistance between pipe fittings and thermal sleeves can be minimized by:
A Increasing the fluid velocity inside the duct
B Using thermal interface materials or conductive pastes
C Decreasing the wall thickness to zero
D Reducing ambient pressure
Rationale:
Thermal interface materials fill microscopic air gaps, reducing contact resistance between solid mating surfaces.
Question 9 of 10 +3 / -1
For a spherical wall with inner radius $r_1$ and outer radius $r_2$, what is the expression for thermal resistance?
A $\frac{r_2 - r_1}{4\pi k r_1 r_2}$
B $\frac{\ln(r_2/r_1)}{4\pi k}$
C $\frac{1}{4\pi k}(r_2 - r_1)$
D $\frac{r_2 - r_1}{2\pi k L}$
Rationale:
Integrating $R_{th} = \int_{r_1}^{r_2} \frac{dr}{4\pi r^2 k}$ gives $\frac{1}{4\pi k} \left(\frac{1}{r_1} - \frac{1}{r_2}\right) = \frac{r_2 - r_1}{4\pi k r_1 r_2}$.
Question 10 of 10 +3 / -1
Under what condition does 1D steady conduction equation simplify to a linear temperature distribution in a cylindrical wall?
A Never, cylindrical conduction temperature profile is always logarithmic
B When the radius ratio $r_2/r_1$ is very large
C When the wall thickness is extremely small compared to radius ($r_2 \approx r_1$), approximating a plane wall
D When thermal conductivity approaches zero
Rationale:
When a cylindrical shell is thin ($r_2 - r_1 \ll r_1$), the area remains nearly constant, degenerating the logarithmic profile into an approximately linear one.
