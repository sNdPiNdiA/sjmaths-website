Subtopic 1 of 7 Fluid Mechanics & Separation

1. Bluff-Body Flow & Vortex Shedding (Von Kármán Vortex Street)

Formal Technical Definition: A bluff-body flow is an external flow field past a non-streamlined body characterized by massive boundary layer separation, large wake regions, and high pressure drag. Vortex shedding refers to the oscillatory, alternating detachment of swirling vortices from opposite sides of the bluff body, forming a staggered double row known as the Von Kármán vortex street.

Basic Physical Concept & Molecular/Continuum Mechanics

At the continuum limit (Navier-Stokes domain), when an unstreamlined structure (e.g., circular chimney, ATC radar tower mast, or hangar column) is placed in a fluid stream, the adverse pressure gradient along its rear surface forces the boundary layer to separate. Fluid parcels lack sufficient kinetic energy to overcome the rising pressure toward the rear stagnation point. Shear layers roll up into discrete vortices. Due to hydrodynamic instability, circulation generated on one side grows until it is shed downstream, triggering asymmetric roll-up on the opposite side. This alternating shedding imposes periodic transverse forces on the structure.

Mathematical Derivation & Governing Equations

The frequency of vortex shedding ($f_s$) is directly proportional to free-stream velocity ($V_\infty$) and inversely proportional to characteristic dimension ($D$, e.g., diameter):

$$f_s = \frac{St \cdot V_\infty}{D}$$

Where:
• $St$ = Strouhal number (dimensionless, $\approx 0.2$ for circular cylinders across subcritical Reynolds numbers).
• $V_\infty$ = Free-stream flow velocity ($\text{m/s}$).
• $D$ = Characteristic transverse dimension ($\text{m}$).

The transverse force (lift force per unit span, $L'(t)$) oscillating at frequency $f_s$ is expressed as:

$$L'(t) = \frac{1}{2} \rho V_\infty^2 D C_L(t)$$

Where $\rho$ is air density ($\text{kg/m}^3$) and $C_L(t) = C_{L0} \sin(2\pi f_s t)$ is the unsteady lift coefficient.

Aircraft Application & DGCA Air Safety Connection

At aerodromes, tall structures such as Air Traffic Control (ATC) communication masts, meteorological wind-sock poles, floodlight towers, and jet blast deflectors act as bluff bodies. Vortex shedding can induce resonant structural vibrations (galloping and aeolian vibration) if $f_s$ matches the natural frequency of the structure ($f_n$). Under DGCA CAR Section 4 and ICAO Annex 14 (Aerodromes), aerodrome obstacle clearance and structural integrity standards demand fatigue life verification. Unmitigated vortex shedding caused the collapse of instrument landing system (ILS) glide path towers and structural fatigue cracking in airport lighting masts worldwide.

UPSC Traps & Common Exam Pitfalls

  • Trap 1: Assuming Strouhal number is constant across all Reynolds numbers. $St \approx 0.2$ is only valid in the subcritical laminar boundary layer / turbulent wake regime ($10^3 < Re < 10^5$). In the supercritical regime, wake narrows and $St$ varies.
  • Trap 2: Confusing vortex shedding frequency with structural natural frequency. Resonance occurs strictly when $f_s = f_n$ (lock-in phenomenon).

Step-by-Step Worked Numerical

Problem:

An airport cylindrical wind-sensor mast has a diameter $D = 0.20 \text{ m}$. Calculate the vortex shedding frequency when a steady crosswind blows at $V_\infty = 15 \text{ m/s}$. Assume Strouhal number $St = 0.21$.

Given Data:

$D = 0.20 \text{ m}$, $V_\infty = 15 \text{ m/s}$, $St = 0.21$.

Formula:

$$f_s = \frac{St \cdot V_\infty}{D}$$

Calculation:

$$f_s = \frac{0.21 \times 15}{0.20} = \frac{3.15}{0.20} = 15.75 \text{ Hz}$$

Aviation Operational Takeaway:

A shedding frequency of $15.75 \text{ Hz}$ can excite high-frequency fatigue in thin-walled tubular mast welded joints if structural damping is insufficient, requiring helical strakes or tuned mass dampers.

Subtopic 2 of 7 Similitude & Dimensionless Analysis

2. The Strouhal Number ($St$) Concept

Formal Technical Definition: The Strouhal number ($St$) is a dimensionless parameter describing oscillating flow mechanisms, representing the ratio of inertial forces due to unsteadiness of the flow or local acceleration to inertial forces due to convective velocity.

Basic Physical Concept & Molecular/Continuum Mechanics

In fluid dynamics involving periodic phenomena (such as vortex shedding, oscillating control surfaces, or fluctuating shock waves in transonic flow), time-dependent inertial terms in the Navier-Stokes momentum equations ($\frac{\partial u_i}{\partial t}$) scale relative to convective acceleration terms ($u_j \frac{\partial u_i}{\partial x_j}$). The Strouhal number bridges frequency domain analysis with spatial length scales and flow velocities. In wind-tunnel scaling, matching $St$ between model and full-scale structures ensures dynamic similarity of unsteady vortex shedding.

Mathematical Derivation & Governing Equations

Using Buckingham $\Pi$ theorem for an unsteady flow field where shedding frequency $f$ depends on velocity $V$, characteristic length $L$, kinematic viscosity $\nu$, and density $\rho$:

$$St = \frac{f \cdot L}{V}$$

Where:
• $f$ = Characteristic oscillation frequency ($\text{Hz}$).
• $L$ = Characteristic length scale ($\text{m}$).
• $V$ = Free-stream flow velocity ($\text{m/s}$).

Relation to Reynolds number ($Re$) and Reduced Frequency ($k$):

$$k = \frac{\omega L}{2 V} = \pi St$$

Aircraft Application & DGCA Air Safety Connection

Strouhal number governs acoustic tones generated by landing gear struts, weapon bay cavities, and unfaired antenna masts on transport aircraft (cavity resonance / Aeolian tones). In aerodrome engineering, wind turbine towers and meteorological masts erected near runway approach surfaces must be analyzed using $St$ to prevent structural fatigue. DGCA Airworthiness Directorate mandates that modification of external aircraft skin or antennae must not generate excessive cabin noise or structural vibration due to vortex shedding matching structural modes.

UPSC Traps & Common Exam Pitfalls

  • Trap 1: Confusing Strouhal number with Mach number. $St$ deals with unsteadiness and frequency, whereas Mach number ($Ma = V/a$) deals with compressibility.
  • Trap 2: Assuming $St$ is universal. It depends heavily on body cross-sectional geometry, surface roughness, and freestream turbulence intensity.

Step-by-Step Worked Numerical

Problem:

An aircraft pitot-static probe tube of diameter $D = 10 \text{ mm}$ experiences vortex shedding in a wind tunnel at $V = 50 \text{ m/s}$. If the measured shedding frequency is $950 \text{ Hz}$, calculate the experimental Strouhal number.

Given Data:

$D = 0.01 \text{ m}$, $V = 50 \text{ m/s}$, $f = 950 \text{ Hz}$.

Formula:

$$St = \frac{f \cdot D}{V}$$

Calculation:

$$St = \frac{950 \times 0.01}{50} = \frac{9.5}{50} = 0.19$$

Aviation Operational Takeaway:

The calculated $St = 0.19$ falls squarely within the standard cylinder vortex shedding band ($0.19-0.21$), confirming acoustic resonance risk in unfaired probes during high-speed low-altitude flight.

Subtopic 3 of 7 Aerodrome Infrastructure Safety

3. Wind Loading on Aerodrome Structures (Hangars & Terminals)

Formal Technical Definition: Wind loading refers to the static and dynamic forces exerted by atmospheric winds on buildings, hangars, control towers, and signs, converted into design pressure coefficients in accordance with structural design codes (e.g., IS 875 Part 3 / ASCE 7).

Basic Physical Concept & Molecular/Continuum Mechanics

When moving air impinges on a large-span airport hangar or terminal building, kinetic energy is converted into static pressure (at stagnation points) and suction forces (at separated roof and leeward walls). The boundary layer profile dictates how wind speed increases with height above ground. Gusts introduce high-frequency fluctuations that can excite flexible lightweight roof structures (such as cable-net or curved shell roofs common in modern terminals), leading to flutter or catastrophic uplift failure.

Mathematical Derivation & Governing Equations

The design wind pressure ($p_z$) at height $z$ is calculated using basic wind speed ($V_b$), risk coefficients, terrain factors, and pressure coefficients:

$$p_z = 0.6 \cdot V_z^2$$

Where $V_z$ is the design wind speed at height $z$ ($\text{m/s}$):

$$V_z = V_b \cdot k_1 \cdot k_2 \cdot k_3$$

Where:
• $V_b$ = Basic wind speed ($\text{m/s}$).
• $k_1$ = Risk coefficient (probability of exceedance / design life).
• $k_2$ = Terrain, height, and structure size factor.
• $k_3$ = Topography factor (hills, cliffs).

The net wind load force ($F$) on a structural surface is:

$$F = (C_pe - C_pi) \cdot A \cdot p_z$$

Where $C_pe$ and $C_pi$ are external and internal pressure coefficients, and $A$ is surface area.

Aircraft Application & DGCA Air Safety Connection

Aircraft maintenance hangars have large unobstructed door openings and lightweight roofs prone to internal pressure buildup when wind blows directly into open hangars. This internal pressurization combined with roof suction can tear off hangar roofs, destroying parked commercial aircraft inside. DGCA Aerodrome Standards (CAR Section 4, Series 'B') require all aerodrome structures to withstand maximum 3-second gust wind speeds corresponding to a 50-year return period, as mandated by ICAO Annex 14.

UPSC Traps & Common Exam Pitfalls

  • Trap 1: Forgetting that internal pressure ($C_pi$) can act in addition to external suction ($C_pe$), dramatically doubling net uplift forces on hangar roofs.
  • Trap 2: Confusing basic wind speed (3-second gust at 10m height) with hourly mean wind speed used in meteorological reports (METAR/TAF).

Step-by-Step Worked Numerical

Problem:

An airport maintenance hangar is located in terrain category 2 with basic wind speed $V_b = 45 \text{ m/s}$. At height $z = 10 \text{ m}$, factors are $k_1 = 1.0$, $k_2 = 1.0$, $k_3 = 1.0$. Calculate the design wind pressure $p_z$.

Given Data:

$V_b = 45 \text{ m/s}$, $k_1 = 1.0, k_2 = 1.0, k_3 = 1.0$.

Formula:

$$V_z = V_b \cdot k_1 \cdot k_2 \cdot k_3 = 45 \times 1.0 = 45 \text{ m/s}$$

$$p_z = 0.6 \cdot V_z^2$$

Calculation:

$$p_z = 0.6 \times (45)^2 = 0.6 \times 2025 = 1215 \text{ N/m}^2 (\text{Pa})$$

Aviation Operational Takeaway:

A design wind pressure of $1.215 \text{ kN/m}^2$ must be resisted by structural roof ties and cladding fixings to prevent catastrophic progressive failure during cyclonic storms.

Subtopic 4 of 7 Atmospheric Science & Shear

4. Atmospheric Boundary Layer (ABL) & Wind Shear Profiles

Formal Technical Definition: The Atmospheric Boundary Layer (ABL) or planetary boundary layer is the lowest layer of the troposphere where air motion is directly influenced by frictional drag from the Earth's surface, spanning heights from zero to approximately $1000 \text{ m}$ to $3000 \text{ m}$.

Basic Physical Concept & Molecular/Continuum Mechanics

No-slip boundary condition at the terrestrial surface forces horizontal wind speed to zero at ground level. Molecular viscosity is negligible on a geophysical scale; turbulent eddies (thermal and mechanical) dominate momentum exchange across the ABL. As altitude increases, turbulent shear stress diminishes until geostrophic balance between pressure gradient force and Coriolis force is established in the free atmosphere.

Mathematical Derivation & Governing Equations

Two primary mathematical models describe the vertical wind profile in the ABL:

1. Power Law Profile:

$$V(z) = V_{ref} \left( \frac{z}{z_{ref}} \right)^\alpha$$

2. Logarithmic Law Profile (Neutral Stability):

$$V(z) = \frac{u_*}{\kappa} \ln\left( \frac{z}{z_0} \right)$$

Where:
• $u_*$ = Friction velocity ($\text{m/s}$).
• $\kappa$ = Von Kármán constant ($\approx 0.40$).
• $z_0$ = Surface roughness length ($\text{m}$).
• $\alpha$ = Hellmann power law exponent.

Aircraft Application & DGCA Air Safety Connection

During takeoff and landing, aircraft descend through the ABL, transitioning rapidly from higher wind speeds aloft to lower wind speeds near the runway surface. Severe vertical wind shear in the ABL causes sudden airspeed fluctuations, altitude deviations, and stalls if uncorrected. DGCA CAR Section 8 mandates low-level wind shear alert systems (LLWAS) and predictive wind shear equipment at all major Indian instrumented airports to prevent accidents during critical phases of flight.

UPSC Traps & Common Exam Pitfalls

  • Trap 1: Assuming power law is physically derived from first principles; it is an empirical fit, whereas the logarithmic law is derived from mixing length theory.
  • Trap 2: Forgetting that ABL height varies diurnally (shallow stable boundary layer at night, deep convective boundary layer by afternoon).

Step-by-Step Worked Numerical

Problem:

Wind speed at an anemometer height $z_{ref} = 10 \text{ m}$ is $10 \text{ m/s}$ over an airfield with power law exponent $\alpha = 0.14$. Calculate the wind speed at an approach glide slope altitude of $z = 100 \text{ m}$.

Given Data:

$V_{ref} = 10 \text{ m/s}$, $z_{ref} = 10 \text{ m}$, $z = 100 \text{ m}$, $\alpha = 0.14$.

Formula:

$$V(z) = V_{ref} \left( \frac{z}{z_{ref}} \right)^\alpha$$

Calculation:

$$V(100) = 10 \times \left( \frac{100}{10} \right)^{0.14} = 10 \times (10)^{0.14} = 10 \times 1.38038 = 13.80 \text{ m/s}$$

Aviation Operational Takeaway:

An increase of $3.8 \text{ m/s}$ ($7.4 \text{ knots}$) over a $90 \text{ m}$ vertical descent represents significant wind shear that pilots must anticipate during final approach flare.

Subtopic 5 of 7 Aerodrome Ground Safety

5. Wind Effects on Parked Aircraft & Ground Vehicles

Formal Technical Definition: Aerodynamic forces and overturning moments exerted by high-velocity ambient winds and jet blasts on stationary parked aircraft, ground support equipment (GSE), and high-profile airport transport vehicles.

Basic Physical Concept & Molecular/Continuum Mechanics

Parked aircraft act as complex multi-lifting-surface structures. High crosswinds create differential lift and pitching/yawing moments across swept wings and vertical stabilizers. When wind impinges at high angles of attack or sideslip on parked aircraft not secured with heavy mooring chains, aerodynamic lift can exceed aircraft operational tare weight, or tipping moments can exceed stabilizing ground moments.

Mathematical Derivation & Governing Equations

The aerodynamic overturning moment ($M_{ot}$) on a parked aircraft or high-profile vehicle is given by:

$$M_{ot} = \frac{1}{2} \rho V^2 S_{ref} h C_m$$

Where:
• $\rho$ = Air density ($\text{kg/m}^3$).
• $V$ = Wind velocity ($\text{m/s}$).
• $S_{ref}$ = Projected lateral surface area ($\text{m}^2$).
• $h$ = Height of center of pressure above ground ($\text{m}$).
• $C_m$ = Pitching/overturning moment coefficient.

Aircraft Application & DGCA Air Safety Connection

Severe cyclonic storms hitting Indian coastal airports (e.g., Chennai, Mumbai, Visakhapatnam) frequently threaten parked un-hangared aircraft. High winds create lift on wings, pivoting aircraft backward or breaking gear struts. DGCA Aerodrome Advisory Circulars mandate standard operating procedures (SOPs) for issuing severe weather alerts, parking aircraft into the wind, and executing mandatory aircraft mooring and grounding protocols.

UPSC Traps & Common Exam Pitfalls

  • Trap 1: Assuming aircraft are completely safe facing downstream; tailwinds on swept-wing aircraft can generate negative stability moments and tail-tipping.
  • Trap 2: Confusing ground vehicle aerodynamic drag with structural overturning moment arm height ($h$).

Step-by-Step Worked Numerical

Problem:

A parked commuter turboprop has a projected lateral area $S_{ref} = 30 \text{ m}^2$, center of pressure height $h = 2.5 \text{ m}$, and overturning moment coefficient $C_m = 0.80$. Calculate the aerodynamic overturning moment in a $30 \text{ m/s}$ crosswind ($\rho = 1.225 \text{ kg/m}^3$).

Given Data:

$S = 30 \text{ m}^2$, $h = 2.5 \text{ m}$, $C_m = 0.80$, $V = 30 \text{ m/s}$, $\rho = 1.225 \text{ kg/m}^3$.

Formula:

$$M_{ot} = \frac{1}{2} \rho V^2 S_{ref} h C_m$$

Calculation:

$$M_{ot} = 0.5 \times 1.225 \times (30)^2 \times 30 \times 2.5 \times 0.80$$

$$M_{ot} = 0.5 \times 1.225 \times 900 \times 30 \times 2.5 \times 0.80 = 33,075 \text{ N}\cdot\text{m}$$

Aviation Operational Takeaway:

An overturning moment of $33.075 \text{ kN}\cdot\text{m}$ must be counteracted by heavy ballast or tie-down anchors to prevent aircraft tipping during gales.

Subtopic 6 of 7 Experimental Aerodynamics

6. Wind-Tunnel Testing & Similitude Principles

Formal Technical Definition: Wind-tunnel testing is an experimental technique where scaled aerodynamic models are subjected to controlled air streams to measure forces, moments, pressure distributions, and flow visualization, governed by the principles of geometric, kinematic, and dynamic similitude.

Basic Physical Concept & Molecular/Continuum Mechanics

To replicate full-scale flight or atmospheric wind flow conditions in a laboratory facility, dimensionless parameters governing fluid motion must be identically matched between model and prototype. Exact matching of both Reynolds number ($Re$) and Mach number ($Ma$) simultaneously using a scale model in air is mathematically impossible unless cryogenic wind tunnels or pressurized facilities are used. Consequently, test engineers utilize scaling laws and corrections.

Mathematical Derivation & Governing Equations

Dynamic similitude requires equality of key dimensionless groups:

$$Re_m = Re_p \implies \frac{V_m L_m}{\nu_m} = \frac{V_p L_p}{\nu_p}$$
$$Ma_m = Ma_p \implies \frac{V_m}{a_m} = \frac{V_p}{a_p}$$

Aerodynamic force coefficients ($C_L, C_D, C_m$) measured in wind tunnels are defined as:

$$C_L = \frac{L}{\frac{1}{2} \rho V^2 S}$$

UPSC Traps & Common Exam Pitfalls

  • Trap 1: Assuming a 1:10 scale model tested at the same wind speed has the same Reynolds number. ($Re$ drops by factor of 10 unless velocity is increased 10-fold).
  • Trap 2: Neglecting blockage corrections when testing large models in small wind-tunnel test sections.

Step-by-Step Worked Numerical

Problem:

An aircraft prototype has a chord length $L_p = 2.0 \text{ m}$ flying at $V_p = 100 \text{ m/s}$. A scale model with $L_m = 0.4 \text{ m}$ is tested in a wind tunnel using standard air ($\nu_m = \nu_p$). Calculate the wind-tunnel velocity required for strict Reynolds number similitude.

Given Data:

$L_p = 2.0 \text{ m}$, $V_p = 100 \text{ m/s}$, $L_m = 0.4 \text{ m}$, $\nu_m = \nu_p$.

Formula:

$$V_m = V_p \left( \frac{L_p}{L_m} \right) \left( \frac{\nu_m}{\nu_p} \right)$$

Calculation:

$$V_m = 100 \times \left( \frac{2.0}{0.4} \right) \times 1.0 = 100 \times 5.0 = 500 \text{ m/s}$$

Aviation Operational Takeaway:

A wind-tunnel speed of $500 \text{ m/s}$ ($Ma \approx 1.47$) is supersonic, making subsonic Reynolds number matching impossible with standard air; hence pressurized or cryogenic wind tunnels are necessary.

Subtopic 7 of 7 Aeroelasticity & Stability

7. Aeroelastic Effects: Introduction to Flutter, Divergence, and Control Reversal

Formal Technical Definition: Aeroelasticity is the scientific discipline studying the mutual interaction between aerodynamic forces and structural elasticity, resulting in phenomena such as static aeroelasticity (divergence and control reversal) and dynamic aeroelasticity (flutter).

Basic Physical Concept & Molecular/Continuum Mechanics

When an elastic aircraft structure (wing, tail, or control surface) deflects under aerodynamic load, the resulting shape change alters the aerodynamic pressure distribution. This altered pressure changes structural deflection. This positive or negative feedback loop among inertial forces, elastic restoring forces, and aerodynamic forces can lead to catastrophic structural instability if damping is overcome.

Mathematical Derivation & Governing Equations

The coupled equations of motion for an aeroelastic system (Collar's Aeroelastic Triangle) in matrix form:

$$[M]\{\ddot{q}\} + [C]\{\dot{q}\} + ([K] - [K_{aer}])\{q\} = 0$$

Where:
• $[M]$ = Structural mass matrix.
• $[C]$ = Damping matrix.
• $[K]$ = Structural stiffness matrix.
• $[K_{aer}]$ = Aerodynamic stiffness matrix (dependent on dynamic pressure $q_\infty$).

Aircraft Application & DGCA Air Safety Connection

Flutter is one of the most feared phenomena in aviation. Unchecked flutter can disintegrate an aircraft wing in fractions of a second (e.g., historical Lockheed L-188 Electra accidents). DGCA Airworthiness Directorate (CAR Section 2) strictly mandates that all commercial transport aircraft undergo ground vibration testing (GVT) and flight flutter clearance up to $1.15 \times V_{DF}$ (Design Dive Speed) before airworthiness certification is granted.

UPSC Traps & Common Exam Pitfalls

  • Trap 1: Confusing divergence (static aeroelastic instability involving loss of stiffness) with flutter (dynamic instability involving coupling of bending and torsion modes).
  • Trap 2: Assuming control reversal occurs only at supersonic speeds; it can occur at high subsonic speeds on flexible swept wings.

Step-by-Step Worked Numerical

Problem:

An aircraft has a design cruise speed $V_C = 250 \text{ knots}$. According to DGCA / airworthiness regulations, what is the minimum required design dive speed ($V_D$) and flight flutter clearance speed ($V_{DF}$) for flutter safety?

Given Data:

$V_C = 250 \text{ knots}$.

Formula:

$$V_D \ge 1.25 \cdot V_C \quad \text{and} \quad V_{DF} = 1.15 \cdot V_D$$

Calculation:

$$V_D = 1.25 \times 250 = 312.5 \text{ knots}$$

$$V_{DF} = 1.15 \times 312.5 = 359.375 \text{ knots}$$

Aviation Operational Takeaway:

Flight test engineers must clear the aircraft for flutter-free operation up to $359.4 \text{ knots}$ EAS before the aircraft type can be certified for passenger service.

Aviation Case Studies & DGCA Air Safety Connections

Real-world accident investigations and regulatory frameworks linking industrial aerodynamics to air safety.

Case Study 1: Tacoma Narrows Bridge Collapse (1940) — Aeroelastic Flutter & Resonance

Incident Overview: Although not an aircraft, the collapse of the Tacoma Narrows suspension bridge due to $42 \text{ mph}$ winds is the textbook foundational case study for aeroelastic flutter and torsional divergence. Plate girders installed on the bridge deck acted as bluff bodies, shedding vortices that excited torsional natural frequencies of the structure.

Air Safety Connection: The physics governing bridge deck flutter are identical to aircraft wing flutter. Following this disaster, aeronautical and civil engineering regulations worldwide mandated wind-tunnel testing of all suspension bridges, airport control tower masts, and aircraft lifting surfaces to verify dynamic stability limits.

Case Study 2: Lockheed L-188 Electra Whirl Flutter Accidents (1959–1960)

Incident Overview: Two Braniff and Northwest Airlines turboprop aircraft disintegrated mid-air due to a hitherto unknown aeroelastic phenomenon termed "propeller-nacelle whirl flutter." Gyroscopic precession coupling with flexible wing bending modes caused violent, self-excited oscillations that tore wings off the aircraft.

DGCA & FAA Regulatory Response: The entire global Electra fleet was grounded, engine mount stiffness was drastically increased, and airworthiness codes (CAR Section 2 / FAR Part 25) were rewritten to mandate rigorous aeroelastic stability analysis for all turbine-powered aircraft.

Case Study 3: Cyclone Damage to Hangar Infrastructure at Indian Coastal Airports

Incident Overview: Severe cyclonic storms (e.g., Cyclone Vardah in Chennai, Cyclone Fani in Odisha) have repeatedly caused catastrophic structural failures of airport maintenance hangars, radar domes (radomes), and signages.

DGCA Aerodrome Standards: CAR Section 4 (Aerodromes) mandates that all aerodrome operators perform structural wind-load audits every 5 years, ensuring hangars withstand 50-year peak gust velocities and feature pressure relief panels to prevent internal pressurization uplift failures.

🎯 UPSC Interview Corner (Air Safety Officer Board Q&A)

Q1: As an Air Safety Officer investigating an incident where an aircraft control surface suffered fatigue cracking near an unfaired antenna mast, what aerodynamic phenomenon would you investigate? ▼

Model Answer: The primary phenomenon to investigate is vortex shedding (Von Kármán vortex street) from the unfaired antenna mast acting as a bluff body. If the shedding frequency ($f_s = St \cdot V / D$) matches the natural structural frequency of the control surface skin or attachment brackets, resonance occurs ("lock-in"). This induces high-cycle fatigue cracking, violating DGCA CAR Section 2 airworthiness standards regarding external modifications.

Q2: How does the Atmospheric Boundary Layer affect aircraft stability during the landing flare phase at an Indian aerodrome? ▼

Model Answer: As an aircraft descends through the ABL during landing flare, wind speed drops rapidly due to surface friction (logarithmic or power law profile). This sudden decrease in headwind results in an instantaneous loss of airspeed and lift, causing the aircraft to sink rapidly if the pilot does not apply corrective thrust and pitch input. Low-level wind shear detection systems (LLWAS) are mandatory at major Indian airports to warn pilots of these hazards.
