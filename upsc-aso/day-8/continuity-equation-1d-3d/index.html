Continuity equation – 1D, 3D

Day 8 of 129 Fluid Mechanics & Machinery Phase 1: Foundations 90-120 min Master Class

1. Understand (From Absolute Zero, 5–10 min)

The Continuity Equation is the mathematical formulation of the fundamental physical law of Conservation of Mass applied to fluid flow. Simply stated, mass cannot be created or destroyed within a flow system. In any given control volume, the rate of accumulation of mass must equal the net mass flow rate entering the volume minus the net mass flow rate leaving it.

1. Intuitive Explanation and Physical Meaning

Imagine a highway where cars represent fluid particles. If 100 cars per minute enter a tunnel and only 80 cars per minute exit, the number of cars inside the tunnel must be increasing at a rate of 20 cars per minute (unsteady accumulation). If the tunnel cannot pack cars any closer (incompressible flow), then exactly 100 cars must exit for every 100 that enter. This fundamental balance is the essence of continuity.

In fluid dynamics, we express this concept in two primary ways:

  • 1D Continuity (Integral/Macro View): Used for engineering design in ducts, pipes, nozzles, and streamtubes. It relates bulk flow properties (average velocity, cross-sectional area, density) at different cross-sections.
  • 3D Continuity (Differential/Micro View): Used to analyze complex, point-by-point flow fields around aircraft wings, through turbine blades, or in atmospheric layers. It governs the velocity vector field $\vec{V}(x,y,z,t) = u\hat{i} + v\hat{j} + w\hat{k}$ at every spatial coordinate.

2. SI Units and Dimensions

  • Mass flow rate ($\dot{m}$): $kg/s$ (Dimensions: $[M T-1]$)
  • Volumetric flow rate ($Q$): $m^3/s$ (Dimensions: $[L^3 T-1]$)
  • Density ($ρ$): $kg/m^3$ (Dimensions: $[M L-3]$)
  • Velocity vector ($\vec{V}$): $m/s$ (Dimensions: $[L T-1]$)
  • Area ($A$): $m^2$ (Dimensions: $[L^2]$)

3. Standard UPSC ASO Terminology

  • Control Volume (CV): A defined region in space through which fluid flows.
  • Control Surface (CS): The boundary limiting the Control Volume.
  • Solenoidal Field: A velocity field where the divergence is zero ($\nabla · \vec{V} = 0$), implying incompressible flow.
  • Eulerian Specification: Analyzing fluid flow by focusing on specific points in space as time progresses, which directly yields the differential continuity equation.

2. Learn the Concept (Complete Theoretical Foundation & Derivations, 20–25 min)

To master the continuity equation for UPSC ASO and GATE examinations, we must construct its theoretical foundations systematically from first principles using vector calculus and thermodynamics.

1. Derivation of 1D Continuity (The Streamtube Approach)

Consider a steady, one-dimensional flow through a streamtube or duct with varying cross-sectional area. A streamtube is bounded by streamlines, meaning no fluid can cross its lateral boundaries. Let cross-section 1 have area $A_1$, density $ρ_1$, and average velocity $V_1$. Let cross-section 2 have area $A_2$, density $ρ_2$, and average velocity $V_2$.

The mass entering per unit time through section 1 is:

$$ \dot{m}in = ρ_1 A_1 V_1 $$

The mass leaving per unit time through section 2 is:

$$ \dot{m}out = ρ_2 A_2 V_2 $$

Since the flow is steady, there is no accumulation of mass within the streamtube over time ($∂ mCV / ∂ t = 0$). Conservation of mass dictates $\dot{m}in = \dot{m}out$:

$$ ρ_1 A_1 V_1 = ρ_2 A_2 V_2 = constant = \dot{m} $$

If the fluid is incompressible (constant density, $ρ_1 = ρ_2 = ρ$), the 1D continuity equation simplifies to the conservation of volumetric flow rate:

$$ A_1 V_1 = A_2 V_2 = Q $$ ---

2. Derivation of 3D Continuity in Cartesian Coordinates

Consider an infinitesimal control volume of dimensions $dx$, $dy$, and $dz$ in a Cartesian coordinate system. Let the velocity vector be $\vec{V} = u\hat{i} + v\hat{j} + w\hat{k}$ and the fluid density be $ρ(x,y,z,t)$.

Mass Flow through the $x$-planes:

  • Mass flow entering the left face at $x$ (area $dy \, dz$): $$ \dot{m}x = ρ u \, dy \, dz $$
  • Mass flow leaving the right face at $x + dx$: Using a first-order Taylor series expansion: $$ \dot{m}x+dx = [ ρ u + ∂ (ρ u)∂ x dx ] dy \, dz $$
  • Net mass accumulation rate in the $x$-direction: $$ \dot{m}x - \dot{m}x+dx = -∂ (ρ u)∂ x dx \, dy \, dz $$

Mass Flow through the $y$ and $z$-planes:

By symmetry, the net mass accumulation rates in the $y$ and $z$ directions are:

$$ \dot{m}y - \dot{m}y+dy = -∂ (ρ v)∂ y dx \, dy \, dz $$ $$ \dot{m}z - \dot{m}z+dz = -∂ (ρ w)∂ z dx \, dy \, dz $$

Total Net Mass Inflow Rate:

$$ Net Inflow Rate = -[ ∂ (ρ u)∂ x + ∂ (ρ v)∂ y + ∂ (ρ w)∂ z ] dx \, dy \, dz $$

Rate of Accumulation of Mass inside the Control Volume:

The mass inside the infinitesimal control volume at any instant is $m = ρ \, dx \, dy \, dz$. The rate of change of mass inside the control volume is:

$$ ∂ mCV∂ t = ∂ ρ∂ t dx \, dy \, dz $$

Equating Accumulation to Net Inflow (Conservation of Mass):

$$ ∂ ρ∂ t dx \, dy \, dz = -[ ∂ (ρ u)∂ x + ∂ (ρ v)∂ y + ∂ (ρ w)∂ z ] dx \, dy \, dz $$

Dividing throughout by the differential volume $dx \, dy \, dz$, we obtain the fundamental 3D Differential Continuity Equation:

$$ ∂ ρ∂ t + ∂ (ρ u)∂ x + ∂ (ρ v)∂ y + ∂ (ρ w)∂ z = 0 $$
---

3. Vector Calculus Formulations

The 3D continuity equation can be elegantly written using the vector divergence operator $\nabla · (\quad)$:

$$ ∂ ρ∂ t + \nabla · (ρ \vec{V}) = 0 $$

By applying vector identities, we can expand the second term: $\nabla · (ρ \vec{V}) = ρ (\nabla · \vec{V}) + \vec{V} · \nabla ρ$. Substituting this back gives:

$$ ( ∂ ρ∂ t + \vec{V} · \nabla ρ ) + ρ (\nabla · \vec{V}) = 0 $$

Recalling the definition of the Material Derivative (or Substantial Derivative) $D/Dt = ∂/∂ t + \vec{V} · \nabla$, we express the continuity equation as:

$$ DρDt + ρ (\nabla · \vec{V}) = 0 $$
---

4. Analysis of Critical Sub-Cases

Case A: Incompressible Flow (Steady or Unsteady)

An incompressible flow is one where the density of a fluid element remains constant as it moves along its path. Mathematically, this means the material derivative of density is zero:

$$ DρDt = 0 $$

Substituting this into the material derivative formulation of continuity yields:

$$ \nabla · \vec{V} = 0 \quad or \quad ∂ u∂ x + ∂ v∂ y + ∂ w∂ z = 0 $$

Crucial Examination Insight: A flow field does not need to be steady to satisfy $\nabla · \vec{V} = 0$. Unsteady, incompressible flow must also satisfy this relation.

Case B: Steady Compressible Flow

For steady flow, properties at any fixed spatial point do not change with time, meaning $∂ / ∂ t = 0$. The continuity equation simplifies to:

$$ \nabla · (ρ \vec{V}) = 0 \quad or \quad ∂ (ρ u)∂ x + ∂ (ρ v)∂ y + ∂ (ρ w)∂ z = 0 $$

Case C: Cylindrical Coordinates $(r, θ, z)$

For systems with axisymmetric or rotational geometry (such as pipes, jet engines, and vortices), the continuity equation is written as:

$$ ∂ ρ∂ t + 1r∂ (r ρ v_r)∂ r + 1r∂ (ρ v_θ)∂ θ + ∂ (ρ v_z)∂ z = 0 $$

For incompressible flow in cylindrical coordinates, this simplifies to:

$$ 1r∂ (r v_r)∂ r + 1r∂ v_θ∂ θ + ∂ v_z∂ z = 0 $$ ---

5. Advanced Formulations: Stream Functions and Velocity Potentials

The Stream Function ($ψ$)

To simplify the solution of 2D incompressible fluid flows, we define a scalar function $ψ(x,y,t)$ known as the Stream Function. It is defined such that it automatically satisfies the 2D incompressible continuity equation ($∂ u / ∂ x + ∂ v / ∂ y = 0$):

$$ u = ∂ ψ∂ y, \quad v = -∂ ψ∂ x $$

Let's verify this by substitution:

$$ ∂ u∂ x + ∂ v∂ y = ∂∂ x(∂ ψ∂ y) + ∂∂ y(-∂ ψ∂ x) = ∂^2 ψ∂ x \, ∂ y - ∂^2 ψ∂ y \, ∂ x = 0 $$

Since mixed partial derivatives of continuous functions are equal, any flow field derived from a continuous stream function physically satisfies the conservation of mass. Streamlines are defined by curves where $ψ = constant$.

The Velocity Potential ($φ$)

For irrotational flows ($\nabla × \vec{V} = 0$), there exists a scalar function $φ(x,y,z,t)$ called the Velocity Potential, defined as:

$$ \vec{V} = \nabla φ \implies u = ∂ φ∂ x, \quad v = ∂ φ∂ y, \quad w = ∂ φ∂ z $$

Substituting these velocity components into the incompressible 3D continuity equation yields Laplace's Equation:

$$ \nabla^2 φ = 0 \quad or \quad ∂^2 φ∂ x^2 + ∂^2 φ∂ y^2 + ∂^2 φ∂ z^2 = 0 $$

3. Memorize Important Facts (10 min)

Core Governing Relations

  • General 3D Differential Continuity Equation: $$ ∂ ρ∂ t + \nabla · (ρ \vec{V}) = 0 $$
  • Incompressible 3D Continuity (Solenoidal condition): $$ \nabla · \vec{V} = 0 \implies ∂ u∂ x + ∂ v∂ y + ∂ w∂ z = 0 $$
  • 1D Continuity (Compressible vs Incompressible): $$ ρ_1 A_1 V_1 = ρ_2 A_2 V_2 \quad (Compressible) $$ $$ A_1 V_1 = A_2 V_2 \quad (Incompressible) $$
  • Cylindrical Incompressible Continuity: $$ 1r∂ (r v_r)∂ r + 1r∂ v_θ∂ θ + ∂ v_z∂ z = 0 $$

Key Axioms and Definitions

  • Solenoidal Vector Field: Any vector field whose divergence is zero. For velocity fields, this represents incompressible flow.
  • Stream Function ($ψ$) Relations (2D): $$ u = ∂ ψ∂ y, \quad v = -∂ ψ∂ x $$
  • Laplace's Equation for Potential Flow: $$ \nabla^2 φ = 0 $$ (Valid strictly for steady/unsteady, incompressible, irrotational flow fields).

4. Aviation Application (5–10 min)

The continuity equation is fundamental to aerodynamic analysis, aircraft systems, and instrument operations. Below are three critical aviation applications:

1. Pitot-Static System and Venturi Tubes

Aircraft carburetors and traditional vacuum-driven instruments rely on Venturi tubes. A Venturi is a convergent-divergent duct. According to the 1D continuity equation for an incompressible fluid:

$$ Ainlet Vinlet = Athroat Vthroat $$

Because $Athroat < Ainlet$, the velocity of the air must increase at the throat ($Vthroat > Vinlet$). Applying Bernoulli's equation, this acceleration of flow leads to a corresponding pressure drop:

$$ pthroat = pinlet - 12ρ (Vthroat^2 - Vinlet^2) $$

This localized low pressure is utilized to draw fuel into the airstream in carburetors, or to drive gyroscopic flight instruments.

2. Compressible Aerodynamics: Area-Velocity Relation

In high-speed aviation (transonic and supersonic flight), density variations cannot be ignored. Combining the 1D continuity equation, Euler's momentum equation, and the definition of the speed of sound $a = √dp/dρ$ yields the Area-Velocity Relation:

$$ dAA = (M^2 - 1) dVV $$

Where $M = V/a$ is the local Mach number. This relation dictates nozzle design for jet engines:

  • Subsonic flow ($M < 1$): $(M^2 - 1) < 0$. To increase velocity ($dV > 0$), area must decrease ($dA < 0$). This acts as a nozzle (converging duct).
  • Supersonic flow ($M > 1$): $(M^2 - 1) > 0$. To increase velocity ($dV > 0$), area must increase ($dA > 0$). This acts as a nozzle (diverging duct).

3. Boundary Layer Equations

When analyzing the viscous boundary layer over a wing airfoil, we simplify the 2D Navier-Stokes equations using Prandtl's boundary layer approximations. The scale of boundary layer thickness $\delta$ relative to the wing chord length $L$ is $\delta/L \ll 1$. Applying these scales to the 2D incompressible continuity equation:

$$ ∂ u∂ x + ∂ v∂ y = 0 $$

Since the streamwise velocity $u$ changes significantly over the length scale $L$, and the normal velocity $v$ changes over the microscopic scale $\delta$, we scale the terms:

$$ \mathcal{O}(U_∞L) + \mathcal{O}(v\max\delta) = 0 \implies v\max ≈ U_∞ (\deltaL) $$

This allows aerodynamicists to calculate the small but significant vertical velocity $v(y)$ normal to the wing surface, which contributes to displacement thickness and aerodynamic drag.

5. UPSC ASO Focus (10 min)

Frequently Asked Exam Facts

  • Dimensional Homogeneity: Every term in the 3D differential continuity equation, such as $∂ (ρ u)∂ x$, has dimensions of mass flow rate per unit volume: $[M L-3 T-1]$.
  • Dilation Rate (Volumetric Strain Rate): The term $\nabla · \vec{V}$ is physically defined as the rate of volumetric expansion per unit volume. In an incompressible fluid, this dilation rate is strictly zero.
  • Stream Function Existence: A stream function $ψ$ exists for any 2D flow (steady or unsteady, rotational or irrotational) as long as it satisfies the continuity equation.
  • Velocity Potential Existence: A velocity potential $φ$ exists if and only if the flow is irrotational ($\nabla × \vec{V} = 0$). It does not require the flow to be incompressible or steady.

Conceptual Traps

  • The "Steady vs. Incompressible" Trap: Candidates often mistake $∂ ρ∂ t = 0$ as the condition for incompressible flow. This is false. $∂ ρ∂ t = 0$ is the condition for steady flow. The actual condition for incompressible flow is $DρDt = 0$, which reduces the continuity equation to $\nabla · \vec{V} = 0$.
  • The "1D Continuity Compressibility" Trap: In 1D compressible flow, $A_1 V_1 = A_2 V_2$ is incorrect. You must use $ρ_1 A_1 V_1 = ρ_2 A_2 V_2$. Always check if density variation or Mach number is specified before applying the area-velocity relations.
  • The "Stream Function Definition" Trap: Be meticulous with signs. While some formulations use $u = -∂ ψ∂ y$ and $v = ∂ ψ∂ x$, the standard convention is $u = ∂ ψ∂ y$ and $v = -∂ ψ∂ x$. Confusing signs will lead to incorrect calculations of vorticity or circulation.

Q1. A 3D velocity field is given by $\vec{V} = (ax + by) \hat{i} + (cy + dz) \hat{j} + (ex + fz) \hat{k}$, where $a, b, c, d, e, f$ are non-zero constants. What is the necessary condition for this flow field to represent an incompressible fluid?
A) $a + b + c = 0$
B) $a + c + f = 0$
C) $b + d + e = 0$
D) $a + e + f = 0$
For an incompressible flow, the divergence of the velocity field must be zero: $\nabla · \vec{V} = 0$. $$ ∂ u∂ x + ∂ v∂ y + ∂ w∂ z = 0 $$ Here: $$ u = ax + by \implies ∂ u∂ x = a $$ $$ v = cy + dz \implies ∂ v∂ y = c $$ $$ w = ex + fz \implies ∂ w∂ z = f $$ Substituting these values: $$ a + c + f = 0 $$ Thus, Option B is correct.
Q2. In a 2D steady incompressible flow, the velocity component in the $x$-direction is $u = 3x^2 - 2xy$. If the boundary condition is $v = 0$ at $y = 0$, what is the expression for the velocity component $v$?
A) $v = -6xy + y^2$
B) $v = 6xy - y^2$
C) $v = -6x^2y + xy^2$
D) $v = -3x^2y + y^2$

Option A is correct.
Q3. The density field in an unsteady compressible flow is given by $ρ = ρ_0 e-α t$ (where $ρ_0$ and $α$ are constant). What is the value of $\nabla · \vec{V}$ if the density remains uniform in space?

D) $-α ρ_0$

Q7. What is the physical significance of the term $\nabla · \vec{V}$ in the continuity equation?
A) Volumetric flow rate per unit area
B) Mass flow rate per unit volume
C) Volumetric strain rate (dilation rate) of a fluid element
D) Rate of change of momentum of the fluid element
The mathematical divergence of the velocity field, $\nabla · \vec{V}$, is physically defined as the time rate of change of volume per unit volume of a fluid element. In fluid kinematics, this is known as the volumetric strain rate or dilation rate. For an incompressible fluid, the volume of any fluid element must remain constant, which is why $\nabla · \vec{V} = 0$. Option C is correct.
Q8. For an axisymmetric 2D incompressible flow in cylindrical coordinates, the radial velocity component is $v_r = Cr$ (where $C$ is a constant). What is the angular velocity component $v_θ$ if $v_θ = 0$ at $θ = 0$?
A) $v_θ = -Crθ$
B) $v_θ = 0$
C) $v_θ = Crθ$
D) $v_θ = C$
The 2D incompressible cylindrical continuity equation (axisymmetric / no $z$-variation) is: $$ 1r∂ (r v_r)∂ r + 1r∂ v_θ∂ θ = 0 $$ Substitute $v_r = Cr$ into the first term: $$ r v_r = C \implies ∂ (r v_r)∂ r = ∂ (C)∂ r = 0 $$ Therefore: $$ 1r∂ v_θ∂ θ = 0 \implies ∂ v_θ∂ θ = 0 \implies v_θ = f(r) $$ Using the boundary condition $v_θ = 0$ at $θ = 0$ implies $f(r) = 0$. Thus, $v_θ = 0$. Option B is correct.

D) $w = -2(x+y)z$
Applying the 3D incompressible continuity equation: $$ ∂ u∂ x + ∂ v∂ y + ∂ w∂ z = 0 $$ $$ ∂ u∂ x = 2x, \quad ∂ v∂ y = 2y $$ $$ 2x + 2y + ∂ w∂ z = 0 \implies ∂ w∂ z = -2(x + y) $$ Integrating with respect to $z$: $$ w = ∫ -2(x + y) dz = -2(x + y)z + f(x, y) $$ Using the boundary condition $w = 0$ at $z = 0$ yields $f(x, y) = 0$. $$ w = -2(x+y)z $$ Option A is correct.
Q14. In 2D polar coordinates, if a velocity field is purely tangential ($v_r = 0$), what form must $v_θ(θ, r)$ take to satisfy the incompressible continuity equation?
A) $v_θ$ must be a function of $θ$ only.
B) $v_θ$ must be a function of $r$ only.
C) $v_θ$ must be a linear function of both $r$ and $θ$.
D) $v_θ$ must be zero everywhere.
The 2D incompressible continuity equation in polar coordinates is: $$ 1r∂ (r v_r)∂ r + 1r∂ v_θ∂ θ = 0 $$ Given $v_r = 0$, the first term is zero, which simplifies the equation to: $$ 1r∂ v_θ∂ θ = 0 \implies ∂ v_θ∂ θ = 0 $$ This indicates that $v_θ$ does not vary with $θ$. Therefore, $v_θ$ must be a function of $r$ only: $v_θ = f(r)$. Option B is correct.
Q15. At high supersonic speeds, the area-velocity relation is given by $dAA = (M^2 - 1)dVV$. If $M = 2.0$, what is the effect of an increase in cross-sectional area of the duct ($dA > 0$)?
A) The velocity of the fluid decreases.
B) The velocity of the fluid increases.
C) The velocity of the fluid remains constant.
D) The flow transitions instantly to subsonic.
Given $M = 2.0$, we have $M^2 - 1 = (2)^2 - 1 = 3 > 0$. The area-velocity relation becomes: $$ dAA = 3 dVV $$ Since $dA > 0$, we must have $dV > 0$. Thus, an increase in cross-sectional area causes an increase in flow velocity, which is the defining characteristic of a supersonic diffuser/nozzle. Option B is correct.
Q16. The stream function for a certain flow field is given by $ψ = xy$. Which of the following statements is true about this flow?

Wait, let's check irrotationality.) The vorticity $ω_z$ is: $$ ω_z = ∂ v∂ x - ∂ u∂ y = ∂ (-y)∂ x - ∂ (x)∂ y = 0 - 0 = 0 $$ Since the vorticity is zero, the flow is irrotational. Option B is correct.
Q17. In a pipeline of diameter $D$, the velocity profile is parabolic: $u(r) = U\max[1 - (2rD)^2]$. What is the average velocity ($Vavg$) through the pipe?

Q18. The velocity potential for a 3D flow field is given by $φ = x^2 + y^2 - 2z^2$. Does this represent a physically possible incompressible flow field?

B) No, because $\nabla^2 φ \ne 0$

D) No, because the stream function does not exist

Option A is correct.
Q19. Under what circumstances does a stream function ($ψ$) exist for a 2D fluid flow?

A stream function $ψ$ is defined mathematically to satisfy the continuity equation. For any 2D incompressible flow (whether steady or unsteady, rotational or irrotational), a stream function $ψ$ exists as long as mass conservation (continuity) holds. Option C is correct.
Q20. An incompressible fluid flows steadily through a pipe of diameter $D_1 = 10 \, cm$ which then branches into two pipes of diameters $D_2 = 5 \, cm$ and $D_3 = 8 \, cm$. If the average velocities are $V_1 = 4 \, m/s$ and $V_2 = 3 \, m/s$, what is the velocity $V_3$ in the third branch?

Q21. The mass flow rate entering a certain non-steady 3D system is $\dot{m}in = 5 \, kg/s$ and leaving is $\dot{m}out = 3 \, kg/s$. What is the rate of change of mass inside the control volume?

Q22. Consider a 1D steady compressible flow through a convergent duct. As the flow proceeds downstream, the density increases by $10%$ and the area decreases by $20%$. What is the percentage change in the flow velocity?

Q23. For a 2D compressible steady flow, the density field is $ρ = ρ_0 e-x$ and the velocity component in the $y$-direction is $v = 0$. What is the $x$-component of velocity $u$ if $u = U_0$ at $x = 0$?

B) $u = U_0 e-x$

Q24. Which of the following is the generalized vector form of the conservation of mass?

The fundamental mathematical formulation of the law of conservation of mass in fluid mechanics is the continuity equation, which in generalized vector notation is: $$ ∂ ρ∂ t + \nabla · (ρ \vec{V}) = 0 $$ Option B is correct.
Q25. Assertion (A): The velocity potential function $φ$ can exist for both compressible and incompressible flows.
Reason (R): The existence of $φ$ depends only on the irrotational nature of the flow field and is independent of mass conservation or density variations.
A) Both A and R are true and R is the correct explanation of A
B) Both A and R are true but R is NOT the correct explanation of A
C) A is true but R is false
D) A is false but R is true
The velocity potential $φ$ is mathematically defined such that $\vec{V} = \nabla φ$. For $φ$ to exist, we must have $\nabla × \vec{V} = \nabla × (\nabla φ) = 0$, which is the condition for irrotational flow. This condition does not depend on density variations. Therefore, $φ$ can exist in compressible flows, though it might not satisfy Laplace's equation in those cases. Both A and R are true, and R is the correct explanation. Option A is correct.
Q26. A 2D velocity field has components $u = A y$ and $v = B x$ (where $A \ne B$). Is this flow field physically possible for an incompressible fluid?
A) Yes, for any values of $A$ and $B$.
B) Yes, but only if $A = B$.
C) No, because $A \ne B$ implies rotationality.
D) Yes, but only if $A = -B$.
Let's evaluate the incompressible continuity equation for $u = Ay$ and $v = Bx$: $$ ∂ u∂ x = ∂ (Ay)∂ x = 0 $$ $$ ∂ v∂ y = ∂ (Bx)∂ y = 0 $$ $$ ∂ u∂ x + ∂ v∂ y = 0 + 0 = 0 $$ Because the sum is zero, the continuity equation is satisfied for any values of $A$ and $B$. The flow is rotational (since $∂ v∂ x - ∂ u∂ y = B - A \ne 0$), but rotationality does not prevent a flow from being physically possible or incompressible. Option A is correct.
Q27. The cross-sectional area of a horizontal pipe is given by $A(x) = A_0 e-λ x$. If an incompressible fluid is pumped through it at a constant volumetric rate $Q$, what is the acceleration of a fluid particle at position $x$?

Q28. Under what condition does the 3D continuity equation reduce to $∂ u∂ x + ∂ v∂ y + ∂ w∂ z = 0$?

Q1. Statement 1: The continuity equation $∂ ρ∂ t + \nabla · (ρ \vec{V}) = 0$ is valid only for Newtonian fluids.

A) Both statements are true and Statement 2 is the correct explanation of Statement 1
B) Both statements are true but Statement 2 is NOT the correct explanation of Statement 1
C) Statement 1 is false but Statement 2 is true
D) Both statements are false
Statement 1 is false. The continuity equation is derived solely from the law of conservation of mass, meaning it is valid for *all* fluids (including Newtonian, non-Newtonian, viscous, and inviscid fluids). Statement 2 is true, pointing out that mass conservation is independent of the constitutive properties of the fluid. Option C is correct.
Q2. A 3D velocity field is given by $u = kx$, $v = -ky$, and $w = C$. Under what condition is this flow both incompressible and irrotational?

B) Only if $C = 0$
C) It is always incompressible and irrotational for any values of $k$ and $C$.
D) It is incompressible, but can never be irrotational.

Irrotationality ($\nabla × \vec{V} = 0$): $$ ω_x = ∂ w∂ y - ∂ v∂ z = 0 - 0 = 0 $$ $$ ω_y = ∂ u∂ z - ∂ w∂ x = 0 - 0 = 0 $$ $$ ω_z = ∂ v∂ x - ∂ u∂ y = 0 - 0 = 0 $$ Vorticity is zero everywhere, meaning the flow is always irrotational. Option C is correct.

By definition, for a steady flow, all properties at any point in space are constant with respect to time ($∂/∂ t = 0$). This means there can be no net mass accumulation or depletion within the nozzle: $d mCVdt = 0 \, kg/s$. Option C is correct.

8. Error Analysis & Common Traps (5 min)

Common Student Pitfalls & Calculation Traps

  • Confusing "Steady Flow" with "Incompressible Flow": Students often write $∂ ρ∂ t = 0$ as the condition for incompressibility. This is a critical error. $∂ ρ∂ t = 0$ means the flow is *steady*. The correct condition for incompressibility is $DρDt = 0$, which yields $\nabla · \vec{V} = 0$.
  • Sign Errors in Stream Function Definitions: Always stick to a single, consistent sign convention. The most common standard is: $$ u = ∂ ψ∂ y, \quad v = -∂ ψ∂ x $$ Mixing these signs up will yield incorrect vorticity and normal velocity components.
  • Applying 1D Incompressible Formula to Jet Engines: In nozzle and diffuser questions, always check the Mach number ($M$). If $M > 0.3$, the flow is compressible, and using $A_1 V_1 = A_2 V_2$ instead of $ρ_1 A_1 V_1 = ρ_2 A_2 V_2$ will lead to significant numerical errors.
  • Cylindrical Coordinate Volume Element Error: When integrating to find the mass flow rate in circular ducts, do not forget the radial scaling factor $r$. The differential area is $2π r \, dr$, *not* $2π \, dr$.
