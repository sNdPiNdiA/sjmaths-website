Bernoulli's equation – derivation & assumptions

Day 8 of 129 Fluid Mechanics & Machinery Phase 1: Foundations 90-120 min Master Class

1. Understand (From Absolute Zero, 5–10 min)

Bernoulli's equation is the cornerstone of fluid kinematics and dynamics, representing the principle of conservation of energy applied to a flowing fluid. At its most fundamental level, it states that for an idealized flow, the sum of all forms of energy in a fluid along a streamline is constant. This module unpacks this elegant relationship from first principles, bridging elementary mechanical energy concepts with the complex velocity and pressure fields encountered in aeronautical applications.

The Intuitive Foundation: Mechanical Energy Conservation

In classical mechanics, the total mechanical energy of a conservative system is the sum of its kinetic energy ($KE$) and potential energy ($PE$). For a solid mass $m$ moving under gravity: $$E = 12mv^2 + mgh = constant$$ For a flowing fluid, we cannot easily track a single localized mass particle over long periods. Instead, we analyze a continuous medium. If we divide this energy equation by the volume of the fluid element ($Vvol$), and introduce the work done by pressure forces (known as flow work), we get energy per unit volume:

  1. Static Pressure ($p$): Represents the internal thermodynamic pressure of the fluid. Physically, it is the flow work or pressure energy per unit volume ($p = Force × distanceArea × distance = WorkVolume$). Its SI unit is Pascals ($Pa$ or $N/m^2$).
  2. Dynamic Pressure ($12ρ V^2$): Represents the kinetic energy of the fluid per unit volume, where $ρ$ is the fluid density ($kg/m^3$) and $V$ is the local flow velocity ($m/s$). Its SI unit is also Pascals ($Pa$).
  3. Hydrostatic Pressure ($ρ g z$): Represents the gravitational potential energy of the fluid per unit volume, where $g$ is the acceleration due to gravity ($m/s^2$) and $z$ is the elevation above an arbitrary datum ($m$). Its SI unit is Pascals ($Pa$).

Standard UPSC Terminology

In UPSC Air Safety Officer exams, questions demand familiarity with different representations of Bernoulli's equation. The three most common forms are:

  • Pressure Form: Sum of pressures is constant along a streamline. $$p + 12ρ V^2 + ρ g z = p_0 \quad [Unit: N/m^2 or Pa]$$ Here, $p_0$ is the Total Pressure (or Stagnation Pressure if elevation changes are neglected).
  • Head Form: Dividing the pressure form by $ρ g$ yields terms with dimensions of length (meters of fluid column). $$pρ g + V^22g + z = H \quad [Unit: m]$$ Where:
    • $pρ g$ is the Pressure Head.
    • $V^22g$ is the Velocity Head (or Kinetic Head).
    • $z$ is the Elevation Head (or Datum Head).
    • $H$ is the Total Head.
    • The sum $(pρ g + z)$ is defined as the Piezometric Head.
  • Energy per Unit Mass Form: Dividing the pressure form by $ρ$ yields terms in energy per unit mass. $$pρ + 12V^2 + g z = constant \quad [Unit: J/kg or m^2/s^2]$$

2. Learn the Concept (Complete Theoretical Foundation & Derivations, 20–25 min)

To fully master Bernoulli's equation for advanced competitive examinations, we must derive it using two distinct, highly rigorous mathematical approaches: (1) Integrating Euler's Equation of Motion along a streamline, and (2) Applying the general vector form of the Navier-Stokes equations under specialized conditions. This dual derivation ensures complete conceptual clarity regarding the limits of applicability.

1. Derivation of Euler's Equation along a Streamline

Consider an inviscid, steady flow of an incompressible fluid. Let us isolate a small fluid element of cross-sectional area $dA$ and differential length $ds$ along a streamline, as illustrated below:

The forces acting on this fluid element in the direction of motion ($s$-direction) are:

  1. The pressure force acting on the upstream face: $p \, dA$ (in the $+s$ direction).
  2. The pressure force acting on the downstream face: $-(p + ∂ p∂ s ds) dA$ (in the $-s$ direction).
  3. The component of gravitational force acting on the mass of the element: $$dW = dm · g = ρ \, dA \, ds · g$$ If the streamline is inclined at an angle $θ$ to the vertical datum, the gravitational component along the streamline is: $$-dW \cosθ = -ρ g \, dA \, ds \cosθ$$ Since $\cosθ = dzds$ (where $dz$ is the differential vertical elevation change), this becomes: $$-ρ g \, dA \, ds dzds = -ρ g \, dA \, dz$$

Applying Newton's Second Law of Motion along the streamline coordinate $s$:

$$\Sigma F_s = dm · a_s$$

Where $a_s$ is the total acceleration along the streamline. By the chain rule of differentiation, the material derivative of velocity $V(s, t)$ is:

$$a_s = D VDt = ∂ V∂ t + V ∂ V∂ s$$

For a steady flow, the local acceleration term $∂ V∂ t = 0$, leaving only the convective acceleration $a_s = V ∂ V∂ s$. Substituting the forces and mass ($dm = ρ \, dA \, ds$):

$$p \, dA - (p + ∂ p∂ s ds) dA - ρ g \, dA \, dz = ρ \, dA \, ds (V ∂ V∂ s)$$

Simplifying the algebraic expression by canceling $p \, dA$:

$$-∂ p∂ s ds \, dA - ρ g \, dA \, dz = ρ \, dA \, ds \, V ∂ V∂ s$$

Dividing the entire equation by the mass of the fluid element $ρ \, dA \, ds$:

$$-1ρ∂ p∂ s - g dzds = V ∂ V∂ s$$

Since the motion is restricted entirely along the streamline $s$, the partial derivatives can be written as total derivatives ($dp = ∂ p∂ s ds$ and $dz = ∂ z∂ s ds$). Rearranging the terms gives Euler's Equation of Motion along a streamline:

$$ dpρ + V dV + g dz = 0 $$

2. Integration to Obtain Bernoulli's Equation

To integrate Euler's equation, we apply the crucial physical assumption of incompressibility, meaning density $ρ$ is constant. Integrating each term of Euler's equation:

$$∫ dpρ + ∫ V dV + ∫ g dz = constant$$ $$pρ + V^22 + g z = constant$$

This is the classical **Bernoulli Equation** valid along a single streamline. If we multiply by $ρ$ (constant), we obtain the pressure form: $p + 12ρ V^2 + ρ g z = C$.

3. General Vector Derivation & The Irrotational Flow Condition

What if we want to apply Bernoulli's equation between any two points in a flow field, even if they do not lie on the same streamline? To answer this, we must examine the vector equation of motion for an inviscid, steady fluid (the vector Euler equation):

$$(\mathbf{V} · \nabla)\mathbf{V} = -1ρ\nabla p - \nabla(gz)$$

We apply the vector identity for convective acceleration:

$$(\mathbf{V} · \nabla)\mathbf{V} = \nabla(12V^2) - \mathbf{V} × (\nabla × \mathbf{V})$$

Where $\mathbf{ω} = \nabla × \mathbf{V}$ is the vorticity vector. Substituting this identity back into the vector Euler equation yields:

$$\nabla(12V^2) - \mathbf{V} × \mathbf{ω} = -1ρ\nabla p - \nabla(gz)$$

Assuming barotropic flow where $ρ = ρ(p)$, we can write $1ρ\nabla p = \nabla ∫ dpρ$. Rearranging terms:

$$\nabla( ∫ dpρ + 12V^2 + gz ) = \mathbf{V} × \mathbf{ω}$$

Let us analyze the term $\mathbf{V} × \mathbf{ω}$:

  1. Along a Streamline: If we take the dot product of the entire vector equation with a differential displacement vector $d\mathbf{r}$ along a streamline, the term $(\mathbf{V} × \mathbf{ω}) · d\mathbf{r} = 0$ because $(\mathbf{V} × \mathbf{ω})$ is always perpendicular to $\mathbf{V}$ (and thus to $d\mathbf{r}$). This proves Bernoulli's equation is always constant along a streamline in steady, inviscid, incompressible flow.
  2. Irrotational Flow Field ($\nabla × \mathbf{V} = \mathbf{ω} = 0$): If the flow is completely irrotational throughout the domain, the vorticity $\mathbf{ω}$ is zero everywhere. Thus: $$\mathbf{V} × \mathbf{ω} = 0$$ This leaves: $$\nabla( ∫ dpρ + 12V^2 + gz ) = 0$$ If the gradient of a scalar field is zero everywhere, the scalar field itself must be a global constant. Hence: $$pρ + 12V^2 + gz = constant (Everywhere in the flow field)$$

4. Extension: Unsteady, Irrotational Bernoulli Equation

If the flow is unsteady ($∂ \mathbf{V}∂ t \neq 0$) but remains irrotational ($\nabla × \mathbf{V} = 0$), there exists a velocity potential function $φ(x,y,z,t)$ such that $\mathbf{V} = \nabla φ$. The vector momentum equation becomes:

$$∂ (\nabla φ)∂ t + \nabla(12V^2) = -\nabla(∫ dpρ) - \nabla(gz)$$ $$\nabla( ∂ φ∂ t + ∫ dpρ + 12V^2 + gz ) = 0$$

Integrating with respect to spatial coordinates yields the unsteady Bernoulli equation:

$$ ∂ φ∂ t + pρ + 12V^2 + gz = f(t) $$

Where $f(t)$ is an arbitrary function of time, often called the integration constant, which can be absorbed into $φ$ without loss of generality.

3. Memorize Important Facts (10 min)

For instant recall under high-pressure UPSC exam conditions, memorize the following absolute essentials:

  • The 4 Golden Assumptions: Bernoulli's equation is valid only if the flow is:
    1. Inviscid ($μ = 0$): Viscous forces (shear stress) are negligible.
    2. Steady ($∂∂ t = 0$): Flow properties at any point do not change with time.
    3. Incompressible ($ρ = constant$): Density changes are negligible (typically Mach number $M < 0.3$).
    4. Flow along a streamline: Can be applied across streamlines only if the flow is irrotational ($\nabla × \mathbf{V} = 0$).
  • The Fundamental Form: $$p + 12ρ V^2 + ρ g z = C$$
    • Dimension of each term: $[M L-1 T-2]$ (Pressure)
    • Unit of each term: $N/m^2$ or $Pa$ or $J/m^3$ (Energy per unit volume).
  • The Head Form: $$pρ g + V^22g + z = H$$
    • Dimension of each term: $[L]$ (Length)
    • Unit of each term: $meters$ (of the flowing fluid column).
  • Compressible Flow (Isentropic) Bernoulli: $$γγ-1pρ + 12V^2 + gz = constant$$ Where $γ = C_p / C_v$ is the ratio of specific heats ($≈ 1.4$ for air).

4. Aviation Application (5–10 min)

In aviation and flight testing, Bernoulli's equation is not merely an academic exercise—it is the operational basis for critical cockpit instruments and fluid systems.

1. Pitot-Static Tube Airspeed Measurement

The primary flight instrument for measuring airspeed is the Pitot-static tube. It relies entirely on Bernoulli's principle to find the relative velocity between the aircraft and the ambient air.

Consider a Pitot-static probe aligned with the local flow direction. The probe has two ports:

  1. Static Port: Positioned parallel to the flow to measure the undisturbed ambient static pressure $p∞$ (or $p_s$).
  2. Pitot (Stagnation) Port: Positioned facing directly into the oncoming flow. The fluid entering this tube is brought to a complete standstill ($V_0 = 0$) isentropically. The pressure measured here is the stagnation pressure $p_t$ (or $p_0$).

Applying Bernoulli's equation along the stagnation streamline (neglecting negligible elevation changes $z_s ≈ z_t$):

$$p_s + 12ρ∞ V∞^2 = p_t + 12ρ∞ (0)^2$$ $$p_t = p_s + 12ρ∞ V∞^2$$

Solving for the true airspeed (TAS), $V∞$:

$$ V∞ = √2(p_t - p_s)ρ∞ $$

The quantity $(p_t - p_s)$ is measured directly by a differential pressure transducer (or mechanical bellows) in the airspeed indicator. If the density $ρ∞$ is set to sea-level standard density ($ρ_0 = 1.225 kg/m^3$), the instrument displays the Indicated Airspeed (IAS).

2. Lift Generation and Streamline Squeezing

When air flows over a lifting airfoil, the upper camber of the wing forces the streamtubes to compress. By mass conservation (continuity equation $A_1 V_1 = A_2 V_2$), compressing a streamtube forces the air velocity to increase ($Vupper > V∞$).

Applying Bernoulli's equation, as the velocity on the upper surface increases, the local static pressure must drop below the ambient atmospheric pressure ($pupper < p∞$). Conversely, on the lower surface, streamtubes undergo less constriction (or even expansion), keeping the velocity lower and static pressure higher ($plower ≈ p∞$ or $> p∞$). This pressure difference ($Δ p = plower - pupper$) acts across the wing chord, generating the aerodynamic lift force:

$$L = ∫c (plower - pupper) \, dA$$

3. Venturi Tubes in Legacy Aircraft Carburetors

Before fuel injection systems dominated, aircraft piston engines used carburetors containing a Venturi tube to meter fuel. As air enters the converging section of the Venturi, its velocity increases, reaching a maximum at the throat. According to Bernoulli's equation, this throat velocity spike causes a severe drop in static pressure. A fuel discharge nozzle is placed exactly at this low-pressure throat. The pressure difference between the fuel reservoir (at atmospheric pressure) and the Venturi throat draws fuel into the air stream, automatically self-regulating the fuel-air mixture based on the engine's air intake volume flow rate.

5. UPSC ASO Focus (10 min)

UPSC ASO / GATE Critical Constants & Relationships

  • Stagnation Pressure Ratio for Compressible Flow: When Mach number exceeds $0.3$, the incompressible Bernoulli equation introduces significant error. For compressible isentropic flow, the exact stagnation pressure $p_0$ is given by: $$p_0p = ( 1 + γ-12 M^2 )γγ-1$$ Using binomial expansion, this simplifies to: $$p_0 = p + 12ρ V^2 ( 1 + 14M^2 + 2-γ24M^4 + \dots )$$ Note that the compressible stagnation pressure is *higher* than the incompressible approximation $p + 12ρ V^2$. The ASO exam frequently tests this inequality.
  • The Pitot-Static Tube Manometer Relation: When a differential U-tube manometer filled with a liquid of density $ρ_m$ is connected to a Pitot-static tube measuring air velocity (density $ρ$), the velocity is: $$V = √2 g h (ρ_m - ρ)ρ$$
  • Dimensions of Bernoulli Constants:
    • $pρ g + V^22g + z = H \implies$ Dimension $[L]$, Unit: $meters$.
    • $p + 12ρ V^2 + ρ g z = C \implies$ Dimension $[M L-1 T-2]$, Unit: $N/m^2$ or $Pa$.
    • $pρ + V^22 + g z = constant \implies$ Dimension $[L^2 T-2]$, Unit: $J/kg$.

Conceptual Traps to Avoid

CRITICAL UPSC TRAP: Boundary Layer Applications

Can Bernoulli's equation be applied inside the boundary layer of an airfoil? NO. Inside the boundary layer, viscous shear forces dominate ($μ \neq 0$), violating the "inviscid flow" assumption of Bernoulli's derivation. You must use the Navier-Stokes equations or Prandtl's boundary layer equations. Bernoulli's equation is only valid outside the boundary layer in the inviscid freestream region. This is a favorite conceptual question in UPSC oral interviews and written papers.

ROTATIONAL FLOW TRAP: Comparing Streamlines

Remember that if a flow field has vorticity ($\nabla × \mathbf{V} \neq 0$), the Bernoulli constant $C$ is constant *only* along a single given streamline. You cannot equate $p_1 + 12ρ V_1^2 + ρ g z_1$ on Streamline A to $p_2 + 12ρ V_2^2 + ρ g z_2$ on Streamline B. This is only valid if the entire flow field is irrotational.

6. Practice Questions (30 Exhaustive UPSC ASO MCQs)

Analyze each question carefully. Standard physical values (unless stated otherwise): $g = 9.81 m/s^2$, $ρwater = 1000 kg/m^3$, $ρair = 1.2 kg/m^3$.

Q1. Which of the following defines the exact set of physical conditions required to integrate Euler's equation of motion along a streamline to obtain the classical Bernoulli's equation?
A) Steady, Viscous, Compressible, Rotational
B) Unsteady, Inviscid, Incompressible, Irrotational
C) Steady, Inviscid, Incompressible, Flow along a streamline
D) Steady, Viscous, Incompressible, Flow across streamlines
To obtain the classical Bernoulli's equation, we integrate Euler's equation along a streamline. This integration requires: (1) Steady flow ($∂/∂ t = 0$), (2) Inviscid flow ($μ=0$ so there are no shear stresses), (3) Incompressible flow ($ρ = constant$), and (4) The analysis must be along a single streamline (unless the entire flow is irrotational, in which case it is valid everywhere). Therefore, option C is correct.
Q2. An aircraft is flying at a certain altitude where the ambient air density is $0.90 kg/m^3$. A Pitot-static tube mounted on the wing measures a differential pressure ($p_t - p_s$) of $4500 N/m^2$. What is the True Airspeed (TAS) of the aircraft, assuming incompressible flow?
A) $70.7 m/s$
B) $100.0 m/s$
C) $141.4 m/s$
D) $50.0 m/s$
Applying Bernoulli's equation for stagnation conditions: $$p_t - p_s = 12ρ V^2 \implies V = √2(p_t - p_s)ρ$$ Substituting the given values: $$V = √2 × 45000.90 = √90000.90 = √10000 = 100 m/s$$
Q3. The piezometric head in a fluid flow system is defined mathematically as the sum of which of the following heads?
A) Velocity head and Elevation head
B) Pressure head and Velocity head
C) Pressure head and Elevation head
D) Total dynamic head and Datum head
By definition, piezometric head represents the sum of the static pressure head and the datum (elevation) head: $$hpiezo = pρ g + z$$ It is physically measured by a simple piezometer tube inserted into the flow.
Q4. If a steady, incompressible, and inviscid flow field is known to be rotational ($\nabla × \mathbf{V} \neq 0$), which statement about Bernoulli's constant $C = p + 12ρ V^2 + ρ g z$ is completely true?
A) $C$ is constant everywhere in the flow field.
B) $C$ is constant only along any individual streamline, but can vary from streamline to streamline.
C) $C$ is constant only along lines perpendicular to streamlines.
D) Bernoulli's equation is completely invalid and cannot be applied even along a streamline.
In a rotational flow field, the vorticity vector $\mathbf{ω} = \nabla × \mathbf{V}$ is non-zero. The term $\mathbf{V} × \mathbf{ω}$ in the momentum equation is non-zero, meaning the gradient of the total head is not zero across streamlines. However, because $\mathbf{V} × \mathbf{ω}$ is perpendicular to the velocity vector $\mathbf{V}$, its component along the streamline remains zero. Thus, the Bernoulli constant $C$ is constant along any individual streamline but varies between different streamlines.
Q5. What are the SI units of each term in the head form of Bernoulli's equation, $pρ g + V^22g + z = H$?
A) $N/m^2$
B) $J/kg$
C) $m$
D) $kg/m^3$
Each term represents energy per unit weight of the fluid: - Pressure head: $[p]/[ρ g] = (N/m^2) / (kg/m^3 · m/s^2) = meters$ (m). - Velocity head: $[V^2]/[2g] = (m^2/s^2) / (m/s^2) = meters$ (m). - Elevation head: $[z] = meters$ (m).

Q8. For unsteady, irrotational, incompressible flow of a fluid, which of the following is the correct mathematical expression for the integrated Bernoulli's equation (where $φ$ is the velocity potential)?
A) $pρ + 12V^2 + gz = constant$
B) $∂ φ∂ s + pρ + 12V^2 + gz = f(t)$
C) $∂ φ∂ t + pρ + 12V^2 + gz = f(t)$
D) $∫ ∂ V∂ t dt + pρ + 12V^2 + gz = 0$
For unsteady irrotational flow, $\mathbf{V} = \nabla φ$. Integrating the vector equation of motion yields the unsteady Bernoulli equation: $$∂ φ∂ t + pρ + 12V^2 + gz = f(t)$$ where $f(t)$ is an arbitrary function of time.
Q9. For compressible flow undergoing an isentropic process ($p/ρ^γ = constant$), what is the integrated form of the Bernoulli equation along a streamline (neglecting elevation changes)?
A) $pρ + 12V^2 = constant$
B) $γγ-1pρ + 12V^2 = constant$
C) $γ-1γpρ + 12V^2 = constant$
D) $γ pρ + 12V^2 = constant$
For compressible isentropic flow, $∫ dpρ + 12V^2 = C$. Since $p = C_1 ρ^γ$, we have: $$∫ dpρ = ∫ γ C_1 ργ-2 dρ = γ C_1 ργ-1γ-1 = γγ-1pρ$$ Thus, the compressible Bernoulli equation is: $$γγ-1pρ + 12V^2 = constant$$
Q10. How do the Total Energy Line (TEL) and the Hydraulic Grade Line (HGL) behave along a horizontal pipe of constant cross-section conducting a real fluid with friction?
A) Both lines remain completely horizontal and parallel.
B) Both lines slope downwards in the direction of flow, and the vertical distance between them remains constant.
C) TEL slopes downwards while HGL slopes upwards due to pressure recovery.
D) TEL remains horizontal, but HGL slopes downwards.
- TEL represents the total head: $pρ g + V^22g + z$. In a real fluid, head loss due to friction causes TEL to slope downwards in the direction of flow. - HGL represents the piezometric head: $pρ g + z$. - The vertical distance between TEL and HGL is the velocity head $V^22g$. Because the horizontal pipe has a constant cross-section, the velocity $V$ is constant by continuity. Therefore, $V^22g$ is constant, meaning both lines slope downwards at the same angle, keeping the vertical gap between them constant.

Q12. A Pitot tube is inserted into the center of a water pipe. The stagnation pressure head is measured to be $5.2 m$ of water and the static pressure head is $4.0 m$ of water. If the coefficient of the tube $C_p = 1.0$, what is the local velocity of water at the center of the pipe?

The velocity using a Pitot tube is given by: $$V = C_p √2g(h_t - h_s)$$ Where $h_t$ is stagnation head and $h_s$ is static head. $$V = 1.0 × √2 × 9.81 × (5.2 - 4.0) = √2 × 9.81 × 1.2 = √23.544 ≈ 4.85 m/s$$
Q13. Under what exact condition do the Navier-Stokes equations simplify directly to the Euler equations of motion?
A) When the flow is steady ($∂ \mathbf{V}/∂ t = 0$).
B) When the dynamic viscosity of the fluid is zero ($μ = 0$).
C) When the flow is incompressible ($\nabla · \mathbf{V} = 0$).
D) When the flow is irrotational ($\nabla × \mathbf{V} = 0$).
The Navier-Stokes equations describe the motion of viscous fluids by incorporating viscosity ($μ$). If we set the viscosity of the fluid to zero ($μ = 0$, meaning inviscid flow), the viscous stress terms vanish, leaving only the pressure and body force terms. This simplified set of equations is known as the Euler equations of motion.
Q14. Why is Bernoulli's equation invalid inside the boundary layer over an aircraft's wing?
A) Because the density of the air changes drastically inside the boundary layer.
B) Because viscous forces are highly dominant inside the boundary layer, violating the inviscid assumption.
C) Because flow velocity inside the boundary layer exceeds the speed of sound.
D) Because the flow inside the boundary layer is completely unsteady at all times.
One of the primary assumptions in the derivation of Bernoulli's equation is that the fluid is inviscid ($μ = 0$, meaning no viscous shear forces). Inside the boundary layer, the velocity gradient $∂ u/∂ y$ is very large, making viscous shear stresses ($τ = μ ∂ u/∂ y$) dominant. Therefore, the inviscid flow assumption is violated, rendering Bernoulli's equation invalid within the boundary layer.
Q15. Consider a fluid element flowing through a pipeline. If the kinetic head increases by $4 m$ of water due to narrowing of the pipe, what is the corresponding change in the piezometric head, assuming steady, inviscid, incompressible flow with no external work?
A) It increases by $4 m$.
B) It decreases by $4 m$.
C) It remains constant.
D) It decreases by $2 m$.
The total head is the sum of the piezometric head ($pρ g + z$) and the velocity (kinetic) head ($V^22g$): $$Total Head = (pρ g + z) + V^22g = H$$ Since the flow is inviscid, steady, and incompressible without losses, the Total Head $H$ must remain constant along a streamline. If the velocity head increases by $4 m$, the piezometric head must decrease by exactly $4 m$ to keep the sum constant.
Q16. In which of the following scenarios is it mathematically valid to apply Bernoulli's equation between any two arbitrary points in a flow field without restriction to a single streamline?
A) Any steady, incompressible flow.
B) A steady, incompressible, and inviscid flow that is also irrotational.
C) A steady, viscous, incompressible, and laminar flow.
D) A steady, compressible, and rotational flow.
If the flow is irrotational ($\nabla × \mathbf{V} = 0$), then the vorticity is zero everywhere. From the general momentum equation derivation, $\nabla H = \mathbf{V} × \mathbf{ω} = 0$, meaning the total head gradient is zero across the entire field. Thus, the Bernoulli constant $C$ is identical everywhere, allowing the equation to be applied between any two arbitrary points in the flow field.
Q17. The pressure coefficient $C_p$ at a point on an airfoil is defined as $C_p = p - p∞12ρ∞ V∞^2$. Using Bernoulli's equation, what is the alternative representation of $C_p$ in terms of local velocity $V$ and freestream velocity $V∞$?
A) $1 - (V∞V)^2$
B) $1 - (VV∞)^2$
C) $(VV∞)^2 - 1$
D) $(V∞V)^2$
Applying Bernoulli's equation between the freestream and a point on the airfoil: $$p∞ + 12ρ V∞^2 = p + 12ρ V^2$$ $$p - p∞ = 12ρ V∞^2 - 12ρ V^2 = 12ρ V∞^2 [1 - (VV∞)^2]$$ Substituting this into the definition of $C_p$: $$C_p = 12ρ V∞^2 [1 - (VV∞)^2]12ρ V∞^2 = 1 - (VV∞)^2$$
Q18. Torricelli's Law states that the velocity of efflux $V$ of a liquid from an orifice under a head $h$ is $V = √2gh$. This law is derived directly from Bernoulli's equation under which of the following assumptions?

B) The orifice area is negligible compared to the tank's cross-sectional area, and the tank is open to the atmosphere.

In Torricelli's derivation, we assume a reservoir of surface area $A_1$ discharging through an orifice of area $A_2$. If $A_2 \ll A_1$, then by continuity, $V_1 ≈ 0$. Applying Bernoulli's equation between the open top surface (1) and the open jet (2), where both are at atmospheric pressure: $$patm + ρ g h + 0 = patm + 0 + 12ρ V_2^2 \implies V_2 = √2gh$$

Q20. In a non-uniform flow field, the kinetic energy correction factor $α$ is introduced to correct the average velocity head. The modified Bernoulli's equation in head form is written as:
A) $pρ g + α V^22g + z = constant$
B) $α pρ g + V^22g + z = constant$
C) $pρ g + V^22g + α z = constant$
D) $α (pρ g + V^22g + z) = constant$
When using the average velocity $V$ in a 1D flow approximation, the actual kinetic energy transport is underestimated. To correct this, the kinetic energy correction factor $α$ (defined as $1A∫ (u/V)^3 dA$) is multiplied by the velocity head term: $$Corrected Velocity Head = α V^22g$$ Thus, the modified Bernoulli equation is $pρ g + α V^22g + z = constant$.
Q21. A glider is flying at sea level where the density of air is $1.225 kg/m^3$. A U-tube water manometer connected to its Pitot-static probe shows a column height difference of $5 cm$. What is the glider's velocity? (Take $ρwater = 1000 kg/m^3$)

Q22. Consider steady, inviscid flow. Along a highly curved streamline, which coordinate system and acceleration components are used to derive Euler's equations perpendicular to the streamline?
A) Cartesian coordinates with only convective terms.
B) Streamline coordinates ($s, n$) with centrifugal acceleration $a_n = V^2/R$.
C) Polar coordinates with angular acceleration $α$.
D) Cylindrical coordinates with Coriolis acceleration.
To analyze flow normal to a curved streamline, we use the intrinsic (streamline) coordinate system ($s, n$), where $s$ is along the streamline and $n$ is normal to it (pointing towards the center of curvature). The acceleration in the normal direction is the centrifugal acceleration $a_n = V^2R$ (where $R$ is the local radius of curvature). The resulting Euler equation normal to the streamline is: $$∂ p∂ n = ρ V^2R - ρ g ∂ z∂ n$$
Q23. Based on the normal Euler equation $∂ p∂ n = ρ V^2R$ (neglecting gravity), how does pressure change as one moves radially outwards (away from the center of curvature) across curved streamlines?
A) Pressure must decrease.
B) Pressure must increase.
C) Pressure remains constant.
D) Pressure becomes zero.
The equation is $∂ p∂ n = ρ V^2R$. Since density $ρ$, velocity squared $V^2$, and radius $R$ are all positive quantities, the gradient $∂ p∂ n$ is positive. The normal coordinate $n$ points *towards* the center of curvature, meaning moving *away* from the center of curvature is the negative $n$ direction. Therefore, pressure must increase as you move radially outwards (increasing radius of curvature), matching the physical behavior of centrifugal force pushing fluid outwards.
Q24. What is the limit of the Mach number ($M$) below which air flow is generally assumed to be incompressible, making the standard incompressible Bernoulli equation highly accurate?
A) $M < 0.1$
B) $M < 0.3$
C) $M < 0.5$
D) $M < 1.0$
In gas dynamics, compressibility effects are neglected if density variations are less than about $5%$. The density variation is proportional to $M^2/2$. At $M = 0.3$, the density change is $≈ 2.2%$. Thus, $M = 0.3$ is universally accepted as the threshold below which the flow can be treated as incompressible.
Q25. An aerodynamicist measures the pressure at the stagnation point of an airfoil in a low-speed wind tunnel. If the freestream static pressure is $101.3 kPa$ and the dynamic pressure is $2 kPa$, what is the value of the pressure coefficient $C_p$ at this stagnation point?
A) $0.0$
B) $1.0$
C) $2.0$
D) $-1.0$
At the stagnation point, velocity $V = 0$. Using Bernoulli's equation: $$pstag = p∞ + 12ρ V∞^2$$ The pressure coefficient is defined as: $$C_p = pstag - p∞12ρ V∞^2 = 12ρ V∞^212ρ V∞^2 = 1.0$$ Therefore, the pressure coefficient at any stagnation point in steady, incompressible, inviscid flow is always exactly $1.0$.
Q26. Consider a water pipeline containing a sudden enlargement from area $A_1$ to $A_2$. Why can we NOT apply the standard Bernoulli's equation across this transition to find the pressure rise?
A) Because the flow becomes compressible.
B) Because flow separation and turbulent mixing create significant local head loss, violating the inviscid flow assumption.
C) Because continuity is violated.
D) Because the elevation changes abruptly.
In a sudden expansion, the fluid cannot follow the sharp boundary, leading to flow separation, jet formation, and strong turbulent eddying. These eddies dissipate mechanical energy into heat, causing a significant "minor head loss" ($h_L = (V_1 - V_2)^22g$). Because of this viscous energy dissipation, the inviscid assumption of Bernoulli's equation is violated. We must instead use the momentum and continuity equations to determine the pressure rise.
Q27. Water flows through a horizontal converging nozzle. The inlet velocity is $3 m/s$ and the outlet velocity is $9 m/s$. What is the pressure drop ($Δ p = pinlet - poutlet$) across the nozzle?
A) $18 kPa$
B) $36 kPa$
C) $72 kPa$
D) $54 kPa$
Using Bernoulli's equation on a horizontal streamline: $$p_1 + 12ρ V_1^2 = p_2 + 12ρ V_2^2$$ $$Δ p = p_1 - p_2 = 12ρ (V_2^2 - V_1^2)$$ Substitute $ρ = 1000 kg/m^3$, $V_1 = 3 m/s$, and $V_2 = 9 m/s$: $$Δ p = 12(1000)(9^2 - 3^2) = 500(81 - 9) = 500(72) = 36,000 Pa = 36 kPa$$
Q28. ASSERTION (A): Bernoulli's equation is a statement of conservation of momentum.
REASON (R): Euler's equation of motion along a streamline is derived by applying Newton's second law of motion to a fluid element.
A) Both A and R are true, and R is the correct explanation of A.
B) Both A and R are true, but R is NOT the correct explanation of A.
C) A is true, but R is false.
D) A is false, but R is true.
- The Assertion (A) is **false**. Bernoulli's equation is a statement of the conservation of **energy** (or the work-energy theorem) for a fluid flow, not momentum. - The Reason (R) is **true**. Euler's equation is indeed derived by applying Newton's second law of motion ($\mathbf{F} = m\mathbf{a}$, which is a momentum balance statement) to a fluid element. When integrated, this force balance yields the work-energy relation (Bernoulli's equation). Thus, A is false and R is true.
Q29. In a free vortex (irrotational flow with velocity profile $V_θ ∝ 1/r$), how does the total pressure $p_0$ vary radially across the streamlines?
A) Total pressure increases linearly with radius.
B) Total pressure decreases with radius.
C) Total pressure remains completely constant radially across all streamlines.
D) Total pressure is zero everywhere.
A free vortex is an irrotational flow field ($\nabla × \mathbf{V} = 0$). As proven in Section 2, for any steady, incompressible, inviscid, and irrotational flow field, the Bernoulli constant (which is the total pressure $p_0$) is constant everywhere, including across different streamlines. Thus, total pressure remains completely constant radially. Note that while static pressure and dynamic pressure vary radially, their sum remains constant.
Q30. A pipeline carries water with a flow velocity of $5 m/s$. Due to pipe friction, there is a head loss of $2 m$ over a certain length. If the pipe is horizontal and has a constant diameter, what is the drop in static pressure over this length?
A) $9.81 kPa$
B) $19.62 kPa$
C) $25.0 kPa$
D) $4.9 kPa$
Applying the energy equation for real fluids (with head loss $h_L$): $$p_1ρ g + V_1^22g + z_1 = p_2ρ g + V_2^22g + z_2 + h_L$$ Since the pipe is horizontal ($z_1 = z_2$) and has a constant diameter ($V_1 = V_2$): $$p_1ρ g = p_2ρ g + h_L \implies p_1 - p_2 = ρ g h_L$$ $$Δ p = 1000 × 9.81 × 2 = 19,620 Pa = 19.62 kPa$$

8. Error Analysis & Common Traps (5 min)

Common Student Misconceptions & Calculation Traps

  • viscous flow application: Students frequently try to use Bernoulli's equation to calculate pressure drops in pipes with long runs. They completely forget that pipe friction is a viscous effect ($μ \neq 0$).
    Incorrect: $p_1 + 12ρ V_1^2 = p_2 + 12ρ V_2^2$ in a $100m$ long pipeline.
    Correct: Use the modified energy equation with head loss: $p_1 + 12ρ V_1^2 = p_2 + 12ρ V_2^2 + ρ g h_L$.
  • The Boundary Layer Error: Applying Bernoulli's equation to find the velocity profile inside the boundary layer over an airfoil. Because the boundary layer is dominated by viscous shear, Bernoulli's equation is completely invalid there.
  • Incompressible Airspeed Calculation above Mach 0.3: In UPSC problems, students often apply the simple formula $V = √2Δ p/ρ$ for supersonic or high subsonic military aircraft ($M > 0.3$). This introduces massive errors because density changes are not accounted for. You must use the compressible isentropic relation instead.
  • Vorticity Misunderstanding: Assuming that because a flow is steady and inviscid, they can equate the Bernoulli constant $C$ between any two arbitrary points. If the flow has vorticity (e.g., behind a propeller or in a boundary layer wake), $C$ is constant *only* along the same streamline.
