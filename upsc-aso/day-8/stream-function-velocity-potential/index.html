Stream function, velocity potential

Day 8 of 129 Fluid Mechanics & Machinery Phase 1: Foundations 90-120 min Master Class

1. Understand (From Absolute Zero, 5–10 min)

In fluid mechanics, modeling the movement of fluid elements without tracking every individual molecule is achieved using field equations. For two-dimensional, incompressible, and irrotational flows, we define two mathematical functions: the Stream Function ($\psi$) and the Velocity Potential ($\phi$). These functions simplify the Navier-Stokes equations into linear partial differential equations (Laplace equations), which can be solved analytically or numerically.

1. The Velocity Potential ($\phi$)

The velocity potential $\phi$ is a scalar function of space and time. It is defined such that its gradient yields the velocity vector field $\vec{V}$:

$$\vec{V} = \nabla \phi$$

In Cartesian coordinates, the velocity components are:

$$u = \frac{\partial \phi}{\partial x}, \quad v = \frac{\partial \phi}{\partial y}, \quad w = \frac{\partial \phi}{\partial z}$$

Physical Meaning: The velocity potential represents the state of irrotationality. By vector calculus identity, the curl of a gradient is always zero ($\nabla \times \nabla \phi = 0$). Therefore, the existence of $\phi$ mathematically guarantees that the flow is irrotational ($\vec{\omega} = \nabla \times \vec{V} = 0$). Lines where $\phi = \text{constant}$ are called equipotential lines. The fluid flows in the direction of the steepest rate of change of $\phi$. Its SI unit is $\text{m}^2/\text{s}$.

2. The Stream Function ($\psi$)

The stream function $\psi$ is defined for two-dimensional flows (and axisymmetric flows using Stokes' stream function). It is defined such that it automatically satisfies the incompressible continuity equation ($\nabla \cdot \vec{V} = 0$):

$$u = \frac{\partial \psi}{\partial y}, \quad v = -\frac{\partial \psi}{\partial x}$$

Physical Meaning: Lines of constant $\psi$ ($\psi = \text{constant}$) are streamlines. Since the velocity vector is everywhere tangent to a streamline, there is zero mass flux across a streamline. The volumetric flow rate per unit depth ($Q$) between any two streamlines $\psi_1$ and $\psi_2$ is equal to the absolute difference between their values:

$$Q = |\psi_2 - \psi_1|$$

Its SI unit is also $\text{m}^2/\text{s}$.

3. Geometric Interpretation and Orthogonality

For a 2D, irrotational, incompressible flow, the streamlines ($\psi = \text{const}$) and equipotential lines ($\phi = \text{const}$) form a mutually perpendicular grid known as a flow net. The slope of an equipotential line ($d\phi = 0$) is:

$$\left(\frac{dy}{dx}\right)_{\phi} = -\frac{u}{v}$$

The slope of a streamline ($d\psi = 0$) is:

$$\left(\frac{dy}{dx}\right)_{\psi} = \frac{v}{u}$$

Since the product of their slopes is $-1$, they intersect at right angles everywhere except at stagnation points where the velocity magnitude is zero ($u = v = 0$), making the slopes indeterminate.

2. Learn the Concept (Complete Theoretical Foundation & Derivations, 20–25 min)

To establish a rigorous theoretical foundation, let us derive the governing equations of these potential functions from the first principles of conservation of mass and momentum.

1. Derivation of Laplace's Equation for Velocity Potential ($\phi$)

For an incompressible fluid, the conservation of mass (continuity equation) in 3D is:

$$\nabla \cdot \vec{V} = \frac{\partial u}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial v}{\partial y} + \frac{\partial w}{\partial z} = 0$$

Assuming the flow is irrotational, we substitute $u = \frac{\partial \phi}{\partial x}$, $v = \frac{\partial \phi}{\partial y}$, and $w = \frac{\partial \phi}{\partial z}$ into the continuity equation:

$$\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\left(\frac{\partial \phi}{\partial x}\right) + \frac{\partial}{\partial y}\left(\frac{\partial \phi}{\partial y}\right) + \frac{\partial}{\partial z}\left(\frac{\partial \phi}{\partial z}\right) = 0 \implies \frac{\partial^2 \phi}{\partial x^2} + \frac{\partial^2 \phi}{\partial y^2} + \frac{\partial^2 \phi}{\partial z^2} = 0$$

This is the 3D Laplace equation:

$$ \nabla^2 \phi = 0 $$

Crucial Insight: Any flow field that satisfies Laplace's equation for $\phi$ is physically realistic (satisfies continuity) and irrotational. Because Laplace's equation is linear, we can use the principle of superposition: complex potential flows can be constructed by summing simpler elemental solutions (uniform flow, source, sink, doublet, and vortex).

2. Derivation of Laplace's Equation for Stream Function ($\psi$)

By definition, the stream function satisfies the 2D incompressible continuity equation:

$$\frac{\partial u}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial v}{\partial y} = \frac{\partial}{\partial x}\left(\frac{\partial \psi}{\partial y}\right) + \frac{\partial}{\partial y}\left(-\frac{\partial \psi}{\partial x}\right) = \frac{\partial^2 \psi}{\partial x \partial y} - \frac{\partial^2 \psi}{\partial y \partial x} = 0$$

This holds true regardless of whether the flow is rotational or irrotational. Now, we enforce the condition of irrotationality about the $z$-axis (vorticity $\omega_z = 0$):

$$\omega_z = \frac{\partial v}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial u}{\partial y} = 0$$

Substituting the stream function definitions ($u = \frac{\partial \psi}{\partial y}$, $v = -\frac{\partial \psi}{\partial x}$) into the irrotationality condition:

$$\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\left(-\frac{\partial \psi}{\partial x}\right) - \frac{\partial}{\partial y}\left(\frac{\partial \psi}{\partial y}\right) = 0 \implies -\left(\frac{\partial^2 \psi}{\partial x^2} + \frac{\partial^2 \psi}{\partial y^2}\right) = 0$$

This yields the 2D Laplace equation for the stream function:

$$ \nabla^2 \psi = 0 $$

Key Takeaway: While $\psi$ exists for any 2D incompressible flow (rotational or irrotational), it only satisfies Laplace's equation if the flow is also irrotational.

3. Mathematical Properties and Cauchy-Riemann Equations

Equating the velocity definitions from both functions in 2D:

$$u = \frac{\partial \phi}{\partial x} = \frac{\partial \psi}{\partial y}$$ $$v = \frac{\partial \phi}{\partial y} = -\frac{\partial \psi}{\partial x}$$

These are the exact Cauchy-Riemann equations from complex analysis. Consequently, we can define a complex potential $w(z)$, where $z = x + iy$ is a complex variable:

$$w(z) = \phi(x, y) + i\psi(x, y)$$

The derivative of the complex potential with respect to $z$ yields the conjugate complex velocity:

$$\frac{dw}{dz} = \frac{\partial \phi}{\partial x} + i\frac{\partial \psi}{\partial x} = u - iv$$

4. Cylindrical Polar Coordinates

In polar coordinates ($r, \theta$), the relations for velocity components ($v_r$ and $v_\theta$) are:

$$ v_r = \frac{\partial \phi}{\partial r} = \frac{1}{r}\frac{\partial \psi}{\partial \theta} $$ $$ v_\theta = \frac{1}{r}\frac{\partial \phi}{\partial \theta} = -\frac{\partial \psi}{\partial r} $$

3. Memorize Important Facts (10 min)

  • Core Formula 1 & Units: Cartesian Velocity Relations: $u = \frac{\partial \phi}{\partial x} = \frac{\partial \psi}{\partial y}$ and $v = \frac{\partial \phi}{\partial y} = -\frac{\partial \psi}{\partial x}$. Units of both $\phi$ and $\psi$: $\text{m}^2/\text{s}$ (Dimensions: $[L^2 T^{-1}]$).
  • Polar Velocity Relations: $v_r = \frac{\partial \phi}{\partial r} = \frac{1}{r}\frac{\partial \psi}{\partial \theta}$ and $v_\theta = \frac{1}{r}\frac{\partial \phi}{\partial \theta} = -\frac{\partial \psi}{\partial r}$.
  • Laplace Conditions: $\nabla^2 \phi = 0$ requires incompressible and irrotational flow. $\nabla^2 \psi = 0$ requires irrotational flow (for 2D incompressible flow, $\psi$ always exists).
  • Stagnation Points: Occur where $\vec{V} = \mathbf{0} \implies \frac{\partial \phi}{\partial x} = \frac{\partial \phi}{\partial y} = \frac{\partial \psi}{\partial x} = \frac{\partial \psi}{\partial y} = 0$.
  • Kutta-Joukowski Theorem: Lift per unit span $L' = \rho_\infty V_\infty \Gamma$, where circulation $\Gamma = \oint \vec{V} \cdot d\vec{s} = \oint \nabla \phi \cdot d\vec{s}$.

4. Aviation Application (5–10 min)

Potential flow theory using stream functions and velocity potentials is a foundational tool in aeronautical engineering. It allows aerodynamicists to model flows around complex geometries before deploying full Navier-Stokes computational solvers.

1. Potential Flow over Wing Airfoils

Using the principle of superposition, the flow around a circular cylinder can be modeled by combining a uniform flow, a doublet, and a line vortex. Applying a Conformal Mapping (specifically the Joukowsky Transformation, $z = \zeta + \frac{c^2}{\zeta}$), the flow field around the cylinder is mapped to a flow field around a cambered airfoil. This enables analytical calculation of velocity potentials and pressure distributions across the airfoil surface.

2. Circulation and Lift Generation

According to potential flow theory, a symmetric airfoil in a uniform stream without circulation generates zero lift (D'Alembert's Paradox). To model the real fluid physics where the flow leaves the sharp trailing edge smoothly (the Kutta Condition), aerodynamicists superimpose a line vortex of strength $\Gamma$ (circulation) at the center of the mapped cylinder. The value of $\Gamma$ is chosen such that the stagnation point on the trailing edge is precisely fixed at the tip. This circulation directly correlates to the lift via the Kutta-Joukowski theorem:

$$L' = \rho_\infty V_\infty \Gamma$$

3. Panel Methods in Aircraft Design

Modern conceptual design software (such as AVL or XFOIL) solves the velocity potential Laplace equation ($\nabla^2 \phi = 0$) by placing distributions of source, doublet, or vortex singularities along the airfoil mean camber line or surface boundaries (panels). By enforcing the kinematic boundary condition (velocity normal to the surface is zero, i.e., $\frac{\partial \phi}{\partial n} = 0$), these panel methods compute the velocity and pressure distribution across the aircraft wing, predicting lift curves and pitching moments with minimal computational expense.

5. UPSC ASO Focus (10 min)

  • The Compressibility Trap: $\nabla^2 \phi = 0$ is valid ONLY for incompressible irrotational flows. For compressible flows, the potential equation becomes non-linear (e.g., $(1-M^2)\frac{\partial^2 \phi}{\partial x^2} + \frac{\partial^2 \phi}{\partial y^2} = 0$ in linearized subsonic/supersonic potential flow).
  • Existence Criteria:
    • $\psi$ exists for any 2D incompressible flow (whether rotational or irrotational).
    • $\phi$ exists for any irrotational flow (whether 2D/3D, compressible or incompressible).
  • Stokes' Stream Function: For axisymmetric flows (3D flows with rotational symmetry like over a missile or fuselage), $\psi$ is defined such that $v_r = \frac{1}{r^2 \sin\theta}\frac{\partial \psi}{\partial \theta}$ and $v_\theta = -\frac{1}{r \sin\theta}\frac{\partial \psi}{\partial r}$ (or similar in cylindrical coordinates). Note the $r^2$ and $r$ scaling terms, which differ from 2D.
  • Cauchy-Riemann Equations Sign Conventions: Ensure consistency with $u = \frac{\partial \phi}{\partial x} = \frac{\partial \psi}{\partial y}$ and $v = \frac{\partial \phi}{\partial y} = -\frac{\partial \psi}{\partial x}$. Some outdated texts use $u = -\frac{\partial \phi}{\partial x}$. Always state/check conventions!

6. Practice Questions (30 Exhaustive UPSC ASO MCQs)

Q1. A two-dimensional, incompressible fluid flow is described by the stream function $\psi = 3x^2y - y^3$. What is the magnitude of the volumetric flow rate per unit depth passing between the coordinates $(1, 1)$ and $(2, 2)$?
A) $10\text{ m}^2/\text{s}$
B) $12\text{ m}^2/\text{s}$
C) $14\text{ m}^2/\text{s}$
D) $16\text{ m}^2/\text{s}$
To find the flow rate per unit depth $Q$ between two points, we compute the difference in the stream function values at those points:
At $(1, 1)$:
$$\psi(1,1) = 3(1)^2(1) - (1)^3 = 3 - 1 = 2\text{ m}^2/\text{s}$$
At $(2, 2)$:
$$\psi(2,2) = 3(2)^2(2) - (2)^3 = 24 - 8 = 16\text{ m}^2/\text{s}$$
$$Q = |\psi(2,2) - \psi(1,1)| = |16 - 2| = 14\text{ m}^2/\text{s}$$
Hence, option C is correct.
Q2. For which of the following flow fields does a velocity potential $\phi$ exist?
A) Steady, rotational flow of a compressible fluid
B) Unsteady, irrotational flow of a compressible fluid
C) Steady, rotational flow of an incompressible fluid
D) Unsteady, rotational flow of an incompressible fluid
The sole necessary and sufficient condition for the existence of a velocity potential $\phi$ is that the flow field must be irrotational ($\nabla \times \vec{V} = 0$). It does not depend on whether the flow is steady/unsteady or compressible/incompressible. Thus, option B is correct.
Q3. Under what conditions does the stream function $\psi$ satisfy the 2D Laplace equation $\frac{\partial^2 \psi}{\partial x^2} + \frac{\partial^2 \psi}{\partial y^2} = 0$?
A) The flow is incompressible and rotational
B) The flow is compressible and irrotational
C) The flow is incompressible and irrotational
D) The stream function always satisfies Laplace's equation for any 2D flow
The stream function is defined based on continuity ($u = \partial\psi/\partial y, v = -\partial\psi/\partial x$), which assumes incompressible flow. The irrotationality condition requires:
$$\omega_z = \frac{\partial v}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial u}{\partial y} = 0 \implies -\frac{\partial^2 \psi}{\partial x^2} - \frac{\partial^2 \psi}{\partial y^2} = 0 \implies \nabla^2 \psi = 0$$
Thus, Laplace's equation is satisfied if the flow is both incompressible and irrotational.
Q4. If a flow field has the velocity potential $\phi = x^2 - y^2$, find the corresponding stream function $\psi$ assuming $\psi(0,0) = 0$.
A) $\psi = 2xy$
B) $\psi = -2xy$
C) $\psi = x^2 + y^2$
D) $\psi = x^2y - y^2x$
Using the Cauchy-Riemann relations:
$$u = \frac{\partial \phi}{\partial x} = 2x = \frac{\partial \psi}{\partial y} \implies \psi = \int 2x \, dy = 2xy + f(x)$$
$$v = \frac{\partial \phi}{\partial y} = -2y = -\frac{\partial \psi}{\partial x} \implies \frac{\partial \psi}{\partial x} = 2y$$
Differentiating $\psi = 2xy + f(x)$ with respect to $x$ gives:
$$2y + f'(x) = 2y \implies f'(x) = 0 \implies f(x) = C$$
Using the boundary condition $\psi(0,0) = 0$, we find $C = 0$. Therefore, $\psi = 2xy$.
Q5. In polar coordinates, if a flow field has a stream function $\psi = U_\infty r \sin\theta \left(1 - \frac{R^2}{r^2}\right)$, what is the radial velocity component $v_r$ at any point?
A) $v_r = U_\infty \cos\theta \left(1 - \frac{R^2}{r^2}\right)$
B) $v_r = U_\infty \sin\theta \left(1 + \frac{R^2}{r^2}\right)$
C) $v_r = -U_\infty \cos\theta \left(1 - \frac{R^2}{r^2}\right)$
D) $v_r = \frac{U_\infty}{r} \cos\theta \left(1 - \frac{R^2}{r^2}\right)$
In polar coordinates, the radial velocity component $v_r$ is defined as:
$$v_r = \frac{1}{r}\frac{\partial \psi}{\partial \theta}$$
Given $\psi = U_\infty r \sin\theta \left(1 - \frac{R^2}{r^2}\right)$:
$$\frac{\partial \psi}{\partial \theta} = U_\infty r \cos\theta \left(1 - \frac{R^2}{r^2}\right)$$
$$v_r = \frac{1}{r} \left[ U_\infty r \cos\theta \left(1 - \frac{R^2}{r^2}\right) \right] = U_\infty \cos\theta \left(1 - \frac{R^2}{r^2}\right)$$
This represents the flow over a circular cylinder of radius $R$.
Q6. Which of the following is true at a stagnation point in a 2D potential flow?
A) Streamlines and equipotential lines must be parallel to each other.
B) The gradient of both velocity potential and stream function are zero.
C) The velocity potential is always zero.
D) The stream function must be zero.
At a stagnation point, the velocity vector is zero ($\vec{V} = 0$). Since $\vec{V} = \nabla\phi$ and $|\vec{V}| = |\nabla\psi|$, the gradients of both $\phi$ and $\psi$ must be zero ($\nabla \phi = \mathbf{0}$ and $\nabla \psi = \mathbf{0}$). Thus, option B is correct.
Q7. A flow field is defined by $u = 3x^2 - 3y^2$ and $v = -6xy$. What is the velocity potential $\phi(x,y)$ if $\phi(0,0)=0$?
A) $\phi = x^3 - 3xy^2$
B) $\phi = 3x^2y - y^3$
C) $\phi = x^3 - 3x^2y$
D) $\phi = -3x^2y + y^3$
We have:
$$u = \frac{\partial \phi}{\partial x} = 3x^2 - 3y^2 \implies \phi = \int (3x^2 - 3y^2) dx = x^3 - 3xy^2 + f(y)$$
$$v = \frac{\partial \phi}{\partial y} = -6xy + f'(y)$$
Given $v = -6xy$, we find $f'(y) = 0 \implies f(y) = C$. Since $\phi(0,0)=0$, we have $C=0$. Therefore, $\phi = x^3 - 3xy^2$.
Q8. For an axisymmetric flow, which of the following is the correct definition of the Stokes' stream function $\psi$ in cylindrical coordinates $(r, \theta, z)$ where $r$ is the radial distance from the axis of symmetry?
A) $v_r = \frac{\partial \psi}{\partial z}$, $v_z = -\frac{\partial \psi}{\partial r}$
B) $v_r = \frac{1}{r}\frac{\partial \psi}{\partial z}$, $v_z = -\frac{1}{r}\frac{\partial \psi}{\partial r}$
C) $v_r = -\frac{1}{r}\frac{\partial \psi}{\partial z}$, $v_z = \frac{1}{r}\frac{\partial \psi}{\partial r}$
D) $v_r = -\frac{1}{r^2}\frac{\partial \psi}{\partial z}$, $v_z = \frac{1}{r^2}\frac{\partial \psi}{\partial r}$
In cylindrical coordinates $(r, \theta, z)$ for axisymmetric flow (independent of $\theta$), the continuity equation is:
$$\frac{1}{r}\frac{\partial}{\partial r}(r v_r) + \frac{\partial v_z}{\partial z} = 0$$
To satisfy this identically, we define the Stokes' stream function $\psi$ such that:
$$r v_r = \frac{\partial \psi}{\partial z} \implies v_r = \frac{1}{r}\frac{\partial \psi}{\partial z}$$
$$r v_z = -\frac{\partial \psi}{\partial r} \implies v_z = -\frac{1}{r}\frac{\partial \psi}{\partial r}$$
Thus, option B is correct.
Q9. The complex potential of a flow is given by $w(z) = U_\infty z$. What does this flow represent?
A) A source flow at the origin
B) A line vortex flow at the origin
C) A uniform parallel flow along the x-axis
D) A doublet flow at the origin
The complex potential is $w(z) = \phi + i\psi$. Given $w(z) = U_\infty z$ where $z = x + iy$:
$$w(z) = U_\infty(x + iy) = U_\infty x + i U_\infty y$$
Equating real and imaginary parts:
$$\phi = U_\infty x, \quad \psi = U_\infty y$$
This yields $u = \frac{\partial \phi}{\partial x} = U_\infty$ and $v = \frac{\partial \phi}{\partial y} = 0$. This represents a uniform flow along the positive x-axis with velocity magnitude $U_\infty$.
Q10. What is the value of the circulation $\Gamma$ around a closed contour enclosing a single source of strength $m$?
A) $m$
B) $2\pi m$
C) Zero
D) $-m$
A source flow is purely radial ($v_r = \frac{m}{2\pi r}$, $v_\theta = 0$). The circulation $\Gamma$ is defined as the line integral of velocity tangent to a closed curve:
$$\Gamma = \oint \vec{V} \cdot d\vec{s} = \int_{0}^{2\pi} v_\theta (r d\theta) = 0$$
Because $v_\theta = 0$, the circulation is identically zero.
Q11. The stream function for a two-dimensional flow is given by $\psi = x^2 - y^2$. Find the acceleration component $a_x$ at the point $(1, 1)$ assuming steady-state conditions.
A) $-4\text{ units}$
B) $4\text{ units}$
C) $-8\text{ units}$
D) $8\text{ units}$
First, find the velocity components from $\psi = x^2 - y^2$:
$$u = \frac{\partial \psi}{\partial y} = -2y$$
$$v = -\frac{\partial \psi}{\partial x} = -2x$$
For steady flow, the acceleration in the x-direction is given by:
$$a_x = u\frac{\partial u}{\partial x} + v\frac{\partial u}{\partial y}$$
Calculate the partial derivatives of $u$:
$$\frac{\partial u}{\partial x} = 0, \quad \frac{\partial u}{\partial y} = -2$$
Substitute these into the acceleration formula:
$$a_x = (-2y)(0) + (-2x)(-2) = 4x$$
At $(1, 1)$:
$$a_x = 4(1) = 4 \text{ units}$$
Wait, let's re-verify the values:
$$u = \frac{\partial \psi}{\partial y} = -2y \implies \frac{\partial u}{\partial x} = 0, \frac{\partial u}{\partial y} = -2$$
$$v = -\frac{\partial \psi}{\partial x} = -2x$$
$$a_x = u(0) + v(-2) = (-2x)(-2) = 4x = 4$$
Therefore, the correct value is $+4$ (Option B).
Q12. If $w(z) = -\frac{\mu}{2\pi z}$ represents a doublet at the origin, what is the corresponding equation for the streamlines ($\psi = \text{constant}$)?
A) $r = C \cos\theta$
B) $r^2 = C \sin\theta$
C) $r = C \sin\theta$
D) $r = C \tan\theta$
In polar coordinates, $z = r e^{i\theta}$. The complex potential of a doublet of strength $\mu$ aligned along the x-axis is:
$$w(z) = \frac{\mu}{2\pi z} = \frac{\mu}{2\pi r e^{i\theta}} = \frac{\mu}{2\pi r} (\cos\theta - i\sin\theta)$$
Since $w(z) = \phi + i\psi$:
$$\psi = -\frac{\mu \sin\theta}{2\pi r}$$
For a constant streamline, $\psi = \text{const} = K$:
$$K = -\frac{\mu \sin\theta}{2\pi r} \implies r = \left(-\frac{\mu}{2\pi K}\right) \sin\theta \implies r = C \sin\theta$$
These curves represent a family of circles tangent to the x-axis at the origin.
Q13. Which of the following physical flows is represented by the superposition of a uniform parallel flow and a source?
A) Rankine Oval
B) Rankine Half-Body
C) Flow past a circular cylinder
D) Kelvin Oval
The superposition of a uniform flow and a single source produces a Rankine Half-Body (a semi-infinite body shape). Adding a source and a sink of equal strength to a uniform flow produces a Rankine Oval.
Q14. In potential flow theory, what does the 'Kutta Condition' physically enforce at the trailing edge of an airfoil?
A) The velocity at the trailing edge is infinite.
B) The flow leaves the trailing edge smoothly, ensuring the velocity is finite.
C) The pressure at the trailing edge drops to zero.
D) The circulation around the airfoil becomes zero.
The Kutta condition ensures that for a sharp trailing edge, the fluid leaves the trailing edge smoothly, preventing infinite velocities which are physically impossible in viscous fluids. This determines the exact amount of circulation ($\Gamma$) generated by the airfoil.
Q15. The stream function for a two-dimensional flow field is given by $\psi = A x^2 y$. Under what condition is this flow irrotational?
A) Always irrotational
B) Never irrotational for any non-zero value of $A$
C) Only if $A = 0$
D) Only if $x = y$
The flow is irrotational if the stream function satisfies the 2D Laplace equation ($\nabla^2 \psi = 0$):
$$\frac{\partial \psi}{\partial x} = 2Axy \implies \frac{\partial^2 \psi}{\partial x^2} = 2Ay$$
$$\frac{\partial \psi}{\partial y} = Ax^2 \implies \frac{\partial^2 \psi}{\partial y^2} = 0$$
$$\nabla^2 \psi = 2Ay + 0 = 2Ay$$
For the flow to be irrotational everywhere, we must have $2Ay = 0$ for all $y$, which is only possible if $A = 0$. For any non-zero value of $A$, the flow is rotational. Thus, B is the most complete statement.
Q16. Find the velocity components $u$ and $v$ if the velocity potential is given by $\phi = U(x \cos\alpha + y \sin\alpha)$.
A) $u = U \sin\alpha, v = -U \cos\alpha$
B) $u = U \cos\alpha, v = U \sin\alpha$
C) $u = U \cos\alpha, v = -U \sin\alpha$
D) $u = -U \cos\alpha, v = U \sin\alpha$
Using the definition of velocity potential:
$$u = \frac{\partial \phi}{\partial x} = \frac{\partial}{\partial x} [U(x \cos\alpha + y \sin\alpha)] = U \cos\alpha$$
$$v = \frac{\partial \phi}{\partial y} = \frac{\partial}{\partial y} [U(x \cos\alpha + y \sin\alpha)] = U \sin\alpha$$
This represents a uniform flow of velocity magnitude $U$ at an angle of attack $\alpha$ to the x-axis.
Q17. A source of strength $m = 4\pi\text{ m}^2/\text{s}$ is placed at the origin in a uniform flow of velocity $U_\infty = 2\text{ m/s}$ moving in the positive x-direction. What is the distance of the stagnation point from the origin?
A) $0.5\text{ m}$
B) $1.0\text{ m}$
C) $2.0\text{ m}$
D) $4.0\text{ m}$
For a source of strength $m$ in a uniform flow $U_\infty$, the radial velocity is:
$$v_r = U_\infty \cos\theta + \frac{m}{2\pi r}$$
The stagnation point lies on the negative x-axis ($\theta = \pi$), where $\cos\pi = -1$. Setting $v_r = 0$:
$$-U_\infty + \frac{m}{2\pi r} = 0 \implies r = \frac{m}{2\pi U_\infty}$$
Given $m = 4\pi$ and $U_\infty = 2$:
$$r = \frac{4\pi}{2\pi(2)} = 1.0\text{ m}$$
Hence, the stagnation point is located at $x = -1.0\text{ m}$.
Q18. The velocity components of a flow field are $u = y^2 - x^2$ and $v = 2xy$. What is the vorticity vector $\vec{\omega}$?
A) $4y\hat{k}$
B) $-4y\hat{k}$
C) Zero
D) $2y\hat{k}$
The out-of-plane vorticity component $\omega_z$ is calculated as:
$$\omega_z = \frac{\partial v}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial u}{\partial y}$$
Given $u = y^2 - x^2 \implies \frac{\partial u}{\partial y} = 2y$
$v = 2xy \implies \frac{\partial v}{\partial x} = 2y$
$$\omega_z = 2y - 2y = 0$$
Since the flow is 2D, the other vorticity components are zero. Thus, the vorticity is zero, meaning the flow is completely irrotational.
Q19. For a 2D line vortex of circulation strength $\Gamma$ centered at the origin, what is the expression for the stream function $\psi$?
A) $\psi = \frac{\Gamma}{2\pi} \ln(r)$
B) $\psi = -\frac{\Gamma}{2\pi} \ln(r)$
C) $\psi = \frac{\Gamma}{2\pi} \theta$
D) $\psi = -\frac{\Gamma}{2\pi} \theta$
For a line vortex, the velocity components are $v_r = 0$ and $v_\theta = \frac{\Gamma}{2\pi r}$.
Using the polar definition of the stream function:
$$v_r = \frac{1}{r}\frac{\partial \psi}{\partial \theta} = 0 \implies \psi \text{ is a function of } r \text{ only.}$$
$$v_\theta = -\frac{\partial \psi}{\partial r} = \frac{\Gamma}{2\pi r} \implies \psi = -\frac{\Gamma}{2\pi} \ln(r) + C$$
Thus, option B is correct.
Q20. If two stream functions $\psi_1(x,y)$ and $\psi_2(x,y)$ are both solutions to Laplace's equation, then their linear combination $\psi = a\psi_1 + b\psi_2$ is also a solution because:
A) Stream functions are scalar quantities
B) The governing equation is a linear partial differential equation
C) The boundary conditions are always homogeneous
D) The flow is always steady-state
Laplace's equation $\nabla^2 \psi = 0$ is a linear differential equation. The principle of superposition applies directly to linear systems, allowing any linear combination of solutions to also be a valid solution.
Q21. A two-dimensional source of strength $m = 6\pi\text{ m}^2/\text{s}$ is placed at $(1, 0)$ and a sink of equal strength is placed at $(-1, 0)$. What is the velocity potential $\phi$ at the origin?
A) $3 \ln(2)\text{ m}^2/\text{s}$
B) Zero
C) $6\pi\text{ m}^2/\text{s}$
D) $-3 \ln(2)\text{ m}^2/\text{s}$
The velocity potential for a source at $(1,0)$ is $\phi_{\text{source}} = \frac{m}{2\pi} \ln(r_1)$ where $r_1 = \sqrt{(x-1)^2 + y^2}$.
The velocity potential for a sink at $(-1,0)$ is $\phi_{\text{sink}} = -\frac{m}{2\pi} \ln(r_2)$ where $r_2 = \sqrt{(x+1)^2 + y^2}$.
By superposition, the total potential is:
$$\phi = \frac{m}{2\pi} \ln(r_1) - \frac{m}{2\pi} \ln(r_2) = \frac{m}{2\pi} \ln\left(\frac{r_1}{r_2}\right)$$
At the origin $(0,0)$:
$$r_1 = \sqrt{(-1)^2 + 0^2} = 1, \quad r_2 = \sqrt{(1)^2 + 0^2} = 1$$
$$\phi(0,0) = \frac{m}{2\pi} \ln\left(\frac{1}{1}\right) = \frac{m}{2\pi} \ln(1) = 0$$
Thus, option B is correct.
Q22. In potential flow past a circular cylinder of radius $R$ without circulation, what is the maximum velocity magnitude on the surface of the cylinder if the freestream velocity is $U_\infty$?
A) $U_\infty$
B) $1.5 U_\infty$
C) $2.0 U_\infty$
D) $4.0 U_\infty$
The surface velocity on a cylinder of radius $R$ in potential flow is pure tangential:
$$v_\theta = -2 U_\infty \sin\theta$$
The maximum magnitude of this velocity occurs at $\theta = \pm \pi/2$ (top and bottom points of the cylinder), where $|\sin\theta| = 1$. This yields:
$$|v_\theta|_{\text{max}} = 2 U_\infty$$
Thus, the correct option is C.
Q23. If the stream function is given by $\psi = xy$, the streamlines represent a family of:
A) Concentric circles
B) Parallel straight lines
C) Rectangular hyperbolas
D) Parabolas
Streamlines are lines of constant $\psi$. Thus, $xy = C$, which is the equation of a family of rectangular hyperbolas asymptotic to the coordinate axes. This describes 2D flow near a stagnation point or in a corner.
Q24. A flow has a velocity potential $\phi = A e^x \cos y$. Is this flow physically possible for an incompressible fluid?
A) No, because $\phi$ must be a linear function of $x$ and $y$.
B) Yes, because it satisfies the Laplace equation.
C) Yes, but only if $A = 0$.
D) No, because exponential velocity fields violate mass conservation.
For an incompressible, irrotational flow, physical possibility is determined by whether the velocity potential satisfies the Laplace equation ($\nabla^2 \phi = 0$):
$$\frac{\partial \phi}{\partial x} = A e^x \cos y \implies \frac{\partial^2 \phi}{\partial x^2} = A e^x \cos y$$
$$\frac{\partial \phi}{\partial y} = -A e^x \sin y \implies \frac{\partial^2 \phi}{\partial y^2} = -A e^x \cos y$$
$$\nabla^2 \phi = \frac{\partial^2 \phi}{\partial x^2} + \frac{\partial^2 \phi}{\partial y^2} = A e^x \cos y - A e^x \cos y = 0$$
Since it satisfies Laplace's equation, the flow field satisfies the continuity equation and is physically possible.
Q25. Which of the following is the physical statement of the Kinematic Boundary Condition on a solid, stationary boundary in potential flow?
A) The tangential velocity must be zero.
B) The normal component of velocity must be zero.
C) The velocity potential must be zero.
D) The stream function gradient must be zero.
In inviscid (potential) flow, there is no physical mechanism to enforce a no-slip condition (zero tangential velocity). Therefore, the only kinematic boundary condition is the **no-penetration** condition, meaning the fluid cannot cross a solid boundary. This is expressed as $\vec{V} \cdot \hat{n} = \frac{\partial \phi}{\partial n} = 0$.
Q26. What is the complex potential $w(z)$ of a vortex of circulation $\Gamma$ (clockwise) located at the origin?
A) $w(z) = \frac{\Gamma}{2\pi} \ln(z)$
B) $w(z) = i\frac{\Gamma}{2\pi} \ln(z)$
C) $w(z) = -i\frac{\Gamma}{2\pi} \ln(z)$
D) $w(z) = -\frac{\Gamma}{2\pi z}$
By convention, a counter-clockwise vortex has a complex potential of $w(z) = i\frac{\Gamma}{2\pi} \ln(z)$. Let us verify:
$$w(z) = i\frac{\Gamma}{2\pi} \ln(r e^{i\theta}) = i\frac{\Gamma}{2\pi} (\ln r + i\theta) = -\frac{\Gamma}{2\pi} \theta + i\frac{\Gamma}{2\pi} \ln r$$
Here, the imaginary part is $\psi = \frac{\Gamma}{2\pi} \ln r$, which yields $v_\theta = -\frac{\partial \psi}{\partial r} = -\frac{\Gamma}{2\pi r}$ (clockwise flow). Thus, B is correct.
Q27. The stream function for a two-dimensional, incompressible flow is $\psi = 4xy$. What is the velocity magnitude $|\vec{V}|$ at the point $(1, 2)$?
A) $4 \sqrt{5}\text{ m/s}$
B) $20\text{ m/s}$
C) $4\text{ m/s}$
D) $8\text{ m/s}$
Find the velocity components:
$$u = \frac{\partial \psi}{\partial y} = 4x$$
$$v = -\frac{\partial \psi}{\partial x} = -4y$$
At $(1, 2)$:
$$u = 4(1) = 4\text{ m/s}$$
$$v = -4(2) = -8\text{ m/s}$$
The velocity magnitude is:
$$|\vec{V}| = \sqrt{u^2 + v^2} = \sqrt{4^2 + (-8)^2} = \sqrt{16 + 64} = \sqrt{80} = 4 \sqrt{5}\text{ m/s}$$
Thus, option A is correct.
Q28. For a flow field with velocity components $u = e^x \cosh y$ and $v = -e^x \sinh y$, the value of the velocity potential $\phi$ is:
A) $\phi = e^x \sinh y$
B) $\phi = e^x \cosh y$
C) $\phi = -e^x \cosh y$
D) No velocity potential exists because the flow is rotational.
Check irrotationality first:
$$\frac{\partial v}{\partial x} = -e^x \sinh y$$
$$\frac{\partial u}{\partial y} = e^x \sinh y$$
Wait, $\frac{\partial v}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial u}{\partial y} = -e^x \sinh y - e^x \sinh y = -2 e^x \sinh y \neq 0$. Thus, the flow is **rotational**, and no velocity potential exists.
Let's check if we can construct one: if $\phi = e^x \cosh y$, then:
$$\frac{\partial \phi}{\partial x} = e^x \cosh y = u$$
$$\frac{\partial \phi}{\partial y} = e^x \sinh y \neq v \text{ (which is } -e^x \sinh y\text{)}$$
Thus, no velocity potential can exist for this rotational flow. Option D is correct.
Q29. A critical limit of potential flow theory is its inability to predict skin friction drag. This limitation is known as:
A) Kutta-Joukowski Paradox
B) Prandtl's Boundary Layer Dilemma
C) D'Alembert's Paradox
D) Magnus Effect Paradox
D'Alembert's Paradox states that for a 2D body moving through an inviscid potential flow, the net aerodynamic drag force on the body is zero. This contradicts experimental observations where viscous skin friction and pressure drag exist.
Q30. If the complex potential of a flow is $w(z) = \ln(z)$, the flow represents:
A) A line source at the origin
B) A line vortex at the origin
C) A doublet at the origin
D) A stagnation point flow
Let $z = r e^{i\theta}$:
$$w(z) = \ln(r e^{i\theta}) = \ln(r) + i\theta$$
Since $w(z) = \phi + i\psi$, we have:
$$\phi = \ln(r)$$
$$\psi = \theta$$
Evaluating the velocity components:
$$v_r = \frac{\partial \phi}{\partial r} = \frac{1}{r}, \quad v_\theta = \frac{1}{r}\frac{\partial \phi}{\partial \theta} = 0$$
This pure radial flow, where velocity is inversely proportional to $r$, represents a 2D line source at the origin.

7. Grand Mini Test (10 Exam-Level Questions)

Q1. Statement I: The stream function $\psi$ always exists for any 2D incompressible flow field, even if the flow is rotational.
Statement II: The velocity potential $\phi$ only exists if the flow is irrotational.
A) Both statements are true and Statement II is the correct explanation of Statement I.
B) Both statements are true but Statement II is not the correct explanation of Statement I.
C) Statement I is true, Statement II is false.
D) Both statements are false.
Statement I is true because $\psi$ is defined directly from the continuity equation ($u = \frac{\partial \psi}{\partial y}$, $v = -\frac{\partial \psi}{\partial x}$), which only requires incompressibility. Statement II is true because the existence of $\phi$ implies $\nabla \times \vec{V} = \nabla \times \nabla \phi = \mathbf{0}$, which is the mathematical condition for irrotationality. However, Statement II does not explain why Statement I is true.
Q2. Assertion (A): At any non-stagnation point, a streamline and an equipotential line must intersect at right angles.
Reason (R): The product of the slopes of the streamline and the equipotential line in a 2D plane is $-1$.
A) Both A and R are true and R is the correct explanation of A.
B) Both A and R are true but R is not the correct explanation of A.
C) A is true, but R is false.
D) A is false, but R is true.
The slope of an equipotential line is $(dy/dx)_\phi = -u/v$ and the slope of a streamline is $(dy/dx)_ \psi = v/u$. The product of these slopes is $(-u/v) \times (v/u) = -1$. This mathematically proves that they are orthogonal (intersect at $90^\circ$). At stagnation points, the slopes are indeterminate ($0/0$), so the theorem holds except at stagnation points.
Q3. A velocity potential function is given by $\phi = x(3y^2 - x^2)$. Determine the value of the stream function $\psi(x, y)$ if $\psi(0,0) = 0$.
A) $\psi = y(3x^2 - y^2)$
B) $\psi = y(y^2 - 3x^2)$
C) $\psi = x(3y^2 - x^2)$
D) $\psi = -y(3x^2 - y^2)$
$$\phi = 3xy^2 - x^3$$
$$u = \frac{\partial \phi}{\partial x} = 3y^2 - 3x^2 = \frac{\partial \psi}{\partial y}$$
$$\psi = \int (3y^2 - 3x^2) dy = y^3 - 3x^2y + f(x)$$
Now use the second Cauchy-Riemann relation:
$$v = \frac{\partial \phi}{\partial y} = 6xy = -\frac{\partial \psi}{\partial x}$$
$$\frac{\partial \psi}{\partial x} = -6xy + f'(x) = -6xy \implies f'(x) = 0 \implies f(x) = C$$
Given $\psi(0,0) = 0$, we have $C = 0$. So:
$$\psi = y^3 - 3x^2y = y(y^2 - 3x^2) = -y(3x^2 - y^2)$$
Wait, let's re-verify the sign!
$$\frac{\partial \psi}{\partial y} \text{ for } y(3x^2 - y^2) = 3x^2 - 3y^2$$
But $u = 3y^2 - 3x^2$. Thus, we must have:
$$\psi = y(y^2 - 3x^2) = -y(3x^2 - y^2)$$
Let's check Option B: $y(y^2 - 3x^2) = y^3 - 3x^2y$. This is indeed equivalent to Option B. Let's make sure the choice is evaluated correctly. B is indeed $\psi = y(y^2-3x^2)$.
Q4. A doublet of strength $\mu$ is placed at the origin in a uniform flow $U_\infty$ moving in the positive x-direction. What is the radius $R$ of the circular cylinder formed by the dividing streamline ($\psi = 0$)?
A) $R = \sqrt{\frac{\mu}{2\pi U_\infty}}$
B) $R = \frac{\mu}{2\pi U_\infty}$
C) $R = \sqrt{\frac{\mu}{\pi U_\infty}}$
D) $R = \left(\frac{\mu}{2\pi U_\infty}\right)^{1/3}$
The stream function for a doublet in a uniform flow is:
$$\psi = U_\infty r \sin\theta - \frac{\mu \sin\theta}{2\pi r} = U_\infty r \sin\theta \left(1 - \frac{\mu}{2\pi U_\infty r^2}\right)$$
On the boundary of the cylinder, $\psi = 0$. Since $\sin\theta \neq 0$ everywhere, we must have:
$$1 - \frac{\mu}{2\pi U_\infty R^2} = 0 \implies R^2 = \frac{\mu}{2\pi U_\infty} \implies R = \sqrt{\frac{\mu}{2\pi U_\infty}}$$
Thus, option A is correct.
Q5. For a 2D incompressible potential flow, the complex potential is $w(z) = A z^2$ where $A > 0$ is a constant. This complex potential describes:
A) Flow around a $90^\circ$ sharp corner
B) Flow over a flat plate aligned with the stream
C) Flow in a $180^\circ$ turn
D) Flow inside a circular cylinder
Using $z = r e^{i\theta}$:
$$w(z) = A (r e^{i\theta})^2 = A r^2 (\cos 2\theta + i\sin 2\theta)$$
Thus, $\psi = A r^2 \sin 2\theta$. The streamlines $\psi = 0$ occur where $\sin 2\theta = 0 \implies \theta = 0$ and $\theta = \pi/2$. This represents the physical solid boundaries of a $90^\circ$ corner (or wedge flow), meaning option A is correct.
Q6. Which of the following boundary layer parameters can be computed directly using potential flow theory?
A) Boundary layer displacement thickness ($\delta^*$)
B) Skin friction coefficient ($C_f$)
C) Velocity distribution at the edge of the boundary layer ($U_e$)
D) Separation point location
Potential flow theory assumes inviscid ($\mu = 0$) flow and cannot predict any viscous parameters such as displacement thickness, skin friction, or boundary layer separation. However, it is used to calculate the outer flow velocity $U_e(x)$ immediately outside the boundary layer, which is then used as the boundary condition for solving the boundary layer equations.
Q7. If a potential flow is represented by the complex potential $w(z) = U_\infty \left( z + \frac{R^2}{z} \right)$, what is the tangential velocity $v_\theta$ on the cylinder surface $r=R$?
A) $-2 U_\infty \sin\theta$
B) $-2 U_\infty \cos\theta$
C) $-U_\infty \sin\theta$
D) Zero
Substitute $z = r e^{i\theta}$ into $w(z)$:
$$w(z) = U_\infty \left( r e^{i\theta} + \frac{R^2}{r} e^{-i\theta} \right)$$
Taking the imaginary part to find the stream function $\psi$:
$$\psi = U_\infty \left( r - \frac{R^2}{r} \right) \sin\theta$$
The tangential velocity $v_\theta$ is:
$$v_\theta = -\frac{\partial \psi}{\partial r} = -U_\infty \left( 1 + \frac{R^2}{r^2} \right) \sin\theta$$
Evaluating this on the surface of the cylinder where $r = R$:
$$v_\theta = -U_\infty \left( 1 + 1 \right) \sin\theta = -2 U_\infty \sin\theta$$
Thus, option A is correct.
Q8. The circulation $\Gamma$ around an airfoil is related to the lift force per unit span $L'$ by the Kutta-Joukowski theorem. What is the correct dimension of circulation $\Gamma$?
A) $[L^2 T^{-1}]$
B) $[L T^{-1}]$
C) $[L^2 T^{-2}]$
D) $[M L^{-1} T^{-1}]$
Circulation is defined as:
$$\Gamma = \oint \vec{V} \cdot d\vec{s}$$
In terms of dimensions:
$$[\Gamma] = [\text{Velocity}] \times [\text{Length}] = [L T^{-1}] \times [L] = [L^2 T^{-1}]$$
This is identical to the dimensions of the stream function and velocity potential. Thus, option A is correct.
Q9. Statement I: The velocity potential $\phi$ must satisfy the Laplace equation for any steady compressible irrotational flow field.
Statement II: For a compressible fluid, the continuity equation contains density gradients that modify the governing potential equation.
A) Both statements are true and Statement II is the correct explanation of Statement I.
B) Statement I is false, Statement II is true.
C) Statement I is true, Statement II is false.
D) Both statements are false.
Statement I is false because the Laplace equation $\nabla^2 \phi = 0$ is derived directly by assuming constant density (incompressible flow). For compressible flow, density is not constant, and the governing potential equation becomes non-linear (e.g., $(1-M^2)\frac{\partial^2\phi}{\partial x^2} + \frac{\partial^2\phi}{\partial y^2} = 0$ in linearized subsonic flow). Statement II is true because the compressible continuity equation is $\nabla \cdot (\rho \vec{V}) = 0$, which includes density gradients. Thus, option B is correct.
Q10. A free vortex and a forced vortex are distinguished by their rotational characteristics. Which of the following is true?
A) Both possess a velocity potential $\phi$ everywhere in space.
B) A free vortex is rotational, while a forced vortex is irrotational.
C) A free vortex is irrotational everywhere except at its origin, while a forced vortex is rotational everywhere.
D) Neither flow can be represented by a stream function.
A free vortex has $v_\theta = \frac{C}{r}$, yielding $\omega_z = 0$ everywhere except at the singular origin ($r=0$). Thus, it is irrotational everywhere else and has a velocity potential. A forced vortex rotates like a solid body ($v_\theta = \omega r$), yielding $\omega_z = 2\omega \neq 0$ everywhere. Thus, it is rotational and does not possess a velocity potential. Both are 2D incompressible flows and can be modeled using stream functions. Thus, C is correct.

8. Error Analysis & Common Traps (5 min)

1. The "Irrotational-Stream Function" Trap

Many students incorrectly assume that because $\psi$ and $\phi$ are taught together, $\psi$ can only exist for irrotational flows. This is false. The stream function $\psi$ is derived solely from the 2D incompressible conservation of mass (continuity equation). It exists for any 2D incompressible flow, whether rotational or irrotational. It only satisfies Laplace's equation ($\nabla^2\psi = 0$) if the flow is also irrotational.

2. The Compressibility Oversight

A classic UPSC trap is to ask whether $\nabla^2 \phi = 0$ applies to high-speed aerodynamics. Always remember: $\nabla^2 \phi = 0$ is strictly for incompressible irrotational flow. For compressible irrotational flow, the governing equation is the non-linear Full Potential Equation, which simplifies to the linearized Prandtl-Glauert equation under small-disturbance subsonic flows:

$$(1 - M_\infty^2)\frac{\partial^2 \phi}{\partial x^2} + \frac{\partial^2 \phi}{\partial y^2} = 0$$

3. Sign Conventions in Cauchy-Riemann Relations

Be extremely cautious with the sign in $v = -\frac{\partial \psi}{\partial x}$. Missing this negative sign during integrations to find $\psi$ from $\phi$ (or vice versa) will lead to incorrect signs in velocity components and stagnation point coordinates.

4. Stagnation Point Confusion in Cylindrical Coordinates

When finding stagnation points for polar potential flows, students often solve only $v_r = 0$, forgetting to check $v_\theta = 0$. Stagnation requires both components to vanish simultaneously.
