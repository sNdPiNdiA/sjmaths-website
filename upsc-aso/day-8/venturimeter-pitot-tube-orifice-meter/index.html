Venturimeter, Pitot tube, Orifice meter

Day 8 of 129 Fluid Mechanics & Machinery Phase 1: Foundations 90-120 min Master Class

1. Understand (From Absolute Zero, 5–10 min)

In fluid mechanics and aeronautical engineering, measuring the state of a moving fluid—specifically its velocity and flow rate—is of paramount importance. To do this, we rely on three classical flow-measurement instruments that exploit the fundamental trade-off between pressure energy and kinetic energy: the Venturimeter, the Orifice meter, and the Pitot tube (along with its counterpart, the Pitot-static tube).

1. The Venturimeter

The Venturimeter is a highly streamlined, low-head-loss device installed directly in a pipeline to measure the volumetric flow rate ($Q$). It consists of three primary components:

  • Convergent Cone: A rapidly narrowing section where the cross-sectional area decreases, forcing the fluid to accelerate. This acceleration causes a corresponding drop in static pressure according to Bernoulli's principle.
  • Throat: The narrowest section of constant minimum area where the fluid reaches its maximum velocity and minimum static pressure.
  • Divergent Cone: A gradually widening section designed to decelerate the fluid back to the original pipe velocity while converting kinetic energy back into static pressure (pressure recovery). It is elongated with a gentle angle ($5^\circ$ to $7^\circ$) to prevent boundary layer separation and flow stall.
The difference in static pressure between the inlet and the throat is measured (typically using a differential U-tube manometer or transducer) to determine the flow rate.

2. The Orifice Meter

The Orifice meter operates on the exact same physical principle as the Venturimeter but uses a starkly different design geometry. It consists of a thin, flat metal plate with a sharp-edged circular hole (the orifice) clamped between two pipe flanges. As fluid passes through the orifice, the streamlines contract, continuing to narrow even after leaving the physical opening. The point of minimum cross-sectional area, where the streamlines become perfectly parallel and the velocity is maximum, is called the vena contracta. While cheap, compact, and easy to install, the Orifice meter creates intense turbulence, flow separation, and vortex formation downstream, leading to high permanent energy losses (head loss).

3. The Pitot Tube and Pitot-Static Tube

Unlike the Venturimeter and Orifice meter, which measure the bulk flow rate through a pipe, the Pitot tube is a point-measurement device. It is designed to measure the local velocity ($V$) at a specific point in a fluid stream.

  • Pitot Tube: A simple, forward-facing L-shaped tube with its open end pointing directly into the oncoming flow. The fluid entering the tube is decelerated to zero velocity. The point where the velocity becomes zero is the stagnation point. The pressure measured here is the Total Pressure ($P_0$ or $P_{stag}$), which is the sum of static pressure and dynamic pressure.
  • Pitot-Static Tube: An integrated instrument containing two concentric tubes. The inner tube measures the total (stagnation) pressure at the nose, while the outer tube has small lateral orifices perpendicular to the flow direction to measure the local ambient Static Pressure ($P_s$). The difference between these two readings yields the Dynamic Pressure ($q = \frac{1}{2} \rho V^2$), which directly determines the local flow velocity.

Physical Quantities and SI Units

To analyze these instruments, we track several critical thermodynamic and dynamic properties:

  • Static Pressure ($P$): The actual thermodynamic pressure of the fluid. Measured in Pascals ($\text{Pa} = \text{N/m}^2$).
  • Dynamic Pressure ($q$): The pressure equivalent of kinetic energy per unit volume, defined as $\frac{1}{2}\rho V^2$ (for incompressible flow). Unit: Pascals ($\text{Pa}$).
  • Total/Stagnation Pressure ($P_0$): The pressure a fluid achieves when decelerated to zero velocity isentropically. $P_0 = P + q$. Unit: Pascals ($\text{Pa}$).
  • Volumetric Flow Rate ($Q$): The volume of fluid passing through a cross-section per unit time. Unit: cubic meters per second ($\text{m}^3/\text{s}$).
  • Head ($h$): Pressure expressed as an equivalent column height of a fluid of density $\rho$. $h = \frac{P}{\rho g}$. Unit: meters ($\text{m}$).

2. Learn the Concept (Complete Theoretical Foundation & Derivations, 20–25 min)

To construct a rigorous mathematical foundation for these three flow devices, we start from the fundamental equations of fluid motion: conservation of mass (Continuity Equation) and conservation of mechanical energy (Bernoulli's Equation for inviscid, steady, incompressible flow along a streamline).

1. Derivation of the Venturimeter Governing Equations

Let us consider a horizontal Venturimeter installed in a pipe of cross-sectional area $A_1$ (at the inlet) and throat area $A_2$ (at the throat). A fluid of density $\rho$ flows through it. Let $V_1, P_1$ and $V_2, P_2$ be the velocities and static pressures at the inlet and throat, respectively.

$$ \text{Continuity Equation:} \quad A_1 V_1 = A_2 V_2 \implies V_1 = \left(\frac{A_2}{A_1}\right) V_2 $$ $$ \text{Incompressible Bernoulli Equation:} \quad \frac{P_1}{\rho g} + \frac{V_1^2}{2g} = \frac{P_2}{\rho g} + \frac{V_2^2}{2g} $$

Rearranging the Bernoulli equation to group the pressure heads and velocity heads together, we define the differential pressure head ($h$):

$$ h = \frac{P_1 - P_2}{\rho g} = \frac{V_2^2 - V_1^2}{2g} $$

Substitute the continuity relation $V_1 = \frac{A_2}{A_1} V_2$ into the equation:

$$ 2gh = V_2^2 - \left(\frac{A_2}{A_1} V_2\right)^2 = V_2^2 \left[ 1 - \left(\frac{A_2}{A_1}\right)^2 \right] = V_2^2 \left( \frac{A_1^2 - A_2^2}{A_1^2} \right) $$

Solving for the theoretical velocity at the throat ($V_{2,\text{th}}$):

$$ V_{2,\text{th}} = \frac{A_1}{\sqrt{A_1^2 - A_2^2}} \sqrt{2gh} $$

The theoretical discharge ($Q_{\text{th}}$) is the throat area multiplied by the theoretical throat velocity:

$$ Q_{\text{th}} = A_2 V_{2,\text{th}} = \frac{A_1 A_2}{\sqrt{A_1^2 - A_2^2}} \sqrt{2gh} $$

In practice, viscosity creates shear stresses, causing a thin boundary layer to develop along the walls, which slightly reduces the effective flow area and causes minor head loss. To correct for this, we introduce the Coefficient of Discharge ($C_d$), defined as the ratio of actual discharge to theoretical discharge:

$$ Q_{\text{actual}} = C_d Q_{\text{th}} = C_d \frac{A_1 A_2}{\sqrt{A_1^2 - A_2^2}} \sqrt{2gh} $$

For a standard Venturimeter, $C_d$ is remarkably high, typically ranging from $0.95$ to $0.99$, because its streamlined design minimizes flow separation and turbulence.

Finding $h$ with a Differential U-Tube Manometer

If a differential manometer containing a heavy liquid of specific gravity $S_m$ is connected between the inlet and throat, and the pipe contains a fluid of specific gravity $S_f$ (where $S_m > S_f$), the differential head $h$ in terms of the manometer deflection $y$ is: $$ h = y \left( \frac{S_m}{S_f} - 1 \right) $$ Conversely, if the manometer fluid is lighter than the pipe fluid ($S_m < S_f$): $$ h = y \left( 1 - \frac{S_m}{S_f} \right) $$ An extraordinary property of this relationship is that it remains completely independent of the inclination of the Venturimeter. Whether the meter is horizontal, vertical, or inclined, the difference in the piezometric head is accurately captured by the manometer deflection formula without modification.

2. The Orifice Meter and the Vena Contracta

Due to the sharp edge of the orifice plate, the fluid cannot make a sharp $90^\circ$ turn. It detaches from the plate, forming a jet that continues to contract down to section $c-c$ (the vena contracta). Let $A_0$ be the physical area of the orifice, and $A_c$ be the area of the jet at the vena contracta.

We define the Coefficient of Contraction ($C_c$) as: $$ C_c = \frac{A_c}{A_0} $$ The Coefficient of Velocity ($C_v$) accounts for frictional losses between the inlet and the vena contracta: $$ C_v = \frac{V_{\text{actual}}}{V_{\text{theoretical}}} $$ The overall Coefficient of Discharge for an orifice meter is the product of these two coefficients:

$$ C_d = C_c \times C_v $$
Substituting the orifice area $A_0$ and $C_d$ into the Venturi-style derivation yields: $$ Q_{\text{actual}} = C_d \frac{A_1 A_0}{\sqrt{A_1^2 - A_0^2}} \sqrt{2gh} $$ Because of the highly turbulent nature of the jet expansion downstream of the vena contracta, $C_d$ for an orifice meter is much lower, typically $0.60$ to $0.65$.

3. Pitot-Static Tube and Compressibility Effects

For incompressible, low-speed flow, applying Bernoulli's equation along a streamline starting from the free stream (where pressure is $P_{\infty}$ and velocity is $V_{\infty}$) to the stagnation point (where velocity is $0$ and pressure is $P_0$) yields: $$ P_0 = P_{\infty} + \frac{1}{2}\rho V_{\infty}^2 \implies V_{\infty} = \sqrt{\frac{2(P_0 - P_{\infty})}{\rho}} $$ To account for minor wall friction and misalignment, we define: $$ V_{\text{actual}} = C_v \sqrt{\frac{2(P_0 - P_{\infty})}{\rho}} = C_v \sqrt{2gh} $$ where $C_v \approx 0.98$ to $1.00$.

Compressible Flow Analysis (High Mach Numbers)

When the flight Mach number $M = \frac{V}{a}$ exceeds $0.3$, density variations can no longer be ignored. Under compressible conditions, the deceleration to stagnation must be treated as an isentropic thermodynamic process. Using the 1D steady isentropic relations for an ideal gas: $$ \frac{P_0}{P_{\infty}} = \left( 1 + \frac{\gamma - 1}{2} M^2 \right)^{\frac{\gamma}{\gamma - 1}} $$ where $\gamma$ is the ratio of specific heats ($1.4$ for air). Using the binomial expansion for $M < 1$, we can express the total pressure as: $$ P_0 = P_{\infty} + \frac{1}{2} \rho_{\infty} V_{\infty}^2 \left( 1 + \frac{M^2}{4} + \frac{2-\gamma}{24} M^4 + \dots \right) $$ Setting $\gamma = 1.4$ yields:

$$ P_0 - P_{\infty} = \frac{1}{2} \rho_{\infty} V_{\infty}^2 \left( 1 + \frac{M^2}{4} + \frac{M^4}{40} + \dots \right) $$
This shows that the actual dynamic pressure under compressible conditions ($P_0 - P_{\infty}$) is *larger* than the incompressible estimation $\frac{1}{2}\rho V^2$. If we fail to correct for compressibility at high subsonic speeds, our airspeed calculation will be significantly underestimated.

For supersonic flows ($M > 1$), a detached bow shock wave forms in front of the nose of the Pitot tube. The flow decelerates across the shock wave (which is highly non-isentropic, causing a drop in total pressure) and then decelerates isentropically from the shock to the stagnation point. In this regime, we must calculate the free-stream Mach number using the Rayleigh Pitot Tube Formula: $$ \frac{P_{02}}{P_{\infty}} = \left( \frac{\gamma+1}{2} M^2 \right)^{\frac{\gamma}{\gamma-1}} \left( \frac{\gamma+1}{2\gamma M^2 - (\gamma-1)} \right)^{\frac{1}{\gamma-1}} $$ where $P_{02}$ is the total pressure measured behind the shock wave.

3. Memorize Important Facts (10 min)

  • Theoretical Venturi Discharge: $Q_{\text{th}} = \frac{A_1 A_2}{\sqrt{A_1^2 - A_2^2}} \sqrt{2gh}$ (Units: $\text{m}^3/\text{s}$)
  • Manometer Head Formula: $h = y \left(\frac{S_m}{S_f} - 1\right)$ (independent of pipe inclination angle $\theta$).
  • Discharge Coefficients ($C_d$):
    • Venturimeter: $C_d \approx 0.95 - 0.99$ (streamlined, low head loss).
    • Orifice Meter: $C_d \approx 0.60 - 0.65$ (sharp-edged constriction, high head loss).
    • Pitot Tube: $C_v \approx 0.98 - 1.00$.
  • Fundamental Coefficient Relation: $C_d = C_c \times C_v$.
  • Incompressible Pitot Velocity: $V = \sqrt{\frac{2(P_0 - P_s)}{\rho}} = \sqrt{2gh}$.
  • Compressible Subsonic Expansion ($\gamma = 1.4$): $q_c = P_0 - P_{\infty} = \frac{1}{2} \rho_{\infty} V_{\infty}^2 \left( 1 + \frac{M^2}{4} + \frac{M^4}{40} + \dots \right)$.
  • Venturi Angles: Convergent Angle $\approx 20^\circ \text{ to } 22^\circ$, Divergent Angle $\approx 5^\circ \text{ to } 7^\circ$ (to avoid flow separation).

4. Aviation Application (5–10 min)

The concepts of total, static, and dynamic pressure are the absolute foundation of aircraft speed and altitude measurement systems, specifically within the Pitot-Static System.

1. Pitot-Static System Instruments

The Pitot-static tube supplies pressures directly to three vital cockpit instruments:

  • Airspeed Indicator (ASI): The only instrument connected to *both* the Pitot tube (total pressure) and the static port (static pressure). It contains an internal diaphragm that physically subtracts static pressure from total pressure: $$ \Delta P = P_0 - P_s = \frac{1}{2}\rho V^2 $$ The mechanical expansion of this diaphragm drives the ASI needle, displaying the airspeed.
  • Altimeter: Connected *only* to the static port. It measures the ambient atmospheric static pressure ($P_s$), which decreases predictably with altitude. An internal aneroid capsule expands or contracts in response to this pressure change.
  • Vertical Speed Indicator (VSI): Connected *only* to the static port, but features a calibrated leak (restrictor). During climbs or descents, the pressure inside the instrument case changes slower than inside the capsule, creating a temporary differential pressure that measures the rate of altitude change.

2. Airspeed Definitions

To navigate safely, pilots must distinguish between several types of airspeed:

  • Indicated Airspeed (IAS): The raw speed read directly from the cockpit dial, uncorrected for instrument or installation errors.
  • Calibrated Airspeed (CAS): IAS corrected for instrument errors and "position error" (aerodynamic interference caused by the physical placement of the Pitot tube and static ports on the fuselage).
  • Equivalent Airspeed (EAS): CAS corrected for compressibility effects of air at high speeds. Extremely important above $200\,\text{knots}$ or Mach $0.3$. $$ EAS = TAS \times \sqrt{\frac{\rho}{\rho_0}} $$
  • True Airspeed (TAS): The actual speed of the aircraft relative to the airmass. It accounts for the decrease in air density ($\rho$) at altitude. $$ TAS = EAS \times \sqrt{\frac{\rho_0}{\rho}} $$ At high altitudes, because density $\rho < \rho_0$ (standard sea-level density), the TAS is significantly *higher* than the IAS/EAS.

3. Hazardous Blockage Scenarios

UPSC Air Safety Officer exams frequently test the diagnostic behavior of cockpit instruments under sensor failures:

  1. Blocked Pitot Tube with open Drain Hole: If water or ice blocks the main entry hole but the drain hole remains open, the pressure inside the Pitot line vents completely to the atmosphere. The pressure in the diaphragm drops to static pressure, causing the **ASI to fall immediately to zero**.
  2. Blocked Pitot Tube AND Drain Hole: If both holes freeze over, a volume of air becomes trapped inside the Pitot line.
    • During a climb, ambient static pressure ($P_s$) decreases. Since the trapped pressure inside the Pitot line remains constant, the differential pressure ($P_{\text{trapped}} - P_s$) increases. This causes the **ASI to read incorrectly high (acting as an altimeter)**.
    • During a descent, $P_s$ increases, reducing the differential pressure, which causes the **ASI to read incorrectly low**.
  3. Blocked Static Port: If the static port is completely blocked (e.g., due to ice) while the Pitot tube remains fully functional:
    • The **Altimeter freezes** at the altitude where the blockage occurred.
    • The **VSI freezes at zero**, since no pressure change can pass through the static port.
    • The **ASI reads low in a climb** (because the dynamic pressure is measured against a static pressure that is higher than actual atmospheric pressure at that higher altitude) and **high in a descent**.

5. UPSC ASO Focus (10 min)

  • Optimum Venturi Throat Ratio: The ratio of throat-to-pipe diameter ($d/D$) is strictly kept between $\frac{1}{3}$ and $\frac{1}{2}$ (typically $0.5$). A ratio lower than $1/3$ increases velocity excessively, causing static pressure to drop below the vapor pressure of the liquid, triggering severe **cavitation**. A ratio higher than $1/2$ reduces the differential pressure, lowering measurement sensitivity.
  • Divergent Cone Angle Constraints: The divergent angle is limited to $5^\circ - 7^\circ$ to prevent boundary layer separation. If the angle is widened, the adverse pressure gradient ($dp/dx > 0$) forces the boundary layer to detach, leading to massive eddy current losses. The length of the divergent cone is consequently $3$ to $4$ times longer than the convergent cone.
  • Vena Contracta Location: The vena contracta in an Orifice meter occurs at approximately $0.5$ pipe diameters ($0.5D$) downstream of the orifice plate.
  • Irrotational vs Rotational Flow Trap: Bernoulli’s equation is valid everywhere in a flow field *only* if the flow is irrotational ($\nabla \times \vec{V} = 0$). If the flow is rotational, the Bernoulli constant $C$ changes from one streamline to another, meaning Bernoulli's equation can only be applied along the exact same streamline.
  • Dynamic Pressure Compressibility Boundary: Incompressible equations are considered unacceptable above Mach $0.3$ because the density variation exceeds $2\%$, which introduces an unacceptable margin of error in air safety calculations.

C) $25.0\text{ m/s}$

For incompressible flow, the velocity measured by a Pitot tube is:

- $P_0 - P_s = 1500\text{ Pa}$
- $\rho = 1.2\text{ kg/m}^3$

Q5. Why is the divergent section of a Venturimeter made significantly longer and with a smaller angle ($5^\circ$ to $7^\circ$) compared to the convergent section ($20^\circ$ to $22^\circ$)?
A) To increase the kinetic energy of the fluid exiting the meter.
B) To minimize the risk of cavitation at the outlet.
C) To prevent boundary layer separation due to the adverse pressure gradient.
D) To maximize the acceleration of the fluid.
Step-by-step Solution:
In the convergent section, the pressure decreases in the direction of flow ($dp/dx < 0$, favorable pressure gradient), keeping the boundary layer attached. In the divergent section, the pressure increases in the direction of flow ($dp/dx > 0$, adverse pressure gradient). If the angle of divergence is larger than $7^\circ$, the fluid lacks sufficient momentum to overcome this adverse gradient, causing the boundary layer to detach (flow separation), which results in massive eddy formations and head loss.

Q1. Statement 1: A Venturimeter is highly reliable for measuring flow rates of fluids carrying suspended solids.
Statement 2: The gradual convergent and divergent sections of a Venturimeter minimize regions of stagnation where solids could settle and clog the pressure taps.
A) Both Statement 1 and Statement 2 are true, and Statement 2 is the correct explanation.
B) Both Statement 1 and Statement 2 are true, but Statement 2 is not the correct explanation.
C) Statement 1 is true, but Statement 2 is false.
D) Both Statement 1 and Statement 2 are false.
Detailed Explanation:
Both statements are false. A Venturimeter is actually *not* recommended for fluids containing suspended solids or slurries. The suspended solids tend to accumulate in the throat and clog the delicate pressure taps, altering the effective area ratio and introducing severe calibration errors. For slurries, electromagnetic flow meters or specialized eccentric orifice plates are used.
Q2. Assertion (A): In a supersonic flow field, a standard Pitot tube will overestimate the local free-stream Mach number if calculated directly using the subsonic isentropic relation.
Reason (R): A detached bow shock wave forms ahead of the Pitot tube in supersonic flow, causing a non-isentropic pressure drop and reducing the measured total stagnation pressure.
A) Both A and R are true, and R is the correct explanation of A.
B) Both A and R are true, but R is not the correct explanation of A.
C) A is false, but R is true.
D) A is true, but R is false.
Detailed Explanation:
The assertion is false, but the reason is true. Because a bow shock wave forms in front of the Pitot tube, the flow undergoes a highly irreversible (non-isentropic) pressure drop across the shock. As a result, the measured total pressure behind the shock ($P_{02}$) is *lower* than the true free-stream total pressure ($P_{01}$). If one naively uses the subsonic isentropic formula, which assumes no shock losses, the lower measured total pressure will lead to an *underestimation* of the free-stream Mach number. To get the correct Mach number, the Rayleigh Pitot formula must be used.
Q3. An Orifice meter is used to measure the flow of water in a pipe of area $A_1$. If the orifice area is $A_0 = 0.5 A_1$ and the discharge coefficient $C_d = 0.60$, what is the actual flow rate when the differential head is $h$?
A) $0.346 A_1 \sqrt{2gh}$
B) $0.150 A_1 \sqrt{2gh}$
C) $0.274 A_1 \sqrt{2gh}$
D) $0.412 A_1 \sqrt{2gh}$
Detailed Explanation:
The actual discharge is:
$$ Q = C_d \frac{A_1 A_0}{\sqrt{A_1^2 - A_0^2}} \sqrt{2gh} $$ Since $A_0 = 0.5 A_1$, substitute this into the equation:
$$ Q = 0.60 \times \frac{A_1 (0.5 A_1)}{\sqrt{A_1^2 - 0.25 A_1^2}} \sqrt{2gh} $$ $$ Q = 0.60 \times \frac{0.5 A_1^2}{\sqrt{0.75 A_1^2}} \sqrt{2gh} $$ $$ Q = 0.60 \times \frac{0.5 A_1}{\sqrt{0.75}} \sqrt{2gh} $$ Note that $\sqrt{0.75} \approx 0.866$.
$$ Q = 0.60 \times \frac{0.5}{0.866} A_1 \sqrt{2gh} = 0.60 \times 0.577 A_1 \sqrt{2gh} \approx 0.346 A_1 \sqrt{2gh} $$ Thus, Option A is the correct answer.
Q4. A aircraft flies at a constant pressure altitude of $15,000\text{ feet}$. During flight, a heating failure causes the Pitot tube nose to freeze over completely (drain hole remains open), while the static port remains perfectly clear. How will the Airspeed Indicator (ASI) react?
A) The ASI will freeze at its current reading.
B) The ASI will register an immediate drop to zero.
C) The ASI will read high as the aircraft climbs.
D) The ASI will oscillate wildly.
Detailed Explanation:
If the Pitot tube nose is blocked by ice but the drain hole remains open, the pressure inside the Pitot line escapes through the open drain hole and vents to the static pressure of the cabin/atmosphere. Since both the Pitot line and static line are now at the same static pressure, the differential pressure across the ASI diaphragm becomes zero. Consequently, the **ASI will drop to zero**.
Q5. An inclined manometer connected to a Venturimeter has its tube tilted at an angle of $30^\circ$ to the horizontal. If the actual deflection along the inclined tube is $200\text{ mm}$, and the manometer fluid is mercury ($S=13.6$) measuring water flow, what is the value of the piezometric head difference $h$?
A) $1.26\text{ m}$
B) $2.52\text{ m}$
C) $1.09\text{ m}$
D) $1.89\text{ m}$
Detailed Explanation:
The vertical deflection $y$ of the manometer fluid is related to the inclined deflection $L$ by:
$$ y = L \sin\theta $$ Given:
- $L = 200\text{ mm} = 0.2\text{ m}$
- $\theta = 30^\circ \implies \sin(30^\circ) = 0.5$
$$ y = 0.2 \times 0.5 = 0.1\text{ m} $$ Now calculate the head $h$:
$$ h = y \left( \frac{S_m}{S_f} - 1 \right) = 0.1 \left( \frac{13.6}{1.0} - 1 \right) = 0.1 \times 12.6 = 1.26\text{ m} $$
Q6. Which of the following statements is mathematically true regarding the Coefficient of Contraction ($C_c$) of an orifice meter?
A) It is always greater than $1.0$ because the flow jet expands after the orifice.
B) It is defined as the ratio of orifice area to the vena contracta area.
C) It is always less than $1.0$ and represents the physical contraction of the fluid jet down to the vena contracta.
D) It is independent of the Reynolds number of the flow.
Detailed Explanation:
The coefficient of contraction is $C_c = \frac{A_c}{A_0}$, where $A_c$ is the cross-sectional area of the jet at the vena contracta and $A_0$ is the area of the orifice. Because the jet must contract as streamlines converge, $A_c < A_0$, meaning $C_c < 1.0$ (typically around $0.62$ for sharp-edged circular orifices).
Q7. The altimeter of an aircraft with a blocked static port will read:
A) Higher than actual altitude in a climb.
B) Lower than actual altitude in a descent.
C) Exactly zero.
D) Constant, indicating the altitude at which the blockage occurred.
Detailed Explanation:
Because the static port is blocked, the pressure inside the altimeter case is completely trapped and cannot change. The altimeter aneroid capsule responds only to pressure changes. With no pressure change possible, the instrument stays frozen and continues to display the altitude where the blockage first took place.
Q8. For high precision in wind tunnel testing, micro-manometers are filled with low-density fluids (like alcohol, $S = 0.8$) rather than mercury. Why?
A) Alcohol has lower vapor pressure and will not cavitate.
B) A lower-density fluid yields a larger manometer deflection for the same small pressure difference, improving measurement resolution.
C) Alcohol is less viscous and reacts faster.
D) Mercury reacts chemically with wind tunnel wall materials.
Detailed Explanation:
The deflection of a manometer is given by $y = \frac{h}{(S_m/S_f - 1)}$. If the manometer fluid has a very low specific gravity $S_m$ (closer to the flowing fluid's density), a very tiny pressure head change $h$ will generate a much larger physical deflection $y$. This amplification of deflection makes it much easier to read small pressure differences accurately, significantly improving measurement sensitivity.
Q9. Statement 1: In a subsonic compressible flow, the dynamic pressure measured by a Pitot-static tube is larger than $\frac{1}{2}\rho V^2$.
Statement 2: The deceleration of a gas as it approaches the stagnation point compresses the fluid, which increases its local density and temperature, resulting in a higher stagnation pressure.
A) Both Statement 1 and Statement 2 are true, and Statement 2 is the correct explanation of Statement 1.
B) Both Statement 1 and Statement 2 are true, but Statement 2 is not the correct explanation of Statement 1.
C) Statement 1 is true, but Statement 2 is false.
D) Statement 1 is false, but Statement 2 is true.
Detailed Explanation:
Under compressible conditions (typically above Mach $0.3$), the fluid cannot be treated as a constant density medium. As the flow decelerates isentropically to zero velocity at the stagnation point, it is compressed, increasing both its density and temperature. This compression adds a compressibility term to the stagnation pressure, making $P_0 - P_{\infty} = \frac{1}{2}\rho V^2 (1 + \frac{M^2}{4} + \dots)$, which is indeed larger than the incompressible value.
Q10. What is the effect of an alternate static source (often located inside the unpressurized aircraft cabin) being opened during flight?
A) The Altimeter will read lower, and the ASI will read lower.
B) The Altimeter will read higher, and the ASI will read higher.
C) The Altimeter will read lower, and the ASI will read higher.
D) The Altimeter will read higher, and the ASI will read lower.
Detailed Explanation:
Due to the Venturi effect of air flowing around the aircraft cabin, the static pressure *inside* the unpressurized aircraft cabin is slightly lower than the actual ambient atmospheric static pressure outside. When the alternate static source is opened, the instruments are exposed to this lower cabin pressure: - **Altimeter:** Since the measured static pressure is lower, the altimeter interprets this as a higher altitude and reads **higher than actual**. - **Airspeed Indicator (ASI):** Since $\Delta P = P_0 - P_{s,\text{cabin}}$ and $P_{s,\text{cabin}}$ is lower than the true external static pressure, the measured differential pressure increases, causing the ASI to read **higher than actual**.

8. Error Analysis & Common Traps (5 min)

To succeed in the UPSC Air Safety Officer exam, candidates must avoid several highly recurrent conceptual traps:

  1. Piezometric Head vs. Elevation Confusion: Many students mistakenly subtract or add the elevation difference ($z_1 - z_2$) when calculating the head $h$ from a manometer deflection in an inclined Venturimeter. Remember: **The manometer deflection $y$ automatically accounts for elevation differences.** The formula $h = y(\frac{S_m}{S_f} - 1)$ gives the difference in the *piezometric head* ($P/\rho g + z$), not just static pressure head. Do not modify it for inclined pipes!
  2. Vapor Pressure and Cavitation: When checking for cavitation at the throat, always work in absolute pressures rather than gauge pressures. Vapor pressure of water is given in absolute terms (e.g., $2.34\text{ kPa (abs)}$ at $20^\circ\text{C}$). If you mistakenly compare gauge throat pressure directly to absolute vapor pressure without adding atmospheric pressure ($101.3\text{ kPa}$), your calculations will be completely incorrect.
  3. The $C_d$ Definition Mix-up: In orifice meters, students often confuse $C_d$, $C_c$, and $C_v$. Remember: $C_d = C_c \times C_v$. Ensure you do not substitute $C_c$ in place of $C_d$ in the main flow rate equation.
  4. Compressibility Limits: Do not use the simple incompressible formula $V = \sqrt{2\Delta P / \rho}$ for airspeeds above $200\text{ knots}$ (or Mach $0.3$). In these regimes, the density changes significantly, and you must apply the compressible binomial expansion terms.
