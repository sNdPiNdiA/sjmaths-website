Back-work ratio

Textbook-grade UPSC Air Safety Officer deep-dive: Comprehensive theoretical foundations, analytical derivations, engineering reference data, DGCA/ICAO airworthiness integration, 14 practice MCQs, and timed mini-test.

How This Topic Is Useful for Air Safety Officers & Aircraft Operations
DGCA CAR & ICAO Mandate

In aviation operations and UPSC ASO assessments, this parameter is critical across 4 core operational pillars:

  • 1. Aircraft Systems & Flight Deck Monitoring: Cockpit Engine Indicating and Crew Alertting System (EICAS) monitors Interstage Turbine Temperature (ITT) and Core Speed ($N_2$) to detect compressor degradation altering the back-work ratio.
  • 2. Flight Safety & Dispatch Envelopes: An abnormally high back-work ratio due to blade fouling reduces surge margin, directly restricting maximum takeoff gross weight and hot-day dispatch profiles.
  • 3. DGCA CAR & ICAO Airworthiness Compliance: DGCA CAR Section 2 Series 'B' Part VII requires strict adherence to engine performance trend monitoring to detect shifts in internal work distribution before catastrophic stalls.
  • 4. Air Safety Investigation & Incident Prevention: Air Safety Officers analyze FDR compressor discharge pressures and fuel flow records during uncontained engine failures to check if back-work ratio instability triggered stall/surge.

Pillar 1: Formal Technical Definition & Nomenclature

Back-work ratio ($r_{bw}$) is defined as the ratio of compressor work input to turbine work output in a gas turbine engine: $$r_{bw} = \frac{W_c}{W_t}$$
  • Physical Meaning: It measures the fraction of turbine power consumed internally by the compressor to sustain gas generation.
  • Governing Law: Governed by the First and Second Laws of Thermodynamics applied to open steady-flow gas turbine control volumes.
  • Continuum Boundary: Valid across all continuum fluid domains from subsonic compressors through hypersonic turbine staging.

Nomenclature & Symbols

  • $W_c$ = Compressor work input per unit mass ($kJ/kg$)
  • $W_t$ = Turbine work output per unit mass ($kJ/kg$)
  • $r_{bw}$ = Back-work ratio (Dimensionless)
  • $T_1, T_2$ = Compressor inlet and exit temperatures ($K$)
  • $T_3, T_4$ = Turbine inlet and exit temperatures ($K$)
  • $c_p$ = Specific heat at constant pressure ($kJ/(kg\cdot K)$)

Pillar 2: Microscopic Physics & Continuum Mechanics

1. Molecular Kinetic State: Working fluid molecules experience rapid momentum exchange and temperature rises as mechanical shaft work is converted into fluid enthalpy within compressor blades.
2. Continuum Forces & Gradients: Pressure gradients build continuously against adverse static pressures, creating boundary layer diffusion challenges across multi-stage compressor blades.
3. Aerodynamic Consequence: A high back-work ratio means a major portion of gross turbine power is instantly re-routed internally, leaving low net thrust power margin.

Pillar 3: Mathematical Derivations & Governing Laws

Step 1: Governing Relation

Compressor and turbine work are expressed via enthalpy changes assuming constant specific heats.

$$W_c = h_2 - h_1 = c_p (T_2 - T_1), \quad W_t = h_3 - h_4 = c_p (T_3 - T_4)$$
Step 2: Boundary / Thermodynamic Conditions

Substitute work equations into the back-work ratio definition formula.

$$r_{bw} = \frac{c_p (T_2 - T_1)}{c_p (T_3 - T_4)} = \frac{T_2 - T_1}{T_3 - T_4}$$
Step 3: Integration & Substitution

Introduce compressor pressure ratio $\pi_c$ and turbine expansion ratio $\pi_t$ assuming isentropic efficiency.

$$T_2 = T_1 \left[ 1 + \frac{\pi_c^{\frac{\gamma-1}{\gamma}} - 1}{\eta_c} \right]$$
Step 4: Final Working Law

Express back-work ratio in terms of ambient temperature and turbine inlet temperature limits.

$$r_{bw} = \frac{T_1 \left( \pi_c^{\frac{\gamma-1}{\gamma}} - 1 \right)}{T_3 \left( 1 - \frac{1}{\pi_t^{\frac{\gamma-1}{\gamma}}} \right)}$$

Pillar 4: Dimensional Analysis, SI/CGS Units & Conversion Factors

  • Fundamental Dimensions: Dimensionless ratio $[M^0 L^0 T^0 \theta^0]$ as it represents a ratio of specific energies.
  • SI Unit: Dimensionless (Ratio of $kJ/kg$ to $kJ/kg$).
  • CGS Unit: Dimensionless (Ratio of $erg/g$ to $erg/g$).
  • Aeronautical & Imperial Equivalents: Ratio of $BTU/lb$ to $BTU/lb$, remaining strictly dimensionless across all aviation engineering systems.

Pillar 6: Aircraft Airworthiness, Systems & DGCA CAR / ICAO Compliance

  • Regulatory Standards (DGCA CAR & ICAO): Compliance with DGCA CAR Section 2 and ICAO Annex 8 Airworthiness of Aircraft mandates continuous performance monitoring of thermodynamic cycle parameters.
  • Critical Aircraft Systems: Directly impacts FADEC scheduling, bleed air extraction limits, compressor variable stator vane (VSV) actuation, and surge bleed valve timing.

Pillar 7: High-Frequency UPSC Examiner Traps & Misconceptions

Trap 1: Back-Work Ratio Equals Thermal Efficiency

❌ Examiner Trap: Assuming $r_{bw}$ is the complement of thermal efficiency or equal to turbine work minus compressor work divided by fuel input.

✅ UPSC Truth: Back-work ratio is strictly $W_c/W_t$, representing internal power parasitism, whereas thermal efficiency is net work divided by heat added.

Trap 2: Steam Turbines Have High Back-Work Ratios

❌ Examiner Trap: Assuming steam power plants and gas turbines share similar back-work ratios because both use fluid expansion.

✅ UPSC Truth: Steam power plants pump incompressible liquid water requiring minimal work, resulting in a tiny back-work ratio ($<0.01$), whereas gas turbines compress high-volume air ($r_{bw} = 0.4 - 0.7$).

Trap 3: Increasing Pressure Ratio Decreases Back-Work Ratio

❌ Examiner Trap: Believing higher compressor pressure ratios reduce the back-work ratio to save turbine power.

✅ UPSC Truth: Increasing pressure ratio $\pi_c$ increases compressor work significantly faster than turbine work, thereby increasing the back-work ratio.

Trap 4: Back-Work Ratio Is Dimensionless Energy

❌ Examiner Trap: Thinking back-work ratio has units of $kJ/kg$ because it deals with specific enthalpies.

✅ UPSC Truth: Being a ratio of specific works, the units cancel out completely, making it strictly dimensionless.

Pillar 8: Step-by-Step Worked Aircraft Numerical Problem

Realistic Aircraft Scenario

A gas turbine engine operates with a compressor inlet temperature of $300\text{ K}$ and an exit temperature of $520\text{ K}$. The turbine inlet temperature is $1400\text{ K}$ and its exit temperature is $950\text{ K}$. Assuming constant specific heat, calculate the back-work ratio.

Given Flight Parameters:
$T_1 = 300\text{ K}, \quad T_2 = 520\text{ K}, \quad T_3 = 1400\text{ K}, \quad T_4 = 950\text{ K}$
Governing Formula:
$$r_{bw} = \frac{T_2 - T_1}{T_3 - T_4}$$
Step-by-Step Analytical Solution:
Step 1: Compressor Work Parameter

Calculate temperature rise across the compressor.

$$T_2 - T_1 = 520 - 300 = 220\text{ K}$$
Step 2: Turbine Work Parameter

Calculate temperature drop across the turbine.

$$T_3 - T_4 = 1400 - 950 = 450\text{ K}$$
Step 3: Ratio Computation

Divide compressor work parameter by turbine work parameter.

$$r_{bw} = \frac{220}{450} = 0.4889$$
Final Answer: $0.4889 (or 48.89%)$
Air Safety Officer Operational Takeaway:

An engine operating with a back-work ratio of nearly 49% requires robust turbine blade cooling technology to ensure safe operation without material failure during maximum thrust operations.

Question 1 • conceptual
What does a high back-work ratio signify in a gas turbine engine?
A A large fraction of turbine power is consumed by the compressor
B The engine has achieved 100 percent thermal efficiency
C The compressor requires zero shaft power input
D The turbine work output is lower than compressor work output
Solution & Technical Rationale:
A high back-work ratio means a significant portion of the work produced by the turbine is consumed internally by the compressor to sustain the cycle.
Question 2 • conceptual
Why is the back-work ratio in gas turbines significantly higher than in vapor power cycles (Rankine cycle)?
A Gas turbines compress a high-specific-volume gas whereas Rankine cycles pump an incompressible liquid
B Gas turbines operate at lower turbine inlet temperatures
C Steam turbines have zero mechanical friction
D Gas turbines use centrifugal pumps instead of axial compressors
Solution & Technical Rationale:
Compressing a gas requires substantially more specific work than pumping an incompressible liquid water, leading to much higher back-work ratios in gas turbines.
Question 3 • numerical
In a gas turbine cycle, $T_1 = 288\text{ K}$, $T_2 = 550\text{ K}$, $T_3 = 1350\text{ K}$, and $T_4 = 900\text{ K}$. What is the back-work ratio?
A 0.582
B 0.425
C 0.650
D 0.385
Solution & Technical Rationale:
Using $r_{bw} = (T_2 - T_1) / (T_3 - T_4) = (550 - 288) / (1350 - 900) = 262 / 450 = 0.5822$.
Question 4 • statement
Consider the following statements regarding back-work ratio: 1. It increases with an increase in compressor pressure ratio. 2. It decreases with an increase in turbine inlet temperature. Which of the statements given above is/are correct?
A Both 1 and 2
B 1 only
C 2 only
D Neither 1 nor 2
Solution & Technical Rationale:
Both statements are correct. Higher pressure ratios increase compressor work demand, and higher turbine inlet temperatures increase turbine work output, reducing the ratio.
Question 5 • conceptual
What is the primary operational consequence of compressor blade fouling on the back-work ratio?
A Increases back-work ratio due to reduced compressor efficiency
B Decreases back-work ratio by improving mass flow
C Has zero impact on internal engine work distribution
D Eliminates compressor surge entirely
Solution & Technical Rationale:
Compressor fouling degrades aerodynamic efficiency, requiring more work input for the same pressure rise, thereby increasing the back-work ratio.
Question 6 • assertion-reason
Assertion (A): Gas turbine engines require advanced turbine blade cooling. Reason (R): High back-work ratios demand high turbine inlet temperatures to maintain a positive net power output.
A Both (A) and (R) are true and (R) is the correct explanation of (A)
B Both (A) and (R) are true but (R) is not the correct explanation of (A)
C (A) is true but (R) is false
D (A) is false but (R) is true
Solution & Technical Rationale:
Because gas turbines consume 40-60% of turbine work in the compressor, high turbine inlet temperatures are essential to produce useful net thrust, necessitating advanced cooling.
Question 7 • numerical
A compressor consumes $300\text{ kJ/kg}$ of work, and the turbine produces $650\text{ kJ/kg}$ of gross work. What is the back-work ratio?
A 0.4615
B 2.166
C 0.5385
D 0.3500
Solution & Technical Rationale:
$r_{bw} = W_c / W_t = 300 / 650 = 0.4615$.
Question 8 • statement
Which of the following methods effectively reduces the back-work ratio in gas turbine power plants? 1. Intercooling during compression. 2. Reheating during expansion. 3. Regenerative heat exchange. Which are correct?
A 1 and 2 only
B 1 only
C 1, 2 and 3
D 2 and 3 only
Solution & Technical Rationale:
Intercooling reduces compressor work input, and reheating increases turbine work output. Both actions reduce the back-work ratio.
Question 9 • conceptual
How does ambient temperature variation (e.g., hot day vs. standard day) affect the back-work ratio?
A Higher ambient temperature increases compressor inlet temperature, raising the back-work ratio
B Higher ambient temperature decreases the back-work ratio
C Ambient temperature has no effect on internal engine work
D It only affects turbine expansion ratios
Solution & Technical Rationale:
Higher ambient temperature increases inlet air temperature $T_1$, raising compressor work $W_c = c_p(T_2 - T_1)$, which increases the back-work ratio.
Question 10 • numerical
An aircraft engine has a compressor work input of $350\text{ kJ/kg}$ and a net work output of $400\text{ kJ/kg}$. What is the turbine work output?
A $750\text{ kJ/kg}$
B $350\text{ kJ/kg}$
C $400\text{ kJ/kg}$
D $50\text{ kJ/kg}$
Solution & Technical Rationale:
$W_{net} = W_t - W_c \implies W_t = W_{net} + W_c = 400 + 350 = 750\text{ kJ/kg}$.
Question 11 • conceptual
In the context of DGCA Air Safety Officer audits, why is tracking engine back-work ratio critical for airworthiness?
A It serves as an early indicator of mechanical degradation, compressor stall vulnerability, and efficiency loss
B It determines landing gear hydraulic pressure limits
C It dictates cabin pressurization outflow valve settings
D It regulates fuel tank inerting system schedules
Solution & Technical Rationale:
Shifts in back-work ratio indicate internal aerodynamic degradation, alerting engineers to impending surge or performance loss before failure occurs.
Question 12 • statement
Statement I: The back-work ratio in modern turbofan engines typically ranges between 0.50 and 0.70. Statement II: High bypass ratios eliminate the core engine compressor entirely.
A Statement I is correct but Statement II is incorrect
B Both Statement I and Statement II are correct
C Statement I is incorrect but Statement II is correct
D Both Statement I and Statement II are incorrect
Solution & Technical Rationale:
Statement I is correct. Statement II is incorrect because high bypass turbofans still require a core compressor to drive the core gas generator.

If a gas turbine has $W_c = 400\text{ kJ/kg}$ and $W_t = 800\text{ kJ/kg}$, what is the back-work ratio and net work?
A $r_{bw} = 0.50$, $W_{net} = 400\text{ kJ/kg}$

D $r_{bw} = 0.25$, $W_{net} = 400\text{ kJ/kg}$
Rationale:
$r_{bw} = 400/800 = 0.50$ and $W_{net} = 800 - 400 = 400\text{ kJ/kg}$.
Question 3 of 10 +3 / -1
Which thermodynamic process reduces compressor work input and consequently lowers the back-work ratio?
A Intercooling
B Reheating
C Regeneration
D Throttling
Rationale:
Intercooling divides compression into stages with cooling in between, reducing specific work input.
Question 4 of 10 +3 / -1
Why are back-work ratios high in gas turbine engines compared to steam power plants?
A Gases undergo large specific volume changes during compression
B Steam turbines operate at supersonic speeds
C Gas turbines have zero mechanical efficiency

Rationale:
Compressing gases requires significant energy compared to pumping incompressible liquids.
Question 5 of 10 +3 / -1
What happens to the back-work ratio when compressor isentropic efficiency decreases due to blade erosion?
A Increases
B Decreases
C Remains unchanged
D Drops to zero
Rationale:
Lower compressor efficiency requires more work input for the same pressure ratio, raising $r_{bw}$.
Question 6 of 10 +3 / -1
In an aircraft gas turbine, if $T_1 = 300\text{ K}$, $T_2 = 600\text{ K}$, $T_3 = 1500\text{ K}$, and $T_4 = 1000\text{ K}$, calculate $r_{bw}$.
A 0.60
B 0.50
C 0.75
D 0.40
Rationale:
$r_{bw} = (600 - 300) / (1500 - 1000) = 300 / 500 = 0.60$.
Question 7 of 10 +3 / -1
How does raising the turbine inlet temperature ($T_3$) affect the back-work ratio?
A Decreases the back-work ratio
B Increases the back-work ratio
C Has no effect on back-work ratio
D Causes the back-work ratio to exceed 1.0
Rationale:
Raising $T_3$ increases turbine work output ($W_t$), which appears in the denominator of $r_{bw} = W_c / W_t$, thereby decreasing the ratio.
Question 8 of 10 +3 / -1
Which DGCA airworthiness guideline governs engine performance trend monitoring to detect internal cycle anomalies?
A DGCA CAR Section 2 Series 'B'
B ICAO Annex 1 only
C CAR Section 4 Aircraft Accident Investigation

DGCA CAR Section 2 sets airworthiness requirements including engine condition monitoring.
Question 9 of 10 +3 / -1
What is the physical significance of a back-work ratio approaching 1.0?
A Net work output approaches zero, meaning the engine cannot sustain itself
B Engine thermal efficiency reaches 100 percent
C Turbine work output equals compressor work output multiplied by two
D Thrust production is maximized
Rationale:
If $r_{bw} = 1.0$, $W_c = W_t$, meaning $W_{net} = 0$, so the engine produces no net power.

A Both 1 and 2
B 1 only

D Neither 1 nor 2
Rationale:
Both statements are correct; $r_{bw}$ is dimensionless and increases with compressor pressure ratio.
