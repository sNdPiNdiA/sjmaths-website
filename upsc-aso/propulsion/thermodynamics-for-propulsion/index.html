Thermodynamics for Propulsion

Comprehensive textbook-grade reference covering the first and second laws, property relations (enthalpy, entropy, specific heats), ideal-gas equations, isentropic gas dynamics, and nozzle/diffuser thermodynamic analysis for aviation safety engineering.

1 First & Second Laws of Thermodynamics

Fundamental Principles
1. Formal Technical Definition
The First Law of Thermodynamics is the principle of conservation of energy applied to thermodynamic systems, asserting that energy can neither be created nor destroyed, only transformed. The Second Law establishes the direction of spontaneous processes, stating that the total entropy of an isolated system can never decrease over time ($\Delta S_{univ} \ge 0$) and imposes absolute limits on the thermal efficiency of heat engines.
2. Basic Concept & Physical Mechanism

At the continuum level, a gas turbine engine operates as an open thermodynamic system (control volume) through which air and fuel continuously flow. The First Law accounts for the balance between heat added via combustion, work extracted by the turbine and consumed by the compressor, and changes in enthalpy and kinetic energy. At the molecular level, temperature is a measure of translational and rotational kinetic energy, while pressure is molecular momentum flux against boundary walls.

The Second Law introduces irreversibilities—such as viscous boundary layer friction, shock wave losses, and incomplete combustion. These viscous dissipation mechanisms degrade useful mechanical work into random thermal motion, permanently increasing entropy and diminishing turbine engine polytropic efficiency.

3. Mathematical Derivation & Equations

For an open steady-flow control volume (e.g., combustor, turbine stage), the First Law (Conservation of Energy) is expressed in rate form:

$$\dot{Q} - \dot{W}_c = \sum_{out} \dot{m} \left( h + \frac{V^2}{2} + gz \right) - \sum_{in} \dot{m} \left( h + \frac{V^2}{2} + gz \right)$$

Neglecting potential energy changes ($gz$) and shaft work ($\dot{W}_c = 0$) across stationary flow passages like diffusers and nozzles:

$$h_{in} + \frac{V_{in}^2}{2} = h_{out} + \frac{V_{out}^2}{2} = h_t = \text{constant}$$

The Second Law for an adiabatic control volume states:

$$\dot{S}_{gen} = \sum_{out} \dot{m} s - \sum_{in} \dot{m} s \ge 0 \implies s_{out} \ge s_{in}$$

Where $\dot{m}$ is mass flow rate (kg/s), $h$ is specific enthalpy (J/kg), $V$ is flow velocity (m/s), $s$ is specific entropy (J/kg·K), and $\dot{S}_{gen}$ is entropy generation rate (W/K).

6. Aircraft Application & Air Safety Officer Connection

In turbofan engines (e.g., CFM LEAP, Pratt & Whitney GTF), the First Law governs the thermal energy release in the combustor (burning Jet A-1) and work extraction across the high-pressure turbine (HPT) which drives the compressor. The Second Law dictates that blade cooling losses, tip clearance leakage, and boundary layer separation in the turbine produce unrecoverable entropy, lowering overall thermal efficiency and reducing aircraft range.

DGCA Air Safety Connection: Under DGCA CAR Section 2 (Airworthiness), engine performance degradation monitoring is mandatory. Exceeding Exhaust Gas Temperature (EGT) margins due to internal component degradation (increased entropy generation from blade erosion) violates Type Certificate data sheets, requiring immediate shop-visit overhaul to prevent catastrophic uncontained turbine failure (Reference: AAIB India engine failure investigations).

7. UPSC Traps & Common Misconceptions
⚠️ UPSC Examination Trap

UPSC often tricks candidates by stating that "entropy of an aircraft engine control volume decreases because air is cooled in a bypass duct." Remember: The Second Law applies strictly to the isolated universe ($\Delta S_{univ} \ge 0$). While local entropy of a working fluid can decrease via heat rejection to the ambient atmosphere, the corresponding entropy increase of the surroundings always exceeds the local decrease.

⚙️ Step-by-Step Worked Numerical: Combustor Energy Balance

Given: Air enters a gas turbine combustor at $T_3 = 800\text{ K}$, $h_3 = 822.1\text{ kJ/kg}$, with air mass flow rate $\dot{m}_a = 50\text{ kg/s}$. Jet A-1 fuel enters at $T_{fuel} = 298\text{ K}$ with mass flow rate $\dot{m}_f = 1.2\text{ kg/s}$ and Lower Heating Value $\text{LHV} = 43,200\text{ kJ/kg}$. Combustion efficiency $\eta_b = 0.98$.

Step 1: Apply Energy Balance to Combustor

$$\dot{m}_a h_3 + \dot{m}_f \cdot \text{LHV} \cdot \eta_b = (\dot{m}_a + \dot{m}_f) h_4$$

Step 2: Substitute Values

$$(50 \times 822.1) + (1.2 \times 43200 \times 0.98) = 51.2 \times h_4$$ $$41,105 + 50,803.2 = 51.2 h_4 \implies 91,908.2 = 51.2 h_4$$ $$h_4 = 1,795.08 \text{ kJ/kg}$$

Step 3: Operational Takeaway

The resulting turbine inlet enthalpy ($h_4$) corresponds to a Turbine Inlet Temperature (TIT) of approximately $1,450\text{ K}$. A/S officers must verify that fuel metering units (FMU) maintain precise fuel-air ratios to prevent exceeding metallurgical limits of single-crystal nickel superalloy turbine blades.

2 Enthalpy & Entropy

Thermodynamic Properties
1. Formal Technical Definition
Enthalpy ($H$) is a thermodynamic state function defined as the sum of internal energy ($U$) and the product of pressure and volume ($PV$), representing total thermal energy content including flow work. Entropy ($S$) is a measure of thermal energy unavailability per unit temperature, quantifying the degree of microscopic disorder and irreversibility in a system.
2. Basic Concept & Physical Mechanism

Enthalpy is indispensable in propulsion because open-flow devices (compressors, burners, turbines, nozzles) operate under constant pressure boundaries or steady flow where flow work ($P\nu$) is vital. Stagnation (total) enthalpy combines internal thermal energy and kinetic energy of the flow.

Entropy measures the degradation of energy quality. In an ideal reversible adiabatic (isentropic) compression process, entropy remains constant ($\Delta s = 0$). In real gas turbine compressors, boundary layer friction, shock waves, and turbulence generate entropy, shifting the state away from the ideal isentropic line on a $T$-$s$ diagram and requiring more specific work input than theoretically predicted.

3. Mathematical Derivation & Equations

The differential change in specific enthalpy for an ideal gas with constant specific heats is:

$$dh = C_p dT$$

Integrating the Gibbs equation for specific entropy change yields the ideal gas entropy relations:

$$ds = C_p \frac{dT}{T} - R \frac{dP}{P}$$ $$s_2 - s_1 = C_p \ln\left(\frac{T_2}{T_1}\right) - R \ln\left(\frac{P_2}{P_1}\right)$$

For an isentropic process ($ds = 0$), this integrates into the familiar polytropic/isentropic relations:

$$\frac{T_2}{T_1} = \left(\frac{P_2}{P_1}\right)^{\frac{\gamma-1}{\gamma}}$$

7. UPSC Traps & Common Misconceptions
⚠️ UPSC Examination Trap

Candidates often confuse adiabatic with isentropic. An adiabatic process involves zero heat transfer ($\dot{Q}=0$), but if friction is present, it is irreversible and entropy increases ($\Delta s > 0$). An isentropic process must be both adiabatic and internally reversible ($\Delta s = 0$).

⚙️ Step-by-Step Worked Numerical: Entropy Change Across a Diffuser

Given: Air enters an aircraft engine supersonic diffuser at $P_1 = 25\text{ kPa}$, $T_1 = 220\text{ K}$, and exits at $P_2 = 120\text{ kPa}$, $T_2 = 310\text{ K}$. Given $C_p = 1005\text{ J/(kg}\cdot\text{K)}$ and $R = 287\text{ J/(kg}\cdot\text{K)}$. Calculate the specific entropy change.

Step 1: Apply Entropy Change Formula

$$\Delta s = C_p \ln\left(\frac{T_2}{T_1}\right) - R \ln\left(\frac{P_2}{P_1}\right)$$

Step 2: Substitute Values

$$\Delta s = 1005 \ln\left(\frac{310}{220}\right) - 287 \ln\left(\frac{120}{25}\right)$$ $$\Delta s = 1005 (0.3442) - 287 (1.5686) = 345.92 - 450.19 = -104.27 \text{ J/(kg}\cdot\text{K)}$$

Step 3: Operational Takeaway

Wait! The calculated $\Delta s$ is negative, which violates the Second Law ($\Delta s \ge 0$). This proves that the assumed exit pressure and temperature combination is thermodynamically impossible for adiabatic flow, or shock losses were omitted. A/S officers must spot physically impossible sensor telemetry in flight data reconstruction.

3 Specific Heats ($C_p, C_v$) & Ratio ($\gamma$)

Thermal Properties
1. Formal Technical Definition
Specific heat at constant volume ($C_v$) is the rate of change of internal energy with respect to temperature ($dU/dT$), whereas specific heat at constant pressure ($C_p$) is the rate of change of enthalpy with respect to temperature ($dH/dT$). Their ratio, $\gamma = C_p / C_v$, is the adiabatic index governing compressible gas dynamics and sonic velocity.
2. Basic Concept & Physical Mechanism

Molecular degrees of freedom dictate specific heats. Monatomic gases (helium) have 3 translational degrees of freedom ($\gamma = 1.67$). Diatomic gases (air: $\text{N}_2, \text{O}_2$) possess translational and rotational degrees of freedom, giving $\gamma \approx 1.40$ at standard ambient temperatures. At high turbine combustion temperatures ($>1200\text{ K}$), molecular vibration modes become active, causing $C_p$ to increase and $\gamma$ to drop toward $1.33$.

In propulsion cycle analysis, assuming constant $\gamma = 1.4$ throughout the entire engine is a major simplification; accurate performance codes use temperature-dependent specific heats.

3. Mathematical Derivation & Equations

For an ideal gas, the relationship between specific heats and the universal gas constant ($R$) is Mayer's relation:

$$C_p - C_v = R$$

The specific heat ratio is defined as:

$$\gamma = \frac{C_p}{C_v}$$

Combining these yields explicit expressions for $C_p$ and $C_v$ in terms of $\gamma$ and $R$:

$$C_p = \frac{\gamma R}{\gamma - 1}, \quad C_v = \frac{R}{\gamma - 1}$$

7. UPSC Traps & Common Misconceptions
⚠️ UPSC Examination Trap

UPSC questions frequently ask whether $C_p$ and $C_v$ are constant for all gases. Candidates must remember that for real gases (and even ideal gases at extreme temperatures), $C_p$ is a function of temperature ($T$). Only for a strictly "cold air standard" assumption are they treated as constant.

⚙️ Step-by-Step Worked Numerical: Calculating $C_p$ and $C_v$ for Turbine Gases

Given: Turbine combustion gases have a gas constant $R = 287\text{ J/(kg}\cdot\text{K)}$ and an effective specific heat ratio $\gamma = 1.33$. Calculate $C_p$ and $C_v$.

Step 1: Compute $C_p$

$$C_p = \frac{\gamma R}{\gamma - 1} = \frac{1.33 \times 287}{1.33 - 1} = \frac{381.71}{0.33} = 1,156.7 \text{ J/(kg}\cdot\text{K)}$$

Step 2: Compute $C_v$

$$C_v = \frac{R}{\gamma - 1} = \frac{287}{0.33} = 869.7 \text{ J/(kg}\cdot\text{K)}$$

Step 3: Operational Takeaway

Higher $C_p$ in combustion gases means more thermal energy is required to raise the temperature by $1\text{ K}$, which directly impacts turbine blade thermal stress modeling.

4 Ideal-Gas Equations of State

Gas Laws
1. Formal Technical Definition
The Ideal Gas Law is an equation of state relating absolute pressure ($P$), specific volume ($\nu$) or density ($\rho$), absolute temperature ($T$), and the specific gas constant ($R$), expressed as $P\nu = RT$ or $P = \rho RT$, valid under conditions of low density where intermolecular forces and molecular volume are negligible.
2. Basic Concept & Physical Mechanism

At the continuum level, the ideal gas model assumes that gas molecules are point masses experiencing perfectly elastic collisions with no intermolecular potential energy. In aviation, air behaves as an ideal gas up to altitudes of $80\text{ km}$ and moderate pressures. However, in high-pressure compressor stages (where delivery pressures exceed $40\text{ atm}$), compressibility factor ($Z = P\nu/RT$) deviates from 1.0, requiring real-gas corrections (e.g., Van der Waals or Redlich-Kwong equations).

3. Mathematical Derivation & Equations

Derived from kinetic theory of gases, the universal gas law is:

$$P V = m R_{specific} T = n R_u T$$

Where $R_u = 8.314\text{ kJ/(kmol}\cdot\text{K)}$ is the universal gas constant, and $R_{specific} = R_u / M_{molar}$ (for air, $M_{molar} = 28.97\text{ kg/kmol}$, giving $R = 287.05\text{ J/(kg}\cdot\text{K)}$).

Air Data Computers (ADC) rely entirely on ideal-gas relations and the compressible Bernoulli equation to compute calibrated airspeed (CAS), true airspeed (TAS), and Mach number from pitot-static pressure inputs. Erroneous pressure inputs due to pitot probe blockage (e.g., ice crystals) corrupt ADC calculations.

DGCA Air Safety Connection: Pitot-static icing incidents (such as Air France 447 or historical narrow-body incidents in India) highlight how incorrect air density and pressure assumptions feed erroneous data into Flight Management Systems (FMS), leading to autopilot disconnects and spatial disorientation.

7. UPSC Traps & Common Misconceptions
⚠️ UPSC Examination Trap

UPSC questions frequently test gauge pressure vs. absolute pressure. The Ideal Gas Law requires absolute pressure ($P$) and absolute temperature ($T$ in Kelvin). Using gauge pressure ($P_{gauge}$) or Celsius temperature ($^\circ\text{C}$) results in catastrophic calculation errors.

⚙️ Step-by-Step Worked Numerical: High Altitude Air Density Calculation

Given: An aircraft cruises at an altitude where static pressure is $P = 30\text{ kPa}$ and ambient temperature is $T = 225\text{ K}$. Given $R = 287\text{ J/(kg}\cdot\text{K)}$. Calculate air density ($\rho$).

Step 1: Rearrange Ideal Gas Law

$$\rho = \frac{P}{RT}$$

Step 2: Substitute Values (convert kPa to Pa)

$$P = 30,000 \text{ Pa}$$ $$\rho = \frac{30,000}{287 \times 225} = \frac{30,000}{64,575} = 0.4646 \text{ kg/m}^3$$

Step 3: Operational Takeaway

Lower air density at cruise altitude directly reduces engine mass flow rate ($\dot{m} = \rho A V$), reducing maximum available thrust and requiring careful climb scheduling.

5 Isentropic Processes & Gas Dynamics

Compressible Flow
1. Formal Technical Definition
An isentropic process is a reversible, adiabatic thermodynamic process during which entropy remains constant ($ds = 0$). In compressible aerodynamics, isentropic relations connect static properties to total (stagnation) properties as a function of Mach number ($M$) and specific heat ratio ($\gamma$).
2. Basic Concept & Physical Mechanism

Stagnation properties represent the state a fluid would reach if brought to rest isentropically. In gas turbine inlets, diffusers, and ideal nozzles, friction and heat transfer are minimized, allowing engineers to use isentropic equations as a baseline benchmark. The Mach number ($M = V/a$, where $a = \sqrt{\gamma R T}$) determines compressibility effects; when $M > 0.3$, density variations become significant.

3. Mathematical Derivation & Equations

Starting from energy conservation and the definition of stagnation enthalpy:

$$h_t = h + \frac{V^2}{2} \implies C_p T_t = C_p T + \frac{V^2}{2}$$

Substituting $C_p = \frac{\gamma R}{\gamma - 1}$ and speed of sound $a = \sqrt{\gamma R T}$, the isentropic total-to-static temperature, pressure, and density relations are derived:

$$\frac{T_t}{T} = 1 + \frac{\gamma - 1}{2} M^2$$ $$\frac{P_t}{P} = \left(1 + \frac{\gamma - 1}{2} M^2\right)^{\frac{\gamma}{\gamma - 1}}$$ $$\frac{\rho_t}{\rho} = \left(1 + \frac{\gamma - 1}{2} M^2\right)^{\frac{1}{\gamma - 1}}$$

6. Aircraft Application & Air Safety Officer Connection

Aircraft intake design relies on isentropic compression ramps (e.g., on supersonic fighters like the MiG-29 or Concorde intake spike) to decelerate supersonic freestream air down to subsonic speeds before it enters the compressor face, minimizing total pressure recovery losses.

DGCA Air Safety Connection: Unstarts in supersonic inlets or severe shock-boundary layer interactions cause violent flow breakdown and thrust asymmetry, leading to directional control issues during high-speed military or experimental test flights.

7. UPSC Traps & Common Misconceptions
⚠️ UPSC Examination Trap

UPSC frequently tests whether stagnation pressure ($P_t$) remains constant through a normal shock wave. Crucial rule: Stagnation pressure drops across a shock wave due to entropy generation, even though stagnation temperature ($T_t$) remains constant in an adiabatic flow!

⚙️ Step-by-Step Worked Numerical: Stagnation Pressure at Supersonic Cruise

Given: A fighter aircraft flies at Mach $M = 1.5$ at an altitude where static pressure is $P = 20\text{ kPa}$. Assuming isentropic flow through the intake diffuser, calculate stagnation pressure ($P_t$). Take $\gamma = 1.4$.

Step 1: Apply Isentropic Pressure Relation

$$P_t = P \left(1 + \frac{\gamma - 1}{2} M^2\right)^{\frac{\gamma}{\gamma - 1}}$$

Step 2: Substitute Values

$$P_t = 20 \left(1 + \frac{1.4 - 1}{2} (1.5)^2\right)^{\frac{1.4}{0.4}}$$ $$P_t = 20 \left(1 + 0.2 \times 2.25\right)^{3.5} = 20 (1 + 0.45)^{3.5} = 20 (1.45)^{3.5}$$ $$1.45^{3.5} \approx 3.671 \implies P_t = 20 \times 3.671 = 73.42 \text{ kPa}$$

Step 3: Operational Takeaway

The high stagnation pressure recovered at the diffuser face provides the necessary pressure head to drive the multi-stage axial compressor without stalling.

6 Nozzle & Diffuser Thermodynamics

Propulsion Flow Passages
1. Formal Technical Definition
A nozzle is a thermodynamic flow passage designed to accelerate a fluid by converting enthalpy into kinetic energy (pressure drop), whereas a diffuser decelerates a fluid, converting kinetic energy into static pressure recovery.
2. Basic Concept & Physical Mechanism

The governing area-velocity relation for compressible flow (derived from continuity and momentum equations) is:

$$\frac{dA}{A} = (M^2 - 1)\frac{dV}{V}$$

This yields the fundamental laws of compressible flow passages:

  • Subsonic flow ($M < 1$): To accelerate flow ($dV > 0$), area must decrease ($dA < 0$, converging nozzle). To decelerate flow ($dV < 0$), area must increase ($dA > 0$, diffuser).
  • Supersonic flow ($M > 1$): To accelerate flow ($dV > 0$), area must increase ($dA > 0$, diverging nozzle).
  • Sonic throat ($M = 1$): Occurs strictly at the minimum cross-sectional area ($dA = 0$).
3. Mathematical Derivation & Equations

The exit velocity $V_e$ of an adiabatic, isentropic nozzle expanding from stagnation enthalpy $h_t$ to static enthalpy $h_e$ is derived from the First Law:

$$V_e = \sqrt{2 (h_t - h_e)} = \sqrt{2 C_p T_t \left(1 - \left(\frac{P_e}{P_t}\right)^{\frac{\gamma - 1}{\gamma}}\right)}$$

Mass flow rate through a choked nozzle throat ($A^*$) is:

$$\dot{m} = \frac{P_t A^*}{\sqrt{T_t}} \sqrt{\frac{\gamma}{R} \left(\frac{2}{\gamma + 1}\right)^{\frac{\gamma + 1}{\gamma - 1}}}$$

Modern combat aircraft feature variable-geometry convergent-divergent (C-D) exhaust nozzles controlled by Full Authority Digital Engine Control (FADEC) systems to optimize thrust across subsonic cruise and supersonic dash regimes.

7. UPSC Traps & Common Misconceptions
⚠️ UPSC Examination Trap

UPSC frequently asks: "Does reducing the back pressure on a choked convergent nozzle increase mass flow rate?" Answer: No! Once a nozzle throat becomes choked ($M=1$), mass flow rate reaches its maximum limit ($\dot{m}_{max}$) and is independent of further reductions in ambient back pressure.

⚙️ Step-by-Step Worked Numerical: Convergent Nozzle Exit Velocity

Given: Hot gases enter a convergent aircraft exhaust nozzle at stagnation temperature $T_t = 1200\text{ K}$ and stagnation pressure $P_t = 400\text{ kPa}$. The ambient back pressure is $P_{amb} = 250\text{ kPa}$. Given $C_p = 1150\text{ J/(kg}\cdot\text{K)}$ and $\gamma = 1.33$. Calculate the nozzle exit velocity ($V_e$).

Step 1: Check Critical Pressure Ratio for Choking

$$\left(\frac{P}{P_t}\right)^* = \left(\frac{2}{\gamma + 1}\right)^{\frac{\gamma}{\gamma - 1}} = \left(\frac{2}{2.33}\right)^{\frac{1.33}{0.33}} = (0.8584)^{4.03} = 0.537$$ $$P_{crit} = 0.537 \times 400 = 214.8 \text{ kPa}$$

Step 2: Determine Flow State

Since ambient back pressure ($250\text{ kPa}$) is greater than critical pressure ($214.8\text{ kPa}$), the nozzle is unchoked, and exit pressure $P_e = P_{amb} = 250\text{ kPa}$.

Step 3: Calculate Exit Velocity

$$V_e = \sqrt{2 C_p T_t \left(1 - \left(\frac{P_e}{P_t}\right)^{\frac{\gamma - 1}{\gamma}}\right)}$$ $$V_e = \sqrt{2 \times 1150 \times 1200 \left(1 - \left(\frac{250}{400}\right)^{\frac{0.33}{1.33}}\right)}$$ $$V_e = \sqrt{2,760,000 \left(1 - (0.625)^{0.248}\right)} = \sqrt{2,760,000 (1 - 0.8878)} = \sqrt{309,672} = 556.5 \text{ m/s}$$

Step 4: Operational Takeaway

The exit velocity of $556.5\text{ m/s}$ generates specific thrust proportional to momentum flux ($\dot{m} V_e$), forming the primary propulsive force of the aircraft.

Aviation Safety Case Studies & DGCA CAR Compliance

Thermodynamic principles in gas turbine propulsion are directly linked to several milestone air safety investigations and regulatory frameworks enforced by the Directorate General of Civil Aviation (DGCA) India and international bodies (ICAO, FAA, EASA).

1. Case Study: British Airways Flight 38 (Boeing 777, 2008) — Fuel Icing & Thermodynamic Heat Exchanger Failure

Incident Summary: On 17 January 2008, a Boeing 777 powered by Rolls-Royce Trent 800 engines lost all thrust during final approach to London Heathrow Airport, resulting in a hard landing short of the runway.

Thermodynamic Root Cause: Dissolved water in Jet A-1 fuel separated out as ice crystals during prolonged cruise at high latitudes in extremely cold ambient temperatures ($-76^\circ\text{C}$). These ice crystals accumulated on the face of the Fuel Oil Heat Exchanger (FOHE), which uses warm engine oil to heat incoming fuel. The ice blockage restricted fuel flow to the combustion chambers, causing an immediate drop in high-pressure turbine work and loss of thrust despite throttle inputs.

Air Safety Officer Takeaway: Demonstrates the critical intersection of First Law energy balances and fuel system thermodynamics. Fuel temperature management and anti-icing additive monitoring are now strictly regulated under DGCA CAR Section 2 airworthiness directives.

2. DGCA CAR Section 2 & Airworthiness Compliance

🎤 UPSC Interview Corner (Model Answers)

Q1: As an Air Safety Officer investigating an uncontained turbine failure, how would you apply thermodynamic principles to determine if the engine was over-temperatured prior to failure?

Model Answer: I would examine the microstructural changes in single-crystal nickel superalloy turbine blades using metallurgical analysis (scanning electron microscopy for gamma-prime phase coalescence and incipient melting). Thermodynamically, prolonged operation above the rated Turbine Inlet Temperature (TIT) increases creep rate and alters material grain boundaries. Additionally, I would download Quick Access Recorder (QAR) data to reconstruct historical $h$-$s$ operating paths and verify whether EGT margins were exceeded.

Q2: Explain why supersonic inlets require variable geometry (such as movable ramps or spikes) from a gas dynamics perspective.

Model Answer: At supersonic flight speeds, bringing incoming air to rest directly via a single normal shock wave results in massive stagnation pressure losses and excessive entropy generation. Variable geometry ramps generate a series of oblique shock waves followed by a weak normal shock, achieving isentropic compression deceleration closer to the Oswatitsch optimum curve. This maximizes total pressure recovery and maintains stable subsonic flow at the compressor face.
