Subtopic 1 of 4 Compressor Aerodynamics

Axial & Centrifugal Compressors

1. Formal Technical Definition

An axial compressor is a rotary flow gas compression device in which air flows parallel to the axis of rotation, imparting energy via alternating rows of rotating blades (rotors) and stationary blades (stators) to achieve high mass flow rates at moderate pressure ratios per stage. A centrifugal compressor is a radial-flow turbo-machine where air enters axially into an impeller, is flung outward by centrifugal force acquiring high kinetic energy, and subsequently passes through a diffuser to convert kinetic energy into static pressure with high pressure ratio per stage.

2. Basic Concept & Physical Mechanism

At the continuum and molecular level, compression is the mechanical reduction of specific volume accompanied by an increase in total enthalpy and static pressure. In an axial stage, the rotor blade passage acts as a moving diffuser: the relative velocity of the air decreases within the blade channel while static pressure rises due to centrifugal and aerodynamic lift effects. Stators then redirect the absolute flow vector to the correct incidence angle for the subsequent rotor row.

In centrifugal compressors, the rotating impeller accelerates air radially. The large radius ratio ($r_2/r_1$) dramatically increases angular momentum ($\Omega r^2$), generating immense centrifugal pressure fields alongside diffusion within the impeller channels. A vaned or vaneless diffuser then decelerates the high-Mach radial outflow, converting dynamic pressure into static pressure.

3. Mathematical Derivation & Equations

Applying Euler's Turbine Equation, the specific work input ($\Delta W_c$) per unit mass to a compressor rotor with angular velocity $\omega$ and tangential velocities $U_1, U_2$ and swirl components $V_{\theta1}, V_{\theta2}$ is:

\[ \Delta W_c = U_2 V_{\theta2} - U_1 V_{\theta1} \]

For an axial compressor with repeating stages, assuming mean radius $r_m$ and blade speed $U$:

\[ \Delta h_0 = U (V_{\theta2} - V_{\theta1}) = C_p (T_{02} - T_{01}) \]

Where:

  • $U_1, U_2$: Rotor blade linear speeds at inlet and exit [m/s]
  • $V_{\theta1}, V_{\theta2}$: Tangential components of absolute air velocity (swirl) [m/s]
  • $\Delta h_0$: Stagnation enthalpy change [J/kg]
  • $C_p$: Specific heat at constant pressure [J/(kg·K)]
  • $T_{01}, T_{02}$: Stagnation temperatures [K]

4. Dimensions & Fundamental Units

  • Work / Enthalpy Change ($\Delta W_c$): $[L^2 T^{-2}]$ | SI: $\text{J/kg}$ or $\text{m}^2/\text{s}^2$
  • Blade Speed ($U$): $[L T^{-1}]$ | SI: $\text{m/s}$
  • Mass Flow Rate ($\dot{m}$): $[M T^{-1}]$ | SI: $\text{kg/s}$
  • Pressure Ratio ($\pi_c$): Dimensionless $[-]$

Conversions & CGS

  • $1\text{ J/kg} = 10^4\text{ erg/g}$
  • $1\text{ atm} = 101,325\text{ Pa} = 1.01325\text{ bar} = 14.696\text{ psi}$
  • Rotational Speed: $\text{rad/s} = \text{RPM} \times \frac{\pi}{30}$

6. Aircraft Application & DGCA Air Safety Connection

Axial compressors dominate modern high-bypass turbofans (e.g., GE CFM56, Pratt & Whitney GTF, Rolls-Royce Trent) powering commercial airliners (A320, B737, B777) certified under DGCA CAR Section 2 (Airworthiness) and international airworthiness standards (ICAO Annex 8). Centrifugal compressors are utilized in auxiliary power units (APUs), helicopter turboshafts (e.g., Pratt & Whitney PT6), and older small turbojets due to their ruggedness and high single-stage pressure ratio.

Air Safety Implication: Foreign Object Damage (FOD) or blade tip rub causes severe mechanical imbalance, leading to high-frequency casing vibrations monitored by Vibration Health Monitoring (VHM) systems per maintenance schedule limits.

7. UPSC Traps & Common Misconceptions

  • Trap: Assuming centrifugal compressors have higher overall efficiency than multi-stage axial compressors in large thrust engines. Correction: While centrifugal stages achieve higher pressure ratios per stage, multi-stage axial compressors achieve vastly superior large-scale mass flow rates and peak polytropic efficiencies ($\approx 92\%$).
  • Trap: Confusing static pressure rise with stagnation pressure rise. Enthalpy changes are governed by stagnation conditions.

8. Step-by-Step Worked Numerical

Problem: An axial compressor rotor stage operates with air entering at stagnation temperature $T_{01} = 288\text{ K}$. The mean blade speed is $U = 350\text{ m/s}$. The tangential component of absolute velocity changes from $V_{\theta1} = 30\text{ m/s}$ (swirl with rotation) to $V_{\theta2} = 220\text{ m/s}$. Assuming constant specific heat $C_p = 1005\text{ J/(kg}\cdot\text{K)}$ and ratio of specific heats $\gamma = 1.4$, calculate the stagnation temperature after the stage ($T_{02}$) and the ideal stage total pressure ratio assuming isentropic efficiency $\eta_c = 0.88$.

Step 1: Calculate Stagnation Enthalpy Increase ($\Delta h_0$)

\[ \Delta h_0 = U (V_{\theta2} - V_{\theta1}) = 350 \times (220 - 30) = 350 \times 190 = 66,500\text{ J/kg} \]

Step 2: Determine Exit Stagnation Temperature ($T_{02}$)

\[ \Delta h_0 = C_p (T_{02} - T_{01}) \implies T_{02} = T_{01} + \frac{\Delta h_0}{C_p} \]

\[ T_{02} = 288 + \frac{66500}{1005} = 288 + 66.17 = 354.17\text{ K} \]

Step 3: Calculate Ideal Stagnation Temperature ($T_{02s}$) & Pressure Ratio ($\pi_c$)

\[ \eta_c = \frac{T_{02s} - T_{01}}{T_{02} - T_{01}} \implies T_{02s} = 288 + 0.88 \times 66.17 = 346.23\text{ K} \]

\[ \pi_c = \left(\frac{T_{02s}}{T_{01}}\right)^{\frac{\gamma}{\gamma-1}} = \left(\frac{346.23}{288}\right)^{\frac{1.4}{0.4}} = (1.2022)^{3.5} = 1.835 \]

Operational Takeaway: The single axial stage produces a pressure ratio of 1.835 with a discharge stagnation temperature of 354.17 K, representing high aerodynamic loading typical of modern transonic fan stages.

Subtopic 2 of 4 Compressor Characteristic Maps

Pressure Ratio & Compressor Maps

1. Formal Technical Definition

A compressor map is a graphical representation of performance characteristics where corrected mass flow rate ($\dot{m}\sqrt{T_{01}}/P_{01}$) is plotted against total pressure ratio ($P_{02}/P_{01}$), parameterized by corrected rotational speed ($N/\sqrt{T_{01}}$) and constant efficiency islands. The surge line bounds the unstable operating regime on the left, while the choke line (or stonewall) limits maximum mass flow on the right.

2. Basic Concept & Physical Mechanism

Compressor maps govern engine matching and off-design operation. As throttle setting changes, the operating point shifts along an equilibrium running line. Corrected parameters account for ambient variations in temperature and pressure.

At high rotational speeds, blade passages experience local sonic velocities ($M = 1.0$), creating a throat area limit where further pressure ratio reduction cannot increase mass flow—this is choking. Conversely, operating at reduced mass flow for a given speed forces the compressor against an adverse pressure gradient too steep for the boundary layer, leading to flow breakdown.

3. Mathematical Derivation & Equations

Using dimensional analysis (Buckingham Pi Theorem), compressor performance parameters are normalized using reference ambient conditions ($P_{ref} = 101.325\text{ kPa}$, $T_{ref} = 288.15\text{ K}$):

\[ \text{Corrected Mass Flow: } \dot{m}_{corr} = \dot{m} \frac{\sqrt{T_{01}/T_{ref}}}{P_{01}/P_{ref}} \]
\[ \text{Corrected Speed: } N_{corr} = \frac{N}{\sqrt{T_{01}/T_{ref}}} \]

Polytropic efficiency ($\eta_p$) for a compressor is defined as:

\[ \eta_p = \frac{\frac{\gamma-1}{\gamma} \ln\left(\frac{P_{02}}{P_{01}}\right)}{\ln\left(\frac{T_{02}}{T_{01}}\right)} \]

4. Dimensions & Fundamental Units

  • Corrected Mass Flow ($\dot{m}_{corr}$): $[M T^{-1}]$ | SI: $\text{kg/s}$ (at standard conditions)
  • Corrected Speed ($N_{corr}$): $[T^{-1} \theta^{-1/2}]$ | SI: $\text{RPM}/\sqrt{\text{K}}$
  • Pressure Ratio ($\pi_c$): Dimensionless $[-]$
  • Efficiency ($\eta_p$): Dimensionless $[-]$

Conversions & CGS

  • Temperature: Kelvin [K] or Rankine [°R] ($\text{K} = \text{°C} + 273.15$)
  • Pressure: $\text{Pa}$, $\text{bar}$, $\text{psia}$, $\text{mmHg}$

5. Engineering Reference Values Table

6. Aircraft Application & DGCA Air Safety Connection

Full Authority Digital Engine Control (FADEC) systems continuously calculate compressor operating points relative to the surge line on stored digital compressor maps. Transient bleed valve operation and variable stator vanes (VSV) schedule angles are dynamically adjusted to prevent the operating line from intersecting the surge boundary.

Air Safety Implication: Erroneous air data computer inputs (e.g., pitot-static icing leading to incorrect Mach/altitude data) can cause FADEC to mischedule VSVs, inducing uncommanded compressor surge during climb or descent, leading to engine flameout.

7. UPSC Traps & Common Misconceptions

  • Trap: Assuming corrected mass flow is independent of ambient temperature. Correction: Temperature square-root terms ($\sqrt{T_{01}}$) are mandatory to collapse multi-temperature test data onto a single characteristic line.
  • Trap: Confusing the surge line with the choke line. Surge is the left boundary (low flow, high pressure ratio), while choke is the right boundary (maximum flow, constant corrected flow).

8. Step-by-Step Worked Numerical

Problem: An engine test bed measures an uncorrected mass flow $\dot{m} = 45\text{ kg/s}$, ambient temperature $T_{01} = 295\text{ K}$, and ambient pressure $P_{01} = 95.0\text{ kPa}$. The reference conditions are $T_{ref} = 288.15\text{ K}$ and $P_{ref} = 101.325\text{ kPa}$. Calculate the corrected mass flow rate ($\dot{m}_{corr}$). If the physical rotational speed is $N = 10,500\text{ RPM}$, calculate the corrected rotational speed ($N_{corr}$).

Step 1: Calculate Corrected Mass Flow Rate ($\dot{m}_{corr}$)

\[ \dot{m}_{corr} = \dot{m} \times \frac{\sqrt{T_{01}/T_{ref}}}{P_{01}/P_{ref}} = 45 \times \frac{\sqrt{295 / 288.15}}{95.0 / 101.325} \]

\[ \sqrt{295 / 288.15} = \sqrt{1.02377} = 1.0118 \]

\[ \frac{95.0}{101.325} = 0.93757 \]

\[ \dot{m}_{corr} = 45 \times \frac{1.0118}{0.93757} = 45 \times 1.07915 = 48.56\text{ kg/s} \]

Step 2: Calculate Corrected Rotational Speed ($N_{corr}$)

\[ N_{corr} = \frac{N}{\sqrt{T_{01}/T_{ref}}} = \frac{10500}{\sqrt{295 / 288.15}} = \frac{10500}{1.0118} = 10,377.5\text{ RPM} \]

Operational Takeaway: Under warm ambient test conditions ($295\text{ K}$), the compressor passes a higher corrected mass flow than physical mass flow for the same corrected speed, shifting the operating point on the compressor map.

Subtopic 3 of 4 Aerodynamic Instabilities

Stall, Surge & Rotating Stall

1. Formal Technical Definition

Compressor stall occurs when local blade incidence angles exceed critical limits, causing boundary layer separation on compressor blades. Rotating stall is a localized breakdown where stalled regions (stall cells) propagate circumferentially around the compressor annulus at a fraction of rotor speed. Compressor surge is a severe, system-wide aerodynamic instability characterized by complete flow reversal, violent pressure oscillations, high acoustic roar, and rapid turbine overheating.

2. Basic Concept & Physical Mechanism

When the back-pressure from the combustor exceeds the pressure rise capability of the compressor, the adverse pressure gradient causes flow reversal over portions of individual blades. In rotating stall, one or more blade passages become blocked by separated flow (stall cell). Because the blockage deflects incoming air into adjacent blade passages, the adjacent blades experience increased angle of attack on one side and decreased angle on the other, causing the stall cell to propagate around the rotor annulus at 30% to 70% of rotor speed.

If the entire compression system interacts with downstream plenum volume (combustor/ducts), intermittent local stall can trigger global Helmholtz resonance—compressor surge—where mass flow oscillates violently between forward flow and complete reversal.

3. Mathematical Derivation & Equations

Greitzer’s B-parameter ($\mathbf{B}$) governs whether a compressor exhibits pure rotating stall or full compressor surge:

\[ B = \frac{U}{2 \omega_H L_c} = \frac{U}{2 a_0} \sqrt{\frac{V_c}{A_c L_c}} \]

Where:

  • $U$: Mean blade speed [m/s]
  • $\omega_H$: Helmholtz resonator frequency $[\text{rad/s}]$
  • $L_c$: Equivalent duct length of compressor [m]
  • $V_c$: Downstream plenum volume $[\text{m}^3]$
  • $A_c$: Compressor frontal area $[\text{m}^2]$, $a_0$: Speed of sound [m/s]

If $B > B_{crit}$ (typically $\approx 0.8$ to $1.0$), disturbances lead to surge. If $B < B_{crit}$, the system develops rotating stall.

4. Dimensions & Fundamental Units

  • Greitzer B-Parameter ($B$): Dimensionless $[-]$
  • Stall Propagation Speed: $[L T^{-1}]$ | SI: $\text{m/s}$
  • Plenum Volume ($V_c$): $[L^3]$ | SI: $\text{m}^3$
  • Frequency ($\omega_H$): $[T^{-1}]$ | SI: $\text{Hz}$ or $\text{rad/s}$

Conversions & Operational metrics

  • Surge frequency: Typically $5\text{ Hz}$ to $20\text{ Hz}$ in large turbofans.
  • Rotating stall cell speed: $0.35 \times N$ to $0.55 \times N$.

5. Engineering Reference Values Table

Instability Type Acoustic Signature EGT Response Engine Damage Risk
Mild Rotating Stall Low-frequency rumble / buzz Moderate EGT rise Low (if brief; vibration fatigue)
Full Compressor Surge Loud bangs, deep roar, airframe shudder Rapid EGT spike (Over-temperature) High (Turbine blade destruction)
Stall Margin ($SM$) Design limit: 15% – 20% minimum Maintained via bleed valves & VSVs Prevents surge onset

6. Aircraft Application & DGCA Air Safety Connection

Compressor stall and surge are major hazards during aircraft operations, especially during rapid throttle bursts, crosswinds, ingestion of exhaust gas (re-ingestion during reverse thrust or missile firing), or bird strikes. DGCA mandates rigorous engine certification testing under AC 33.14-1 / CAR requirements for ingestion and surge recovery.

Air Safety Implication: Uncorrected surge leads to over-temperature thermal distress of turbine blades, uncontained engine failure, or dual-engine flameout during heavy precipitation or volcanic ash ingestion.

7. UPSC Traps & Common Misconceptions

  • Trap: Assuming rotating stall and compressor surge are identical phenomena. Correction: Rotating stall is a localized flow breakdown with rotating cells and mild vibration; surge is a whole-engine thermodynamic instability with mass flow reversal and violent pressure pulsations.
  • Trap: Believing a high Greitzer B-parameter favors rotating stall. Correction: High B-parameter ($B > 1$) promotes full surge due to large plenum volume storage effects.

8. Step-by-Step Worked Numerical

Problem: A gas turbine compressor has a mean blade speed $U = 380\text{ m/s}$ and an equivalent compressor duct length $L_c = 0.6\text{ m}$. The downstream combustion chamber and plenum volume is $V_c = 0.15\text{ m}^3$, with a compressor frontal area $A_c = 0.12\text{ m}^2$. The ambient speed of sound is $a_0 = 340\text{ m/s}$. Calculate Greitzer's B-parameter and determine whether the system is prone to rotating stall or full compressor surge.

Step 1: Apply Greitzer's B-Parameter Formula

\[ B = \frac{U}{2 a_0} \sqrt{\frac{V_c}{A_c L_c}} \]

Step 2: Substitute Values

\[ \frac{U}{2 a_0} = \frac{380}{2 \times 340} = \frac{380}{680} = 0.5588 \]

\[ \frac{V_c}{A_c L_c} = \frac{0.15}{0.12 \times 0.6} = \frac{0.15}{0.072} = 2.0833 \]

\[ \sqrt{2.0833} = 1.4434 \]

\[ B = 0.5588 \times 1.4434 = 0.8066 \]

Operational Takeaway: Since $B = 0.8066$ is near the critical threshold ($B_{crit} \approx 0.8$), this compressor system is right on the boundary. Any increase in downstream plenum volume $V_c$ will push $B > 0.8$, causing localized rotating stall to rapidly escalate into destructive full-annulus compressor surge.

Subtopic 4 of 4 Air Intake Aerodynamics

Inlet/Diffuser Performance

1. Formal Technical Definition

An aircraft engine inlet (diffuser) is an aerodynamic duct designed to capture free-stream air and decelerate it from flight Mach number to lower Mach numbers ($M \approx 0.4$ to $0.5$) suitable for compressor entry, while converting kinetic energy into static pressure with minimum total pressure loss. Inlet performance is quantified by total pressure recovery factor ($\eta_i$ or $\pi_d$) and flow distortion metrics.

2. Basic Concept & Physical Mechanism

At subsonic speeds, divergent subsonic diffusers decelerate flow via an adverse pressure gradient. At supersonic speeds (e.g., fighter aircraft operating at $M > 1.0$), supersonic diffusers use a system of oblique shocks followed by a normal shock (in mixed-compression or external-compression inlets) to reduce flow velocity to subsonic levels before entering the engine face.

Boundary layer growth along the duct walls, shock-wave/boundary-layer interactions, and flow separation cause stagnation pressure losses. Poor inlet design generates circumferential and radial flow distortion at the compressor face, precipitating premature compressor stall.

3. Mathematical Derivation & Equations

Total pressure recovery ($\eta_d$ or $\pi_r$) is defined as the ratio of stagnation pressure at the compressor face ($P_{02}$) to free-stream stagnation pressure ($P_{0}$):

\[ \pi_r = \frac{P_{02}}{P_0} \]

For isentropic deceleration in a subsonic diffuser:

\[ \frac{P_0}{P} = \left(1 + \frac{\gamma-1}{2} M^2\right)^{\frac{\gamma}{\gamma-1}} \]

Ram recovery efficiency accounts for adiabatic shock losses in supersonic flight:

\[ \eta_{ram} = \frac{h_{02} - h_0}{h_{0\text{is}} - h_0} \]

4. Dimensions & Fundamental Units

  • Pressure Recovery ($\pi_r$): Dimensionless $[-]$ ($\le 1.0$)
  • Flight Mach Number ($M$): Dimensionless $[-]$
  • Stagnation Pressure ($P_0$): $[M L^{-1} T^{-2}]$ | SI: $\text{Pa}$ or $\text{kPa}$

Conversions & Standards

  • $1\text{ kPa} = 1000\text{ Pa} = 0.01\text{ bar}$
  • Distortion Index ($DC60$ or $DP/P_{avg}$): Dimensionless metric evaluated per ARP 1420 standards.

5. Engineering Reference Values Table

Inlet Type Flight Regime Typical Pressure Recovery ($\pi_r$) Distortion Level
Subsonic Transport Inlet $M = 0.0 \text{ to } 0.85$ 0.98 – 0.995 Very Low (< 1%)
Supersonic External Compression $M = 1.0 \text{ to } 2.0$ 0.90 – 0.95 Low to Moderate
Supersonic Mixed Compression $M > 2.0$ 0.85 – 0.92 Controlled via variable ramps

6. Aircraft Application & DGCA Air Safety Connection

Civil airliner nacelle inlets (e.g., Boeing 787, Airbus A350) are designed with large lip radii to prevent flow separation and boundary layer detachment during high-alpha takeoffs and crosswinds. Fighter inlets (e.g., Sukhoi Su-30MKI, Tejas LCA) use movable wedges or boundary layer bleed systems to swallow or divert low-energy air before reaching the compressor face.

Air Safety Implication: Inlet icing or bird ingestion blocking acoustic liners and pressure sensors can distort airflow into the compressor, resulting in uncommanded engine stall and surging during critical takeoff roll.

7. UPSC Traps & Common Misconceptions

  • Trap: Assuming an inlet creates pressure. Correction: Inlets/diffusers always experience total pressure losses ($\pi_r < 1.0$); they convert dynamic pressure into static pressure, but stagnation pressure decreases due to friction and shock waves.
  • Trap: Ignoring flow distortion. Even with high total pressure recovery, severe circumferential distortion can trigger compressor stall.

8. Step-by-Step Worked Numerical

Problem: An aircraft flies at an altitude where ambient static pressure is $P_0 = 30.0\text{ kPa}$ and free-stream Mach number is $M_1 = 0.82$. The specific heat ratio for air is $\gamma = 1.4$. The inlet diffuser total pressure recovery is $\pi_r = 0.985$. Calculate free-stream stagnation pressure ($P_{01}$) and the stagnation pressure at the compressor face ($P_{02}$).

Step 1: Calculate Free-stream Stagnation Pressure ($P_{01}$)

\[ P_{01} = P_0 \left(1 + \frac{\gamma-1}{2} M_1^2\right)^{\frac{\gamma}{\gamma-1}} \]

\[ 1 + \frac{0.4}{2} (0.82)^2 = 1 + 0.2 \times 0.6724 = 1 + 0.13448 = 1.13448 \]

\[ \left(1.13448\right)^{3.5} = 1.5423 \]

\[ P_{01} = 30.0 \times 1.5423 = 46.27\text{ kPa} \]

Step 2: Calculate Compressor Face Stagnation Pressure ($P_{02}$)

\[ P_{02} = \pi_r \times P_{01} = 0.985 \times 46.27 = 45.57\text{ kPa} \]

Operational Takeaway: Due to minor aerodynamic friction and diffusion losses ($\pi_r = 0.985$), the stagnation pressure drops from $46.27\text{ kPa}$ in the free stream to $45.57\text{ kPa}$ at the compressor face.

Aviation Safety Case Studies & DGCA CAR Compliance

Real-world aviation incidents linked to compressor failures, inlet icing, surge events, and regulatory airworthiness frameworks.

Case Study 1

British Airways Flight 38 (Boeing 777, 2008) - Fuel Icing & Engine Flameout

On January 17, 2008, a Boeing 777 powered by Rolls-Royce Trent 800 engines experienced dual-engine power loss during final approach to London Heathrow Airport. The aircraft landed short of the runway, resulting in substantial damage but no fatalities. The investigation by the UK AAIB revealed that ice crystals accumulated in the fuel feed system, restricting fuel flow to the engine oil coolers/fuel heaters (FOHE), causing starvation and uncommanded rollback/surge.

DGCA CAR & Airworthiness Implications:

• CAR Section 2 - Airworthiness: Mandated design modifications for fuel-oil heat exchangers (FOHE) to prevent ice crystal accretion under extremely low total air temperature (TAT) cruise conditions.

• Compressor Connection: Fuel starvation directly alters combustor equivalence ratios, triggering lean blowout and compressor surge as airflow dynamics decouple from rotational inertia.

Case Study 2

US Airways Flight 1549 (Airbus A320, 2009) - Multiple Bird Ingestion & Compressor Stall

Shortly after takeoff from LaGuardia Airport, the Airbus A320 (powered by CFM56-5B engines) ingested a flock of Canada geese. Both engines suffered immediate severe compressor stalls, loss of thrust, and loud concussive bangs as rotor blades were deformed and airflow paths were choked by ingested biological mass.

UPSC ASO Safety Engineering Analysis:

• Blade Deflection & Surge: Ingestion of large birds disrupts blade incidence angles across the low-pressure and high-pressure compressors, instantly forcing operation to the left of the surge line.

• Certification Standards: FAA/DGCA certification (FAR/CAR 33) requires engines to withstand single large bird ingestion without uncontained failure and medium bird flock ingestion with retention of minimum emergency thrust.

High-Yield Revision Summary

Core formulas, key definitions, and exam-focused takeaways for quick review before the UPSC ASO exam.

1. Axial vs. Centrifugal Compressors

  • Axial: High mass flow, moderate stage pressure ratio (1.2–1.4), multi-stage, high efficiency ($\approx 90\%$). Used in turbofans.
  • Centrifugal: High stage pressure ratio (4–10), compact, rugged, lower mass flow. Used in APUs and small turboshaft engines.

2. Compressor Map Boundaries

  • Surge Line: Left boundary separating stable operation from unstable flow reversal.
  • Choke Line: Right boundary where corrected mass flow reaches sonic velocity ($M=1.0$) in blade throats.
  • Corrected Parameters: $\dot{m}_{corr} = \dot{m}\frac{\sqrt{T_{01}/T_{ref}}}{P_{01}/P_{ref}}$ and $N_{corr} = \frac{N}{\sqrt{T_{01}/T_{ref}}}$.

3. Stall vs. Surge & Greitzer's B-Parameter

  • Rotating Stall: Localized flow separation rotating at $0.35$–$0.55\times$ rotor speed.
  • Compressor Surge: System-wide Helmholtz resonance with complete flow reversal and high acoustic noise.
  • Greitzer B-Parameter: $B = \frac{U}{2a_0}\sqrt{\frac{V_c}{A_c L_c}}$. $B > 0.8 \implies \text{Surge}$.

4. Inlet & Diffuser Performance

  • Pressure Recovery: $\pi_r = \frac{P_{02}}{P_0}$. Always $< 1.0$ due to friction and shock losses.
  • Subsonic Diffuser: Decelerates flow and converts kinetic energy to static pressure.
  • Supersonic Inlet: Uses oblique and normal shocks to reduce Mach number safely before compressor face.

🎯 UPSC Air Safety Officer Interview Corner

Board-style viva questions with expert model answers for your Air Safety Officer interview.

Q: As an Air Safety Officer investigating an uncontained engine failure involving compressor surge, what specific maintenance records and FADEC parameters would you immediately impound? ▼

Model Answer: As an ASO, I would immediately secure:

1. FADEC / EEC Non-Volatile Memory (NVM): To extract exceedance logs, transient rotor speeds ($N_1, N_2$), EGT spikes, and variable stator vane (VSV) scheduled angles leading up to the event.

2. Quick Access Recorder (QAR) & Flight Data Recorder (FDR): For high-frequency pressure, altitude, Mach number, and throttle lever angle data.

3. Maintenance Logs (CAR Section 2 Compliance): Reviewing borescope inspection reports, compressor blade tip clearance checks, bleed valve actuation history, and recent Airworthiness Directives (ADs) concerning FOD or anti-ice system performance.

Q: Why do multi-stage axial compressors require variable stator vanes (VSVs) and bleed valves during engine acceleration and deceleration? ▼

Model Answer: At low rotational speeds (e.g., during start-up or idle), front compressor stages operate at positive incidence angles (tending toward stall) because air velocity is too low relative to blade speed, while rear stages operate at negative incidence (choking) because air density increases disproportionately. Variable stator vanes adjust blade angles to optimize incidence across all stages, and inter-stage bleed valves vent excess air from intermediate stages to prevent front-stage choking and rear-stage stall.
