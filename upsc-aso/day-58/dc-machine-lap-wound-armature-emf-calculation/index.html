DC machine - lap-wound armature, EMF calculation

Day 58 of 129 Aircraft Systems and Avionics Phase 3: Systems and Applied 60-90 min Master Cycle

Step 1: Understand the Core Concepts

  • Armature Winding Fundamentals: In a DC machine, the armature winding is the site of electromagnetic energy conversion. In a lap winding, successive coils overlap each other. The finishing end of one coil is connected to the starting end of the adjacent coil under the same pole, connected to adjacent commutator segments.
  • Parallel Paths ($A$): The defining feature of a lap winding is that the number of parallel current paths ($A$) is directly proportional to the number of poles ($P$) and the duplexity ($m$) of the winding. For a simplex lap winding, $A = P$. This configuration divides the total armature current into many parallel streams, making it ideal for high-current applications.
  • Circulating Currents & Equalizers: Due to minor manufacturing asymmetries or unequal wear of the pole faces, the flux per pole may vary slightly. This magnetic imbalance induces unequal EMFs in the parallel paths, causing heavy circulating currents. To prevent these currents from passing through the brushes (which causes severe sparking), low-resistance copper conductors called equalizer rings are installed to short-circuit these unbalanced potentials.

Step 2: Formulas & Key Comparisons

General EMF Equation of a DC Generator: $$E_g = \frac{\Phi Z N P}{60 A}$$

Where:

  • $E_g$ = Induced EMF (Volts)
  • $\Phi$ = Flux per pole (Webers, Wb)
  • $Z$ = Total number of armature conductors ($Z = 2 \times \text{Turns per coil} \times \text{Total coils}$)
  • $N$ = Armature speed in Revolutions Per Minute (RPM)
  • $P$ = Number of poles
  • $A$ = Number of parallel paths
For Simplex Lap Winding ($A = P$): $$E_g = \frac{\Phi Z N}{60}$$

Note: The number of poles ($P$) and parallel paths ($A$) cancel out completely!

For Multiplex Lap Winding ($A = mP$): $$E_g = \frac{\Phi Z N P}{60 (mP)} = \frac{\Phi Z N}{60 m}$$

Where $m$ is the plexity (e.g., $m=1$ for simplex, $m=2$ for duplex, $m=3$ for triplex).

Lap Winding vs. Wave Winding

Parameter Lap Winding (Lap = Loop) Wave Winding (Wave = Two)
Parallel Paths ($A$) $A = mP$ (For Simplex, $A = P$) $A = 2m$ (For Simplex, $A = 2$)
Voltage & Current Rating Low Voltage, High Current High Voltage, Low Current
Equalizer Rings Mandatory for multi-pole machines Not required
Number of Brushes Equal to number of poles ($P$) Always 2 (regardless of poles)
Application Starter motors, heavy-duty generators High-voltage generators, small alternators

Step 3: Memorize & Retain

  • Conductor Current: The current flowing through each individual armature conductor ($I_c$) is given by: $$I_c = \frac{I_a}{A}$$ where $I_a$ is the total armature current.
  • Brush Current: Each brush carries a current equal to $2 \times I_c$ (except for the end brushes).
  • Equalizer Ring Criteria: Equalizer rings connect points on the armature winding which are at the same electrical potential. Therefore, the number of coils must be a multiple of the number of pole pairs.

Mnemonic: L-A-P vs. W-A-V-E

  • L-A-P: Large Amperage, Parallel paths = Poles ($A = P$).
  • W-A-V-E: Wonderful Amperage is Very low, Equals 2 ($A = 2$).
Exam Trap: UPSC numericals often state the number of "turns" or "coils" instead of "conductors". Always convert: $$\text{Conductors } (Z) = 2 \times \text{Turns}$$ If a coil has 4 turns, it has 8 active conductors.
Brush Placement Tip: In a lap-wound machine, the total number of brushes is exactly equal to the number of poles. If some brushes are removed to save space, the current-carrying capacity of the remaining brushes must be proportionally increased.

Step 4: Aviation Applications

1. 28V DC Aircraft Starter-Generators

Aviation starter-generators (such as those used on turboprop aircraft like the ATR 72 or regional jets) require massive starting currents (up to 1000A) to break engine inertia during the start cycle. Because lap windings provide a high number of parallel paths ($A = P$), they split this massive starting current across multiple paths, preventing thermal breakdown of the windings while operating at a low nominal voltage of 28V DC.

2. Heavy-Duty Landing Gear Actuator Motors

Emergency gear-extension or retraction systems utilize high-torque DC motors. These motors are lap-wound to maximize the armature current capacity, which directly translates to high electromagnetic starting torque ($T_e \propto \Phi I_a$).

3. Brush Wear and Commutator Maintenance in ASO Audits

During airworthiness inspections of DC starter-generators, an ASO must verify commutator health. Uneven brush wear or localized bar burning on the commutator is a classic symptom of a broken equalizer ring connection in a lap-wound armature, leading to unbalanced circulating currents.

Step 5: UPSC ASO Exam Focus

  • The "Duplex" Trap: UPSC frequently asks questions involving "duplex lap" or "triplex lap" windings. Always remember to multiply the parallel paths: $A = 2P$ (duplex) or $A = 3P$ (triplex).
  • No-Load vs. Full-Load EMF: In practical generators, the terminal voltage $V = E_g - I_a R_a$. UPSC may ask you to calculate the generated EMF ($E_g$) first, then find the terminal voltage by subtracting the armature resistance drop.
  • Direct Proportionality Shortcuts: If speed ($N$) is doubled and flux ($\Phi$) is halved, the induced EMF ($E_g$) remains unchanged. Use this scaling shortcut to save time during the exam.
Q1. A 6-pole, simplex lap-wound DC generator has 600 armature conductors. If the flux per pole is 0.02 Wb and the speed is 1000 RPM, calculate the generated EMF.
A) 100 V
B) 200 V
C) 400 V
D) 600 V
Correct Answer: B (200 V)
For a simplex lap winding, $A = P$. Therefore, the EMF formula simplifies to:
$E_g = \frac{\Phi Z N}{60}$
$E_g = \frac{0.02 \times 600 \times 1000}{60} = \frac{12 \times 1000}{60} = 200\text{ V}$.
Q2. What is the primary function of equalizer rings in a multi-pole lap-wound DC machine?
A) To increase the overall generated EMF of the machine
B) To reduce armature reaction and field distortion
C) To prevent circulating currents from passing through the brushes
D) To mechanically balance the armature rotor
Correct Answer: C (To prevent circulating currents from passing through the brushes)
Equalizer rings connect points of equal potential in the winding. This provides a low-resistance path for circulating currents (caused by magnetic asymmetry) to flow internally, bypassing the brushes and preventing severe sparking.
Q3. A 4-pole DC machine is wound with a duplex lap winding. How many parallel paths ($A$) exist in this armature?
A) 2
B) 4
C) 8
D) 16
Correct Answer: C (8)
For a multiplex lap winding, the number of parallel paths is $A = mP$.
Since it is duplex ($m = 2$) and has 4 poles ($P = 4$):
$A = 2 \times 4 = 8$ parallel paths.
Q4. An aircraft starter-generator armature has 120 coils, and each coil has 4 turns. What is the total number of armature conductors ($Z$) to be used in the EMF calculation?
A) 120
B) 240
C) 480
D) 960
Correct Answer: D (960)
Each turn has 2 active conductors. Therefore:
Total Conductors ($Z$) = $2 \times \text{Turns per coil} \times \text{Total coils}$
$Z = 2 \times 4 \times 120 = 960$ conductors.
Q5. Which type of armature winding is selected for an aircraft DC starter motor that requires high starting torque and operates on a 28V DC bus?
A) Simplex wave winding
B) Simplex lap winding
C) Duplex wave winding
D) Concentric winding
Correct Answer: B (Simplex lap winding)
Lap winding provides a large number of parallel paths ($A = P$), making it highly suited for low-voltage, high-current applications. High current is essential to produce the high starting torque ($T_e \propto I_a$) needed to start aircraft engines.
Q6. In a simplex lap-wound generator delivering a total load current of 120 A, if the machine has 6 poles, what is the current flowing through each armature conductor?
A) 120 A
B) 60 A
C) 20 A
D) 10 A
Correct Answer: C (20 A)
For a simplex lap-wound machine, $A = P = 6$.
Current per parallel path (conductor current) $I_c = \frac{I_a}{A} = \frac{120}{6} = 20\text{ A}$.
