Determinants – properties, Cramer's rule

Day 1 of 129 Engineering Fundamentals Phase 1: Foundations 60-90 min Master Cycle

1. Understand (5–10 min)

In Aeronautical Engineering and Mathematical Physics, the determinant is a scalar value uniquely computed for any square matrix $A \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$ or $\mathbb{C}^{n \times n}$. Geometrically, it measures the $n$-dimensional volume scaling factor of the linear transformation defined by the matrix $A$. If $\det(A) = 0$, the transformation collapses the domain into a subspace of lower dimension, rendering the operator non-invertible (singular).

In the domain of UPSC Air Safety Officer examination and Advanced Flight Dynamics, determinants form the analytical backbone for evaluating flight trim states, stability derivatives, structural stiffness matrices, and trajectory estimation algorithms. Cramer's Rule provides an explicit, exact algebraic method for solving non-singular systems of linear equations ($A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$) directly in terms of quotients of determinants.

Standard Terminology:

  • Minor ($M_{ij}$): The determinant of the $(n-1) \times (n-1)$ submatrix obtained by deleting the $i$-th row and $j$-th column of matrix $A$.
  • Cofactor ($C_{ij}$): The signed minor defined by $C_{ij} = (-1)^{i+j} M_{ij}$.
  • Singular Matrix: A square matrix $A$ such that $\det(A) = 0$, implying non-invertibility and non-trivial null space.
  • Cramer's System: A linear system $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$ where $A$ is square ($n \times n$) and non-singular ($\det(A) \neq 0$).

2. Learn the Concept (15–20 min)

The formal Leibniz definition of the determinant of an $n \times n$ matrix $A = [a_{ij}]$ utilizes permutations over the symmetric group $S_n$:

$$ \det(A) = |A| = \sum_{\sigma \in S_n} \text{sgn}(\sigma) \prod_{i=1}^{n} a_{i, \sigma(i)} $$

where $\text{sgn}(\sigma)$ denotes the signature of the permutation $\sigma$ (+1 for even permutations, -1 for odd permutations).

Fundamental Axiomatic Properties of Determinants

  1. Identity Matrix: $\det(I_n) = 1$.
  2. Transpose Invariance: $\det(A^T) = \det(A)$. All properties applicable to rows apply identically to columns.
  3. Multiplicativity: For any $A, B \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$, $\det(AB) = \det(A)\det(B)$. Consequently, $\det(A^{-1}) = \frac{1}{\det(A)}$ provided $\det(A) \neq 0$.
  4. Scalar Homogeneity: Scaling an entire $n \times n$ matrix by scalar $k$ yields: $$ \det(k A) = k^n \det(A) $$ Scaling a single row or column by $k$ scales the determinant by $k$.
  5. Alternating Property (Row/Column Swap): Interchanging any two rows or two columns reverses the sign of the determinant: $R_i \leftrightarrow R_j \implies \det(A') = -\det(A)$.
  6. Identical/Proportional Lines: If a matrix has two identical or proportional rows/columns, $\det(A) = 0$.
  7. Elementary Row Addition: Adding a scalar multiple of one row to another ($R_i \to R_i + k R_j$) leaves the determinant unchanged: $\det(A') = \det(A)$.
  8. Triangular/Diagonal Matrices: The determinant of an upper triangular, lower triangular, or diagonal matrix equals the product of its primary diagonal elements: $\det(A) = \prod_{i=1}^{n} a_{ii}$.
  9. Skew-Symmetric Matrices: If $A^T = -A$ for $A \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$, then $\det(A) = \det(-A) = (-1)^n \det(A)$. If $n$ is odd, $\det(A) = 0$.
  10. Adjugate Relations: $$ A \cdot \text{adj}(A) = \text{adj}(A) \cdot A = \det(A) I_n $$ $$ \det(\text{adj}(A)) = (\det A)^{n-1} $$ $$ \det(\text{adj}(\text{adj}(A))) = (\det A)^{(n-1)^2} $$

Derivation of Cramer's Rule

Consider the system of $n$ linear equations in $n$ variables written in matrix form $A \mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$:

$$ \begin{bmatrix} a_{11} & a_{12} & \dots & a_{1n} \\ a_{21} & a_{22} & \dots & a_{2n} \\ \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ a_{n1} & a_{n2} & \dots & a_{nn} \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \\ \vdots \\ x_n \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} b_1 \\ b_2 \\ \vdots \\ b_n \end{bmatrix} $$

Let $A_j$ be the matrix formed by replacing the $j$-th column of $A$ with the column vector $\mathbf{b}$. Consider the identity matrix $I_n$ with its $j$-th column replaced by the unknown vector $\mathbf{x}$, denoted $X_j$:

$$ X_j = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & \dots & x_1 & \dots & 0 \\ 0 & \dots & x_2 & \dots & 0 \\ \vdots & \ddots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ 0 & \dots & x_n & \dots & 1 \end{bmatrix} $$

Evaluating $\det(X_j)$ by Laplace expansion along row $j$ yields $\det(X_j) = x_j$. Now perform matrix multiplication $A X_j$:

$$ A X_j = A [\mathbf{e}_1 \dots \mathbf{x} \dots \mathbf{e}_n] = [A\mathbf{e}_1 \dots A\mathbf{x} \dots A\mathbf{e}_n] = [A_1 \dots \mathbf{b} \dots A_n] = A_j $$

Taking determinants on both sides using the multiplicative property $\det(AB) = \det(A)\det(B)$:

$$ \det(A X_j) = \det(A) \cdot \det(X_j) = \det(A_j) $$ $$ \det(A) \cdot x_j = \det(A_j) \implies x_j = \frac{\det(A_j)}{\det(A)}, \quad \text{for } \det(A) \neq 0 $$
$$ x_j = \frac{\Delta_j}{\Delta} = \frac{\det(A_j)}{\det(A)}, \quad j \in \{1, 2, \dots, n\} $$ $$ \Delta = \det(A), \quad \Delta_j = \det([A_{\cdot 1} \dots \mathbf{b} \dots A_{\cdot n}]) $$

System Classification via Determinants

  • Unique Solution: $\Delta \neq 0$.
  • Inconsistent (No Solution): $\Delta = 0$ AND at least one numerator determinant $\Delta_j \neq 0$.
  • Infinitely Many Solutions / Inconsistent: If $\Delta = 0$ AND $\Delta_1 = \Delta_2 = \dots = \Delta_n = 0$, system requires rank analysis ($\text{rank}(A)$ vs $\text{rank}([A|\mathbf{b}])$). If $\text{rank}(A) = \text{rank}([A|\mathbf{b}]) < n$, infinite solutions exist. If $\text{rank}(A) < \text{rank}([A|\mathbf{b}])$, no solution exists.

3. Memorize Important Facts (10 min)

  • Scalar Multiplication Factor: $\det(k A_{n \times n}) = k^n \det(A)$
  • Adjugate Determinant Theorem: $\det(\text{adj}(A_{n \times n})) = (\det A)^{n-1}$
  • Double Adjugate Determinant: $\det(\text{adj}(\text{adj}(A_{n \times n}))) = (\det A)^{(n-1)^2}$
  • Odd Order Skew-Symmetric Theorem: If $A^T = -A$ and $A \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$ with odd $n$, then $\det(A) = 0$.
  • Vandermonde Determinant (3x3): $$ \begin{vmatrix} 1 & 1 & 1 \\ x & y & z \\ x^2 & y^2 & z^2 \end{vmatrix} = (y - x)(z - x)(z - y) $$
  • Derivative of a Determinant (Leibniz Row Differentiation): $$ \frac{d}{dx} \begin{vmatrix} u_1 & v_1 \\ u_2 & v_2 \end{vmatrix} = \begin{vmatrix} u_1' & v_1' \\ u_2 & v_2 \end{vmatrix} + \begin{vmatrix} u_1 & v_1 \\ u_2' & v_2' \end{vmatrix} $$
  • Cramer's Rule Solvability Condition: Unique solution exists if and only if main determinant $\Delta \neq 0$.

4. Aviation Application (5–10 min)

Determinants and Cramer's Rule play an essential role in core Aeronautical Engineering disciplines:

1. Linearized Aircraft Trim Solutions (Flight Dynamics)

During steady level trim flight, the governing non-linear longitudinal perturbation equations are linearized into a state-space system $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$, where $\mathbf{x} = [\Delta u, \Delta w, \Delta q, \Delta \theta]^T$ represents forward velocity, normal velocity, pitch rate, and pitch angle perturbations. Solving for required elevator deflection $\delta_e$ and throttle setting $\delta_t$ under specified equilibrium gust disturbances utilizes Cramer's rule for immediate closed-form parametric inversion without computing full iterative numerical inverses.

2. Structural Instability and Wing Buckling (Aeroelasticity)

The finite-element formulation of aircraft structural stiffness yields the static equilibrium equation $K \mathbf{u} = \mathbf{F}$. Critical structural failure mode (buckling or divergence airspeed $V_D$) occurs at the boundary where matrix $K(V)$ becomes singular:

$$ \det(K(V_D)) = 0 $$

Air Safety Officers analyze the determinant of stiffness matrices to evaluate structural deformation limits post-incident.

3. Multi-Sensor Flight Data Recorder Trajectory Reconstruction

In aircraft crash investigation, discrete non-equispaced velocity data points obtained from FDR (Flight Data Recorder) are fitted using polynomial interpolation. Calculating polynomial coefficients through Lagrange/Newton interpolation mathematically equates to solving a system with a Vandermonde determinant. Non-zero Vandermonde determinants guarantee a unique smooth path reconstruction for incident simulation.

5. UPSC ASO Focus (10 min)

  • Frequently Asked Facts:
    • Determinant of an orthogonal matrix ($Q^T Q = I$) is always $\pm 1$.
    • Determinant of a unitary matrix ($U^H U = I$) has absolute value $| \det(U) | = 1$.
    • $\det(e^A) = e^{\text{Tr}(A)}$ (Jacobi's formula for matrix exponential).
    • If $A$ and $B$ are $n \times n$ matrices, $\det(A+B) \neq \det(A) + \det(B)$ in general.
    • For a block diagonal matrix $\begin{bmatrix} A & 0 \\ C & B \end{bmatrix}$, $\det = \det(A)\det(B)$.
  • Conceptual Traps:
    • Trap 1: Assuming $\Delta = 0 \implies \text{Infinitely many solutions}$. Fact: If $\Delta = 0$ and any $\Delta_i \neq 0$, the system has NO solution (inconsistent).
    • Trap 2: Computing $\det(kA)$ as $k \det(A)$ instead of $k^n \det(A)$. Always check matrix dimension $n$.
    • Trap 3: Applying Cramer's Rule directly to non-square matrices or systems where $\Delta = 0$. Cramer's Rule is strictly defined only when $\Delta \neq 0$.
    • Trap 4: Assuming skew-symmetric matrix determinants are always zero. This is ONLY true for odd-order skew-symmetric matrices. Even-order skew-symmetric determinants are non-negative perfect squares (e.g., $\det \begin{bmatrix} 0 & a \\ -a & 0 \end{bmatrix} = a^2 \ge 0$).

6. Practice Questions (10-12 UPSC ASO Standard MCQs)

Q1. Let $A$ be a $3 \times 3$ real matrix with $\det(A) = -4$. What is the value of $\det(-2 A^T A^{-1})$?
A) 32
B) -8
C) -32
D) 8
Step-by-step solution:
Given matrix $A$ is of order $n = 3$ and $\det(A) = -4$.
Using scalar multiplication property: $\det(k B) = k^n \det(B)$ where $k = -2$ and $n = 3$:
$\det(-2 A^T A^{-1}) = (-2)^3 \cdot \det(A^T A^{-1}) = -8 \cdot \det(A^T A^{-1})$.
Using multiplicative property: $\det(A^T A^{-1}) = \det(A^T) \cdot \det(A^{-1})$.
Since $\det(A^T) = \det(A)$ and $\det(A^{-1}) = \frac{1}{\det(A)}$:
$\det(A^T A^{-1}) = \det(A) \cdot \frac{1}{\det(A)} = 1$.
Therefore, $\det(-2 A^T A^{-1}) = -8 \times 1 = -8$.
Q2. For a non-singular matrix $A$ of order $4 \times 4$ with $\det(A) = 3$, evaluate $\det(\text{adj}(\text{adj}(A)))$.
A) $3^3$
B) $3^6$
C) $3^9$
D) $3^{12}$
Step-by-step solution:
For a matrix $A$ of order $n$, the determinant of double adjugate is given by:
$\det(\text{adj}(\text{adj}(A))) = (\det A)^{(n-1)^2}$.
Here $n = 4$ and $\det(A) = 3$.
Exponent $= (4 - 1)^2 = 3^2 = 9$.
Hence, $\det(\text{adj}(\text{adj}(A))) = 3^9 = 19683$.
Q3. For what parameter value $\lambda$ does the system of equations $x + y + z = 6$, $x + 2y + 3z = 10$, $x + 2y + \lambda z = 12$ fail to have a unique solution according to Cramer's Rule?
A) $\lambda = 3$
B) $\lambda = 0$
C) $\lambda = 1$
D) $\lambda = 2$
Step-by-step solution:
A linear system fails to have a unique solution when main determinant $\Delta = 0$.
$\Delta = \begin{vmatrix} 1 & 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 1 & 2 & \lambda \end{vmatrix}$.
Perform $R_3 \to R_3 - R_2$:
$\Delta = \begin{vmatrix} 1 & 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 0 & 0 & \lambda - 3 \end{vmatrix}$.
Expanding along $R_3$:
$\Delta = (\lambda - 3)(1 \cdot 2 - 1 \cdot 1) = (\lambda - 3)(1) = \lambda - 3$.
For unique solution to fail: $\Delta = 0 \implies \lambda - 3 = 0 \implies \lambda = 3$.
Q4. Let $S$ be a $5 \times 5$ real skew-symmetric matrix. What is the exact value of $\det(S^3 + 2S^5)$?
A) 1
B) 0
C) -1
D) Depends on entries of $S$
Step-by-step solution:
Notice $S^3 + 2S^5 = S^3(I + 2S^2)$.
Using multiplicativity: $\det(S^3 + 2S^5) = \det(S)^3 \cdot \det(I + 2S^2)$.
Since $S$ is a real $5 \times 5$ skew-symmetric matrix (odd order $n=5$), its determinant $\det(S) = 0$.
Hence $\det(S^3 + 2S^5) = (0)^3 \cdot \det(I + 2S^2) = 0$.

Q6. In an aircraft lateral stability analysis, Cramer's rule is applied to solve $A \mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$ where $A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & -1 & 0 \\ -1 & 2 & -1 \\ 0 & -1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}$ and $\mathbf{b} = \begin{bmatrix} 4 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$. What is the value of variable $x_3$?
A) 1
B) 2
C) 3
D) 4
Step-by-step solution:
First compute $\Delta = \det(A)$:
$\Delta = \begin{vmatrix} 2 & -1 & 0 \\ -1 & 2 & -1 \\ 0 & -1 & 2 \end{vmatrix} = 2(4 - 1) - (-1)(-2 - 0) + 0 = 2(3) + 1(-2) = 6 - 2 = 4$.
Now compute $\Delta_3 = \det(A_3)$ where 3rd column is replaced by $\mathbf{b} = [4, 0, 0]^T$:
$\Delta_3 = \begin{vmatrix} 2 & -1 & 4 \\ -1 & 2 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 & 0 \end{vmatrix}$.
Expanding along column 3:
$\Delta_3 = 4 \cdot [(-1)(-1) - (0)(2)] = 4(1 - 0) = 4$.
By Cramer's Rule: $x_3 = \frac{\Delta_3}{\Delta} = \frac{4}{4} = 1$.
Q7. The determinant $\begin{vmatrix} 1 & a & a^2 \\ 1 & b & b^2 \\ 1 & c & c^2 \end{vmatrix}$ evaluates to zero if and only if:
A) $a + b + c = 0$
B) At least two of $a, b, c$ are equal
C) $a b c = 1$
D) $a^2 + b^2 + c^2 = 0$
Step-by-step solution:
This is the classic $3 \times 3$ Vandermonde determinant.
Standard expansion formula gives:
$V = (b - a)(c - a)(c - b)$.
For $V = 0$, we must have $(b - a) = 0$ OR $(c - a) = 0$ OR $(c - b) = 0$.
This implies $a = b$, $a = c$, or $b = c$, i.e., at least two of the scalars $a, b, c$ are equal.
Q8. If $A$ is a $3 \times 3$ non-singular matrix such that $A^2 = 4 A$, what is the value of $\det(A)$?
A) 4
B) 16
C) 64
D) 256
Step-by-step solution:
Given $A^2 = 4A$ for $n = 3$.
Take determinants of both sides:
$\det(A^2) = \det(4A)$.
Using multiplicativity on LHS: $\det(A^2) = (\det A)^2$.
Using scalar property on RHS ($n=3$): $\det(4A) = 4^3 \det(A) = 64 \det(A)$.
So $(\det A)^2 = 64 \det(A) \implies \det(A) [\det(A) - 64] = 0$.
Since $A$ is given to be non-singular ($\det(A) \neq 0$), we divide by $\det(A)$:
$\det(A) = 64$.
Q9. Consider a system of linear equations $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$ where $A$ is $3 \times 3$. If $\Delta = 0$, $\Delta_1 = 0$, $\Delta_2 = 0$, $\Delta_3 = 0$, which of the following statements is ALWAYS TRUE?
A) The system must have infinitely many solutions.
B) The system must have no solution.
C) The system has either infinitely many solutions or no solution.
D) The system has a unique trivial solution.
Step-by-step solution:
When $\Delta = 0$ and all numerator determinants $\Delta_1 = \Delta_2 = \Delta_3 = 0$, Cramer's Rule becomes inconclusive.
The system can either have infinitely many solutions (if $\text{rank}(A) = \text{rank}([A|\mathbf{b}]) < 3$) or no solution (if $\text{rank}(A) < \text{rank}([A|\mathbf{b}])$).
An example of no solution when all $\Delta_i = 0$ is parallel planes with inconsistent offsets like $x+y+z=1$, $2x+2y+2z=3$, $3x+3y+3z=5$. Here $\Delta = \Delta_i = 0$, but no solution exists.
Hence Option C is the rigorous mathematical truth.
Q10. What is the value of the determinant $\begin{vmatrix} 1+a & 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 1+b & 1 \\ 1 & 1 & 1+c \end{vmatrix}$?
A) $a b c \left(1 + \frac{1}{a} + \frac{1}{b} + \frac{1}{c}\right)$
B) $a b c (1 + a + b + c)$
C) $a + b + c + 1$
D) $a b + b c + c a$

Factor out $a, b, c$ from rows 1, 2, 3 respectively (assuming $a,b,c \neq 0$):
$D = a b c \begin{vmatrix} \frac{1}{a}+1 & \frac{1}{a} & \frac{1}{a} \\ \frac{1}{b} & \frac{1}{b}+1 & \frac{1}{b} \\ \frac{1}{c} & \frac{1}{c} & \frac{1}{c}+1 \end{vmatrix}$.
Apply row operation $R_1 \to R_1 + R_2 + R_3$:
$D = a b c \begin{vmatrix} 1+\frac{1}{a}+\frac{1}{b}+\frac{1}{c} & 1+\frac{1}{a}+\frac{1}{b}+\frac{1}{c} & 1+\frac{1}{a}+\frac{1}{b}+\frac{1}{c} \\ \frac{1}{b} & \frac{1}{b}+1 & \frac{1}{b} \\ \frac{1}{c} & \frac{1}{c} & \frac{1}{c}+1 \end{vmatrix}$.
Factor out $\left(1+\frac{1}{a}+\frac{1}{b}+\frac{1}{c}\right)$ from $R_1$:
$D = a b c \left(1+\frac{1}{a}+\frac{1}{b}+\frac{1}{c}\right) \begin{vmatrix} 1 & 1 & 1 \\ \frac{1}{b} & \frac{1}{b}+1 & \frac{1}{b} \\ \frac{1}{c} & \frac{1}{c} & \frac{1}{c}+1 \end{vmatrix}$.

Thus $D = a b c \left(1 + \frac{1}{a} + \frac{1}{b} + \frac{1}{c}\right) = a b c + b c + a c + a b$.
Q11. Let $A$ and $B$ be two $3 \times 3$ matrices such that $\det(A) = 5$ and $\det(B) = 2$. Find $\det(A B^{-1} A^T)$.
A) 12.5
B) 5
C) 2.5
D) 50
Step-by-step solution:
Using matrix determinant multiplicative properties:
$\det(A B^{-1} A^T) = \det(A) \cdot \det(B^{-1}) \cdot \det(A^T)$.
Substitute known identities: $\det(B^{-1}) = \frac{1}{\det(B)}$ and $\det(A^T) = \det(A)$.
$\det(A B^{-1} A^T) = \det(A) \cdot \frac{1}{\det(B)} \cdot \det(A) = \frac{(\det A)^2}{\det B}$.
Substitute values: $\frac{5^2}{2} = \frac{25}{2} = 12.5$.

Q501. Consider the following statements regarding square matrices and determinants:
1. For any odd-order real skew-symmetric matrix $A$, $\det(A) = 0$.
2. If $A$ is an $n \times n$ matrix, then $\det(\text{adj}(A)) = (\det A)^n$.
3. If $\det(A) \neq 0$, Cramer's rule guarantees a unique solution for $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$.
4. $\det(e^A) = e^{\text{Tr}(A)}$ for any square matrix $A$.
Which of the above statements are CORRECT?
A) 1, 3 and 4 only
B) 1 and 2 only
C) 2, 3 and 4 only
D) 1, 2, 3 and 4
Exhaustive Analysis:
- Statement 1 is CORRECT: If $A^T = -A$ and $n$ is odd, $\det(A) = \det(A^T) = \det(-A) = (-1)^n \det(A) = -\det(A) \implies 2\det(A) = 0 \implies \det(A) = 0$.
- Statement 2 is INCORRECT: The correct identity is $\det(\text{adj}(A)) = (\det A)^{n-1}$, not $(\det A)^n$.
- Statement 3 is CORRECT: Non-zero coefficient determinant ($\Delta \neq 0$) is the precise condition for Cramer's rule to yield a unique solution $x_j = \frac{\Delta_j}{\Delta}$.
- Statement 4 is CORRECT: This is Jacobi's formula relating matrix exponential determinant to trace ($\det(e^A) = e^{\text{Tr}(A)}$).
Therefore, Statements 1, 3, and 4 are correct. Correct Option: A.
Q502. An aircraft wing structural framework is modeled by the parameter-dependent stiffness equation $K(\alpha) \mathbf{u} = \mathbf{f}$ where $K(\alpha) = \begin{bmatrix} \alpha & 1 & 2 \\ 1 & \alpha & 1 \\ 2 & 1 & \alpha \end{bmatrix}$. The aeroelastic instability (structural buckling) occurs when $\det(K(\alpha)) = 0$. What are the real values of $\alpha$ causing instability?
A) $\alpha = 0, \pm \sqrt{5}$
B) $\alpha = 1, \pm \sqrt{3}$
C) $\alpha = -1, \frac{1 \pm \sqrt{21}}{2}$
D) $\alpha = 2, \pm \sqrt{2}$
Exhaustive Analysis:
Evaluate $\det(K(\alpha)) = \begin{vmatrix} \alpha & 1 & 2 \\ 1 & \alpha & 1 \\ 2 & 1 & \alpha \end{vmatrix}$:
$\det(K(\alpha)) = \alpha(\alpha^2 - 1) - 1(\alpha - 2) + 2(1 - 2\alpha)$
$= \alpha^3 - \alpha - \alpha + 2 + 2 - 4\alpha$
$= \alpha^3 - 6\alpha + 4$.
Test integer roots of $\alpha^3 - 6\alpha + 4 = 0$:
For $\alpha = -1$: $(-1)^3 - 6(-1) + 4 = -1 + 6 + 4 = 9 \neq 0$.

8. Error Analysis & Common Traps (5 min)

1. Incorrect Factorization of Scalars from Matrices:

A frequent error made under exam pressure is writing $\det(kA) = k \det(A)$. Remember that scalar $k$ multiplies every row of the matrix. Factoring $k$ out of each of the $n$ rows pulls out a factor of $k$ per row, resulting in $k^n \det(A)$.

2. Cofactor Sign Matrix Errors:

In manual Laplace expansion of $3 \times 3$ or $4 \times 4$ determinants, candidates frequently forget the alternating checkerboard sign rule $(-1)^{i+j}$ for cofactors:

$$ \begin{bmatrix} + & - & + \\ - & + & - \\ + & - & + \end{bmatrix} $$

3. Cramer's Rule Misinterpretation when $\Delta = 0$:

Students often jump to the conclusion that if $\Delta = 0$, the system automatically has "infinitely many solutions". This is mathematically invalid. If $\Delta = 0$ and even ONE $\Delta_j \neq 0$, the system is inconsistent (no solution). If all $\Delta_j = 0$, Cramer's rule is completely inconclusive and matrix rank MUST be evaluated.

4. Misapplying Determinant Linearity:

Assuming $\det(A + B) = \det(A) + \det(B)$. Determinants are multi-linear with respect to individual rows/columns, NOT matrix addition as a whole.

9. Quick Revision & Mind Map (5 min)

Rapid Recall Flash-Facts:

  • Transpose Invariance: $\det(A^T) = \det(A)$. Row operations have identical structural impact as column operations.
  • Multiplicative Property: $\det(A B) = \det(A) \det(B)$.
  • Inverse Determinant: $\det(A^{-1}) = \frac{1}{\det A}$.
  • Scalar Rule: $\det(k A_{n \times n}) = k^n \det(A)$.
  • Adjugate Rule: $\det(\text{adj}(A_{n \times n})) = (\det A)^{n-1}$.
  • Odd Skew-Symmetric Rule: $\det(A) = 0$ if $A^T = -A$ and $n$ is odd.
  • Cramer's Formula: $x_j = \frac{\Delta_j}{\Delta}$. Valid if and only if main determinant $\Delta \neq 0$.
  • Triangular Matrix Rule: Determinant = Product of diagonal entries ($\prod a_{ii}$).
