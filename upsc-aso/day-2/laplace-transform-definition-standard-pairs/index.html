Laplace transform – definition, standard pairs

Day 2 of 129 Engineering Fundamentals Phase 1: Foundations 60-90 min Master Cycle

1. Understand (5–10 min)

The Unilateral Laplace Transform is an integral operational mapping that transforms a real-valued time-domain signal $f(t)$, defined for $t \ge 0$, into a complex frequency-domain function $F(s)$. Mathematically, it is defined as:

$$ F(s) = \mathscr{L}\{f(t)\} = \int_{0}^{\infty} f(t) e^{-st} \, dt $$

where $s = \sigma + i\omega \in \mathbb{C}$ is a complex frequency variable, with $\sigma = \text{Re}(s)$ acting as the real attenuation parameter and $\omega = \text{Im}(s)$ as the angular velocity parameter. The exponential factor $e^{-st}$ serves as the continuous kernel $K(s,t) = e^{-st}$ of the integral operator.

Mathematical Scope & Vector Space Context

In functional analysis, the Laplace transform operates as a linear operator $\mathscr{L}: \mathcal{E}_{\sigma_0} \to \mathcal{H}_{\text{ROC}}$, mapping a function space $\mathcal{E}_{\sigma_0}$ of exponentially bounded functions defined on $[0, \infty)$ into a space of complex analytic (holomorphic) functions $\mathcal{H}_{\text{ROC}}$ defined within a half-plane of the complex $s$-plane called the Region of Convergence (ROC).

Sufficient Conditions for Existence

The Laplace transform $F(s)$ is guaranteed to exist for all $s$ in a region $\text{Re}(s) > \sigma_0$ if $f(t)$ satisfies two classic Dirichlet-type conditions:

  1. Piecewise Continuity: $f(t)$ is piecewise continuous on every finite subinterval $[0, T]$ for $T > 0$. This ensures the Riemann integrability of $f(t) e^{-st}$ over finite bounds.
  2. Exponential Order: $f(t)$ is of exponential order $\sigma_0$ as $t \to \infty$. That is, there exist positive real constants $M > 0$ and $T_0 > 0$ such that: $$ |f(t)| \le M e^{\sigma_0 t} \quad \text{for all } t \ge T_0 $$

The smallest value $\sigma_0 \in \mathbb{R}$ for which this inequality holds is termed the abscissa of convergence. The Laplace integral converges absolutely for $\text{Re}(s) > \sigma_0$. If $\text{Re}(s) \le \sigma_0$, the integral may diverge.

UPSC Standard Terminology

  • Kernel ($K(s,t)$): The function $e^{-st}$ mapping differential operational domain to algebraic domain.
  • Unilateral vs. Bilateral: The Unilateral transform uses lower limit $0^{-}$ (capturing impulses at $t=0$), crucial for initial value problems in flight mechanics. The Bilateral transform integrates from $-\infty$ to $+\infty$.
  • Causality: A system whose impulse response $h(t) = 0$ for $t < 0$ is causal, making unilateral Laplace transforms the primary mathematical tool for dynamic stability analysis.

2. Learn the Concept (15–20 min)

To master the Laplace transform for competitive examinations such as UPSC ASO and GATE, a rigorous theoretical foundation involving first-principles derivations and analytical properties is essential.

1. Derivation of Standard Transform Pairs

A. Unit Step Function $u(t)$ and Constant $1$

For $f(t) = 1$ ($t \ge 0$), under $\text{Re}(s) > 0$:

$$ \mathscr{L}\{1\} = \int_{0}^{\infty} 1 \cdot e^{-st} \, dt = \left[ \frac{e^{-st}}{-s} \right]_{0}^{\infty} = \lim_{T \to \infty} \left( \frac{e^{-sT}}{-s} \right) - \left( \frac{1}{-s} \right) = 0 + \frac{1}{s} = \frac{1}{s}, \quad \text{Re}(s) > 0 $$

B. Exponential Function $e^{at}$ ($a \in \mathbb{C}$)

$$ \mathscr{L}\{e^{at}\} = \int_{0}^{\infty} e^{at} e^{-st} \, dt = \int_{0}^{\infty} e^{-(s-a)t} \, dt = \left[ \frac{e^{-(s-a)t}}{-(s-a)} \right]_{0}^{\infty} = \frac{1}{s-a}, \quad \text{Re}(s) > \text{Re}(a) $$

C. Power Function $t^n$ ($n > -1$) via Gamma Function

By definition: $\mathscr{L}\{t^n\} = \int_{0}^{\infty} t^n e^{-st} \, dt$. Perform the substitution $x = st \implies dt = \frac{dx}{s}$ (assuming $s > 0$):

$$ \mathscr{L}\{t^n\} = \int_{0}^{\infty} \left(\frac{x}{s}\right)^n e^{-x} \frac{dx}{s} = \frac{1}{s^{n+1}} \int_{0}^{\infty} x^n e^{-x} \, dx $$

Recalling Euler's integral of the second kind for the Gamma function, $\Gamma(z) = \int_{0}^{\infty} x^{z-1} e^{-x} \, dx$, we set $z = n+1$:

$$ \mathscr{L}\{t^n\} = \frac{\Gamma(n+1)}{s^{n+1}} $$

For non-negative integers $n \in \mathbb{N}_0$, since $\Gamma(n+1) = n!$, this simplifies to $\frac{n!}{s^{n+1}}$.

D. Trigonometric Functions $\sin(at)$ and $\cos(at)$

Using Euler's identity $e^{i at} = \cos(at) + i \sin(at)$:

$$ \mathscr{L}\{e^{iat}\} = \frac{1}{s - ia} = \frac{s + ia}{(s - ia)(s + ia)} = \frac{s + ia}{s^2 + a^2} = \frac{s}{s^2 + a^2} + i \frac{a}{s^2 + a^2} $$

Equating real and imaginary parts due to linearity:

$$ \mathscr{L}\{\cos(at)\} = \frac{s}{s^2 + a^2}, \quad \mathscr{L}\{\sin(at)\} = \frac{a}{s^2 + a^2} \quad (\text{Re}(s) > 0) $$

E. Hyperbolic Functions $\sinh(at)$ and $\cosh(at)$

$$ \mathscr{L}\{\cosh(at)\} = \mathscr{L}\left\{\frac{e^{at} + e^{-at}}{2}\right\} = \frac{1}{2} \left( \frac{1}{s-a} + \frac{1}{s+a} \right) = \frac{s}{s^2 - a^2}, \quad \text{Re}(s) > |a| $$ $$ \mathscr{L}\{\sinh(at)\} = \mathscr{L}\left\{\frac{e^{at} - e^{-at}}{2}\right\} = \frac{1}{2} \left( \frac{1}{s-a} - \frac{1}{s+a} \right) = \frac{a}{s^2 - a^2}, \quad \text{Re}(s) > |a| $$

F. Dirac Delta Distribution $\delta(t - t_0)$

Utilizing the sifting property of the generalized function $\delta(t)$:

$$ \mathscr{L}\{\delta(t - t_0)\} = \int_{0^-}^{\infty} \delta(t - t_0) e^{-st} \, dt = e^{-s t_0} $$

For $t_0 = 0$, $\mathscr{L}\{\delta(t)\} = 1$.

$$ \begin{array}{|c|c|c|} \hline \mathbf{f(t)} & \mathbf{F(s) = \mathscr{L}\{f(t)\}} & \mathbf{\text{ROC } (\text{Re}(s))} \\ \hline \delta(t) & 1 & \text{All } s \\ u(t) \text{ or } 1 & \frac{1}{s} & \sigma > 0 \\ t^n \; (n \in \mathbb{N}_0) & \frac{n!}{s^{n+1}} & \sigma > 0 \\ t^p \; (p > -1) & \frac{\Gamma(p+1)}{s^{p+1}} & \sigma > 0 \\ e^{at} & \frac{1}{s-a} & \sigma > \text{Re}(a) \\ \sin(at) & \frac{a}{s^2 + a^2} & \sigma > 0 \\ \cos(at) & \frac{s}{s^2 + a^2} & \sigma > 0 \\ \sinh(at) & \frac{a}{s^2 - a^2} & \sigma > |a| \\ \cosh(at) & \frac{s}{s^2 - a^2} & \sigma > |a| \\ e^{at}\sin(bt) & \frac{b}{(s-a)^2 + b^2} & \sigma > a \\ e^{at}\cos(bt) & \frac{s-a}{(s-a)^2 + b^2} & \sigma > a \\ t^n e^{at} & \frac{n!}{(s-a)^{n+1}} & \sigma > a \\ \hline \end{array} $$

2. Linearity and First Shifting (Frequency Shift) Theorem

Linearity Property: If $\mathscr{L}\{f_1(t)\} = F_1(s)$ and $\mathscr{L}\{f_2(t)\} = F_2(s)$, then for any constants $c_1, c_2 \in \mathbb{C}$:

$$ \mathscr{L}\{c_1 f_1(t) + c_2 f_2(t)\} = c_1 F_1(s) + c_2 F_2(s) $$

First Shifting Theorem: If $\mathscr{L}\{f(t)\} = F(s)$ for $\text{Re}(s) > \sigma_0$, then for any constant $a \in \mathbb{C}$:

$$ \mathscr{L}\{e^{at} f(t)\} = F(s - a), \quad \text{Re}(s) > \sigma_0 + \text{Re}(a) $$

Proof:

$$ \mathscr{L}\{e^{at} f(t)\} = \int_{0}^{\infty} e^{at} f(t) e^{-st} \, dt = \int_{0}^{\infty} f(t) e^{-(s-a)t} \, dt = F(s-a) $$

3. Memorize Important Facts (10 min)

  • Definition Integral: $\mathscr{L}\{f(t)\} = \int_{0}^{\infty} f(t) e^{-st} dt, \quad s = \sigma + i\omega$
  • Power Rule (Gamma Function): $\mathscr{L}\{t^p\} = \frac{\Gamma(p+1)}{s^{p+1}} \implies \text{Recall } \Gamma(1/2) = \sqrt{\pi}, \; \Gamma(3/2) = \frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{2}, \; \Gamma(5/2) = \frac{3\sqrt{\pi}}{4}$
  • First Shift Rule: $\mathscr{L}\{e^{at} f(t)\} = F(s-a)$
  • Unit Impulse Pair: $\mathscr{L}\{\delta(t)\} = 1, \quad \mathscr{L}\{\delta(t-a)\} = e^{-as}$
  • Damped Oscillations: $\mathscr{L}\{e^{-a t} \sin(\omega t)\} = \frac{\omega}{(s+a)^2 + \omega^2}, \quad \mathscr{L}\{e^{-a t} \cos(\omega t)\} = \frac{s+a}{(s+a)^2 + \omega^2}$
  • Hyperbolic Pairs: $\mathscr{L}\{\sinh(at)\} = \frac{a}{s^2-a^2}, \quad \mathscr{L}\{\cosh(at)\} = \frac{s}{s^2-a^2}$
  • Abscissa of Convergence: The boundary line $\text{Re}(s) = \sigma_0$ separating convergence and divergence domains in the $s$-plane.

4. Aviation Application (5–10 min)

In Aeronautical Engineering and Flight Mechanics, Laplace transforms convert dynamic system differential equations into linear algebraic expressions in the complex $s$-plane.

1. Aircraft Longitudinal Dynamics (Short-Period Oscillation)

The linearized pitch rate ($q$) and angle-of-attack ($\alpha$) perturbed dynamics for an aircraft subjected to elevator angle deflection $\delta_e(t)$ are modeled by a system of coupled first-order ODEs:

$$ \begin{bmatrix} \dot{\alpha} \\ \dot{q} \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} \frac{Z_\alpha}{U_0} & 1 \\ M_\alpha + M_{\dot{\alpha}}\frac{Z_\alpha}{U_0} & M_q + M_{\dot{\alpha}} \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} \alpha \\ q \end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} \frac{Z_{\delta_e}}{U_0} \\ M_{\delta_e} + M_{\dot{\alpha}}\frac{Z_{\delta_e}}{U_0} \end{bmatrix} \delta_e(t) $$

Applying the unilateral Laplace transform under zero initial conditions ($\alpha(0^-)=0, q(0^-)=0$) yields matrix algebraic equations:

$$ (s\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A})\mathbf{X}(s) = \mathbf{B}\Delta_e(s) $$

The transfer function relating elevator deflection to pitch angle response $\theta(s)$ is obtained directly using standard pairs:

$$ \frac{\theta(s)}{\delta_e(s)} = \frac{K_\theta (s + 1/T_{\theta 1})}{s(s^2 + 2\zeta_{sp}\omega_{sp}s + \omega_{sp}^2)} $$

Here, the denominator pole polynomial matches the standard form $s^2 + 2\zeta_{sp}\omega_{sp}s + \omega_{sp}^2 = (s + a)^2 + \omega_d^2$, whose time-domain impulse response contains damped sinusoidal standard pairs $e^{-at}\sin(\omega_d t)$ and $e^{-at}\cos(\omega_d t)$.

2. Wing Flutter & Atmospheric Gust Response Analysis

When an aircraft encounters a Sharp-Edge Gust modeled as a step function $w_g(t) = w_0 u(t)$ or a Discrete Sharp Peak Gust modeled as an impulse $w_g(t) = w_0 \delta(t)$, the aerodynamic response is computed by applying standard transform pairs. For instance, the transfer function $G(s) = \frac{1}{s^2 + 2\zeta \omega_n s + \omega_n^2}$ excited by a unit impulse gust $\delta(t)$ converts directly to the inverse pair:

$$ h(t) = \mathscr{L}^{-1}\{G(s) \cdot 1\} = \frac{1}{\omega_d} e^{-\zeta \omega_n t} \sin(\omega_d t) u(t) $$

where $\omega_d = \omega_n \sqrt{1 - \zeta^2}$. This directly describes the transient wing bending/torsional flutter response in flight testing.

5. UPSC ASO Focus (10 min)

  • Frequently Asked Facts:
    • Functions grow faster than any exponential (e.g., $e^{t^2}$, $t^t$) do not possess Laplace transforms because their abscissa of convergence is $\infty$.
    • Functions with non-integrable singularities at $t=0$ like $f(t) = t^{-1}$ or $t^{-3/2}$ do not have Laplace transforms due to divergence of the integral near $t=0$ (since $\Gamma(p+1)$ diverges for $p \le -1$).
    • $\lim_{s \to \infty} F(s) = 0$ for all strictly proper algebraic Laplace transforms (a direct consequence of the Riemann-Lebesgue Lemma).
  • Conceptual Traps:
    • Trap 1: Confusing $\mathscr{L}\{t^n\}$ formulas for integer vs fractional powers. For fractional powers, you MUST use $\frac{\Gamma(n+1)}{s^{n+1}}$, remembering $\Gamma(1/2) = \sqrt{\pi}$.
    • Trap 2: Assuming $\mathscr{L}\{f(t) \cdot g(t)\} = F(s) \cdot G(s)$. This is mathematically false! The transform of a product is given by the complex frequency convolution integral $\frac{1}{2\pi i} (F * G)(s)$, not simple multiplication.
    • Trap 3: Misapplying the shift theorem sign. $\mathscr{L}\{e^{-at} f(t)\} = F(s+a)$, NOT $F(s-a)$. Neglecting sign changes in $(s-a)$ is a frequent cause of errors.

6. Practice Questions (Exhaustive UPSC ASO MCQs)

Q1. What is the unilateral Laplace transform of $f(t) = t^{-1/2}$ for $t > 0$?
A) $\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{s}}$
B) $\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\pi s}}$
C) $\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{2 s^{3/2}}$
D) Integral diverges; Laplace transform does not exist
Detailed Solution:
Using the general power formula $\mathscr{L}\{t^n\} = \frac{\Gamma(n+1)}{s^{n+1}}$ with $n = -1/2 > -1$: $$ \mathscr{L}\{t^{-1/2}\} = \frac{\Gamma(-1/2 + 1)}{s^{-1/2 + 1}} = \frac{\Gamma(1/2)}{s^{1/2}} $$ Since $\Gamma(1/2) = \sqrt{\pi}$, we obtain: $$ \mathscr{L}\{t^{-1/2}\} = \frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{\sqrt{s}} = \sqrt{\frac{\pi}{s}} \quad (\text{Re}(s) > 0) $$ Thus, Option A is correct.
Q2. Evaluate the Laplace transform of $f(t) = \cos^2(at)$.
A) $\frac{s^2 + 2a^2}{s(s^2 + 4a^2)}$
B) $\frac{s}{s^2 + 4a^2}$
C) $\frac{2a^2}{s(s^2 + 4a^2)}$
D) $\frac{s^2 - 2a^2}{s(s^2 + 4a^2)}$
Detailed Solution:
Use the trigonometric identity $\cos^2(at) = \frac{1 + \cos(2at)}{2}$. Applying linearity: $$ \mathscr{L}\{\cos^2(at)\} = \mathscr{L}\left\{\frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{2}\cos(2at)\right\} = \frac{1}{2}\mathscr{L}\{1\} + \frac{1}{2}\mathscr{L}\{\cos(2at)\} $$ $$ = \frac{1}{2}\left( \frac{1}{s} \right) + \frac{1}{2}\left( \frac{s}{s^2 + (2a)^2} \right) = \frac{1}{2s} + \frac{s}{2(s^2 + 4a^2)} $$ Find a common denominator: $$ = \frac{(s^2 + 4a^2) + s^2}{2s(s^2 + 4a^2)} = \frac{2s^2 + 4a^2}{2s(s^2 + 4a^2)} = \frac{s^2 + 2a^2}{s(s^2 + 4a^2)} $$ Thus, Option A is correct.
Q3. Which of the following functions DOES NOT possess a valid unilateral Laplace transform?
A) $f(t) = e^{100t} \sin(3t)$
B) $f(t) = t^{100}$
C) $f(t) = e^{t^2}$
D) $f(t) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{t}}$
Detailed Solution:
A function $f(t)$ must be of exponential order as $t \to \infty$ to possess a Laplace transform. For $f(t) = e^{t^2}$, as $t \to \infty$, $\frac{e^{t^2}}{e^{M t}} = e^{t(t-M)} \to \infty$ for any constant $M$. Since $e^{t^2}$ grows faster than any exponential $e^{Mt}$, it is not of exponential order, and the integral $\int_0^\infty e^{t^2 - st} dt$ diverges for all finite real values of $s$. Thus, Option C is correct.
Q4. Compute the Laplace transform of the derivative of the Dirac delta distribution, $\mathscr{L}\{\delta'(t)\}$.
A) $\frac{1}{s}$
B) $1$
C) $s$
D) $s^2$
Detailed Solution:
Using the transform property of derivatives: $\mathscr{L}\{f'(t)\} = s F(s) - f(0^-)$. For $f(t) = \delta(t)$, we know $\mathscr{L}\{\delta(t)\} = 1$ and $\delta(0^-) = 0$. Therefore: $$ \mathscr{L}\{\delta'(t)\} = s \mathscr{L}\{\delta(t)\} - \delta(0^-) = s(1) - 0 = s $$ Alternatively, using distribution theory and sifting property: $\int_{0^-}^\infty \delta'(t) e^{-st} dt = - \left. \frac{d}{dt}(e^{-st}) \right|_{t=0} = -(-s e^0) = s$. Thus, Option C is correct.
Q5. Evaluate the definite integral $I = \int_{0}^{\infty} e^{-2t} t^3 \, dt$ using Laplace transform properties.
A) $\frac{3}{8}$
B) $\frac{3}{16}$
C) $\frac{3}{4}$
D) $\frac{1}{4}$
Detailed Solution:
By definition of the Laplace transform: $$ \int_0^\infty e^{-st} f(t) \, dt = \mathscr{L}\{f(t)\} = F(s) $$ Here $f(t) = t^3$ and $s = 2$. The Laplace transform of $t^3$ is: $$ \mathscr{L}\{t^3\} = \frac{3!}{s^{3+1}} = \frac{6}{s^4} $$ Evaluating at $s = 2$: $$ I = \left. \frac{6}{s^4} \right|_{s=2} = \frac{6}{2^4} = \frac{6}{16} = \frac{3}{8} $$ Thus, Option A is correct.
Q6. Find the Laplace transform of $f(t) = e^{-3t} \sin(4t)$.
A) $\frac{4}{s^2 + 6s + 25}$
B) $\frac{s+3}{s^2 + 6s + 25}$
C) $\frac{4}{s^2 - 6s + 25}$
D) $\frac{s-3}{s^2 + 6s + 25}$
Detailed Solution:
First, find the Laplace transform of $\sin(4t)$: $$ \mathscr{L}\{\sin(4t)\} = \frac{4}{s^2 + 4^2} = \frac{4}{s^2 + 16} = F(s) $$ By the First Shifting Theorem, $\mathscr{L}\{e^{-3t} \sin(4t)\} = F(s - (-3)) = F(s+3)$: $$ F(s+3) = \frac{4}{(s+3)^2 + 16} = \frac{4}{s^2 + 6s + 9 + 16} = \frac{4}{s^2 + 6s + 25} $$ Thus, Option A is correct.
Q7. What is the abscissa of convergence $\sigma_0$ for the function $f(t) = e^{5t} \cosh(2t)$?
A) $\sigma_0 = 5$
B) $\sigma_0 = 3$
C) $\sigma_0 = 7$
D) $\sigma_0 = 2$
Detailed Solution:
Express $f(t)$ in exponential form: $$ f(t) = e^{5t} \left(\frac{e^{2t} + e^{-2t}}{2}\right) = \frac{1}{2} e^{7t} + \frac{1}{2} e^{3t} $$ Applying Laplace transform: $$ \mathscr{L}\{f(t)\} = \frac{1}{2}\left( \frac{1}{s-7} \right) + \frac{1}{2}\left( \frac{1}{s-3} \right) $$ The first term converges for $\text{Re}(s) > 7$ and the second for $\text{Re}(s) > 3$. For the combined expression to converge absolutely, both terms must converge, which requires $\text{Re}(s) > \max(7, 3) = 7$. Hence, the abscissa of convergence is $\sigma_0 = 7$. Thus, Option C is correct.
Q8. Determine the Laplace transform of $f(t) = t^{5/2}$.
A) $\frac{15\sqrt{\pi}}{8 s^{7/2}}$
B) $\frac{15\sqrt{\pi}}{4 s^{7/2}}$
C) $\frac{3\sqrt{\pi}}{4 s^{5/2}}$
D) $\frac{15\sqrt{\pi}}{8 s^{5/2}}$
Detailed Solution:
Using $\mathscr{L}\{t^p\} = \frac{\Gamma(p+1)}{s^{p+1}}$ with $p = 5/2$: $$ \mathscr{L}\{t^{5/2}\} = \frac{\Gamma(5/2 + 1)}{s^{5/2 + 1}} = \frac{\Gamma(7/2)}{s^{7/2}} $$ Evaluate $\Gamma(7/2)$ using recurrence $\Gamma(z+1) = z \Gamma(z)$: $$ \Gamma(7/2) = \frac{5}{2} \cdot \Gamma(5/2) = \frac{5}{2} \cdot \frac{3}{2} \cdot \Gamma(3/2) = \frac{5}{2} \cdot \frac{3}{2} \cdot \frac{1}{2} \cdot \Gamma(1/2) = \frac{15}{8} \sqrt{\pi} $$ Substituting this back: $$ \mathscr{L}\{t^{5/2}\} = \frac{15\sqrt{\pi}}{8 s^{7/2}} $$ Thus, Option A is correct.
Q9. Find the Laplace transform of $f(t) = \sinh(3t) \cos(3t)$.
A) $\frac{3(s^2 - 18)}{s^4 + 324}$
B) $\frac{3(s^2 + 18)}{s^4 + 324}$
C) $\frac{3s}{s^4 + 324}$
D) $\frac{3(s^2 - 18)}{s^4 - 324}$
Detailed Solution:
Express $\sinh(3t)$ as $\frac{e^{3t} - e^{-3t}}{2}$: $$ f(t) = \frac{1}{2} e^{3t} \cos(3t) - \frac{1}{2} e^{-3t} \cos(3t) $$ Recall $\mathscr{L}\{\cos(3t)\} = \frac{s}{s^2 + 9}$. By first shifting theorem: $$ \mathscr{L}\{e^{3t} \cos(3t)\} = \frac{s-3}{(s-3)^2 + 9} = \frac{s-3}{s^2 - 6s + 18} $$ $$ \mathscr{L}\{e^{-3t} \cos(3t)\} = \frac{s+3}{(s+3)^2 + 9} = \frac{s+3}{s^2 + 6s + 18} $$ Subtracting the two terms and multiplying by $1/2$: $$ F(s) = \frac{1}{2} \left[ \frac{(s-3)(s^2 + 6s + 18) - (s+3)(s^2 - 6s + 18)}{(s^2 - 6s + 18)(s^2 + 6s + 18)} \right] $$ Denominator: $((s^2 + 18) - 6s)((s^2 + 18) + 6s) = (s^2 + 18)^2 - 36s^2 = s^4 + 36s^2 + 324 - 36s^2 = s^4 + 324$. Numerator expansion: $(s-3)(s^2+6s+18) = s^3 + 3s^2 - 18$ $(s+3)(s^2-6s+18) = s^3 - 3s^2 + 18$ Difference = $(s^3 + 3s^2 - 18) - (s^3 - 3s^2 + 18) = 6s^2 - 36 = 6(s^2 - 18)$. Multiply by $1/2$: $\frac{1}{2} \cdot 6(s^2 - 18) = 3(s^2 - 18)$. So $F(s) = \frac{3(s^2 - 18)}{s^4 + 324}$. Thus, Option A is correct.
Q10. Given $f(t) = u(t - 4)$, where $u(t)$ is the unit step function, what is its Laplace transform $\mathscr{L}\{u(t-4)\}$?
A) $\frac{e^{-4s}}{s}$
B) $\frac{e^{4s}}{s}$
C) $\frac{1}{s - 4}$
D) $e^{-4s}$
Detailed Solution:
By definition of the unilateral Laplace transform for a shifted step function: $$ \mathscr{L}\{u(t - a)\} = \int_{0}^{\infty} u(t - a) e^{-st} \, dt = \int_{a}^{\infty} 1 \cdot e^{-st} \, dt $$ $$ = \left[ \frac{e^{-st}}{-s} \right]_{a}^{\infty} = 0 - \frac{e^{-as}}{-s} = \frac{e^{-as}}{s} $$ For $a = 4$, $\mathscr{L}\{u(t-4)\} = \frac{e^{-4s}}{s}$ with $\text{Re}(s) > 0$. Thus, Option A is correct.
Q11. Assertion (A): The function $f(t) = \frac{1}{t}$ does not possess a Laplace transform.
Reason (R): The integral $\int_{0}^{\infty} \frac{e^{-st}}{t} \, dt$ diverges near $t = 0$ because $\frac{1}{t}$ is non-integrable at the origin.
A) Both (A) and (R) are true and (R) is the correct explanation of (A).
B) Both (A) and (R) are true but (R) is NOT the correct explanation of (A).
C) (A) is true but (R) is false.
D) (A) is false but (R) is true.
Detailed Solution:
Check Assertion (A): $\mathscr{L}\{1/t\} = \int_{0}^\infty \frac{e^{-st}}{t} dt$. Near $t=0$, $e^{-st} \approx 1$, so the integral behaves like $\int_{0}^{\epsilon} \frac{1}{t} dt = [\ln t]_{0}^\epsilon = \infty$. Thus the transform fails to exist. Assertion (A) is true. Check Reason (R): The cause of non-existence is indeed the logarithmic non-integrable divergence at $t=0$. Reason (R) is true and correctly explains (A). Thus, Option A is correct.
Q12. If $\mathscr{L}\{f(t)\} = F(s)$, then what is the Laplace transform of $f(at)$ where $a > 0$?
A) $\frac{1}{a} F\left(\frac{s}{a}\right)$
B) $a F(as)$
C) $F\left(\frac{s}{a}\right)$
D) $\frac{1}{a} F(as)$
Detailed Solution:
By definition: $$ \mathscr{L}\{f(at)\} = \int_{0}^{\infty} f(at) e^{-st} \, dt $$ Substitute $u = at \implies t = \frac{u}{a}$ and $dt = \frac{du}{a}$. Limits remain $0$ to $\infty$: $$ \mathscr{L}\{f(at)\} = \int_{0}^{\infty} f(u) e^{-s(u/a)} \frac{du}{a} = \frac{1}{a} \int_{0}^{\infty} f(u) e^{-\left(\frac{s}{a}\right) u} \, du = \frac{1}{a} F\left(\frac{s}{a}\right) $$ This is the Time Scaling Theorem. Thus, Option A is correct.
Q13. Calculate the Laplace transform of $f(t) = t e^{2t} \sin(3t)$.
A) $\frac{6(s-2)}{[(s-2)^2 + 9]^2}$
B) $\frac{6s}{(s^2 + 9)^2}$
C) $\frac{3(s-2)^2}{[(s-2)^2 + 9]^2}$
D) $\frac{6(s+2)}{[(s+2)^2 + 9]^2}$
Detailed Solution:
First, find $\mathscr{L}\{\sin(3t)\} = \frac{3}{s^2 + 9}$. Use Frequency Differentiation for multiplication by $t$: $\mathscr{L}\{t g(t)\} = -\frac{d}{ds} G(s)$. $$ \mathscr{L}\{t \sin(3t)\} = -\frac{d}{ds} \left( \frac{3}{s^2 + 9} \right) = -3 \cdot \frac{-2s}{(s^2 + 9)^2} = \frac{6s}{(s^2 + 9)^2} $$ Now apply First Shift Theorem for $e^{2t}$: replace $s$ with $(s-2)$: $$ \mathscr{L}\{e^{2t} \cdot t \sin(3t)\} = \frac{6(s-2)}{[(s-2)^2 + 9]^2} $$ Thus, Option A is correct.

Q601. An aircraft elevator control system responds to a control stick pulse modeled by $f(t) = t^2 e^{-a t} u(t)$. If the stability transfer function involves the integral transform of this input, what is the exact algebraic representation of $\mathscr{L}\{f(t)\}$ in the complex frequency plane?
A) $\frac{2}{(s+a)^3}$ for $\text{Re}(s) > -a$
B) $\frac{2}{(s-a)^3}$ for $\text{Re}(s) > a$
C) $\frac{1}{(s+a)^2}$ for $\text{Re}(s) > -a$
D) $\frac{2s}{(s+a)^3}$ for $\text{Re}(s) > -a$
Detailed Solution:
We know that $\mathscr{L}\{t^2\} = \frac{2!}{s^{2+1}} = \frac{2}{s^3}$ for $\text{Re}(s) > 0$. Applying the First Shifting Theorem $\mathscr{L}\{e^{-at} g(t)\} = G(s - (-a)) = G(s+a)$: $$ \mathscr{L}\{t^2 e^{-at}\} = \frac{2}{(s+a)^3} $$ The region of convergence shifts from $\text{Re}(s) > 0$ to $\text{Re}(s+a) > 0 \implies \text{Re}(s) > -a$. Thus, Option A is correct.
Q602. Consider the piecewise continuous flight surface signal defined by: $$ f(t) = \begin{cases} \sin(\omega t), & 0 \le t < \frac{\pi}{\omega} \\ 0, & t \ge \frac{\pi}{\omega} \end{cases} $$ Which of the following represents $\mathscr{L}\{f(t)\}$?
A) $\frac{\omega (1 + e^{-\pi s / \omega})}{s^2 + \omega^2}$
B) $\frac{\omega (1 - e^{-\pi s / \omega})}{s^2 + \omega^2}$
C) $\frac{s (1 + e^{-\pi s / \omega})}{s^2 + \omega^2}$
D) $\frac{\omega}{s^2 + \omega^2}$
Detailed Solution:
Express $f(t)$ using unit step functions $u(t)$: $$ f(t) = \sin(\omega t) \left[ u(t) - u\left(t - \frac{\pi}{\omega}\right) \right] = \sin(\omega t) u(t) - \sin(\omega t) u\left(t - \frac{\pi}{\omega}\right) $$ Rewrite the second term in shifted form $t' = t - \frac{\pi}{\omega}$: $$ \sin(\omega t) = \sin\left(\omega \left(t - \frac{\pi}{\omega}\right) + \pi\right) = -\sin\left(\omega \left(t - \frac{\pi}{\omega}\right)\right) $$ Thus: $$ f(t) = \sin(\omega t) u(t) + \sin\left(\omega \left(t - \frac{\pi}{\omega}\right)\right) u\left(t - \frac{\pi}{\omega}\right) $$ Taking the Laplace transform using the Second Shifting Theorem $\mathscr{L}\{g(t-a)u(t-a)\} = e^{-as} G(s)$: $$ F(s) = \frac{\omega}{s^2 + \omega^2} + e^{-\frac{\pi}{\omega} s} \frac{\omega}{s^2 + \omega^2} = \frac{\omega \left(1 + e^{-\pi s / \omega}\right)}{s^2 + \omega^2} $$ Thus, Option A is correct.
Q603. Evaluated at $s=3$, what is the Laplace transform of the atmospheric gust perturbation signal $f(t) = \sqrt{t} \, e^{t}$?
A) $\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{2\sqrt{2}}$
B) $\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{4\sqrt{2}}$
C) $\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{4}$
D) $\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{2}$
Detailed Solution:
First, find $\mathscr{L}\{t^{1/2}\}$ using power rule: $$ \mathscr{L}\{t^{1/2}\} = \frac{\Gamma(1/2 + 1)}{s^{1/2 + 1}} = \frac{\Gamma(3/2)}{s^{3/2}} = \frac{\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\pi}}{s^{3/2}} = \frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{2 s^{3/2}} $$ Next, apply First Shift Theorem for $e^{1t}$: $$ \mathscr{L}\{\sqrt{t} \, e^t\} = \frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{2 (s - 1)^{3/2}} $$ Evaluate at $s = 3$: $$ F(3) = \frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{2 (3 - 1)^{3/2}} = \frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{2 (2^{3/2})} = \frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{2 (2\sqrt{2})} = \frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{4\sqrt{2}} $$ Thus, Option B is correct.
Q604. Statement I: The unilateral Laplace transform operator is a non-linear mapping because it scales non-uniformly across different regions of convergence.
Statement II: If $f(t)$ is piecewise continuous on $[0,\infty)$ and satisfies $|f(t)| \le M e^{\sigma_0 t}$, then $F(s)$ is analytic in the half-plane $\text{Re}(s) > \sigma_0$.
A) Statement I is correct, Statement II is incorrect.
B) Statement I is incorrect, Statement II is correct.
C) Both Statement I and Statement II are correct.
D) Both Statement I and Statement II are incorrect.
Detailed Solution:
Statement I is FALSE: The Laplace transform integral is inherently a linear operator satisfying $\mathscr{L}\{a f(t) + b g(t)\} = a \mathscr{L}\{f(t)\} + b \mathscr{L}\{g(t)\}$. Statement II is TRUE: By standard complex analysis theorems (Leibniz rule for integrals under ROC), any function of exponential order $\sigma_0$ yields a transform $F(s)$ that is holomorphic (analytic) in the half-plane $\text{Re}(s) > \sigma_0$. Thus, Option B is correct.
