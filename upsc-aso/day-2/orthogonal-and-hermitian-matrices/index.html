Orthogonal and Hermitian matrices

Day 2 of 129 Engineering Fundamentals Phase 1: Foundations 60-90 min Master Cycle

1. Understand (5–10 min)

In Aeronautical Engineering and Aerospace Systems analysis, dynamic transformations, dynamic decoupling, state-space stability analysis, and structural vibration modes rely fundamentally on specific algebraic structures: Orthogonal and Hermitian matrices. These matrix classes form the backbone of linear operators on inner product spaces over real ($\mathbb{R}^n$) and complex ($\mathbb{C}^n$) vector spaces.

1. Mathematical Definition & Vector Space Scope:

  • Orthogonal Matrix ($A \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$): A real square matrix $A$ is defined as orthogonal if its transpose equals its inverse: $$A^T A = A A^T = I_n \iff A^{-1} = A^T$$ In the inner product space $\mathbb{R}^n$ with standard Euclidean inner product $\langle x, y \rangle = x^T y$, an orthogonal transformation preserves lengths (norms) and angles between vectors: $$\langle Ax, Ay \rangle = (Ax)^T (Ay) = x^T A^T A y = x^T y = \langle x, y \rangle$$ Hence, $A$ represents a rigid body rotation or reflection (an isometry). The column (and row) vectors of $A$ form an orthonormal basis for $\mathbb{R}^n$, satisfying $c_i^T c_j = \delta_{ij}$, where $\delta_{ij}$ is the Kronecker delta.
  • Hermitian Matrix ($A \in \mathbb{C}^{n \times n}$): A complex square matrix $A$ is defined as Hermitian (or self-adjoint) if it equals its conjugate transpose (also known as Hermitian transpose or Hermitian conjugate, denoted $A^H$ or $A^\dagger$): $$A^H = (\bar{A})^T = A$$ In terms of individual elements, $a_{ij} = \bar{a}_{ji}$ for all $i, j$. Consequently, the main diagonal elements $a_{ii}$ must be strictly real numbers ($\operatorname{Im}(a_{ii}) = 0$). Hermitian matrices are the complex generalization of real symmetric matrices ($A^T = A$).
  • Unitary Matrix ($U \in \mathbb{C}^{n \times n}$): The complex analog of an orthogonal matrix. A complex matrix $U$ is unitary if: $$U^H U = U U^H = I_n \iff U^{-1} = U^H$$ Unitary transformations preserve the complex inner product $\langle x, y \rangle = x^H y$ and complex vector norm $\| Ux \|_2 = \| x \|_2$.
  • Skew-Hermitian Matrix ($A \in \mathbb{C}^{n \times n}$): Defined by $A^H = -A$. Its diagonal elements must be purely imaginary or zero.

2. UPSC Standard Terminology & Core Scope:

For the UPSC Air Safety Officer (ASO) and GATE Aeronautical examinations, candidates are evaluated on:

  • Orthonormality Conditions: Proving columns/rows form unit orthogonal sets.
  • Spectral Theory & Diagonalization: Unitary similarity transformations ($A = U \Lambda U^H$) and Orthogonal Diagonalization ($A = Q \Lambda Q^T$).
  • Rayleigh Quotient: Bounds on $R(x) = \frac{x^H A x}{x^H x}$ for Hermitian systems.
  • Matrix Operations: Inversion shortcuts, determinant evaluations ($\det(A) = \pm 1$ for orthogonal matrices; $\det(A) \in \mathbb{R}$ for Hermitian matrices), and trace properties.

2. Learn the Concept (15–20 min)

To master the analytical properties of Orthogonal and Hermitian matrices, we derive their fundamental theorems rigorously from first principles.

$$ \begin{aligned} \text{Orthogonal Condition:} \quad & A^T A = I_n \implies \det(A) = \pm 1, \quad A^{-1} = A^T \\ \text{Hermitian Condition:} \quad & A^H = A \implies \lambda_i \in \mathbb{R}, \quad x_i^H x_j = 0 \quad (i \neq j) \\ \text{Unitary Condition:} \quad & U^H U = I_n \implies |\lambda_i| = 1, \quad |\det(U)| = 1 \\ \text{Skew-Hermitian Condition:} \quad & A^H = -A \implies \lambda_i \in i\mathbb{R} \text{ or } 0 \end{aligned} $$

1. Fundamental Theoretical Derivations & Proofs

Theorem 1: The Eigenvalues of a Hermitian Matrix are Purely Real

Proof: Let $A \in \mathbb{C}^{n \times n}$ be a Hermitian matrix ($A^H = A$). Let $\lambda$ be an eigenvalue of $A$ with a non-zero eigenvector $x \in \mathbb{C}^n \setminus \{0\}$, such that: $$A x = \lambda x$$ Premultiplying both sides by $x^H$: $$x^H A x = x^H (\lambda x) = \lambda (x^H x)$$ Now, take the conjugate transpose (Hermitian conjugate) of both sides of $x^H A x$: $$(x^H A x)^H = x^H A^H x$$ Since $A^H = A$, we have $(x^H A x)^H = x^H A x$. Using the property $(x^H (\lambda x))^H = (\lambda (x^H x))^H = \bar{\lambda} (x^H x)$ (since $(x^H x)^H = x^H x$ is a real scalar): $$\lambda (x^H x) = \bar{\lambda} (x^H x)$$ Subtracting the two expressions gives: $$(\lambda - \bar{\lambda})(x^H x) = 0$$ Since $x \neq 0$, the Euclidean inner product $x^H x = \|x\|^2 > 0$. Therefore, we must have: $$\lambda - \bar{\lambda} = 0 \implies \lambda = \bar{\lambda}$$ Thus, $\lambda \in \mathbb{R}$. $\blacksquare$

Theorem 2: Eigenvectors of a Hermitian Matrix Corresponding to Distinct Eigenvalues are Orthogonal

Proof: Let $x_1, x_2$ be eigenvectors of Hermitian matrix $A$ corresponding to distinct real eigenvalues $\lambda_1 \neq \lambda_2$: $$A x_1 = \lambda_1 x_1 \quad \text{and} \quad A x_2 = \lambda_2 x_2$$ Consider the expression $x_2^H A x_1$: $$x_2^H A x_1 = x_2^H (\lambda_1 x_1) = \lambda_1 (x_2^H x_1)$$ Alternatively, express $x_2^H A x_1$ using the Hermitian property of $A$: $$x_2^H A x_1 = (A^H x_2)^H x_1 = (A x_2)^H x_1 = (\lambda_2 x_2)^H x_1 = \bar{\lambda}_2 x_2^H x_1$$ Since $\lambda_2$ is real ($\bar{\lambda}_2 = \lambda_2$), we have $x_2^H A x_1 = \lambda_2 (x_2^H x_1)$. Equating the two expressions: $$\lambda_1 (x_2^H x_1) = \lambda_2 (x_2^H x_1) \implies (\lambda_1 - \lambda_2)(x_2^H x_1) = 0$$ Given $\lambda_1 \neq \lambda_2$, it follows that $x_2^H x_1 = 0$. Hence, $x_1$ and $x_2$ are orthogonal in $\mathbb{C}^n$. $\blacksquare$

Theorem 3: Determinant of Real Orthogonal Matrix is $\pm 1$

Proof: Let $A$ be a real orthogonal matrix ($A^T A = I$). Taking determinant on both sides: $$\det(A^T A) = \det(I)$$ Using product property of determinants $\det(A B) = \det(A)\det(B)$ and transpose property $\det(A^T) = \det(A)$: $$\det(A^T) \det(A) = 1 \implies (\det(A))^2 = 1 \implies \det(A) = \pm 1$$ Matrices with $\det(A) = +1$ are called Proper Orthogonal (pure rotations), while those with $\det(A) = -1$ are Improper Orthogonal (rotations with reflection). $\blacksquare$

Theorem 4: Eigenvalues of Unitary / Real Orthogonal Matrix lie on the Unit Circle ($|\lambda| = 1$)

Proof: Let $U$ be unitary ($U^H U = I$) with $U x = \lambda x$, $x \neq 0$. $$\| U x \|^2 = (U x)^H (U x) = x^H U^H U x = x^H I x = x^H x = \| x \|^2$$ Also, using $U x = \lambda x$: $$\| U x \|^2 = (\lambda x)^H (\lambda x) = \bar{\lambda} \lambda x^H x = |\lambda|^2 \| x \|^2$$ Equating both sides: $$|\lambda|^2 \| x \|^2 = \| x \|^2 \implies |\lambda|^2 = 1 \implies |\lambda| = 1$$ Hence, eigenvalues of orthogonal/unitary matrices are complex numbers of unit modulus ($\lambda = e^{i\theta} = \cos\theta + i\sin\theta$). $\blacksquare$

2. Key Analytical Properties & Matrix Operations Summary

  • Closure under Multiplication: If $A, B$ are $n \times n$ orthogonal matrices, then $(A B)^T (A B) = B^T A^T A B = B^T I B = I$. Thus, product $AB$ is also orthogonal.
  • Skew-Hermitian Eigenvalues: If $A^H = -A$, then $i A$ is Hermitian. Hence, eigenvalues of skew-Hermitian matrices are purely imaginary ($\lambda \in i\mathbb{R}$) or zero.
  • Trace and Determinant Relations:
    • For Hermitian $A$: $\operatorname{Tr}(A) = \sum \lambda_i \in \mathbb{R}$, $\det(A) = \prod \lambda_i \in \mathbb{R}$.
    • For Skew-Hermitian $A$: $\operatorname{Tr}(A)$ is purely imaginary or zero.
  • Spectral Theorem (Unitary Similarity): Every Hermitian matrix $A$ can be unitarily diagonalized: $$A = U \Lambda U^H$$ where $U$ is a unitary matrix whose columns are orthonormal eigenvectors of $A$, and $\Lambda = \operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1, \lambda_2, \dots, \lambda_n)$ is a real diagonal matrix.

3. Memorize Important Facts (10 min)

  • Orthogonal Matrix Definition: $A^T A = A A^T = I_n \iff A^{-1} = A^T$
  • Hermitian Matrix Definition: $A^H = (\bar{A})^T = A \iff a_{ij} = \bar{a}_{ji}$
  • Unitary Matrix Definition: $U^H U = U U^H = I_n \iff U^{-1} = U^H$
  • Skew-Hermitian Definition: $A^H = -A \iff a_{ij} = -\bar{a}_{ji}$
  • Orthogonal Matrix Determinant: $\det(A) = \pm 1$ ($\det(A) = +1 \implies$ Proper Rotation)
  • Eigenvalues of Hermitian Matrix: Purely Real ($\lambda_i \in \mathbb{R}$)
  • Eigenvalues of Skew-Hermitian Matrix: Purely Imaginary or Zero ($\lambda_i = i b, b \in \mathbb{R}$)
  • Eigenvalues of Unitary/Orthogonal Matrix: Unit Modulus ($|\lambda_i| = 1 \implies \lambda_i = e^{i\theta}$)
  • Hermitian Diagonal Elements: $a_{ii} \in \mathbb{R}$ (Strictly Real)
  • Skew-Hermitian Diagonal Elements: $a_{ii} \in i\mathbb{R}$ or $0$ (Purely Imaginary or Zero)
  • Relationship between Hermitian and Skew-Hermitian: If $A$ is Hermitian, $iA$ is Skew-Hermitian; if $S$ is Skew-Hermitian, $iS$ is Hermitian.
  • Rayleigh Quotient Bounds: For a Hermitian matrix $A$, $\lambda_{\min} \le \frac{x^H A x}{x^H x} \le \lambda_{\max}$
  • Norm Preservation: $\| A x \|_2 = \| x \|_2$ for any Orthogonal or Unitary matrix $A$

4. Aviation Application (5–10 min)

Orthogonal and Hermitian matrices are directly applied across multiple flight physics, structural dynamics, and control domains in Aerospace Engineering:

1. Aircraft Kinematics & Direction Cosine Matrix (DCM)

In flight mechanics, transforming vectors between the North-East-Down (NED) Navigation Frame $n$ and the Body-Fixed Frame $b$ relies on the Direction Cosine Matrix $C_b^n$, parametrized by Euler angles ($\phi, \theta, \psi$):

$$C_b^n = R_z(\psi) R_y(\theta) R_x(\phi)$$

Because coordinate transformations preserve physical vector lengths (e.g., total velocity magnitude $V = \| \vec{V} \|$), $C_b^n$ is a Proper Orthogonal Matrix ($\det(C_b^n) = +1$):

$$(C_b^n)^T C_b^n = I \implies (C_b^n)^{-1} = (C_b^n)^T$$

This orthogonal inverse property allows real-time flight control computers to reverse coordinate transformations instantly by matrix transposition without performing computationally expensive numerical matrix inversions.

2. Structural Dynamics, Aircraft Vibration & Flutter Analysis

An aircraft fuselage or swept wing is modeled in Finite Element Analysis (FEA) as a multi-degree-of-freedom continuous system governed by the matrix differential equation:

$$[M] \ddot{x}(t) + [C] \dot{x}(t) + [K] x(t) = F(t)$$

where mass matrix $[M]$ and stiffness matrix $[K]$ are real, symmetric, and positive-definite ($M^T = M > 0, K^T = K > 0$). In the complex plane, symmetric matrices behave as Hermitian operators. The undamped free vibration problem reduces to the generalized symmetric eigenvalue problem:

$$[K] \phi_r = \omega_r^2 [M] \phi_r$$

Because $[M]$ and $[K]$ are symmetric (Hermitian), all natural frequencies $\omega_r^2$ are guaranteed to be real positive numbers, and normal mode shapes $\phi_r$ satisfy the orthogonal orthogonality conditions:

$$\Phi^T [M] \Phi = I, \quad \Phi^T [K] \Phi = \operatorname{diag}(\omega_1^2, \omega_2^2, \dots, \omega_n^2) = \Omega^2$$

This orthogonal decoupling transforms $n$ coupled differential equations into $n$ independent single-degree-of-freedom modal equations, critical for analyzing flutter boundary speeds ($V_{\text{flutter}}$) and wing dynamic loading.

3. Modern Flight Control & Robust Stability (Lyapunov Analysis)

For linearized aircraft state-space dynamics $\dot{x} = A x$, dynamic stability requires evaluating state transition matrices and Lyapunov functions $V(x) = x^T P x$. The continuous-time Lyapunov equation is expressed as:

$$A^T P + P A = -Q$$

When extending state-space analysis to complex frequency domains, optimal control synthesis ($H_2 / H_\infty$ modern control) requires $P$ and $Q$ to be Hermitian Positive Definite matrices ($P^H = P > 0$). The spectral decomposition of Hermitian matrices guarantees non-conservative stability margins for flight control augmentation systems (FCAS).

5. UPSC ASO Focus (10 min)

  • Frequently Asked Facts:
    • Every real symmetric matrix is Hermitian, and every real skew-symmetric matrix is skew-Hermitian.
    • Eigenvalues of a real orthogonal matrix always appear in complex conjugate pairs ($e^{i\theta}, e^{-i\theta}$) or real values ($\pm 1$).
    • If $A$ is Hermitian, then $\det(A)$ is strictly real, even if elements of $A$ are complex numbers.
    • If $A$ is skew-Hermitian, then $\operatorname{Tr}(A)$ is either $0$ or purely imaginary.
    • Matrix $A$ is orthogonal if and only if its rows (or columns) form an orthonormal set in $\mathbb{R}^n$.
    • For any Unitary matrix $U$, $|\det(U)| = 1$. (Note: $\det(U)$ can be a complex number $e^{i\phi}$, unlike orthogonal where $\det = \pm 1$).
    • Cayley's Transformation: If $S$ is real skew-symmetric ($S^T = -S$), then $A = (I - S)(I + S)^{-1}$ is an orthogonal matrix.
  • Conceptual Traps:
    • Trap 1: Confusing Orthogonal Vectors with Orthogonal Matrices. A matrix whose columns are orthogonal but not normalized (length $\neq 1$) is NOT an orthogonal matrix! $A^T A$ must equal $I$, not a diagonal matrix with non-unity elements.
    • Trap 2: Assuming eigenvalues of an Orthogonal Matrix must be real. False! Eigenvalues of a real orthogonal matrix are of the form $e^{i\theta} = \cos\theta \pm i\sin\theta$. They are real ONLY when $\theta = 0$ ($\lambda = 1$) or $\theta = \pi$ ($\lambda = -1$).
    • Trap 3: Forgetting complex conjugation when taking Hermitian transpose. $A^H = (\bar{A})^T$. A common mistake is taking only transpose without conjugating imaginary terms ($i \to -i$).
    • Trap 4: Assuming skew-Hermitian matrices have zero trace. Their trace is purely imaginary or zero ($\sum i b_k$). It is zero ONLY if the sum of imaginary components happens to cancel or if all diagonal elements are zero.

6. Practice Questions (Exhaustive UPSC ASO MCQs)

Q1. Determine the values of $a$ and $b$ for which the given matrix $A$ is real orthogonal: $$A = \begin{bmatrix} 1/\sqrt{2} & a \\ b & 1/\sqrt{2} \end{bmatrix}$$
A) $a = 1/\sqrt{2}, b = 1/\sqrt{2}$
B) $a = -1/\sqrt{2}, b = 1/\sqrt{2}$
C) $a = 1/\sqrt{2}, b = -1/\sqrt{2}$
D) Both B and C are valid configurations
For $A$ to be orthogonal, $A^T A = I_2$. $$A^T A = \begin{bmatrix} 1/\sqrt{2} & b \\ a & 1/\sqrt{2} \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 1/\sqrt{2} & a \\ b & 1/\sqrt{2} \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} \frac{1}{2} + b^2 & \frac{a}{\sqrt{2}} + \frac{b}{\sqrt{2}} \\ \frac{a}{\sqrt{2}} + \frac{b}{\sqrt{2}} & a^2 + \frac{1}{2} \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$$ Equating diagonal terms: $\frac{1}{2} + b^2 = 1 \implies b^2 = 1/2 \implies b = \pm 1/\sqrt{2}$. Similarly, $a^2 + 1/2 = 1 \implies a = \pm 1/\sqrt{2}$. Equating off-diagonal terms: $\frac{a}{\sqrt{2}} + \frac{b}{\sqrt{2}} = 0 \implies a + b = 0 \implies b = -a$. Therefore, either $(a = -1/\sqrt{2}, b = 1/\sqrt{2})$ or $(a = 1/\sqrt{2}, b = -1/\sqrt{2})$. Both options B and C satisfy $a+b=0$ and norm conditions. Hence D is correct.
Q2. Consider the complex matrix $A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1+i & 3-2i \\ 1-i & 0 & 1+i \\ 3+2i & 1-i & 4 \end{bmatrix}$. Which of the following statements regarding $A$ is strictly TRUE?
A) $A$ is Skew-Hermitian, so all its eigenvalues are purely imaginary.
B) $A$ is Hermitian, and all its eigenvalues are strictly real numbers.
C) $A$ is Unitary, so its determinant must equal $+1$.
D) $A$ is Symmetric, so its eigenvectors are always orthogonal in $\mathbb{R}^3$.
Let's check $A^H = (\bar{A})^T$: Diagonal elements are real: $2, 0, 4$. Off-diagonal elements: $a_{12} = 1+i$, $a_{21} = 1-i = \bar{a}_{12}$. $a_{13} = 3-2i$, $a_{31} = 3+2i = \bar{a}_{13}$. $a_{23} = 1+i$, $a_{32} = 1-i = \bar{a}_{23}$. Since $a_{ij} = \bar{a}_{ji}$, $A$ is a Hermitian matrix ($A^H = A$). By Theorem 1, all eigenvalues of a Hermitian matrix are strictly real numbers.
Q3. If $A$ is an $n \times n$ Skew-Hermitian matrix ($A^H = -A$), which of the following matrices is guaranteed to be Hermitian?
A) $i A$
B) $A^2$
C) $-A^2$
D) All of the above
Let $A^H = -A$. 1) $(i A)^H = \bar{i} A^H = (-i)(-A) = i A$. Thus $i A$ is Hermitian. 2) $(A^2)^H = (A A)^H = A^H A^H = (-A)(-A) = A^2$. Thus $A^2$ is Hermitian. 3) $(-A^2)^H = -(A^2)^H = -A^2$. Wait, $(-A^2)^H = -A^2$ means $-A^2$ is ALSO Hermitian! Since options A, B, and C are all Hermitian matrices, option D ("All of the above") is correct.
Q4. An aircraft's Direction Cosine Matrix $C_b^n$ transforms vectors from the body frame to the navigation frame. If $C_b^n = \begin{bmatrix} \cos\theta & 0 & \sin\theta \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ -\sin\theta & 0 & \cos\theta \end{bmatrix}$, what is $(C_b^n)^{-1}$?
A) $\begin{bmatrix} \cos\theta & 0 & -\sin\theta \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ \sin\theta & 0 & \cos\theta \end{bmatrix}$
B) $\begin{bmatrix} -\cos\theta & 0 & \sin\theta \\ 0 & -1 & 0 \\ \sin\theta & 0 & -\cos\theta \end{bmatrix}$
C) $\begin{bmatrix} \sin\theta & 0 & \cos\theta \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ -\cos\theta & 0 & \sin\theta \end{bmatrix}$
D) $C_b^n$ cannot be inverted because $\det(C_b^n) = 0$.
$C_b^n$ is a real rotation matrix, which is orthogonal. For any orthogonal matrix $M$, $M^{-1} = M^T$. Taking transpose of $C_b^n$: $$(C_b^n)^T = \begin{bmatrix} \cos\theta & 0 & -\sin\theta \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ \sin\theta & 0 & \cos\theta \end{bmatrix}$$ This matches Option A directly.
Q5. Let $U$ be a $3 \times 3$ complex Unitary matrix. If two of its eigenvalues are $\lambda_1 = 1$ and $\lambda_2 = i$, what is the absolute value of the determinant $|\det(U)|$?
A) $0$
B) $1$
C) $\sqrt{2}$
D) Cannot be determined without $\lambda_3$
For any Unitary matrix $U$, $U^H U = I$. Taking the determinant on both sides: $\det(U^H U) = \det(I) \implies \det(U^H)\det(U) = 1$. Since $\det(U^H) = \overline{\det(U)}$, we have $\overline{\det(U)} \cdot \det(U) = |\det(U)|^2 = 1 \implies |\det(U)| = 1$. This holds universally for ALL unitary matrices, regardless of individual eigenvalue specifications.
Q6. The characteristic equation of a $2 \times 2$ real orthogonal matrix $A$ is $\lambda^2 - \sqrt{3}\lambda + 1 = 0$. What is the physical angle of rotation $\theta$ represented by matrix $A$?
A) $30^\circ$ ($\pi/6$)
B) $45^\circ$ ($\pi/4$)
C) $60^\circ$ ($\pi/3$)
D) $90^\circ$ ($\pi/2$)
A $2 \times 2$ real rotation matrix is $A = \begin{bmatrix} \cos\theta & -\sin\theta \\ \sin\theta & \cos\theta \end{bmatrix}$. Its characteristic equation is: $$\det(A - \lambda I) = (\cos\theta - \lambda)^2 + \sin^2\theta = \lambda^2 - 2\cos\theta \lambda + (\cos^2\theta + \sin^2\theta) = \lambda^2 - 2\cos\theta \lambda + 1 = 0$$ Comparing this with the given equation $\lambda^2 - \sqrt{3}\lambda + 1 = 0$: $$2\cos\theta = \sqrt{3} \implies \cos\theta = \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2} \implies \theta = 30^\circ \quad (\pi/6 \text{ rad})$$
Q7. Let $A$ be a $3 \times 3$ real symmetric matrix with eigenvalues $\lambda_1 = 2$, $\lambda_2 = 5$, $\lambda_3 = -1$. Corresponding eigenvectors are $x_1, x_2, x_3$. Which of the following inner products MUST be zero?
A) $x_1^T x_2$ only
B) $x_2^T x_3$ only
C) $x_1^T x_3$ only
D) All three inner products: $x_1^T x_2 = x_2^T x_3 = x_1^T x_3 = 0$
A real symmetric matrix is a special case of a Hermitian matrix. By Theorem 2, eigenvectors corresponding to distinct eigenvalues of a Hermitian matrix are mutually orthogonal. Since $\lambda_1 = 2, \lambda_2 = 5, \lambda_3 = -1$ are three distinct eigenvalues, all three pairs of eigenvectors are mutually orthogonal: $x_1 \perp x_2, x_2 \perp x_3, x_1 \perp x_3 \implies x_1^T x_2 = x_2^T x_3 = x_1^T x_3 = 0$.
Q8. For any vector $x \in \mathbb{R}^n$, an orthogonal transformation matrix $Q$ satisfies which norm preservation equality?
A) $\| Q x \| = \det(Q) \| x \|$
B) $\| Q x \| = \| x \|$
C) $\| Q x \| = \lambda_{\max} \| x \|$
D) $\| Q x \| = \operatorname{Tr}(Q) \| x \|$
Using the Euclidean norm definition $\| v \|^2 = v^T v$: $$\| Q x \|^2 = (Q x)^T (Q x) = x^T Q^T Q x$$ Since $Q$ is orthogonal, $Q^T Q = I_n$: $$\| Q x \|^2 = x^T I_n x = x^T x = \| x \|^2 \implies \| Q x \| = \| x \|$$ Thus orthogonal matrices preserve vector lengths (isometry).
Q9. Consider the matrix $H = \begin{bmatrix} a & i \\ -i & b \end{bmatrix}$. Under what condition on real numbers $a$ and $b$ is $H$ positive definite?
A) $a > 0$ and $a b > 1$
B) $a > 0$ and $a b > 0$
C) $a > 0$ and $a b > -1$
D) $a + b > 0$ and $a b = 0$
Matrix $H$ is Hermitian because $H^H = \begin{bmatrix} a & i \\ -i & b \end{bmatrix}^H = \begin{bmatrix} a & i \\ -i & b \end{bmatrix} = H$ (since $a, b \in \mathbb{R}$). By Sylvester's Criterion, a Hermitian matrix is Positive Definite if and only if all its leading principal minors are positive: 1) First principal minor: $\det([a]) = a > 0$. 2) Second principal minor (determinant of $H$): $$\det(H) = a b - (i)(-i) = a b - (-i^2) = a b - 1 > 0 \implies a b > 1$$ Hence, $a > 0$ and $a b > 1$.
Q10. What is the Rayleigh quotient $R(x) = \frac{x^H A x}{x^H x}$ maximum value for the Hermitian matrix $A = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 0 \\ 0 & 7 \end{bmatrix}$ over all non-zero complex vectors $x \in \mathbb{C}^2$?
A) $3$
B) $5$
C) $7$
D) $10$
By Rayleigh-Ritz theorem for Hermitian matrices: $$\lambda_{\min} \le \frac{x^H A x}{x^H x} \le \lambda_{\max}$$ The eigenvalues of diagonal matrix $A = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 0 \\ 0 & 7 \end{bmatrix}$ are simply its diagonal entries $\lambda_1 = 3$ and $\lambda_2 = 7$. Therefore, $\lambda_{\max} = 7$, which is the maximum achievable value of $R(x)$ (achieved when $x = [0, 1]^T$).
Q11. Let $S$ be a real skew-symmetric matrix ($S^T = -S$). According to Cayley's transformation, the matrix $A = (I - S)(I + S)^{-1}$ is ALWAYS:
A) Symmetric
B) Orthogonal
C) Skew-Symmetric
D) Singular
Let $A = (I - S)(I + S)^{-1}$. Compute $A^T A$: Note that $(I - S)^T = I - S^T = I + S$ and $((I + S)^{-1})^T = (I + S^T)^{-1} = (I - S)^{-1}$. $$A^T = ((I + S)^{-1})^T (I - S)^T = (I - S)^{-1} (I + S)$$ Because $(I - S)$ and $(I + S)$ commute: $$A^T A = (I - S)^{-1} (I + S) (I - S) (I + S)^{-1} = (I - S)^{-1} (I - S) (I + S) (I + S)^{-1} = I \cdot I = I$$ Since $A^T A = I$, $A$ is an Orthogonal matrix.
Q12. Given a real $3 \times 3$ orthogonal matrix $A$ with $\det(A) = -1$. If two of its eigenvalues are $\lambda_1 = -1$ and $\lambda_2 = e^{i\pi/3}$, what is the third eigenvalue $\lambda_3$?
A) $e^{-i\pi/3}$
B) $+1$
C) $-1$
D) $e^{i2\pi/3}$
For a real matrix, complex eigenvalues MUST occur in complex conjugate pairs. Given $\lambda_2 = e^{i\pi/3}$, its complex conjugate MUST be present as an eigenvalue: $$\lambda_3 = \overline{\lambda_2} = e^{-i\pi/3}$$ Let's verify determinant: $\det(A) = \lambda_1 \cdot \lambda_2 \cdot \lambda_3 = (-1) \cdot (e^{i\pi/3}) \cdot (e^{-i\pi/3}) = (-1) \cdot e^0 = -1$. This perfectly satisfies $\det(A) = -1$.
Q13. In structural dynamics, the generalized modal matrix $\Phi$ diagonalizes both mass $[M]$ and stiffness $[K]$ matrices: $\Phi^T M \Phi = I$ and $\Phi^T K \Phi = \Omega^2$. This transformation relies fundamentally on:
A) Orthogonality of mode shapes with respect to mass and stiffness symmetric tensors.
B) Non-singularity of skew-symmetric damping matrices.
C) Unitary equivalence of unsymmetric dynamic influence coefficients.
D) Cayley-Hamilton theorem for defective matrices.
Mass matrix $[M]$ and stiffness matrix $[K]$ are real symmetric (and positive-definite) operators. Symmetric operators guarantee real eigenvalues ($\omega_r^2$) and mutually orthogonal eigenvectors (mode shapes) weighted by mass/stiffness tensors ($x_i^T M x_j = 0$ for $i \neq j$). This mass-orthogonality allows modal decoupling into $n$ independent scalar dynamic systems.

Q601. [Grand Mini-Test] Consider the matrix $P = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$. Evaluate the correctness of the following statements:
I. $P$ is a real Orthogonal matrix.
II. $P^3 = I_3$.
III. All eigenvalues of $P$ are real.
IV. $P^{-1} = P^T = P^2$.
A) I, II, and IV only
B) I and III only
C) II and IV only
D) All statements I, II, III, IV are correct
Let's analyze each statement: Statement I: $P^T P = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} = I$. True. Statement II: $P$ is a cyclic permutation matrix of order 3. $P^3 = I$. True. Statement III: Characteristic equation of $P$: $\det(P - \lambda I) = 1 - \lambda^3 = 0 \implies \lambda^3 = 1$. Roots are $\lambda = 1, e^{i2\pi/3}, e^{-i2\pi/3}$. Two roots are complex! Statement III is FALSE. Statement IV: Since $P$ is orthogonal, $P^{-1} = P^T$. Also $P^3 = I \implies P^2 = P^{-1}$. Thus $P^{-1} = P^T = P^2$. True. Therefore, Statements I, II, and IV only are correct.
Q602. [Grand Mini-Test] Let $A \in \mathbb{C}^{n \times n}$ be a Hermitian matrix and $B \in \mathbb{C}^{n \times n}$ be a Skew-Hermitian matrix. Define $C = A + B$. Which of the following expressions ALWAYS yields a purely real number?
A) $\operatorname{Tr}(C)$
B) $\det(C)$
C) $x^H A x$ for any vector $x \in \mathbb{C}^n$
D) $x^H B x$ for any vector $x \in \mathbb{C}^n$
Let's test $q = x^H A x$ where $A^H = A$: Take conjugate transpose of scalar $q$: $$q^H = (x^H A x)^H = x^H A^H (x^H)^H = x^H A x = q$$ A complex scalar that equals its conjugate transpose ($q^H = \bar{q} = q$) MUST be purely real! Conversely, for skew-Hermitian $B$, $x^H B x$ is purely imaginary or zero. Trace of $C$ has imaginary parts from $B$, so option C is uniquely guaranteed to be strictly real.
Q603. [Grand Mini-Test] Statement I: The product of two Hermitian matrices $A$ and $B$ is Hermitian if and only if $A$ and $B$ commute ($A B = B A$).
Statement II: The trace of any Skew-Hermitian matrix is zero.
A) Both Statement I and Statement II are correct
B) Statement I is correct, but Statement II is incorrect
C) Statement I is incorrect, but Statement II is correct
D) Both Statement I and Statement II are incorrect
Statement I: $(A B)^H = B^H A^H = B A$ (since $A, B$ are Hermitian). For $(A B)^H = A B$, we require $B A = A B$. Thus $A B$ is Hermitian iff $A$ and $B$ commute. Statement I is CORRECT. Statement II: For a skew-Hermitian matrix $S$, diagonal elements $s_{ii}$ are purely imaginary (e.g., $s_{11} = 2i, s_{22} = -i$). Sum of diagonal elements (trace) is $\operatorname{Tr}(S) = 2i - i = i \neq 0$. Trace of skew-Hermitian matrix is purely imaginary or zero, NOT strictly zero. Statement II is INCORRECT.
Q604. [Grand Mini-Test] An aircraft flight stability matrix $A$ satisfies $A^T P + P A = -Q$. If $P$ is a real Symmetric Positive Definite matrix ($P^T = P > 0$) and $Q$ is symmetric positive definite, what can be deduced about the eigenvalues $\lambda_i$ of matrix $A$?
A) All eigenvalues of $A$ have strictly positive real parts ($\operatorname{Re}(\lambda_i) > 0$).
B) All eigenvalues of $A$ have strictly negative real parts ($\operatorname{Re}(\lambda_i) < 0$).
C) All eigenvalues of $A$ are purely imaginary.
D) All eigenvalues of $A$ are real and positive.
This is Lyapunov's Direct Method for Linear Time-Invariant (LTI) continuous systems. Let $A x = \lambda x$ with eigenvector $x \neq 0$. Premultiply Lyapunov equation by $x^H$ and postmultiply by $x$: $$x^H A^T P x + x^H P A x = -x^H Q x$$ $$(A x)^H P x + x^H P (A x) = (\lambda x)^H P x + x^H P (\lambda x) = \bar{\lambda} x^H P x + \lambda x^H P x = (\lambda + \bar{\lambda}) x^H P x = 2 \operatorname{Re}(\lambda) x^H P x$$ Equating: $$2 \operatorname{Re}(\lambda) (x^H P x) = -x^H Q x$$ Since $P$ and $Q$ are Positive Definite, $x^H P x > 0$ and $x^H Q x > 0$. Therefore: $$\operatorname{Re}(\lambda) = \frac{-x^H Q x}{2 (x^H P x)} < 0$$ All eigenvalues of $A$ have strictly negative real parts ($\operatorname{Re}(\lambda_i) < 0$), proving asymptotic stability of the aircraft dynamic mode!

SQUARE MATRICES IN AEROSPACE MATHEMATICS
├── Real Space ($\mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$)
│ ├── Symmetric ($A^T = A$) ──► Real $\lambda_i$, Orthogonal Eigenvectors

└── Complex Space ($\mathbb{C}^{n \times n}$)
├── Hermitian ($A^H = A$) ──► Real $\lambda_i$, Orthogonal Eigenvectors in $\mathbb{C}^n$
├── Skew-Hermitian ($A^H = -A$) ──► Purely Imaginary/Zero $\lambda_i$, Pure Imaginary/Zero Diagonal
└── Unitary ($U^H U = I$) ──► $|\det|=1$, $|\lambda_i|=1$, Preserves Complex Norm
