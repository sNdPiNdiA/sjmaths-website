Fourier transform properties, Riemann-Lebesgue

Day 2 of 129 Engineering Fundamentals Phase 1: Foundations 60-90 min Master Cycle

1. Understand (5–10 min)

The Continuous Fourier Transform (CFT) is a pivotal linear integral transform that maps a continuous signal defined in the time domain $t \in \mathbb{R}$ (or spatial domain $x \in \mathbb{R}$) into a complex spectrum in the angular frequency domain $ω \in \mathbb{R}$ (or wavenumber $k \in \mathbb{R}$). For a complex-valued function $f(t) \in L^1(\mathbb{R})$, the Fourier transform operator $\mathcal{F}: L^1(\mathbb{R}) \to L^∞(\mathbb{R})$ is defined as:

$$ F(ω) = \mathcal{F}\{f(t)\} = ∫-∞∞ f(t) e-iω t \, dt $$

The original function $f(t)$ is recovered via the Inverse Fourier Transform $\mathcal{F}-1$:

$$ f(t) = \mathcal{F}-1\{F(ω)\} = 12π ∫-∞∞ F(ω) eiω t \, dω $$

Vector Space Context & Mathematical Scope

In vector space analysis, $L^1(\mathbb{R})$ denotes the Banach space of absolutely integrable functions equipped with the norm $\|f\|_1 = ∫-∞∞ |f(t)| \, dt < ∞$. The space $L^2(\mathbb{R})$ represents the Hilbert space of square-integrable functions with the inner product $\langle f, g \rangle = ∫-∞∞ f(t) \overline{g(t)} \, dt$. While the fundamental integral for $F(ω)$ is strictly defined on $L^1(\mathbb{R})$, Plancherel's theorem extends the domain of $\mathcal{F}$ as an isometric isomorphism (up to a factor of $2π$) onto $L^2(\mathbb{R})$.

The Riemann-Lebesgue Lemma

The Riemann-Lebesgue Lemma provides the crucial asymptotic boundary condition for Fourier transforms of integrable functions. It asserts that if $f \in L^1(\mathbb{R})$, the magnitude of its Fourier transform decays to zero as the frequency parameter approaches infinity:

$$ \lim|ω| \to ∞ F(ω) = \lim|ω| \to ∞ ∫-∞∞ f(t) e-iω t \, dt = 0 $$

In the context of real-valued integrals, this implies:

$$ \limλ \to ∞ ∫ab f(t) \sin(λ t) \, dt = 0 \quad and \quad \limλ \to ∞ ∫ab f(t) \cos(λ t) \, dt = 0 $$

This property plays a fundamental role in proving the convergence of Fourier series, analyzing high-frequency wave interactions, and guaranteeing spectral decay in dynamic system models.

UPSC Standard Terminology

  • Forward Transform Kernel: $K(t, ω) = e-iω t$
  • Inverse Transform Kernel: $K-1(ω, t) = 12π eiω t$
  • Energy Spectral Density (ESD): $Sff(ω) = |F(ω)|^2$
  • Asymptotic Decay Rate: The rate at which $F(ω) \to 0$ as $ω \to ∞$, governed by the degree of continuity and differentiability of $f(t)$.

2. Learn the Concept (15–20 min)

A thorough mastery of the Continuous Fourier Transform requires rigorous analytical derivations of its foundational operational properties and mathematical theorems.

1. Linearity

For any constants $c_1, c_2 \in \mathbb{C}$ and functions $f_1(t), f_2(t) \in L^1(\mathbb{R})$:

$$ \mathcal{F}\{c_1 f_1(t) + c_2 f_2(t)\} = c_1 F_1(ω) + c_2 F_2(ω) $$

2. Time Shifting Property

Shifting a function in the time domain by a real delay $t_0$ introduces a linear phase shift in the frequency domain:

$$ \mathcal{F}\{f(t - t_0)\} = e-iω t_0 F(ω) $$
$$ Proof: \quad \mathcal{F}\{f(t - t_0)\} = ∫-∞∞ f(t - t_0) e-iω t \, dt $$ $$ Let u = t - t_0 \implies t = u + t_0, \, dt = du $$ $$ \mathcal{F}\{f(t - t_0)\} = ∫-∞∞ f(u) e-iω (u + t_0) \, du = e-iω t_0 ∫-∞∞ f(u) e-iω u \, du = e-iω t_0 F(ω) $$

3. Frequency Shifting (Modulation Property)

Multiplying a time signal by a complex exponential shifts its spectrum along the frequency axis:

$$ \mathcal{F}\{eiω_0 t f(t)\} = F(ω - ω_0) $$

Consequently, for real amplitude modulation using trigonometric carriers:

$$ \mathcal{F}\{f(t) \cos(ω_0 t)\} = 12 [ F(ω - ω_0) + F(ω + ω_0) ] $$ $$ \mathcal{F}\{f(t) \sin(ω_0 t)\} = 12i [ F(ω - ω_0) - F(ω + ω_0) ] $$

4. Time Scaling Property

Compressing a signal in time expands its spectrum in frequency, maintaining an invariant total energy profile:

$$ \mathcal{F}\{f(at)\} = 1|a| F(ωa), \quad a \in \mathbb{R}, \, a \neq 0 $$
$$ Proof: \quad \mathcal{F}\{f(at)\} = ∫-∞∞ f(at) e-iω t \, dt $$ $$ Case 1: a > 0. Let u = at \implies t = ua, \, dt = dua $$ $$ ∫-∞∞ f(u) e-i ωa u dua = 1a F(ωa) $$ $$ Case 2: a < 0. Let u = at \implies t = ua, \, dt = dua (limits swap) $$ $$ ∫∞-∞ f(u) e-i ωa u dua = -1a ∫-∞∞ f(u) e-i ωa u \, du = 1-a F(ωa) $$ $$ Combining both cases yields: \mathcal{F}\{f(at)\} = 1|a| F(ωa) $$

5. Combined Time Shift and Scaling

For a combined transformation $f(at - b) = f(a(t - ba))$:

$$ \mathcal{F}\{f(at - b)\} = 1|a| e-i ba ω F(ωa) $$

6. Differentiation in Time Domain

If $f(t)$ is continuous and piecewise smooth with $f(t) \to 0$ as $|t| \to ∞$, then:

$$ \mathcal{F}\left\{ d^n f(t)dt^n \right\} = (iω)^n F(ω) $$
$$ Proof for n=1: \quad \mathcal{F}\left\{dfdt\right\} = ∫-∞∞ df(t)dt e-iω t \, dt $$ $$ Using Integration by Parts: u = e-iω t, \, dv = df(t)dt dt $$ $$ \mathcal{F}\left\{dfdt\right\} = [ f(t) e-iω t ]-∞∞ - ∫-∞∞ f(t) (-iω e-iω t) \, dt $$ $$ Since \lim|t| \to ∞ f(t) = 0 \implies \mathcal{F}\left\{dfdt\right\} = 0 + iω ∫-∞∞ f(t) e-iω t \, dt = iω F(ω) $$

7. Differentiation in Frequency Domain

Multiplying a signal by $t^n$ in time corresponds to differentiation in frequency:

$$ \mathcal{F}\{t^n f(t)\} = i^n d^n F(ω)dω^n \implies \mathcal{F}\{(-it)^n f(t)\} = d^n F(ω)dω^n $$

8. Convolution Theorem

The Fourier transform of the convolution of two signals $f(t) * g(t) = ∫-∞∞ f(τ) g(t - τ) \, dτ$ equals the pointwise product of their individual transforms:

$$ \mathcal{F}\{(f * g)(t)\} = F(ω) G(ω) $$

Conversely, convolution in the frequency domain corresponds to multiplication in the time domain:

$$ \mathcal{F}\{f(t) g(t)\} = 12π (F * G)(ω) = 12π ∫-∞∞ F(u) G(ω - u) \, du $$

9. Parseval's Identity & Plancherel's Theorem

Parseval's relation guarantees conservation of total energy across the time and frequency domains:

$$ E = ∫-∞∞ |f(t)|^2 \, dt = 12π ∫-∞∞ |F(ω)|^2 \, dω $$

More generally, for two functions $f(t)$ and $g(t)$:

$$ ∫-∞∞ f(t) \overline{g(t)} \, dt = 12π ∫-∞∞ F(ω) \overline{G(ω)} \, dω $$

10. Detailed Derivation of the Riemann-Lebesgue Lemma

Let $f(t)$ be a step function defined on $[a, b]$, i.e., $f(t) = 1$ for $t \in [a, b]$ and $0$ elsewhere. Its Fourier transform is:

$$ F(ω) = ∫ab e-iω t \, dt = [ e-iω t-iω ]ab = e-iω a - e-iω biω $$

Taking the modulus of $F(ω)$:

$$ |F(ω)| ≤ |e-iω a| + |e-iω b||ω| = 1 + 1|ω| = 2|ω| $$

As $|ω| \to ∞$, $|F(ω)| ≤ 2|ω| \to 0$. By linearity, the lemma holds for any finite linear combination of step functions (simple functions). Since simple functions are dense in $L^1(\mathbb{R})$, for any $f \in L^1(\mathbb{R})$ and $ε > 0$, there exists a simple function $ψ$ such that $\|f - ψ\|_1 < ε2$. Thus:

$$ |F(ω)| ≤ ∫-∞∞ |f(t) - ψ(t)| \, dt + | ∫-∞∞ ψ(t) e-iω t \, dt | < ε2 + ε2 = ε $$

for sufficiently large $|ω|$, proving that $\lim|ω| \to ∞ F(ω) = 0$.

4. Aviation Application (5–10 min)

Fourier Transform properties and the Riemann-Lebesgue Lemma form the backbone of several core aerospace engineering disciplines, providing exact analytical tools for structural, control, and acoustic signal evaluations.

1. Dynamic Flutter Analysis & Aeroelasticity

Aircraft structural wings subjected to unsteady aerodynamic forces are modeled via integro-differential motion equations. The flutter boundary is determined by transforming structural displacement vectors $q(t)$ into the frequency domain. Derivatives of structural displacement $\dot{q}(t)$ and $\ddot{q}(t)$ map directly to $iω Q(ω)$ and $-ω^2 Q(ω)$ via the time-differentiation property. This converts complex system ordinary/partial differential equations into algebraic characteristic matrix systems:

$$ [ -ω^2 \mathbf{M} + iω \mathbf{C} + \mathbf{K} - q_∞ \mathbf{A}(k) ] \mathbf{Q}(ω) = \mathbf{0} $$

where $q_∞$ is dynamic pressure, $k = ω bV$ is reduced frequency, and $\mathbf{A}(k)$ is the non-dimensional aerodynamic influence coefficient matrix.

2. Jet Engine Health Monitoring & Acoustic Signal Processing

In turbofan engines, transient pressure spikes from blade passing frequencies (BPF) and structural vibration signals registered by Flight Data Recorders (FDR) are processed via Fourier transforms. According to the Riemann-Lebesgue Lemma, the high-frequency components of any physically integrable acoustic pulse $p(t) \in L^1(\mathbb{R})$ must decay asymptotically to zero ($\limω \to ∞ P(ω) = 0$). Deviations from this mathematical decay rate indicate non-integrable singularities in sensor data, signaling severe mechanical defects such as micro-cracks, blade shearing, or localized acoustic shockwaves (blade stall).

3. Aircraft Stability & Control Systems Identification

In flight control testing, continuous state-space models $\dot{\mathbf{x}}(t) = \mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}(t) + \mathbf{B}\mathbf{u}(t)$ are converted using Fourier transforms to identify system transfer function matrices $\mathbf{H}(jω) = (jω \mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A})-1 \mathbf{B}$. Time-delay characteristics introduced by mechanical actuator lag or digital flight control compute cycles ($t - τ_d$) map directly to phase delays $e-iω τ_d \mathbf{H}(jω)$ via the time-shifting property, enabling precise stability margin calculations (Gain and Phase Margins) on Bode diagrams.

5. UPSC ASO Focus (10 min)

  • Frequently Asked Facts:
    • Scaling property sign trap: $\mathcal{F}\{f(-2t)\} = 1|-2| F(ω-2) = 12 F(-ω2)$. The absolute value $|a|$ in the denominator is essential.
    • Area under curve property: Evaluating $F(ω)$ at $ω = 0$ gives the total area under $f(t)$, i.e., $F(0) = ∫-∞∞ f(t) \, dt$.
    • Duality relation: If $\mathcal{F}\{f(t)\} = F(ω)$, then $\mathcal{F}\{F(t)\} = 2π f(-ω)$.
    • Parseval's factor: The scaling factor $12π$ appears in $∫ |f(t)|^2 dt = 12π∫ |F(ω)|^2 dω$ when using angular frequency $ω$. If ordinary frequency $f$ ($ω = 2π f$) is used, the factor is $1$.
    • Riemann-Lebesgue scope: Applies strictly to $L^1$ functions. It does not apply directly to ideal periodic functions or generalized functions like Dirac delta $\delta(t)$ whose Fourier transform is $1$ (constant for all $ω$).
  • Conceptual Traps:
    • Shift in scaling: For $\mathcal{F}\{f(3t - 6)\}$, students erroneously write $e-i 6 ω 13 F(ω3)$. The correct expression requires factoring out the scaling constant: $f(3(t - 2))$, yielding $13 e-i 2 ω F(ω3)$.
    • Frequency domain differentiation sign: $\mathcal{F}\{t f(t)\} = i d F(ω)dω$, whereas $\mathcal{F}\{-i t f(t)\} = d F(ω)dω$. Mixing up powers of $i$ is a standard UPSC multiple-choice trap.
    • Symmetry properties: If $f(t)$ is real and even, $F(ω)$ is real and even. If $f(t)$ is real and odd, $F(ω)$ is purely imaginary and odd.

B) $39 + (ω/2)^2 e-i 2 ω$
C) $39 + (ω/2)^2 e-i 4 ω$

Using the combined scale and shift property $\mathcal{F}\{f(a(t - t_0))\} = 1|a| e-iω t_0 F(ωa)$:
$\mathcal{F}\{g(t)\} = 12 e-i 2 ω F(ω2) = 12 e-i 2 ω ( 69 + (ω/2)^2 ) = 39 + (ω/2)^2 e-i 2 ω$.
Q2. According to the Riemann-Lebesgue Lemma, if $f(t)$ is an integrable function on $\mathbb{R}$ ($f \in L^1(\mathbb{R})$), which of the following statements regarding its Fourier transform $F(ω)$ is strictly true?
A) $\lim|ω| \to ∞ F(ω) = C$, where $C$ is a non-zero finite constant.
B) $\lim|ω| \to ∞ F(ω) = 0$.
C) $F(ω)$ must be bounded and strictly non-zero for all $ω \in \mathbb{R}$.
D) $∫-∞∞ |F(ω)| \, dω < ∞$ for all $f \in L^1(\mathbb{R})$.
The Riemann-Lebesgue Lemma states directly that for any $f \in L^1(\mathbb{R})$, the Fourier transform vanishes at infinity: $\lim|ω| \to ∞ F(ω) = 0$. Option D is not universally true for all $L^1$ functions (as $F(ω)$ might not be integrable over $\mathbb{R}$).
Q3. Evaluate the definite integral $I = ∫-∞∞ 1(t^2 + 16)^2 \, dt$ using Parseval's identity and the Fourier transform pair $\mathcal{F}\{e-a|t|\} = 2aa^2 + ω^2$ (for $a > 0$).
A) $π32$
B) $π64$
C) $π128$
D) $π16$
Let $a = 4$. Then $f(t) = e-4|t| \xleftrightarrow{\mathcal{F}} F(ω) = 8ω^2 + 16$.
By Parseval's Identity: $∫-∞∞ |f(t)|^2 \, dt = 12π ∫-∞∞ |F(ω)|^2 \, dω$.
Left side: $∫-∞∞ e-8|t| \, dt = 2 ∫0∞ e-8t \, dt = 2 [18] = 14$.
Right side: $12π ∫-∞∞ (8ω^2 + 16)^2 \, dω = 642π ∫-∞∞ 1(ω^2 + 16)^2 \, dω = 32π I$.
Equating both sides: $14 = 32π I \implies I = π128$.
Q4. If $\mathcal{F}\{f(t)\} = F(ω)$, what is the Fourier transform of $g(t) = t^2 e-2t u(t)$, where $u(t)$ is the unit step function?
A) $2(2 + iω)^3$
B) $1(2 + iω)^3$
C) $2i(2 + iω)^3$
D) $-2(2 + iω)^3$
We know $\mathcal{F}\{e-2t u(t)\} = 12 + iω$.
Using frequency differentiation property: $\mathcal{F}\{t^2 f(t)\} = i^2 d^2 F(ω)dω^2 = -d^2dω^2 ( 12 + iω )$.
First derivative w.r.t $ω$: $ddω (2 + iω)-1 = -(2 + iω)-2 · i = -i(2 + iω)^2$.
Second derivative: $ddω [ -i (2 + iω)-2 ] = -i (-2)(2 + iω)-3 · i = 2 i^2 (2 + iω)-3 = -2(2 + iω)^3$.
Therefore, $\mathcal{F}\{t^2 e-2t u(t)\} = - ( -2(2 + iω)^3 ) = 2(2 + iω)^3$.
Q5. For a real and odd function $f(t)$, which statement correctly describes its Fourier transform $F(ω)$?
A) $F(ω)$ is purely real and even.
B) $F(ω)$ is purely real and odd.
C) $F(ω)$ is purely imaginary and even.
D) $F(ω)$ is purely imaginary and odd.
$F(ω) = ∫-∞∞ f(t) [\cos(ω t) - i \sin(ω t)] \, dt$.
If $f(t)$ is real and odd: $f(t)\cos(ω t)$ is odd (integrates to 0), and $f(t)\sin(ω t)$ is even.
Thus $F(ω) = -i ∫-∞∞ f(t) \sin(ω t) \, dt = -2i ∫0∞ f(t) \sin(ω t) \, dt$.
This result is purely imaginary. Replacing $ω$ with $-ω$ flips the sign, proving $F(ω)$ is odd.

Here $F(t) = 6t^2 + 9 \xleftrightarrow{\mathcal{F}} 2π e-3|-ω| = 2π e-3|ω|$.

Q10. Two continuous signals $f(t)$ and $g(t)$ have Fourier transforms $F(ω) = \operatorname{rect}(ω/2)$ and $G(ω) = 12 + iω$. What is the Fourier transform of their time-domain product $h(t) = f(t) · g(t)$?

Substituting gives: $\mathcal{F}\{h(t)\} = 12π ∫-11 12 + i(ω - u) \, du$.

Q14. The dynamic response of a wing section undergoing plunging oscillations produces an unsteady lift force modeled by $L(t) = d^2dt^2[e-2t \cos(5t) u(t)]$. What is $L(ω)$?
A) $-ω^2 (2 + iω)(2 + iω)^2 + 25$

4. $\mathcal{F}\left\{dp(t)dt\right\} = iω(a + iω)^2$.
A) 1 and 2 only
B) 1, 2, and 4 only
C) 2, 3, and 4 only

Q3 (Grand Mini Test). Evaluate the cross-correlation integral $J = ∫-∞∞ f(t) g(t) \, dt$ where $f(t) = \sin(2t)t$ and $g(t) = \sin(3t)t$.
A) $2π$
B) $π$
C) $3π$
D) $2π^2$
Using Plancherel / Parseval inner product theorem: $∫-∞∞ f(t) g(t) dt = 12π ∫-∞∞ F(ω) G(ω) dω$ (since $F, G$ are real).
$F(ω) = π \operatorname{rect}(ω / 4) \implies F(ω) = π$ for $|ω| ≤ 2$.
$G(ω) = π \operatorname{rect}(ω / 6) \implies G(ω) = π$ for $|ω| ≤ 3$.
The overlapping region where both $F(ω)$ and $G(ω)$ are non-zero is $|ω| ≤ 2$.
Thus $J = 12π ∫-22 (π)(π) \, dω = π^22π [ω]-22 = π2 (4) = 2π$.
