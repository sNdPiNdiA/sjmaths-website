Free vortex and forced vortex

Day 9 of 129 Fluid Mechanics & Machinery Phase 1: Foundations 90-120 min Master Class

1. Understand (From Absolute Zero, 5–10 min)

In fluid dynamics, a vortex is any flow configuration where fluid rotates around a common localized axis. To master this concept for the UPSC Air Safety Officer (ASO) exam, we must first establish the physical and mathematical distinction between the two primary classes of vortex motion: Forced Vortex and Free Vortex.

1. Intuitive Physical Analogies

  • The Forced Vortex (Solid-Body Rotation): Imagine a cup of coffee placed on a rotating turntable, or liquid inside a centrifuge. As the container spins, viscous shear forces transmit kinetic energy from the boundary inward until the entire fluid mass rotates as a single, rigid unit. Every fluid element has the exactly same angular velocity ($\omega$). If you place two small floating sticks in this flow, they will rotate about their own axes as they orbit the center. This indicates the flow is rotational.
  • The Free Vortex (Potential Vortex): Imagine water draining out of a sink, or the wind patterns of a tornado outside its core. No external torque or energy is supplied to keep the fluid spinning. The motion is sustained entirely by the fluid's inertia and the conservation of angular momentum. As fluid particles move inward toward the center, their tangential speed must increase. If you place a small floating stick in this flow, it will orbit the center, but its orientation will remain parallel to its initial direction (it does not rotate about its own center of mass). This indicates the flow is irrotational (except at the singular point at the origin $r=0$).

2. Key UPSC Structural Differences

Parameter Forced Vortex Free Vortex
Velocity Profile $v_\theta \propto r$ (linear increase) $v_\theta \propto \frac{1}{r}$ (hyperbolic decrease)
Angular Velocity ($\omega$) Constant throughout ($\omega = \text{const}$) Varies as $\omega(r) = \frac{C}{r^2}$
Vorticity ($\vec{\omega} = \nabla \times \vec{V}$_ Non-zero ($2\omega$) $\rightarrow$ Rotational Flow Zero everywhere ($\nabla \times \vec{V} = 0$) $\rightarrow$ Irrotational Flow (except $r=0$)
Energy Transfer External torque required; work is continuously done. No external torque; total mechanical energy remains constant.
Free Surface Profile Concave Paraboloid ($z \propto r^2$) Hyperbolic Depression ($z \propto -\frac{1}{r^2}$)

3. Standard UPSC Terminology & SI Units

  • Tangential Velocity ($v_\theta$): Velocity component perpendicular to the radial direction. Measured in meters per second ($\text{m/s}$).
  • Circulation ($\Gamma$): The line integral of the velocity vector around a closed curve. $\Gamma = \oint \vec{V} \cdot d\vec{s}$. Measured in square meters per second ($\text{m}^2/\text{s}$).
  • Vorticity ($\zeta$): The curl of the velocity vector. $\zeta = 2\vec{\omega} = \nabla \times \vec{V}$. Measured in reciprocal seconds ($\text{s}^{-1}$).
  • Angular Velocity ($\omega$): Rate of rotation of a fluid element. Measured in radians per second ($\text{rad/s}$).

2. Learn the Concept (Complete Theoretical Foundation & Derivations, 20–25 min)

To establish a rigorous theoretical foundation, we derive the governing differential equations of vortex flow starting from the Navier-Stokes equations in cylindrical polar coordinates $(r, \theta, z)$.

1. Governing Navier-Stokes Equations in Cylindrical Coordinates

For an incompressible, axisymmetric ($\frac{\partial}{\partial \theta} = 0$), and steady ($\frac{\partial}{\partial t} = 0$) flow with zero radial velocity ($v_r = 0$) and zero vertical velocity ($v_z = 0$), the Navier-Stokes momentum equations in the radial ($r$) and vertical ($z$) directions simplify directly to:

$$ \rho \left( -\frac{v_\theta^2}{r} \right) = -\frac{\partial p}{\partial r} + \rho g_r $$ $$ 0 = -\frac{\partial p}{\partial z} - \rho g $$

Assuming gravity acts vertically downwards ($g_r = 0$, $g_z = -g$), the equations governing the pressure field for both free and forced vortices are:

$$ \frac{\partial p}{\partial r} = \rho \frac{v_\theta^2}{r} $$ $$ \frac{\partial p}{\partial z} = -\rho g $$

The total differential of pressure $p(r,z)$ is given by:

$$ dp = \frac{\partial p}{\partial r} dr + \frac{\partial p}{\partial z} dz = \rho \frac{v_\theta^2}{r} dr - \rho g dz $$ ---

2. Rigorous Derivation of Forced Vortex Flow

In a forced vortex, the fluid rotates as a solid body. Therefore, the tangential velocity is linear with radius:

$$ v_\theta = \omega r $$

Substituting this velocity profile into our pressure differential equation:

$$ dp = \rho \frac{(\omega r)^2}{r} dr - \rho g dz = \rho \omega^2 r dr - \rho g dz $$

Integrating both sides between an arbitrary datum $(r_1, z_1)$ and point $(r_2, z_2)$:

$$ p_2 - p_1 = \frac{\rho \omega^2}{2} (r_2^2 - r_1^2) - \rho g (z_2 - z_1) $$

To find the equation of the free surface (where pressure is atmospheric, $p = p_{atm} = \text{constant}$), we set $dp = 0$:

$$ \rho \omega^2 r dr - \rho g dz = 0 \implies \frac{dz}{dr} = \frac{\omega^2 r}{g} $$

Integrating with respect to $r$ where $z = z_0$ at $r=0$:

$$ z = \frac{\omega^2 r^2}{2g} + z_0 $$

This is the equation of a paraboloid of revolution. An important geometric property tested in UPSC is that the volume of a paraboloid of height $H$ and radius $R$ is exactly half the volume of its circumscribing cylinder: $V_{\text{paraboloid}} = \frac{1}{2} \pi R^2 H$. Thus, when a cylinder of liquid rotates, the rise of liquid at the walls ($H/2$) is equal to the fall of liquid at the center ($H/2$) relative to the initial rest height.

---

3. Rigorous Derivation of Free Vortex Flow

For a free vortex, no external torque is applied. The conservation of angular momentum for a fluid element of mass $m$ dictates:

$$ m v_\theta r = \text{constant} \implies v_\theta = \frac{C}{r} $$

The constant $C$ is related to the circulation $\Gamma$. Let us evaluate circulation $\Gamma$ around a concentric circle of radius $r$:

$$ \Gamma = \oint \vec{V} \cdot d\vec{s} = \int_{0}^{2\pi} v_\theta (r d\theta) = v_\theta (2\pi r) \implies v_\theta = \frac{\Gamma}{2\pi r} $$

Substituting $v_\theta = \frac{\Gamma}{2\pi r}$ into our radial pressure differential equation:

$$ dp = \rho \frac{\left( \frac{\Gamma}{2\pi r} \right)^2}{r} dr - \rho g dz = \frac{\rho \Gamma^2}{4\pi^2 r^3} dr - \rho g dz $$

Integrating this equation from an outer radius $r_1$ to $r_2$:

$$ p_2 - p_1 = -\frac{\rho \Gamma^2}{8\pi^2} \left( \frac{1}{r_2^2} - \frac{1}{r_1^2} \right) - \rho g (z_2 - z_1) $$

For a free surface where $p = p_{atm}$, setting $dp = 0$ yields the profile:

$$ z = z_\infty - \frac{\Gamma^2}{8\pi^2 g r^2} $$

This is a hyperbolic profile. Notice that as $r \to 0$, $z \to -\infty$. In real fluids, viscosity prevents an infinite velocity and pressure drop at the core, leading to the formation of a **Rankine Combined Vortex** where the inner core is a forced vortex and the outer field is a free vortex.

---

4. Potential Flow Superpositions & Vorticity Theorems

In potential flow theory, we use complex potential $W(z) = \phi + i\psi$ (where $\phi$ is the velocity potential and $\psi$ is the stream function) to model irrotational flows. Because the free vortex is irrotational everywhere except the origin, it is represented as:

$$ \phi = -\frac{\Gamma}{2\pi} \theta, \quad \psi = \frac{\Gamma}{2\pi} \ln r $$

By superimposing simple flows, we can construct highly complex flows:

  • Source ($m$) + Free Vortex ($\Gamma$): Creates a spiral vortex (representing a drain or tornado inflow). $$ W(z) = \frac{m}{2\pi} \ln z + i \frac{\Gamma}{2\pi} \ln z $$
  • Doublet ($\mu$) + Uniform Flow ($U_\infty$) + Free Vortex ($\Gamma$): Represents the flow over a rotating cylinder, which is the foundational model for the Magnus Effect and the Kutta-Joukowski lift theorem: $$ L' = \rho_\infty V_\infty \Gamma $$
---

5. Boundary Layer Separation and Vorticity

The Navier-Stokes equations demonstrate that vorticity $\vec{\omega} = \nabla \times \vec{V}$ is generated at solid boundaries due to the no-slip condition. Under an adverse pressure gradient ($\frac{\partial p}{\partial x} > 0$), fluid particles in the boundary layer are decelerated. The point of boundary layer separation is mathematically defined when the wall shear stress becomes zero:

$$ \tau_w = \mu \left. \frac{\partial u}{\partial y} \right|_{y=0} = 0 $$

Downstream of this separation point, backflow occurs ($\left. \frac{\partial u}{\partial y} \right|_{y=0} < 0$), shedding concentrated vortices into the wake, which drastically increases pressure drag.

3. Memorize Important Facts (10 min)

  • Forced Vortex Velocity & Units: $v_\theta = \omega r$ [Units: $\text{m/s}$]
  • Free Vortex Velocity & Units: $v_\theta = \frac{\Gamma}{2\pi r}$ where $\Gamma$ is circulation [Units of $\Gamma$: $\text{m}^2/\text{s}$]
  • Vorticity ($\zeta$): $\zeta = 2\omega$ for Forced Vortex, and $\zeta = 0$ (for $r \neq 0$) for Free Vortex [Units: $\text{s}^{-1}$]
  • Forced Vortex Pressure Profile: $p = \frac{\rho \omega^2 r^2}{2} - \rho g z + C$
  • Free Vortex Pressure Profile: $p + \frac{1}{2}\rho v_\theta^2 + \rho g z = \text{constant}$ (Bernoulli's Equation holds globally)
  • Kutta-Joukowski Lift Theorem: $L' = \rho_\infty V_\infty \Gamma$ [Units: $\text{N/m}$]
  • Boundary Layer Separation Condition: $\left. \frac{\partial u}{\partial y} \right|_{y=0} = 0 \quad \text{with} \quad \frac{\partial p}{\partial x} > 0$

4. Aviation Application (5–10 min)

The physics of free and forced vortices governs several critical domains of aeronautical engineering and flight safety:

1. Wingtip Trailing Vortices (Wake Turbulence)

For a finite wing, the pressure difference between the lower surface (high pressure) and upper surface (low pressure) causes air to wrap around the wingtips from bottom to top. This transverse flow rolls up into a pair of counter-rotating trailing vortices behind the aircraft. Outside their core, these trailing vortices behave exactly like a free vortex ($v_\theta \propto 1/r$). This represents a major hazard for trailing aircraft, as the induced downwash can cause uncontrollable rolling moments. The circulation strength scales as:

$$ \Gamma = \frac{L}{\rho_\infty V_\infty b} $$

where $L$ is lift and $b$ is wingspan. Heavy aircraft at low speeds (such as during landing/take-off) generate the strongest, most hazardous free vortices.

2. Wing Stall Control via Vortex Generators

To prevent flow separation ($\left. \frac{\partial u}{\partial y} \right|_{y=0} = 0$) at high angles of attack, small physical blades called vortex generators are placed on the wing's upper surface. These blades act as tiny wings that shed small, highly energetic forced vortices. These vortices mix high-energy freestream air down into the slow-moving boundary layer, delaying the onset of stall and maintaining control effectiveness.

3. Wind Tunnel Scaling via Dimensionless Numbers

When testing scale models of swirling flows (such as helicopter rotors or vortex-induced vibrations of antennae) in a wind tunnel, strict dynamic similarity must be maintained. This requires matching the **Reynolds number** ($Re = \frac{\rho V L}{\mu}$) for viscous forces, and, if free-surface vortex interactions or gravity-dominated vortex cores (such as fuel tank draining vortices) are modeled, the **Froude number** ($Fr = \frac{V}{\sqrt{gL}}$).

5. UPSC ASO Focus (10 min)

  • Bernoulli's Equation Usability: In a free vortex, the flow is irrotational, so Bernoulli's equation can be applied between any two points in the entire flow field. In a forced vortex, the flow is rotational, so Bernoulli's equation can only be applied along the same streamline. Applying it across streamlines in a forced vortex is a classic UPSC trap.
  • Vorticity vs. Angular Velocity: Remember that vorticity is exactly twice the angular velocity vector ($\zeta = 2\omega$). If a question states that a fluid mass rotates at $10\text{ rad/s}$ as a solid body, its vorticity is $20\text{ s}^{-1}$.
  • Closed Cylinder Pressure rise: For a completely filled closed cylinder of radius $R$ and height $H$ rotated at angular speed $\omega$, the total force exerted by the fluid on the top cover is: $$ F = \int_0^R (p_{center} + \frac{1}{2}\rho \omega^2 r^2) \cdot 2\pi r dr = \frac{\pi \rho \omega^2 R^4}{4} + p_{center}\pi R^2 $$
  • Boundary Layer Separation: The critical condition for separation is $\left. \frac{\partial u}{\partial y} \right|_{y=0} = 0$. At this exact location, the wall shear stress $\tau_w$ is zero. The pressure gradient must be adverse ($\frac{\partial p}{\partial x} > 0$). If $\frac{\partial p}{\partial x} < 0$ (favorable pressure gradient), separation can never occur.

6. Practice Questions (30 Exhaustive UPSC ASO MCQs)

Q1. An open cylindrical vessel of radius $0.1\text{ m}$ is filled with water up to a height of $0.5\text{ m}$. If the vessel is rotated about its vertical axis at an angular velocity of $\omega = 20\text{ rad/s}$, calculate the height of the paraboloid of revolution formed at the free surface. (Take $g = 9.81\text{ m/s}^2$)
A) $0.102\text{ m}$
B) $0.204\text{ m}$
C) $0.408\text{ m}$
D) $0.051\text{ m}$
The height $H$ of a paraboloid of revolution formed by a forced vortex at the free surface is given by: $$ H = \frac{\omega^2 R^2}{2g} $$ Substituting the given values: $$ H = \frac{(20)^2 \cdot (0.1)^2}{2 \cdot 9.81} = \frac{400 \cdot 0.01}{19.62} = \frac{4}{19.62} \approx 0.20387\text{ m} \approx 0.204\text{ m} $$ Thus, Option B is correct.
Q2. For the rotating vessel in Q1, what is the vertical displacement of the water surface at the outer wall and at the center of rotation relative to its initial rest level?
A) Rises by $0.204\text{ m}$ at the wall, falls by $0.204\text{ m}$ at the center
B) Rises by $0.102\text{ m}$ at the wall, falls by $0.102\text{ m}$ at the center
C) Rises by $0.051\text{ m}$ at the wall, falls by $0.153\text{ m}$ at the center
D) Rises by $0.153\text{ m}$ at the wall, falls by $0.051\text{ m}$ at the center
By conservation of volume, the volume of a paraboloid is half the volume of a cylinder of the same height and radius. Therefore, the liquid level rises at the outer wall by exactly half of the total paraboloid height ($H/2$) and falls at the center by exactly half of the total paraboloid height ($H/2$). $$ \text{Rise} = \text{Fall} = \frac{H}{2} = \frac{0.204}{2} = 0.102\text{ m} $$ Thus, Option B is correct.
Q3. What is the maximum speed of rotation (in $\text{rad/s}$) to which an open cylinder of radius $R = 0.2\text{ m}$ and height $h = 1.0\text{ m}$, initially half-full of water, can be spun about its vertical axis without spilling any water?
A) $11.08\text{ rad/s}$
B) $22.15\text{ rad/s}$
C) $15.66\text{ rad/s}$
D) $31.32\text{ rad/s}$
The cylinder is half-full, so initial water height $h_0 = 0.5\text{ m}$. The free space is $h - h_0 = 1.0 - 0.5 = 0.5\text{ m}$. To prevent spilling, the maximum rise of water at the outer wall must be exactly equal to the free space: $$ \text{Rise} = \frac{H}{2} = 0.5\text{ m} \implies H = 1.0\text{ m} $$ Now, use the paraboloid formula: $$ H = \frac{\omega^2 R^2}{2g} \implies 1.0 = \frac{\omega^2 (0.2)^2}{2 \cdot 9.81} $$ $$ \omega^2 = \frac{2 \cdot 9.81 \cdot 1.0}{0.04} = \frac{19.62}{0.04} = 490.5 $$ $$ \omega = \sqrt{490.5} \approx 22.147\text{ rad/s} $$ Thus, Option B is correct.
Q4. A closed cylindrical vessel of radius $R = 0.3\text{ m}$ is completely filled with water. If it is rotated at an angular speed of $\omega = 10\text{ rad/s}$ about its vertical axis, what is the difference in pressure between the outer wall and the center of the cylinder at the same horizontal plane? (Density of water $\rho = 1000\text{ kg/m}^3$)
A) $1500\text{ Pa}$
B) $3000\text{ Pa}$
C) $4500\text{ Pa}$
D) $9000\text{ Pa}$
The pressure distribution in a forced vortex in a horizontal plane is: $$ p_2 - p_1 = \frac{\rho \omega^2}{2} (r_2^2 - r_1^2) $$ Let $r_1 = 0$ (center) and $r_2 = R$ (outer wall): $$ \Delta p = \frac{\rho \omega^2 R^2}{2} = \frac{1000 \cdot (10)^2 \cdot (0.3)^2}{2} $$ $$ \Delta p = \frac{1000 \cdot 100 \cdot 0.09}{2} = \frac{9000}{2} = 4500\text{ Pa} $$ Thus, Option C is correct.
Q5. In a free vortex, the tangential velocity at a radius of $0.2\text{ m}$ is $12\text{ m/s}$. Find the circulation $\Gamma$ around a closed circular path of this radius.
A) $2.4\pi\text{ m}^2/\text{s}$
B) $4.8\pi\text{ m}^2/\text{s}$
C) $1.2\pi\text{ m}^2/\text{s}$
D) $9.6\pi\text{ m}^2/\text{s}$
The tangential velocity in a free vortex is related to circulation by: $$ v_\theta = \frac{\Gamma}{2\pi r} \implies \Gamma = 2\pi r v_\theta $$ Substituting the given values: $$ \Gamma = 2\pi \cdot (0.2) \cdot 12 = 4.8\pi\text{ m}^2/\text{s} $$ Thus, Option B is correct.
Q6. Which of the following statements is mathematically TRUE regarding the vorticity of a free vortex and a forced vortex?
A) Vorticity of a free vortex is $2\omega$, while that of a forced vortex is zero.
B) Vorticity of a free vortex is zero (except at $r=0$), while that of a forced vortex is $2\omega$.
C) Vorticity is zero for both types of vortices because they rotate around an axis.
D) Vorticity is constant and non-zero for both types of vortices.
Vorticity $\zeta = \nabla \times \vec{V}$. In a forced vortex, $v_\theta = \omega r$, so $\zeta_z = \frac{1}{r}\frac{\partial(r v_\theta)}{\partial r} = \frac{1}{r}\frac{\partial(\omega r^2)}{\partial r} = 2\omega \neq 0$ (Rotational). In a free vortex, $v_\theta = C/r$, so $\zeta_z = \frac{1}{r}\frac{\partial(C)}{\partial r} = 0$ (Irrotational, except at the origin $r=0$, which is a singular point). Thus, Option B is correct.
Q7. A rotating cylinder of radius $R$ is completely filled with a liquid of density $\rho$. If it rotates at $\omega$, what is the total axial thrust (force) acting on the top cover of the cylinder? Let the pressure at the center of the top cover be $p_c$.
A) $F = p_c \pi R^2 + \frac{\pi \rho \omega^2 R^4}{4}$
B) $F = p_c \pi R^2 + \frac{\pi \rho \omega^2 R^4}{8}$
C) $F = \frac{\pi \rho \omega^2 R^4}{2}$
D) $F = p_c \pi R^2 + \frac{\pi \rho \omega^2 R^4}{2}$
The pressure at a radius $r$ on the top cover is: $$ p(r) = p_c + \frac{\rho \omega^2 r^2}{2} $$ The total thrust force on the top cover is found by integrating over the area of the top disk: $$ F = \int_0^R p(r) \cdot (2\pi r dr) = \int_0^R \left( p_c + \frac{\rho \omega^2 r^2}{2} \right) 2\pi r dr $$ $$ F = 2\pi p_c \left[ \frac{r^2}{2} \right]_0^R + \pi \rho \omega^2 \left[ \frac{r^4}{4} \right]_0^R = p_c \pi R^2 + \frac{\pi \rho \omega^2 R^4}{4} $$ Thus, Option A is correct.
Q8. Under the Kutta-Joukowski lift theorem, an aircraft wing of span $12\text{ m}$ flying at $80\text{ m/s}$ in air ($\rho = 1.2\text{ kg/m}^3$) generates $15\text{ kN}$ of lift. What is the circulation $\Gamma$ around the wing?
A) $15.62\text{ m}^2/\text{s}$
B) $13.02\text{ m}^2/\text{s}$
C) $130.2\text{ m}^2/\text{s}$
D) $26.04\text{ m}^2/\text{s}$
The total lift is $L = L' \cdot b$, where $L' = \rho_\infty V_\infty \Gamma$ is the lift per unit span. $$ L = \rho_\infty V_\infty \Gamma b \implies 15000 = 1.2 \cdot 80 \cdot \Gamma \cdot 12 $$ $$ 15000 = 1152 \cdot \Gamma \implies \Gamma = \frac{15000}{1152} \approx 13.02\text{ m}^2/\text{s} $$ Thus, Option B is correct.
Q9. Consider a Rankine Combined Vortex. The inner core of radius $R_c = 0.1\text{ m}$ rotates as a forced vortex with angular velocity $\omega = 50\text{ rad/s}$. The outer region ($r > 0.1\text{ m}$) behaves as a free vortex. What is the circulation $\Gamma$ of the outer free vortex?
A) $3.14\text{ m}^2/\text{s}$
B) $6.28\text{ m}^2/\text{s}$
C) $1.57\text{ m}^2/\text{s}$
D) $0.785\text{ m}^2/\text{s}$
At the boundary $r = R_c = 0.1\text{ m}$, the velocity must be continuous. Velocity from the forced vortex core: $$ v_\theta(R_c) = \omega R_c = 50 \cdot 0.1 = 5\text{ m/s} $$ Velocity from the free vortex outer region: $$ v_\theta(R_c) = \frac{\Gamma}{2\pi R_c} \implies 5 = \frac{\Gamma}{2\pi (0.1)} $$ $$ \Gamma = 5 \cdot 0.2\pi = \pi \approx 3.1416\text{ m}^2/\text{s} $$ Thus, Option A is correct.
Q10. At what point along a physical surface does flow separation occur in a boundary layer?
A) When the pressure gradient $\frac{\partial p}{\partial x} < 0$
B) When the wall shear stress becomes zero, i.e., $\left. \frac{\partial u}{\partial y} \right|_{y=0} = 0$
C) When the boundary layer thickness $\delta = 0$
D) When the local flow velocity matches the speed of sound
Boundary layer separation occurs when fluid particles in the boundary layer can no longer overcome the adverse pressure gradient ($\frac{\partial p}{\partial x} > 0$). At the point of separation, the velocity gradient at the wall becomes zero: $$ \left. \frac{\partial u}{\partial y} \right|_{y=0} = 0 $$ Downstream of this point, reverse flow occurs. Thus, Option B is correct.
Q11. An open cylindrical tank of $0.4\text{ m}$ diameter and $1.2\text{ m}$ height is filled with water up to $0.8\text{ m}$. It is rotated about its vertical axis. Determine the speed of rotation (in rpm) at which the water just starts to spill over the sides.
A) $134.3\text{ rpm}$
B) $268.6\text{ rpm}$
C) $189.9\text{ rpm}$
D) $95.0\text{ rpm}$
Diameter $D = 0.4\text{ m} \implies R = 0.2\text{ m}$. Height $H_{cyl} = 1.2\text{ m}$. Initial water height $h_0 = 0.8\text{ m}$. The allowable rise of water at the outer wall before spilling is: $$ \text{Rise} = H_{cyl} - h_0 = 1.2 - 0.8 = 0.4\text{ m} $$ Since rise is equal to $H/2$, where $H$ is the total paraboloid height: $$ \frac{H}{2} = 0.4 \implies H = 0.8\text{ m} $$ Now, using the formula: $$ H = \frac{\omega^2 R^2}{2g} \implies 0.8 = \frac{\omega^2 (0.2)^2}{2 \cdot 9.81} $$ $$ \omega^2 = \frac{0.8 \cdot 19.62}{0.04} = 392.4 \implies \omega = \sqrt{392.4} \approx 19.81\text{ rad/s} $$ Convert rad/s to rpm: $$ N = \frac{60 \cdot \omega}{2\pi} = \frac{60 \cdot 19.81}{2\pi} \approx 189.17\text{ rpm} \approx 189.9\text{ rpm} $$ Thus, Option C is correct.
Q12. What is the fundamental physical reason why Bernoulli's equation CANNOT be applied across different streamlines in a forced vortex?
A) The flow is compressible.
B) The flow is unsteady.
C) The flow is rotational, meaning there is external work done to sustain the rotation, causing the total head to vary across streamlines.
D) The flow velocity is zero at the center of the vortex.
In a forced vortex, external torque does work on the fluid, transferring mechanical energy. Consequently, the total head ($H = \frac{p}{\rho g} + \frac{v^2}{2g} + z$) varies from one streamline to another (it increases as we move radially outward). Because the flow is rotational ($\nabla \times \vec{V} = 2\vec{\omega} \neq 0$), Bernoulli's equation is only valid along a single streamline. Thus, Option C is correct.
Q13. In a 2D incompressible irrotational flow field, which of the following represents the velocity potential $\phi(r, \theta)$ of a line free vortex of strength $\Gamma$?
A) $\phi = \frac{\Gamma}{2\pi} \ln r$
B) $\phi = -\frac{\Gamma}{2\pi} \theta$
C) $\phi = \frac{\Gamma}{2\pi r}$
D) $\phi = -\frac{\Gamma}{2\pi} r \theta$
For a free vortex, the velocity components are $v_r = \frac{\partial \phi}{\partial r} = 0$ and $v_\theta = \frac{1}{r} \frac{\partial \phi}{\partial \theta} = -\frac{\Gamma}{2\pi r}$. Integrating these yields: $$ \phi = -\frac{\Gamma}{2\pi} \theta $$ The corresponding stream function is $\psi = \frac{\Gamma}{2\pi} \ln r$. Thus, Option B is correct.
Q14. In a free vortex, how does the radial pressure gradient $\frac{\partial p}{\partial r}$ vary with the radius $r$?
A) $\frac{\partial p}{\partial r} \propto r$
B) $\frac{\partial p}{\partial r} \propto \frac{1}{r^2}$
C) $\frac{\partial p}{\partial r} \propto \frac{1}{r^3}$
D) $\frac{\partial p}{\partial r}$ is constant
The fundamental radial pressure equation for any circular vortex flow is: $$ \frac{\partial p}{\partial r} = \rho \frac{v_\theta^2}{r} $$ For a free vortex, $v_\theta = \frac{C}{r}$. Substituting this: $$ \frac{\partial p}{\partial r} = \rho \frac{(C/r)^2}{r} = \frac{\rho C^2}{r^3} \implies \frac{\partial p}{\partial r} \propto \frac{1}{r^3} $$ Thus, Option C is correct.
Q15. In a forced vortex, how does the radial pressure gradient $\frac{\partial p}{\partial r}$ vary with the radius $r$?
A) $\frac{\partial p}{\partial r} \propto r$
B) $\frac{\partial p}{\partial r} \propto \frac{1}{r}$
C) $\frac{\partial p}{\partial r} \propto r^2$
D) $\frac{\partial p}{\partial r}$ is zero everywhere
The radial pressure equation is: $$ \frac{\partial p}{\partial r} = \rho \frac{v_\theta^2}{r} $$ For a forced vortex, $v_\theta = \omega r$. Substituting this: $$ \frac{\partial p}{\partial r} = \rho \frac{(\omega r)^2}{r} = \rho \omega^2 r \implies \frac{\partial p}{\partial r} \propto r $$ Thus, Option A is correct.
Q16. A liquid of density $800\text{ kg/m}^3$ is rotated as a forced vortex in a closed vessel. At a radius of $0.1\text{ m}$, the tangential velocity is $3\text{ m/s}$. Find the radial pressure gradient ($\partial p/\partial r$) at this point.
A) $72000\text{ Pa/m}$
B) $7200\text{ Pa/m}$
C) $36000\text{ Pa/m}$
D) $720\text{ Pa/m}$

Q17. Which of the following is a direct practical application of the Magnus Effect (Doublet + Uniform Flow + Free Vortex)?

Q18. An aircraft wing of chord $c = 2\text{ m}$ is flying at $50\text{ m/s}$ in standard air ($\rho = 1.225\text{ kg/m}^3$). If the circulation around the wing is $\Gamma = 10\text{ m}^2/\text{s}$, what is the lift generated per unit span?

Q19. For a 2D spiral vortex (Source + Vortex), if the source strength is $m = 4\pi\text{ m}^2/\text{s}$ and the vortex strength (circulation) is $\Gamma = 6\pi\text{ m}^2/\text{s}$, what is the radial velocity $v_r$ and tangential velocity $v_\theta$ at a radius $r = 2\text{ m}$?

Q20. In a Rankine Combined Vortex with transition radius $R = 0.5\text{ m}$ and maximum velocity $v_{\theta,\text{max}} = 10\text{ m/s}$ at $r = R$, find the angular velocity $\omega$ of the forced inner core.

Q21. Statement 1: In a free vortex, the circulation $\Gamma$ along any closed path enclosing the origin is constant and non-zero. Statement 2: The curl of the velocity vector (vorticity) is zero everywhere in a free vortex, including at the origin. Analyze these statements.

Thus, Option B is correct.

Q23. In a boundary layer subject to an adverse pressure gradient ($\frac{\partial p}{\partial x} > 0$), what is the sign of the velocity profile curvature $\left. \frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial y^2} \right|_{y=0}$ at the solid wall?

Q25. For an irrotational free vortex flow, the circulation $\Gamma$ around any closed curve that does NOT enclose the origin (singular point) is:
A) Constant and equal to $\Gamma$
B) Zero
C) Dependent on the size of the curve
D) Infinite
By Stokes' Theorem: $$ \Gamma = \oint \vec{V} \cdot d\vec{s} = \iint_A (\nabla \times \vec{V}) \cdot d\vec{A} $$ Since the flow is irrotational ($\nabla \times \vec{V} = 0$) everywhere except at the origin, if the closed path does not enclose the origin, the area $A$ enclosed by the path does not contain the singularity. Hence, the integral of vorticity over this area is exactly zero. Thus, Option B is correct.
Q26. What is the radial velocity $v_r$ in a pure free vortex flow?
A) $v_r = \frac{C}{r}$
B) $v_r = 0$
C) $v_r = \omega r$
D) $v_r = C \ln r$
A pure free vortex flow has circular streamlines, meaning fluid particles only move tangentially around the center of rotation. There is no radial motion, so $v_r = 0$. Thus, Option B is correct.
Q27. In a wind tunnel experiment, vortex shedding frequency $f$ behind an airfoil depends on velocity $V$, characteristic chord length $L$, and fluid kinematic viscosity $\nu$. If we apply the Buckingham Pi Theorem, what is the resulting primary dimensionless number governing this frequency scaling?
A) Froude Number ($Fr$)
B) Strouhal Number ($St$)
C) Euler Number ($Eu$)
D) Mach Number ($M$)
The Strouhal number is a dimensionless parameter describing oscillating flow mechanisms, defined as: $$ St = \frac{f L}{V} $$ It is the key dimensionless group representing vortex shedding frequency scaling in fluid dynamics. Thus, Option B is correct.
Q28. A liquid in an open vessel of radius $R$ is rotated as a forced vortex. The maximum shear stress in the liquid occurs:
A) At the outer wall $r = R$
B) At the center $r = 0$
C) Nowhere, because the shear stress is zero everywhere in solid-body rotation
D) At $r = R/2$
In solid-body rotation (forced vortex), there is no relative motion (velocity gradient) between adjacent concentric fluid layers. Every fluid element has the exact same angular velocity, meaning: $$ \tau_{r\theta} = \mu \left[ r \frac{\partial}{\partial r}\left(\frac{v_\theta}{r}\right) \right] = \mu \left[ r \frac{\partial}{\partial r}(\omega) \right] = 0 $$ Because $\omega$ is constant, the shear stress is zero everywhere in the fluid field. Thus, Option C is correct.
Q29. What is the shear stress $\tau_{r\theta}$ in a free vortex flow of a Newtonian fluid?
A) Zero everywhere
B) Non-zero and varies as $\tau_{r\theta} = -\frac{2\mu C}{r^2}$
C) Non-zero and varies as $\tau_{r\theta} = \mu \omega$
D) Constant throughout
In cylindrical coordinates, the shear stress component $\tau_{r\theta}$ is: $$ \tau_{r\theta} = \mu \left[ r \frac{\partial}{\partial r}\left(\frac{v_\theta}{r}\right) \right] $$ For a free vortex, $v_\theta = \frac{C}{r} \implies \frac{v_\theta}{r} = \frac{C}{r^2}$. Now, take the derivative: $$ \frac{\partial}{\partial r}\left(\frac{C}{r^2}\right) = -\frac{2C}{r^3} $$ Multiply by $r$: $$ \tau_{r\theta} = \mu \cdot r \cdot \left(-\frac{2C}{r^3}\right) = -\frac{2\mu C}{r^2} $$ Thus, Option B is correct. (This shows that while a free vortex is irrotational, it still has non-zero viscous shear stress in a real fluid, which is why real free vortices decay!).
Q30. Which of the following parameters remains constant along any streamtube in a steady, irrotational, inviscid flow?
A) Dynamic pressure only
B) Total head (Bernoulli's constant)
C) Vorticity only
D) Static pressure only
For a steady, irrotational, inviscid flow, Bernoulli's equation states that the total mechanical energy (total head) remains constant throughout the entire fluid domain, and therefore along any streamline or streamtube. Thus, Option B is correct.

7. Grand Mini Test (10 Exam-Level Questions)

Q1. Assertion (A): In a forced vortex, the rise of liquid level at the wall of a rotating open cylinder is equal to the fall of liquid level at the axis of rotation. Reason (R): The volume of a paraboloid of revolution is exactly one-half of the volume of the circumscribed cylinder of the same height and radius.
A) Both A and R are true, and R is the correct explanation of A.
B) Both A and R are true, but R is not the correct explanation of A.
C) A is true, but R is false.
D) A is false, but R is true.
Because the volume of the paraboloid of revolution is exactly half of the circumscribing cylinder, the volume of liquid displaced upward at the walls must equal the volume of empty space created at the center. This directly results in the rise of the liquid at the wall being equal to its fall at the center ($H/2$). Both statements are true, and R is the correct explanation.
Q2. Statement 1: A Rankine combined vortex model resolves the infinite velocity singularity at the origin of a pure free vortex. Statement 2: The velocity profile in a Rankine vortex increases linearly up to a core radius $R_c$, and then decreases hyperbolically as $1/r$ for $r > R_c$.
A) Statement 1 is true, Statement 2 is false.
B) Statement 1 is false, Statement 2 is true.
C) Both statements are true.
D) Both statements are false.
In a pure free vortex, as $r \to 0$, $v_\theta \to \infty$, which is physically impossible. The Rankine combined vortex model replaces the inner core with a forced vortex ($v_\theta \propto r$) for $0 \le r \le R_c$, resolving the singularity. Outside $R_c$, it remains a free vortex ($v_\theta \propto 1/r$). Both statements are true.
Q3. If a cylindrical vessel containing water is rotated about its vertical axis, what is the shape of the isobaric (constant pressure) surfaces within the fluid?
A) Horizontal planes
B) Vertical cylindrical surfaces
C) Paraboloids of revolution
D) Hyperboloids of revolution
The pressure distribution is $p = \frac{\rho \omega^2 r^2}{2} - \rho g z + C$. For any constant pressure surface ($p = \text{constant}$): $$ z = \frac{\omega^2 r^2}{2g} + \text{constant'} $$ This is the equation of a paraboloid of revolution. Therefore, all isobaric surfaces within the rotating liquid are paraboloids.
Q4. A free vortex is established in a liquid. The tangential velocity at a radius of $0.1\text{ m}$ is $8\text{ m/s}$. Find the difference in pressure head of liquid between $r_1 = 0.1\text{ m}$ and $r_2 = 0.2\text{ m}$. (Take $g = 10\text{ m/s}^2$)
A) $1.2\text{ m}$
B) $2.4\text{ m}$
C) $4.8\text{ m}$
D) $0.6\text{ m}$
For a free vortex, Bernoulli's equation can be applied between any two points on a horizontal plane: $$ \frac{p_1}{\rho g} + \frac{v_1^2}{2g} = \frac{p_2}{\rho g} + \frac{v_2^2}{2g} $$ The difference in pressure head is: $$ \Delta h = \frac{p_2 - p_1}{\rho g} = \frac{v_1^2 - v_2^2}{2g} $$ Since $v \propto 1/r$, and $v_1 = 8\text{ m/s}$ at $r_1 = 0.1\text{ m}$: $$ v_2 = v_1 \cdot \frac{r_1}{r_2} = 8 \cdot \frac{0.1}{0.2} = 4\text{ m/s} $$ Now calculate the difference in head: $$ \Delta h = \frac{8^2 - 4^2}{2 \cdot 10} = \frac{64 - 16}{20} = \frac{48}{20} = 2.4\text{ m} $$ Thus, Option B is correct.
Q5. Which of the following boundary conditions is CORRECT at the point of boundary layer separation?
A) $\frac{\partial u}{\partial y} > 0$ at $y = 0$
B) $\frac{\partial u}{\partial y} < 0$ at $y = 0$
C) $\frac{\partial u}{\partial y} = 0$ at $y = \delta$

The separation point is defined as the boundary between forward flow and backflow at the wall surface ($y = 0$). At this point, the velocity profile has a vertical tangent at the wall: $$ \left. \frac{\partial u}{\partial y} \right|_{y=0} = 0 $$ Thus, Option D is correct.
Q6. In a 2D sink flow combined with a free vortex (spiral vortex), the streamlines are:
A) Concentric circles
B) Straight radial lines
C) Logarithmic spirals
D) Parabolic arcs
The streamlines of a combined sink and free vortex have velocity components $v_r = -q/(2\pi r)$ and $v_\theta = \Gamma/(2\pi r)$. The differential equation for a streamline in polar coordinates is: $$ \frac{dr}{v_r} = \frac{r d\theta}{v_\theta} \implies \frac{dr}{-q / (2\pi r)} = \frac{r d\theta}{\Gamma / (2\pi r)} \implies \frac{dr}{r} = -\frac{q}{\Gamma} d\theta $$ Integrating this yields $r = C e^{-(q/\Gamma)\theta}$, which is the equation of a logarithmic spiral. Thus, Option C is correct.

In a forced vortex, external energy is input to the system. Since $v_\theta = \omega r$ increases with radius and static pressure $p$ also increases with radius ($p \propto r^2$), the total pressure (the sum of static and dynamic pressure) increases significantly as we move radially outward. Q9. For a 2D line doublet of strength $\mu$ placed in a uniform freestream $U_\infty$, what is the radius $R$ of the cylinder that this potential flow model represents?

Q10. What is the physical significance of the Froude number ($Fr$) in modeling vortex systems with a free surface?

8. Error Analysis & Common Traps (5 min)

To score high in the UPSC ASO exam, you must eliminate these extremely common, high-yield conceptual and mathematical errors:

1. Applying Bernoulli's Equation Across Streamlines in a Forced Vortex

This is the single most frequent error. In a forced vortex, the flow is rotational ($\nabla \times \vec{V} = 2\vec{\omega} \neq 0$). Since work is continuously performed on the fluid by an external torque, different streamlines have different mechanical energy levels. The Trap: Trying to calculate the pressure difference between the center and the wall of a rotating container using $p_1 + \frac{1}{2}\rho v_1^2 = p_2 + \frac{1}{2}\rho v_2^2$ will yield a completely wrong answer! The Correct Path: You must integrate the radial momentum equation ($dp = \rho \frac{v_\theta^2}{r} dr$) to get $p_2 - p_1 = \frac{1}{2}\rho \omega^2 (r_2^2 - r_1^2)$.

2. Forgetting the Paraboloid Volume Conservation Rule

When an open cylinder of liquid is rotated, students often assume the liquid only rises at the walls, or they calculate the rise without accounting for the corresponding fall at the center. The Trap: Assuming the liquid level at the wall rises by the full paraboloid height $H$. The Correct Path: Remember that by conservation of liquid volume, the volume of the paraboloid of revolution is exactly $\frac{1}{2}$ of the circumscribing cylinder. This means: $$ \text{Rise at the Wall} = \text{Fall at the Center} = \frac{H}{2} = \frac{\omega^2 R^2}{4g} $$ relative to the initial liquid level when the vessel was at rest.

3. Confusing Vorticity and Angular Velocity

Many students use these terms interchangeably. The Trap: In a forced vortex rotating at angular velocity $\omega$, stating that the vorticity is $\omega$. The Correct Path: Vorticity is defined as the curl of velocity, which is mathematically equal to *twice* the angular velocity vector: $$ \text{Vorticity } \zeta = 2\omega $$

4. Assuming Free Vortices Have No Viscous Shear Stress

Because free vortex flows are irrotational ($\nabla \times \vec{V} = 0$), students often mistake them for being frictionless or inviscid. The Trap: Stating that the shear stress $\tau_{r\theta}$ is zero in a free vortex. The Correct Path: Viscous shear stress in cylindrical coordinates is $\tau_{r\theta} = \mu \left[ r \frac{\partial}{\partial r}\left(\frac{v_\theta}{r}\right) \right]$. Substituting $v_\theta = \frac{C}{r}$ yields $\tau_{r\theta} = -\frac{2\mu C}{r^2}$. While the flow is irrotational, there is still viscous shear stress in a real fluid, which is why a real free vortex gradually decays unless sustained by a source/sink flow.
