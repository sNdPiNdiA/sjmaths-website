NS equation – form and physical meaning

Day 9 of 129 Fluid Mechanics & Machinery Phase 1: Foundations 90-120 min Master Class

1. Understand (From Absolute Zero, 5–10 min)

To master flight dynamics and safety engineering, we must first master the fundamental mathematical engine that dictates how air flows over wings, through jet engines, and around control surfaces: the Navier-Stokes (NS) equations.

At its absolute basic level, the Navier-Stokes equation is nothing more than Newton's Second Law of Motion ($F = ma$) applied to a continuous fluid element. Instead of tracking a solid, discrete billiard ball, we track an infinitesimal "fluid packet" as it deforms, rotates, and moves through space.

1. The Physical Origin: Newton's $F = ma$

Imagine a tiny cube of air with volume $dx · dy · dz$ and density $ρ$. Its mass is $dm = ρ · dx \, dy \, dz$. Newton's Second Law states that the rate of change of momentum of this packet equals the sum of all forces acting upon it:

$$dm · \vec{a} = ∑ \vec{F}$$

Dividing this entire equation by the volume of the element, we get the force balance per unit volume:

$$ρ \vec{a} = \vec{f}forces per unit volume$$

In fluid dynamics, the acceleration term $\vec{a}$ is split into two physical components, and the forces $\vec{f}$ are split into three distinct physical mechanisms:

$$\underbrace{ρ ( ∂ \vec{V}∂ t + (\vec{V} · \nabla)\vec{V} )}Inertial Forces (Mass × Acceleration) = \underbrace{-\nabla p}Pressure Force + \underbrace{μ \nabla^2 \vec{V}}Viscous Force + \underbrace{ρ \vec{g}}Body Force$$

2. Physical Deconstruction of the Terms

  • $ρ ∂ \vec{V}∂ t$ (Local Acceleration / Unsteadiness): This represents how the velocity at a fixed point in space changes over time. If you hold a wind probe stationary behind a flapping wing, the rapid fluctuations in velocity it registers are due to this term. (SI units: $kg · m-2 · s-2$ or $N/m^3$).
  • $ρ (\vec{V} · \nabla)\vec{V}$ (Convective/Advective Acceleration): This represents how velocity changes as a fluid packet moves from one spatial position to another in a spatially varying velocity field. Even in steady flow (where $∂ \vec{V}∂ t = 0$), air accelerating as it passes through a narrowing nozzle experiences convective acceleration. This term is highly nonlinear, which makes the Navier-Stokes equations notoriously difficult to solve analytically.
  • $-\nabla p$ (Pressure Gradient Force): Fluids naturally flow from regions of high pressure to regions of low pressure. The negative sign indicates that the force vector points in the direction of *decreasing* pressure (down the gradient).
  • $μ \nabla^2 \vec{V}$ (Viscous Shear Force): This is the internal friction within the fluid. Adjacent layers of fluid sliding past one another exert shear stresses proportional to the dynamic viscosity ($μ$). It acts to damp out velocity gradients and dissipate kinetic energy into heat.
  • $ρ \vec{g}$ (Body Force): External field forces that act on the entire mass of the fluid element, such as gravity or electromagnetic forces in magnetohydrodynamics.

2. Learn the Concept (Complete Theoretical Foundation & Derivations, 20–25 min)

1. Comprehensive Mathematical Foundation

The Navier-Stokes equations are derived by substituting the Newtonian constitutive relation (which relates stress to deformation rate) into Cauchy's equations of motion. Let's build this step-by-step from zero background.

Step A: Cauchy's Equations of Motion

For any continuous medium, the balance of momentum yields Cauchy's equation:

$$ρ D\vec{V}Dt = \nabla · \overline{\overline{σ}} + ρ \vec{g}$$

where $DDt = ∂∂ t + (\vec{V} · \nabla)$ is the Material (or Substantial) Derivative, and $\overline{\overline{σ}}$ is the total stress tensor. The total stress tensor is split into isotropic pressure and viscous stress (deviatoric stress tensor, $\overline{\overline{τ}}$):

$$σij = -p\deltaij + τij$$

where $\deltaij$ is the Kronecker delta ($\deltaij = 1$ if $i=j$, and $0$ if $i \neq j$).

Step B: The Newtonian Constitutive Relation

For an isotropic, Newtonian fluid, the viscous stress is linearly proportional to the strain rate tensor ($Sij = 12(∂ u_i∂ x_j + ∂ u_j∂ x_i)$):

$$τij = 2μ Sij + λ (\nabla · \vec{V})\deltaij$$

where $λ$ is the second coefficient of viscosity. Under Stokes' Hypothesis for monoatomic gases, $λ = -23μ$. Substituting this back into the momentum equation gives the general compressible Navier-Stokes equation.

Step C: The Incompressible Assumption

For most aerodynamic flows below Mach 0.3, density $ρ$ is constant. This means the conservation of mass (continuity equation) simplifies to:

$$\nabla · \vec{V} = 0 \quad (Divergence-free condition)$$

With $\nabla · \vec{V} = 0$, the second viscosity term vanishes, and the viscous stress becomes $τij = μ ( ∂ u_i∂ x_j + ∂ u_j∂ x_i )$. Substituting this into Cauchy's equation yields the iconic Incompressible Navier-Stokes Equation:

$$ρ ( ∂ \vec{V}∂ t + (\vec{V} · \nabla)\vec{V} ) = -\nabla p + μ \nabla^2 \vec{V} + ρ \vec{g}$$

In Cartesian coordinates, this vector equation unfolds into three coupled, non-linear partial differential equations:

X-momentum:

$$ρ ( ∂ u∂ t + u∂ u∂ x + v∂ u∂ y + w∂ u∂ z ) = -∂ p∂ x + μ ( ∂^2 u∂ x^2 + ∂^2 u∂ y^2 + ∂^2 u∂ z^2 ) + ρ g_x$$

Y-momentum:

$$ρ ( ∂ v∂ t + u∂ v∂ x + v∂ v∂ y + w∂ v∂ z ) = -∂ p∂ y + μ ( ∂^2 v∂ x^2 + ∂^2 v∂ y^2 + ∂^2 v∂ z^2 ) + ρ g_y$$

Z-momentum:

$$ρ ( ∂ w∂ t + u∂ w∂ x + v∂ w∂ y + w∂ w∂ z ) = -∂ p∂ z + μ ( ∂^2 w∂ x^2 + ∂^2 w∂ y^2 + ∂^2 w∂ z^2 ) + ρ g_z$$ ---

2. Dimensional Analysis & Similarity (Buckingham Pi Theorem)

Solving the full NS equations analytically is impossible for arbitrary geometries. We must rely on wind tunnel testing and scale models. To do this, we use the Buckingham Pi Theorem to find the governing dimensionless parameters.

The Buckingham Pi Theorem

If a physical process is governed by $n$ variables involving $m$ primary dimensions (such as Mass $M$, Length $L$, Time $T$, and Temperature $θ$), then the variables can be grouped into exactly $k = n - m$ independent dimensionless parameters called $\Pi$ groups.

For aerodynamic forces ($F_D$) on an airfoil, the variables are:

$$F_D = f(L, V, ρ, μ, a, σ)$$

Here, $n = 7$ variables ($F_D, L, V, ρ, μ, a, σ$). The primary dimensions involved are $M, L, T$ ($m = 3$). This gives us $7 - 3 = 4$ independent dimensionless $\Pi$ groups:

  1. Reynolds Number ($Re$): Ratio of inertial forces to viscous forces. $$Re = ρ V Lμ = V Lν$$
  2. Mach Number ($M$): Ratio of flow velocity to the speed of sound (inertial forces to compressibility forces). $$M = Va$$
  3. Froude Number ($Fr$): Ratio of inertial forces to gravitational forces (critical for free-surface flows and gravity waves). $$Fr = V√gL$$
  4. Weber Number ($We$): Ratio of inertial forces to surface tension forces (critical for fuel atomization and droplet break-up). $$We = ρ V^2 Lσ$$
  5. Euler Number ($Eu$): Ratio of pressure forces to inertial forces. $$Eu = Δ pρ V^2$$

3. Idealized Fluid Dynamics: Potential Flow Superpositions

When the viscous forces are negligible ($μ ≈ 0$, or $Re \to ∞$) and the flow is irrotational ($\nabla × \vec{V} = 0$), the fluid can be modelled as a potential flow. Here, velocity is the gradient of a scalar velocity potential $φ$:

$$\vec{V} = \nabla φ \implies u = ∂ φ∂ x, \quad v = ∂ φ∂ y$$

Because the flow is incompressible ($\nabla · \vec{V} = 0$), substituting $\vec{V} = \nabla φ$ yields Laplace's Equation:

$$\nabla^2 φ = 0$$

Since Laplace's equation is linear, we can superimpose (add) elementary solutions to construct complex, realistic flow fields:

Flow Element Velocity Potential ($φ$) Stream Function ($ψ$) Physical Description
Uniform Flow $U∞ x = U∞ r \cosθ$ $U∞ y = U∞ r \sinθ$ Steady, parallel flow with velocity $U∞$
Source (Strength $q$) $q2π \ln r$ $q2π θ$ Fluid emerges radially outward from a single point
Sink (Strength $q$) $-q2π \ln r$ $-q2π θ$ Fluid flows radially inward to a single point
Vortex (Strength $\Gamma$) $-\Gamma2π θ$ $\Gamma2π \ln r$ Circular streamlines; modeling lift and rotation
Doublet (Strength $\kappa$) $\kappa \cosθr$ $-\kappa \sinθr$ Coalescence of a source and sink brought infinitely close

Superposition Highlight: Flow Past a Non-Rotating Cylinder
By superimposing a uniform flow and a doublet, we get the potential flow around a solid cylinder of radius $R = \sqrt{\kappa / U∞}$:

$$ψ = U∞ r \sinθ (1 - R^2r^2)$$ $$φ = U∞ r \cosθ (1 + R^2r^2)$$ ---

4. Vorticity Theorems & Circulation

Vorticity ($\vec{ω}$): Defined mathematically as the curl of the velocity field. It is a local measure of fluid rotation:

$$\vec{ω} = \nabla × \vec{V}$$

In 2D Cartesian plane flow, the only non-zero component is along the z-axis:

$$ω_z = ∂ v∂ x - ∂ u∂ y$$

Circulation ($\Gamma$): The line integral of the velocity vector around a closed curve $C$. By Stokes' Theorem, it equals the flux of vorticity through the area $A$ enclosed by $C$:

$$\Gamma = \oint_C \vec{V} · d\vec{s} = \iint_A (\nabla × \vec{V}) · d\vec{A} = \iint_A \vec{ω} · d\vec{A}$$

Kelvin’s Circulation Theorem: For an inviscid, barotropic fluid subjected only to conservative body forces, the circulation around a closed material loop is constant over time:

$$D\GammaDt = 0$$

Helmholtz's Vortex Theorems:

  1. The strength of a vortex tube (circulation) is constant along its length.
  2. A vortex tube cannot end within a fluid; it must either extend to the boundaries, form a closed loop (like a smoke ring), or terminate at a solid boundary.
  3. In the absence of external rotational forces, fluid elements that are initially irrotational remain irrotational.

5. Boundary Layer Separation Conditions

While potential flow assumes zero viscosity, real fluids have viscosity. This creates a thin boundary layer next to solid walls, where the velocity drops from the free-stream value $U(x)$ to zero at the wall (the "no-slip" condition).

Inside the boundary layer, the fluid must fight against both viscous drag and the pressure gradient. Under an adverse pressure gradient ($dpdx > 0$), pressure increases in the direction of the flow. This acts as a brake on the fluid.

Near the wall, where the fluid has already lost most of its kinetic energy to friction, the adverse pressure gradient can bring the flow to a complete stop and force it to reverse direction. This causes the flow to detach or "separate" from the surface.

$$Separation Point Condition: \quad \left. ∂ u∂ y |y=0 = 0 \quad (τ_w = 0)$$

We can identify different flow regions near the wall based on the velocity gradient at the wall:

  • Attached Flow: $\left. ∂ u∂ y |y=0 > 0 \implies$ Wall shear stress $τ_w > 0$.
  • Separation Point: $\left. ∂ u∂ y |y=0 = 0 \implies τ_w = 0$. The flow is on the verge of detaching.
  • Separated / Reversed Flow: $\left. ∂ u∂ y |y=0 < 0 \implies τ_w < 0$. Recirculating bubbles and turbulence develop.

The curvature of the velocity profile at the wall is directly tied to the pressure gradient by the x-momentum equation at $y=0$ (where $u=v=0$):

$$μ \left. ∂^2 u∂ y^2 |y=0 = dpdx$$
  • If $dpdx < 0$ (Favorable Pressure Gradient), then $∂^2 u∂ y^2 < 0$ everywhere, and the profile is healthy and highly resistant to separation.
  • If $dpdx > 0$ (Adverse Pressure Gradient), then $∂^2 u∂ y^2 > 0$ at the wall. Because the profile must still curve back to join the free stream ($∂^2 u∂ y^2 < 0$ far from the wall), there must be a point of inflection ($∂^2 u∂ y^2 = 0$) within the boundary layer. This inflection point is a classic sign of an unstable boundary layer that is vulnerable to separation.

4. Aviation Application (5–10 min)

The Navier-Stokes equations and boundary layer separation conditions are critical to practical aeronautics. Here is how these principles apply to real-world flight:

1. Wing Stall and Aerodynamic Separation Control

As an aircraft's angle of attack ($α$) increases, the adverse pressure gradient on the upper surface of the wing grows stronger. Eventually, the boundary layer can no longer overcome this gradient, and the flow separates:

$$\left. ∂ u∂ y |y=0 \leq 0$$

This separation shifts the detachment point toward the leading edge, collapsing the lift force and causing a stall. To delay this, aircraft use dynamic flow control devices:

  • Vortex Generators: Small, angled vertical plates on the wing's upper surface. They create micro-vortices that pull high-energy air from the free stream down into the sluggish boundary layer. This increases the velocity gradient at the wall ($\left. ∂ u∂ y |y=0 \gg 0$), energizing the boundary layer and helping it resist the adverse pressure gradient.
  • Slats and Flaps: Slats open a slot at the leading edge, letting high-pressure air from the lower surface escape onto the upper surface. This high-speed jet re-energizes the boundary layer, delaying separation and enabling much higher angles of attack during takeoff and landing.

2. Wind Tunnel Model Testing & Scale Effects

When testing a sub-scale aircraft model in a wind tunnel, we cannot simply match the physical size. To ensure the model accurately replicates the forces of a full-scale aircraft, we must maintain dynamic similarity. This requires matching the dimensionless $\Pi$ groups:

  • For low-speed flight, we match the Reynolds Number ($Re_m = Re_p$) to ensure the boundary layer transitions and separates at the same locations.
  • For high-speed flight, we must also match the Mach Number ($M_m = M_p$) to ensure the shock wave patterns are identical.

If we scale down the model's length ($L$), we must increase the air velocity ($V$) or compress the wind tunnel air (raising density $ρ$) to keep the Reynolds number constant. This is why pressurized and cryogenic wind tunnels are used.

3. Wingtip Trailing Vortices

Because pressure is lower on the upper surface of a wing than on the lower surface, air curls around the wingtip from bottom to top. This transverse flow rolls up into a pair of high-strength trailing vortices. Modeling these structures relies directly on Helmholtz's vortex theorems and the Biot-Savart law. The strong downward flow (downwash) behind these vortices alters the effective angle of attack, creating induced drag.

5. UPSC ASO Focus (10 min)

  • Navier-Stokes Solvability: The Millennium Prize Problem concerns the mathematical existence and smoothness of solutions to the 3D incompressible Navier-Stokes equations. The presence of the non-linear convective acceleration term $(\vec{V} · \nabla)\vec{V}$ prevents us from finding general analytical solutions.
  • Repeating Variables Selection Rules (Buckingham Pi): When applying the Buckingham Pi theorem, the chosen repeating variables must never form a dimensionless group on their own. They must collectively contain all primary dimensions ($M, L, T$). Usually, you select one size variable (e.g., $L$), one kinematic/flow variable (e.g., $V$), and one fluid property (e.g., $ρ$).
  • Inflection Point Criteria: An adverse pressure gradient ($dpdx > 0$) is a necessary but not *sufficient* condition for boundary layer separation. However, it guarantees the existence of an inflection point ($∂^2 u∂ y^2 = 0$) inside the boundary layer.
  • Doublet Strength and Cylinder Radius Relation: For a cylinder of radius $R$ in a uniform flow $U∞$ created by a doublet of strength $\kappa$, the algebraic relationship is strictly: $$R = √\kappaU∞$$ This relation is highly tested in UPSC ASO numerical problems.
  • Kelvin's Theorem Boundary Conditions: Kelvin's theorem fails when non-conservative body forces act on the fluid, when the fluid is baroclinic (density is a function of both pressure and temperature, $ρ = f(p, T)$), or when viscous forces are significant (inside boundary layers).

Q3. A 1:10 scale model of an aircraft is tested in a wind tunnel under dynamic similarity conditions. If the prototype flies at $15 m/s$ in atmospheric air and viscosity is assumed constant, what velocity must be maintained in the wind tunnel if we match the Reynolds number using air of the same density?
A) $1.5 m/s$
B) $15 m/s$
C) $150 m/s$
D) $1500 m/s$

Q4. A potential flow doublet is placed at the origin with its axis aligned along the negative x-axis (from source to sink). What is the value of the stream function $ψ$ at coordinates $(r, θ) = (2, π/6)$ if the doublet strength $\kappa = 4 m^3/s$?

Q5. A velocity profile in a boundary layer is approximated as $uU∞ = 2(y\delta) - (y\delta)^2$. Determine if the flow is attached, separating, or separated at the wall ($y = 0$).
A) Separated, since $\left. ∂ u∂ y |y=0 < 0$

Q7. What is the vorticity vector $\vec{ω}$ for a two-dimensional velocity field given by $u = 3x^2 y$ and $v = -3xy^2$?

Q12. What is the physical significance of the inflection point ($∂^2 u∂ y^2 = 0$) within a boundary layer velocity profile?

The relationship between the cylinder radius $R$, doublet strength $\kappa$, and uniform velocity $U∞$ is: $$R = √\kappaU∞ \implies R^2 = \kappaU∞$$ $$\kappa = R^2 U∞$$ Given $R = 2 m$ and $U∞ = 5 m/s$: $$\kappa = (2)^2 × 5 = 4 × 5 = 20 m^3/s$$
Q14. Consider an incompressible, viscous flow through a horizontal pipe. If the pressure gradient $dpdx$ is constant, the velocity profile $u(y)$ is parabolic. What is the shear stress $τ$ at any radius $r$ in a pipe of radius $R$ under steady conditions?
A) $τ = -r2 (dpdx)$
B) $τ = -R2 (dpdx)$
C) $τ = -r^2 (dpdx)$
D) $τ = 0$ everywhere
For a fully developed laminar pipe flow (Hagen-Poiseuille flow), a force balance on a cylindrical fluid element of radius $r$ and length $dx$ yields: $$Δ p · (π r^2) = τ · (2π r · dx) \implies τ = r2 Δ pdx \implies τ = -r2(dpdx)$$ This shows that the shear stress varies linearly with radius, from zero at the centerline ($r=0$) to a maximum at the pipe wall ($r=R$).
Q15. The Froude number is defined as $Fr = V√gL$. For which of the following scenarios is the Froude number the most critical similarity parameter?
A) A high-altitude supersonic fighter jet cruising at Mach 2.
B) A micro-air vehicle (MAV) flying at low Reynolds numbers.
C) A seaplane hull taxiing and taking off from the water surface.
D) Flow of highly viscous lubricant through an aircraft engine bearing.
The Froude number is the ratio of inertial forces to gravitational forces. It is critical whenever gravity waves occur on a free liquid-gas interface, such as for ships, seaplane hulls taxiing on water, or open-channel flows.
Q16. If a fluid flow field is defined by the velocity potential $φ = x^2 - y^2$, find the corresponding velocity components $(u, v)$ and verify if the flow is incompressible.
A) $u = 2x, v = -2y$; The flow is incompressible since $\nabla · \vec{V} = 0$.
B) $u = -2y, v = 2x$; The flow is compressible since $\nabla · \vec{V} \neq 0$.
C) $u = 2x, v = 2y$; The flow is compressible since $\nabla · \vec{V} = 4$.
D) $u = x^2, v = -y^2$; The flow is incompressible.
The velocity components derived from the potential function $φ$ are: $$u = ∂ φ∂ x = 2x$$ $$v = ∂ φ∂ y = -2y$$ To check if the flow is incompressible, we calculate the divergence: $$\nabla · \vec{V} = ∂ u∂ x + ∂ v∂ y = ∂(2x)∂ x + ∂(-2y)∂ y = 2 - 2 = 0$$ Since the divergence is zero, the flow is incompressible.
Q17. Which of the following conditions correctly identifies the point of boundary layer separation on a solid wall ($y = 0$)?
A) $\left. ∂^2 u∂ y^2 |y=0 = 0$
B) $\left. ∂ u∂ y |y=0 = 0$
C) $\left. ∂ u∂ x |y=0 = 0$
D) $\left. ∂ p∂ x |y=0 = 0$
The boundary layer separates when the velocity gradient at the wall becomes zero: $\left. ∂ u∂ y |y=0 = 0$. This corresponds to a wall shear stress of zero ($τ_w = 0$).
Q18. In dimensional analysis, the Weber number ($We = ρ V^2 Lσ$) is a ratio of which physical forces?
A) Viscous force to surface tension force.
B) Inertial force to viscous force.
C) Inertial force to surface tension force.
D) Pressure force to gravity force.
The Weber number ($We$) is a dimensionless parameter that represents the ratio of inertial forces to surface tension forces ($σ$). It is important in analyzing multiphase flows, fuel spray atomization, and droplet dynamics.
Q19. Under what conditions is the flow field around a closed body guaranteed to have zero net drag force according to D'Alembert's Paradox?
A) Incompressible, viscous, steady flow.
B) Incompressible, inviscid, irrotational steady flow.
C) Compressible, viscous, unsteady flow.
D) High Reynolds number flow with boundary layer separation.
D'Alembert's Paradox states that for a steady, incompressible, inviscid, and irrotational (potential) flow, the net drag force acting on a closed body of any shape is zero. This occurs because the pressure distribution is perfectly symmetric between the upstream and downstream sides.
Q20. Given the dynamic viscosity of air $μ = 1.8 × 10-5 Pa·s$ and density $ρ = 1.2 kg/m^3$. If the air velocity over a UAV wing is $15 m/s$ and the characteristic chord length is $0.3 m$, calculate the Reynolds number.
A) $100,000$
B) $300,000$
C) $500,000$
D) $1,000,000$
The Reynolds number ($Re$) is: $$Re = ρ V Lμ = 1.2 × 15 × 0.31.8 × 10-5$$ $$Re = 5.41.8 × 10-5 = 3 × 10^5 = 300,000$$
Q21. For a 2D incompressible flow, the stream function is given by $ψ = 2xy$. What is the velocity potential $φ$ (if it exists) for this flow?
A) $φ = y^2 - x^2 + C$
B) $φ = x^2 - y^2 + C$
C) $φ = 2xy + C$
D) Does not exist because the flow is rotational.
From the stream function $ψ = 2xy$: $$u = ∂ ψ∂ y = 2x, \quad v = -∂ ψ∂ x = -2y$$ For a velocity potential $φ$ to exist, the flow must be irrotational. Let's check vorticity: $$ω_z = ∂ v∂ x - ∂ u∂ y = 0 - 0 = 0 \quad (Irrotational)$$ Now we find $φ$: $$u = ∂ φ∂ x = 2x \implies φ = x^2 + f(y)$$ $$v = ∂ φ∂ y = f'(y) = -2y \implies f(y) = -y^2 + C$$ Thus, $φ = x^2 - y^2 + C$.
Q22. Consider a boundary layer flow with a velocity profile $uU∞ = \sin(π y2\delta)$. Determine the value of the second derivative of velocity with respect to $y$ at the wall ($y = 0$).
A) $0$
B) $-π^2 U∞4\delta^2$
C) $π U∞2\delta$
D) $-π U∞2\delta^2$
Let's find the derivatives of $u(y) = U∞ \sin(π y2\delta)$: $$∂ u∂ y = U∞ π2\delta \cos(π y2\delta)$$ $$∂^2 u∂ y^2 = -U∞ (π2\delta)^2 \sin(π y2\delta)$$ At the wall ($y = 0$): $$\left. ∂^2 u∂ y^2 |y=0 = -U∞ (π2\delta)^2 \sin(0) = 0$$ This indicates that the pressure gradient $dpdx$ is zero at this point along the wall (flat plate boundary layer with zero pressure gradient).
Q23. In a wind tunnel model test, we want to maintain similarity for both compressibility (Mach number) and viscous forces (Reynolds number). If the scale of the model is $1:λ$ (where $L_m = L_p / λ$), and the same fluid at the same temperature is used for both model and prototype, can both criteria be met simultaneously?
A) Yes, by keeping the velocity the same ($V_m = V_p$).
B) Yes, but only if we test at hypersonic speeds.
C) No, because Mach similarity requires $V_m = V_p$, whereas Reynolds similarity requires $V_m = λ V_p$ under these conditions.
D) No, because Froude similarity must always take precedence.
Under the same fluid and temperature conditions, the speed of sound $a$ and kinematic viscosity $ν$ are identical for both model and prototype ($a_m = a_p$ and $ν_m = ν_p$). Mach similarity requires: $$V_ma_m = V_pa_p \implies V_m = V_p$$ Reynolds similarity requires: $$V_m L_mν_m = V_p L_pν_p \implies V_m = V_p (L_pL_m) = λ V_p$$ Since $λ \neq 1$, these two requirements conflict. You cannot satisfy both simultaneously in the same working fluid at the same temperature.
Q24. What physical mechanism does the Euler number ($Eu = Δ pρ V^2$) scale?
A) Ratio of pressure forces to viscous forces.
B) Ratio of pressure forces to inertial forces.
C) Ratio of gravity forces to surface tension.
D) Ratio of elastic forces to inertial forces.
The Euler number ($Eu$) represents the ratio of pressure forces ($Δ p$) to inertial forces ($ρ V^2$). It is highly useful in characterizing pressure drops in pipe networks, pumps, and around streamlined bodies.
Q25. Which Helmholtz vortex theorem explains why an aircraft's wingtip trailing vortices cannot simply end in mid-air?
A) First Theorem: Strength of a vortex tube is constant.
B) Second Theorem: A vortex tube cannot end within the fluid; it must form a closed loop or terminate at a boundary.
C) Third Theorem: Fluid elements initially irrotational remain irrotational.
D) Helmholtz's theorem only applies to supersonic shock waves.
The Second Helmholtz Theorem states that a vortex tube cannot terminate inside a fluid. It must either close on itself to form a loop (like a smoke ring) or terminate at a solid boundary (like a tornado on the ground or a wingtip vortex at the wing surface/runway boundary).
Q26. A steady, incompressible flow has the velocity components $u = 2xy$ and $v = a x^2 + b y^2$. What is the relationship between constants $a$ and $b$ to satisfy the continuity equation?
A) $a + b = 0$
B) $2 + b = 0$
C) $y + bx = 0$
D) $2 + 2b = 0 \implies b = -1$
The 2D incompressible continuity equation is: $$∂ u∂ x + ∂ v∂ y = 0$$ Given $u = 2xy \implies ∂ u∂ x = 2y$. Given $v = a x^2 + b y^2 \implies ∂ v∂ y = 2by$. Substituting these into the continuity equation: $$2y + 2by = 0 \implies 2y(1 + b) = 0$$ For this to hold true everywhere, we must have $1 + b = 0 \implies b = -1$.
Q27. The stream function for a two-dimensional, irrotational, incompressible flow is given by $ψ = -5x + 3y$. What is the magnitude of the velocity vector?
A) $√34 m/s$
B) $8 m/s$
C) $2 m/s$
D) $√8 m/s$
The velocity components are derived from the stream function $ψ$: $$u = ∂ ψ∂ y = 3 m/s$$ $$v = -∂ ψ∂ x = -(-5) = 5 m/s$$ The magnitude of the velocity vector is: $$|\vec{V}| = √u^2 + v^2 = √3^2 + 5^2 = √9 + 25 = √34 m/s$$
Q28. In potential flow, what happens to the velocity at the center of a line vortex (at $r = 0$)?
A) It remains zero.
B) It approaches a finite maximum value.
C) It becomes singular (approaches infinity).
D) It oscillates sinusoidally.
For a potential (free) vortex, the tangential velocity is: $$vθ = \Gamma2π r$$ As $r \to 0$, $vθ \to ∞$. This singularity at the origin is a characteristic limitation of the idealized potential vortex model. Real viscous fluids avoid this singularity by forming a solid-body rotation core near the center (Rankine vortex).
Q29. What is the skin friction coefficient $C_f$ at the wall in terms of wall shear stress $τ_w$, dynamic viscosity $μ$, density $ρ$, and free-stream velocity $U∞$?
A) $C_f = τ_wρ U∞^2$
B) $C_f = τ_w12ρ U∞^2$
C) $C_f = μ τ_wρ U∞$
D) $C_f = τ_wμ U∞$
The local skin friction coefficient $C_f$ is defined as the wall shear stress normalized by the dynamic pressure of the free stream: $$C_f = τ_w12ρ U∞^2$$
Q30. Consider a flow where the velocity vector is given by $\vec{V} = (x^2 t)\hat{i} - (2xyt)\hat{j}$. Calculate the local (temporal) acceleration vector at $(x, y) = (1, 2) m$ and $t = 3 s$.
A) $1\hat{i} - 4\hat{j} m/s^2$
B) $3\hat{i} - 12\hat{j} m/s^2$
C) $1\hat{i} - 2\hat{j} m/s^2$
D) $0$
The local acceleration vector is: $$∂ \vec{V}∂ t = ∂ (x^2 t)∂ t\hat{i} + ∂ (-2xyt)∂ t\hat{j} = x^2\hat{i} - 2xy\hat{j}$$ Substituting $(x, y) = (1, 2)$: $$∂ \vec{V}∂ t = (1)^2\hat{i} - 2(1)(2)\hat{j} = 1\hat{i} - 4\hat{j} m/s^2$$ Note that this temporal acceleration is independent of time $t$ for this specific velocity field.

Statement 2: In a favorable pressure gradient ($dpdx < 0$), the velocity profile does not contain an inflection point inside the boundary layer.

Q3. Assertion (A): The non-linear term $(\vec{V} · \nabla)\vec{V}$ in the Navier-Stokes equations is responsible for the transition from laminar flow to chaotic turbulence.

Q4. A potential doublet flow of strength $\kappa = 16 m^3/s$ is superimposed on a uniform flow of $U∞ = 4 m/s$. Find the velocity of the fluid at the coordinates $(r, θ) = (2 m, π/2)$.

C) $ρ u ∂ u∂ x = -dpdx$

At the wall ($y = 0$), the velocity components are zero due to the no-slip condition ($u = v = 0$). Substituting $u=v=0$ into the Prandtl boundary layer equation $ρ ( u ∂ u∂ x + v ∂ u∂ y ) = -dpdx + μ ∂^2 u∂ y^2$ yields: $$0 = -dpdx + μ \left. ∂^2 u∂ y^2 |y=0 \implies μ \left. ∂^2 u∂ y^2 |y=0 = dpdx$$
Q6. Which of the following sets of physical variables constitutes a valid group of "repeating variables" when applying the Buckingham Pi Theorem to aerodynamic drag (where primary dimensions are $M, L, T$)?
A) Density ($ρ$), Velocity ($V$), Chord length ($c$)
B) Viscosity ($μ$), density ($ρ$), kinematic viscosity ($ν$)
C) Drag force ($D$), velocity ($V$), density ($ρ$)
D) Velocity ($V$), speed of sound ($a$), dynamic pressure ($q$)
Repeating variables must meet two main rules: 1. They must collectively contain all primary dimensions ($M, L, T$). 2. They must not be able to form a dimensionless group on their own. The combination of density ($ρ: M L-3$), velocity ($V: L T-1$), and length ($c: L$) meets both criteria and is the standard choice for aerodynamic problems. Q7. The velocity components of a flow field are $u = 3x^2 - 3y^2$ and $v = -6xy$. What is the circulation ($\Gamma$) around a square loop with vertices at $(0,0), (1,0), (1,1), (0,1)$?
A) $0$
B) $1$
C) $2$
D) $-4$
We can find circulation using Stokes' Theorem: $$\Gamma = \iint_A ω_z \, dA$$ First, compute the vorticity $ω_z$: $$ω_z = ∂ v∂ x - ∂ u∂ y = ∂(-6xy)∂ x - ∂(3x^2 - 3y^2)∂ y = -6y - (-6y) = -6y + 6y = 0$$ Since the flow is irrotational ($ω_z = 0$) everywhere, the circulation around any closed loop in this region is zero.
Q8. For high Reynolds number flows over streamlined aerodynamic bodies, the Navier-Stokes equations can be simplified to the Prandtl Boundary Layer equations. Which of the following assumptions is key to this simplification?

C) Viscosity is assumed to be exactly zero throughout the entire flow field.
D) The flow is assumed to be highly rotational outside the boundary layer.
Prandtl's boundary layer theory assumes that because the boundary layer is very thin ($\delta \ll L$), the velocity changes much more rapidly across the layer than along it. This means viscous diffusion along the wall ($∂^2 u∂ x^2$) is negligible compared to diffusion normal to the wall ($∂^2 u∂ y^2$). Additionally, the pressure gradient normal to the wall is assumed to be zero ($∂ p∂ y ≈ 0$).
Q9. A potential flow combines a line sink of strength $q = 20 m^2/s$ at the origin and a uniform flow $U∞ = 5 m/s$ along the positive x-axis. Find the distance of the stagnation point from the origin.

B) $2π m$
C) $4π m$
D) $2π m$
A line sink pulls fluid inward radially. The radial velocity due to the sink of strength $q$ is: $$v_r = -q2π r$$ The uniform flow along the positive x-axis has a radial velocity component of: $$v_r = U∞\cosθ$$ The stagnation point lies on the positive x-axis ($θ = 0$), where the inward sink flow directly opposes the outward uniform flow: $$U∞ - q2π r = 0 \implies r = q2π U∞$$ Substituting the given values ($q = 20 m^2/s$ and $U∞ = 5 m/s$): $$r = 202π × 5 = 2010π = 2π m$$
Q10. What physical condition violates Kelvin's Circulation Theorem, leading to the generation of vorticity over a clean aircraft wing?

B) Shock waves in supersonic flow regimes.

8. Error Analysis & Common Traps (5 min)

To succeed in the UPSC Air Safety Officer exam, watch out for these common mathematical and conceptual traps:

1. Neglecting Convective Acceleration in "Steady" Flows

A classic student mistake is assuming that because a flow is steady ($∂ \vec{V}∂ t = 0$), its acceleration must be zero. Acceleration is the total material derivative:

$$D\vec{V}Dt = \underbrace{∂ \vec{V}∂ t}0 (steady) + \underbrace{(\vec{V} · \nabla)\vec{V}}convective terms$$

In a converging nozzle, the flow is steady, but a fluid particle speeds up as it moves downstream. This means the convective acceleration term is non-zero. Always compute both components.

2. The Buckingham Pi Theorem Variable Count Trap

Students often miscount the number of primary dimensions ($m$) when applying the Buckingham Pi theorem. They assume $m$ is always 3 (for $M, L, T$). However, if none of the physical variables contain mass (for example, if only kinematics are involved, using just $L$ and $T$), then $m = 2$. Always write out the dimensional formulas for all $n$ variables before calculating $k = n - m$.

3. Confusing Free (Potential) and Forced (Solid-Body) Vortices

This is a common point of confusion in exam questions:

  • Free (Potential) Vortex: Irrotational ($\nabla × \vec{V} = 0$). Tangential velocity is inversely proportional to radius ($vθ ∝ 1/r$). It is modeled using a velocity potential ($φ$).
  • Forced (Solid-Body) Vortex: Rotational ($\nabla × \vec{V} = 2\vec{\Omega} \neq 0$). Tangential velocity is directly proportional to radius ($vθ ∝ r$). It cannot be modeled using a velocity potential.

4. Sign Errors in Doublet Potential and Stream Functions

Be careful with the sign of the doublet strength ($\kappa$). If the doublet axis points from the source to the sink (along the positive x-axis), the stream function is:

$$ψ = -\kappa \sinθr$$

If the doublet is oriented along the negative x-axis, the sign flips. Always check the orientation described in the question before selecting your equation.
