Rayleigh flow– heat addition

Day 10 of 129 Compressible Flow & Compressors Phase 1: Foundations 90-120 min Master Class

1. Understand (From Absolute Zero, 5–10 min)

Welcome to Day 10 of your advanced gas dynamics and aeronautical engineering journey tailored specifically for the UPSC Air Safety Officer (ASO) examination. Today, we master Rayleigh Flow, which models one-dimensional, steady, compressible flow through a constant-area duct with the inclusion of heat transfer (heat addition or rejection) and frictionless (or negligible friction) walls.

To build from absolute zero: Imagine air flowing steadily inside a straight tube of constant cross-sectional area $A$. As this air travels, heat is added externally through the walls (such as in a ramjet combustion chamber or a rocket thrust chamber). Because heat energy is added, the fluid's temperature, enthalpy, and Mach number change dramatically. Rayleigh flow provides the exact mathematical framework to predict these changes.

In standard UPSC terminology, Rayleigh flow focuses on the interplay between thermal energy addition ($q$) and compressible gas dynamics. Unlike Fanno flow (which models friction at constant cross-sectional area without heat transfer), Rayleigh flow models heat addition at constant area without friction. A fundamental property of Rayleigh flow is that regardless of whether you start with a subsonic or supersonic Mach number, adding heat drives the local Mach number ($M$) toward $1.0$ (sonic conditions). This phenomenon is known as thermal choking.

SI units governing this module include: Pressure in Pascals ($\text{Pa}$ or $\text{N/m}^2$), Temperature in Kelvin ($\text{K}$), Density in $\text{kg/m}^3$, Velocity in $\text{m/s}$, Heat added per unit mass in Joules per kilogram ($\text{J/kg}$), and Entropy in $\text{J/(kg}\cdot\text{K)}$. Master these definitions, as they form the bedrock of high-speed propulsion and aircraft safety accident investigations related to unstarted inlets and thermal runaway.

2. Learn the Concept (Complete Theoretical Foundation & Derivations, 20–25 min)

Let us build the complete theoretical foundation of Rayleigh flow step-by-step from zero background. Consider a one-dimensional, steady flow of a calorically perfect gas ($\gamma = 1.4$ for air, constant specific heats $c_p$ and $c_v$) through a constant area duct ($A_1 = A_2 = \text{constant}$). The governing conservation equations are:

  • Continuity Equation: $\rho_1 V_1 = \rho_2 V_2 = \frac{\dot{m}}{A} = G = \text{constant}$ (where $G$ is the mass velocity).
  • Momentum Equation: $p_1 + \rho_1 V_1^2 = p_2 + \rho_2 V_2^2 = \text{constant}$. Because the area is constant and friction is neglected, the static pressure plus momentum flux remains invariant.
  • Energy Equation: $h_1 + \frac{1}{2} V_1^2 + q = h_2 + \frac{1}{2} V_2^2$, which simplifies in terms of total enthalpy or total temperature to: $c_p T_{01} + q = c_p T_{02}$, or $q = c_p (T_{02} - T_{01})$.
  • Equation of State: $p = \rho R T$, combined with the definition of the local speed of sound $a = \sqrt{\gamma R T}$ and Mach number $M = \frac{V}{a}$.
$$ \frac{T_0}{T_0^*} = \frac{({\gamma+1}) M^2 \left[ 2 + (\gamma-1) M^2 \right]}{(1 + \gamma M^2)^2} $$

By combining the conservation equations with the ideal gas law and defining the dimensionless parameters, we derive the Rayleigh flow equations. The locus of all possible states for a given mass flow rate and momentum is called the Rayleigh Line on the $T\text{-}s$ (Temperature-Entropy) diagram. The Rayleigh line exhibits two distinct branches: a lower branch representing subsonic flow ($M < 1$) and an upper branch representing supersonic flow ($M > 1$). Both branches merge at a point of maximum entropy where the Mach number equals exactly unity ($M = 1$).

Speed of Sound & Stagnation Relations: The speed of sound is $a = \sqrt{\gamma R T}$. Isentropic stagnation temperature is defined as $T_0 = T \left(1 + \frac{\gamma-1}{2}M^2\right)$. However, in Rayleigh flow, because heat $q$ is added, the stagnation temperature $T_0$ is not constant; it increases when heat is added ($q > 0$) and decreases when heat is removed ($q < 0$). Conversely, the static pressure $p$ decreases when heat is added in subsonic flow, but increases when heat is added in supersonic flow until $M=1$ is reached.

Rankine-Hugoniot & Choking Limits: While normal shocks are governed by Rankine-Hugoniot adiabatic relations ($q=0$), Rayleigh flow incorporates explicit diabatic energy input. The maximum possible heat that can be added to a subsonic stream without choking the flow is achieved precisely when the exit Mach number reaches $M_2 = 1.0$. If additional heat is supplied beyond this maximum thermal limit ($q > q_{\max}$), the mass flow rate must drop (choking occurs), causing upstream pressure disturbances, inlet unstart, or shock detachment in aerospace intakes.

Entropy Changes: The change in specific entropy between any two states 1 and 2 in Rayleigh flow is given by:

$$ s_2 - s_1 = c_p \ln \left( \frac{T_2}{T_1} \right) - R \ln \left( \frac{p_2}{p_1} \right) = c_p \ln \left( \frac{T_2}{T_1} \right) - R \ln \left( \frac{M_1}{M_2} \left( \frac{1 + \gamma M_1^2}{1 + \gamma M_2^2} \right) \right) $$

Entropy increases when heat is added ($q > 0$) regardless of whether the flow is subsonic or supersonic, moving the state towards the maximum entropy point ($M=1$). When heat is rejected ($q < 0$), entropy decreases, moving the state away from $M=1$ along the respective subsonic or supersonic branch of the Rayleigh curve.

3. Memorize Important Facts (10 min)

  • Core Formula 1 & Mach Ratios: Pressure ratio relative to sonic (* state): $\frac{p}{p^*} = \frac{1 + \gamma}{1 + \gamma M^2}$
  • Standard Shock / Isentropic Jump Constant: Temperature ratio relative to sonic (* state): $\frac{T}{T^*} = \left[ \frac{(1 + \gamma) M}{1 + \gamma M^2} \right]^2$
  • Key Thermal / Friction Choking Property: Maximum Heat Addition Limit occurs strictly at $M = 1.0$, where entropy $s$ reaches its absolute maximum along the Rayleigh line ($ds = 0$).

4. Aviation Application (5–10 min)

Rayleigh flow principles are vital in high-speed aeronautics and propulsion engineering. Consider a Ramjet or Scramjet Engine Combustion Chamber: air entering the diffuser is compressed, and fuel is injected and burned. The combustion process releases immense thermal energy into the airflow. Because combustion occurs in a duct of nearly constant cross-sectional area, this process is modeled directly as Rayleigh flow with heat addition ($q > 0$).

If too much fuel is injected, the heat added exceeds $q_{\max}$, causing thermal choking ($M = 1$ at the exit). This chokes the mass flow rate, resulting in severe combustion instability, thrust loss, and potential "unstart" where the supersonic shock wave is expelled out of the engine intake, causing catastrophic engine flameout. Aircraft safety officers and propulsion engineers use Rayleigh flow equations to calculate fuel-air equivalence ratios, maximum heat release limits, and tailpipe nozzle sizing to prevent thermal choking and ensure safe supersonic cruise operation (such as in Concorde or military interceptor engines).

5. UPSC ASO Focus (10 min)

  • Frequently Asked Exam Facts: For air ($\gamma = 1.4$), the static temperature reaches its maximum value at $M = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\gamma}} \approx 0.845$, whereas static enthalpy reaches maximum and entropy reaches absolute maximum at $M = 1.0$. The Rayleigh curve has an infinite slope at $M = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\gamma}}$ on the $T\text{-}s$ diagram.
  • Conceptual Traps: Students often mistakenly assume that total temperature $T_0$ is constant in Rayleigh flow because there is no friction or work. Remember: $T_0$ changes linearly with heat addition ($q = c_p \Delta T_0$). Total pressure ($p_0$), however, always decreases due to diabatic heating in subsonic flow and diabatic cooling in supersonic flow, reflecting thermodynamic irreversibility (entropy generation).

Rayleigh flow models frictionless, one-dimensional flow in a constant-area duct with heat transfer. Thus, heat transfer is the sole driver of property changes.

In Rayleigh flow, adding heat to a subsonic stream drives the Mach number upward toward $1.0$ (thermal choking).

A) $M = 1.0$
B) $M = 0.50$
C) $M = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\gamma}} \approx 0.845$

Differentiation of the Rayleigh temperature relation reveals that static temperature reaches its absolute maximum at $M = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\gamma}}$. For $\gamma = 1.4$, this is $1/\sqrt{1.4} \approx 0.845$.
Q4. In Rayleigh flow, if heat is added to a supersonic stream ($M > 1$), what happens to the Mach number?
A) It increases further above $1.0$
B) It decreases downward toward $1.0$
C) It drops instantaneously to $0.5$
D) It remains unchanged
Adding heat to a supersonic stream in Rayleigh flow decreases its Mach number, driving it down toward $1.0$. Conversely, cooling a supersonic stream increases its Mach number.

A) Static temperature ($T$)

Because friction is neglected and area is constant, applying Newton's second law yields the constant momentum function $p_1 + \rho_1 V_1^2 = p_2 + \rho_2 V_2^2$. Stagnation temperature changes due to heat addition ($q = c_p \Delta T_0$).
Q6. What happens to total pressure ($p_0$) when heat is added ($q > 0$) in subsonic Rayleigh flow?
A) Total pressure increases
B) Total pressure remains constant
C) Total pressure decreases
D) Total pressure drops to zero
Diabatic heating in subsonic Rayleigh flow always introduces thermodynamic irreversibility, resulting in a decrease in total pressure ($p_0$ drops).
Q7. At the point of maximum entropy on the Rayleigh line (where $M = 1$), what is the gradient $\frac{ds}{dT}$?

On the $T\text{-}s$ diagram, the Rayleigh curve is vertical ($ds/dT = \infty$) at the sonic point ($M=1$), which represents the maximum entropy state for given mass flow and momentum.

For subsonic Rayleigh flow, adding heat accelerates the flow ($V$ increases, $M$ increases towards 1), which according to the momentum equation causes static pressure $p$ to decrease.

8. Error Analysis & Common Traps (5 min)

Mastering Rayleigh flow requires avoiding several common conceptual and calculation traps:

  • Assuming Stagnation Temperature is Constant ($T_{01} = T_{02}$): Students often confuse Rayleigh flow with Fanno flow or normal shocks. In Rayleigh flow, heat is added or removed externally ($q \neq 0$), which changes the stagnation temperature linearly: $q = c_p (T_{02} - T_{01})$.
  • Confusing Static Temperature Peak with Sonic Choking: While entropy reaches its maximum and the flow chokes at $M = 1.0$, the static temperature $T$ actually reaches its maximum at $M = 1/\sqrt{\gamma}$ (approx. $0.845$ for air). Adding heat between $M = 0.845$ and $M = 1.0$ causes static temperature to decrease while total temperature increases.
  • Misinterpreting Supersonic Heat Addition: Students frequently assume heat addition always increases Mach number. In supersonic Rayleigh flow ($M > 1$), adding heat decreases the Mach number toward $1.0$. Cooling is required to increase a supersonic Mach number.
  • Ignoring Thermal Choking Limits ($q_{\max}$): Assuming arbitrary amounts of heat can be added to a duct is a critical error. Exceeding $q_{\max}$ leads to thermal choking, mass flow reduction, and catastrophic aircraft propulsion unstart.
