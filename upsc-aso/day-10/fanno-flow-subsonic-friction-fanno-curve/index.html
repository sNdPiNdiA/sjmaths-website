Fanno flow – subsonic friction, Fanno curve

Day 10 of 129 Compressible Flow & Compressors Phase 1: Foundations 90-120 min Master Class

1. Understand (From Absolute Zero, 5–10 min)

Welcome, future Air Safety Officers and Aerospace Engineers! In Day 10 of our advanced Gas Dynamics and Aeronautical Engineering module, we explore Fanno Flow, which models the steady, one-dimensional compressible flow of a calorically perfect gas through a constant-area duct with friction and adiabatic (no heat transfer) walls. From absolute scratch: imagine air or combustion gases rushing through a long aircraft environmental control system (ECS) pipe, a rocket fuel feed line, or an intake bypass duct. As the fluid rubs against the inner pipe walls, wall shear stress ($\tau_w$) exerts a retarding force. This frictional drag is not just a minor pressure drop; in compressible flow, friction fundamentally couples with fluid velocity, density, temperature, and Mach number in counter-intuitive ways.

What is the Fanno Curve? It is the locus of all possible thermodynamic states (defined by enthalpy/temperature and entropy) that a gas can achieve in a constant-area duct under adiabatic frictional flow. For subsonic friction, as a fluid flows down a constant-area duct, friction causes the Mach number to increase ($dM > 0$), driving the flow toward sonic conditions ($M = 1$). Conversely, for supersonic flow, friction causes the Mach number to decrease ($dM < 0$), also driving the flow toward $M = 1$. This ultimate limiting state of $M = 1$ is known as Fanno Choking. The SI units governing these relations include Pascal ($\text{Pa}$) for pressure, Kelvin ($\text{K}$) for temperature, $\text{kg/m}^3$ for density, $\text{J/(kg}\cdot\text{K)}$ for specific heat, and dimensionless parameters like Mach number ($M$) and friction factor ($f$). Mastering this concept is critical for UPSC ASO examinations because it dictates duct sizing, pressure losses in aircraft ducting, and safety margins against choking in engine auxiliary systems.

2. Learn the Concept (Complete Theoretical Foundation & Derivations, 20–25 min)

Let us build the complete theoretical foundation of Fanno flow from zero background, progressing to rigorous differential equations, isentropic relations, speed of sound derivations, and entropy changes.

1. Fundamental Equations of Fanno Flow

Consider a steady, one-dimensional flow of a compressible gas through a duct of constant cross-sectional area $A$ with wall friction. The governing conservation laws in differential form are:

  • Continuity (Mass Conservation): $$\dot{m} = \rho u A = \text{constant} \implies \ln \rho + \ln u + \ln A = \text{constant}$$ Differentiating: $\frac{d\rho}{\rho} + \frac{du}{u} = 0$ (since $dA = 0$).
  • Momentum Conservation: Taking a control volume of length $dx$, the forces acting on the fluid are pressure forces and wall shear stress $\tau_w$: $$pA - (p + dp)A - \tau_w P dx = \dot{m}(u + du - u)$$ where $P$ is the wetted perimeter. Defining the hydraulic diameter $D_h = \frac{4A}{P}$, the momentum equation becomes: $$-Adp - \tau_w \left(\frac{4A}{D_h}\right) dx = \dot{m} du$$ Dividing by $A$: $$dp + \frac{1}{2}\rho u^2 \left( \frac{4 f_{D}}{D_h} \right) dx + \rho u du = 0$$ where $f_D$ is the Darcy friction factor defined by $\tau_w = \frac{1}{8} f_D \rho u^2$ (or using the Fanning friction factor $f = f_D/4$, noting standard gas dynamics convention uses $4f \frac{dx}{D}$).
  • Energy Conservation (Adiabatic): Since there is no heat transfer ($dq = 0$) and no shaft work ($dw_s = 0$), the total enthalpy remains constant: $$h_0 = h + \frac{1}{2}u^2 = \text{constant}$$ Differentiating: $dh + u du = 0$. Since $h = C_p T$, this implies $C_p dT + u du = 0$, meaning the stagnation temperature $T_0$ is constant throughout Fanno flow ($dT_0 = 0$).

2. Speed of Sound and Mach Number Relations

Using the definition of the Mach number $M = \frac{u}{a}$ where $a = \sqrt{\gamma R T}$, we combine the conservation equations with the ideal gas law ($p = \rho R T$) to express property variations strictly as a function of the Mach number and friction parameter $\frac{4f dx}{D_h}$. The fundamental differential equation governing Fanno flow is the Shapiro Fanno Equation:

$$ \frac{4 f_{D} dx}{D_h} = \frac{1 - M^2}{\gamma M^3 \left(1 + \frac{\gamma-1}{2} M^2\right)} dM^2 $$

Let us analyze this master equation in detail:

  • Subsonic Flow ($M < 1$): The term $1 - M^2 > 1$. Since $dx > 0$ in the direction of flow and friction is positive ($f_D > 0$), the right-hand side must be positive. This forces $dM^2 > 0$, meaning $dM > 0$. As friction acts on a subsonic stream, its Mach number increases, accelerating the flow toward $M = 1$.
  • Supersonic Flow ($M > 1$): The term $1 - M^2 < 0$. For the right-hand side to remain positive (since $dx > 0$), $dM^2$ must be negative, meaning $dM < 0$. As friction acts on a supersonic stream, its Mach number decreases, decelerating the flow toward $M = 1$.
  • Choking ($M = 1$): At $M = 1$, the numerator $1 - M^2 = 0$, implying $\frac{4f_{D}dx}{D_h} = 0$. This is the mathematical condition for Fanno Choking. Maximum entropy occurs precisely at $M = 1$. Friction cannot drive a subsonic flow past $M=1$, nor a supersonic flow below $M=1$ in a constant-area duct without changing the duct geometry or upstream conditions.

3. The Fanno Curve and Entropy Change

By combining the continuity and energy equations while eliminating velocity and density, we obtain the Fanno line on the $h-s$ (Enthalpy-Entropy) or $T-s$ plane. The change in entropy relative to the choked reference state ($M=1$, denoted by superscript $*$) is given by:

$$ \Delta s = s - s^* = C_p \ln \left( \frac{T}{T^*} \right) - R \ln \left( \frac{p}{p^*} \right) = \frac{R}{2} \ln \left[ \left( \frac{\gamma+1}{2} \right)^{\frac{\gamma+1}{\gamma}} \frac{M^2}{\left(1 + \frac{\gamma-1}{2}M^2\right)^{\frac{\gamma+1}{\gamma}}} \right] $$

Key properties of the Fanno curve:

  • The upper branch of the Fanno curve represents subsonic flow ($M < 1$). Moving downwards along this branch toward the nose of the curve (where $s$ is maximum), entropy increases ($ds > 0$) due to friction, and Mach number increases toward $1$.
  • The lower branch represents supersonic flow ($M > 1$). Moving upwards along this branch toward the nose, entropy also increases ($ds > 0$), and Mach number decreases toward $1$.
  • The nose of the Fanno curve corresponds to maximum entropy and the sonic point ($M = 1$). The Second Law of Thermodynamics ($\Delta s \ge 0$) dictates that entropy must increase in the direction of flow. Thus, fluid states can only move toward the sonic point, never away from it via frictional adiabatic flow.

3. Memorize Important Facts (10 min)

  • Core Formula 1 & Fanno Friction Differential Equation: $\frac{4 f dx}{D} = \frac{1 - M^2}{\gamma M^3 \left(1 + \frac{\gamma-1}{2} M^2\right)} dM^2$
  • Stagnation Temperature Invariance: $T_0 = \text{constant}$ ($dT_0 = 0$) because Fanno flow is adiabatic with no shaft work ($dq = 0, dw_s = 0$).
  • Maximum Entropy Choking Condition: The entropy is maximum at the sonic state ($M = 1$). $ds = 0$ at $M=1$, and $\frac{ds}{dx} > 0$ for all physical flows.
  • Fanno Parameter Ratio ($4fL_{\max}/D$): $$\frac{4f L_{\max}}{D_h} = \frac{1 - M^2}{\gamma M^2} + \frac{\gamma + 1}{2\gamma} \ln \left[ \frac{(\gamma + 1)M^2}{2 + (\gamma - 1)M^2} \right]$$
  • Static Temperature Ratio in Fanno Flow: $$\frac{T}{T^*} = \frac{\gamma + 1}{2 + (\gamma - 1)M^2}$$
  • Static Pressure Ratio in Fanno Flow: $$\frac{p}{p^*} = \frac{1}{M} \left[ \frac{\gamma + 1}{2 + (\gamma - 1)M^2} \right]^{1/2}$$
  • Stagnation Pressure Ratio in Fanno Flow: $$\frac{p_0}{p_0^*} = \frac{1}{M} \left[ \frac{2 + (\gamma - 1)M^2}{\gamma + 1} \right]^{\frac{\gamma + 1}{2(\gamma - 1)}}$$

4. Aviation Application (5–10 min)

In aerospace engineering and aviation safety, Fanno flow principles govern numerous critical systems:

  • Aircraft Environmental Control Systems (ECS) & Bleed Air Ducting: High-pressure, high-temperature bleed air is extracted from the gas turbine compressor stages and routed through long pneumatic steel ducts to air cycle machines for cabin pressurization and cooling. The significant length-to-diameter ratio ($L/D$) of these ducts means that friction causes considerable total pressure losses and can lead to thermal/frictional choking if duct diameters are undersized or pinched.
  • Rocket Engine Cryogenic Propellant Feed Lines: Liquid oxygen (LOX) and liquid hydrogen (LH2) or RP-1 are pumped through long delivery lines from turbo-pumps to the combustion chamber injector face. Frictional pressure drop reduces net positive suction head (NPSH), and improper pipe sizing can induce cavitation or choked flow anomalies.
  • Jet Engine Auxiliary Air & Bypass Ducts: Nacelle boundary layer bleed ducts, turbine cooling air internal passages, and exhaust mixer lobes experience high-speed internal flows where friction dictates static pressure recovery and mass flow rate limits.
  • Air Safety Investigation Insight: Unaccounted-for duct friction combined with thermal insulation breakdown can cause localized choking in bleed air ducts, resulting in pneumatic system starvation, loss of wing anti-ice capability, or structural duct burst due to pressure redistribution.

5. UPSC ASO Focus (10 min)

  • Frequently Asked Exam Facts:
    • For air ($\gamma = 1.4$), stagnation temperature $T_0$ remains constant throughout Fanno flow ($T_{01} = T_{02}$).
    • Stagnation pressure $p_0$ always decreases in the direction of Fanno flow due to viscous dissipation ($dp_0 < 0$), even though stagnation temperature is constant.
    • At the Fanno choking point ($M=1$), the maximum duct length parameter $\frac{4f L_{\max}}{D} = 0$. If the actual duct length exceeds $L_{\max}$, the mass flow rate chokes and automatically reduces to a lower value.
  • Conceptual Traps:
    • Trap: "Does friction decrease stagnation temperature in Fanno flow?" — Correction: No! Stagnation temperature $T_0$ is strictly constant because Fanno flow is adiabatic ($dq = 0$). Friction converts directed kinetic energy into internal thermal energy, raising static temperature ($T$) while lowering Mach number (in supersonic) or raising Mach number (in subsonic), keeping $T_0 = T(1 + \frac{\gamma-1}{2}M^2)$ constant.
    • Trap: Confusing Fanno flow (constant area, friction, adiabatic) with Rayleigh flow (constant area, heat transfer, frictionless).

Fanno flow is defined as steady, one-dimensional, frictional flow in a constant-area duct with zero heat transfer ($dq = 0$) and no shaft work ($dw_s = 0$). By the First Law of Thermodynamics, total enthalpy and stagnation temperature remain constant ($T_{0} = \text{constant}$). Correct Answer: C.

D) Both Mach number and static pressure decrease
In subsonic Fanno flow ($M < 1$), wall friction causes the Mach number to increase ($dM > 0$) toward the sonic limit ($M=1$). As velocity increases due to acceleration, continuity and momentum dictate that static pressure $p$ must decrease. Correct Answer: A.
Q3. What happens to the stagnation pressure ($p_0$) along the length of a duct in Fanno flow?
A) It increases monotonically
B) It remains constant because stagnation temperature is constant
C) It decreases monotonically due to viscous irreversibilities (entropy increase)
D) It increases in subsonic flow and decreases in supersonic flow
Fanno flow involves friction, which is an irreversible process. According to the Second Law of Thermodynamics, entropy must increase ($ds > 0$), resulting in a continuous drop in stagnation pressure ($dp_0 < 0$) along the flow direction regardless of whether the flow is subsonic or supersonic. Correct Answer: C.
Q4. In a supersonic Fanno flow ($M > 1$), how does friction influence the Mach number?
A) Mach number increases toward infinity
B) Mach number decreases toward 1
C) Mach number remains constant
D) Mach number drops immediately to zero
From the Shapiro Fanno relation $\frac{4f dx}{D} = \frac{1-M^2}{\gamma M^3 (1 + \frac{\gamma-1}{2}M^2)} dM^2$, for $M > 1$, the term $1-M^2$ is negative, requiring $dM^2 < 0$. Thus, supersonic friction decelerates the flow, decreasing $M$ toward the sonic limit $M=1$. Correct Answer: B.

C) $0.5$

Fanno choking occurs when the duct length equals or exceeds the maximum allowable length ($L \ge L_{\max}$), causing the exit Mach number to reach exactly $M = 1$. Correct Answer: C.

A) Zero velocity ($M=0$)
B) Sonic condition ($M=1$)
C) Infinite Mach number ($M \to \infty$)

The nose of the Fanno curve represents the maximum possible entropy state for a given mass flux, which occurs precisely at the sonic point ($M=1$). Correct Answer: B.
Q7. If a subsonic Fanno flow enters a duct with inlet Mach number $M_1 = 0.3$ and friction factor $f = 0.005$ in a pipe of diameter $D = 0.05\text{ m}$, what happens to the static temperature ($T$) along the flow?

B) Static temperature increases
C) Static temperature remains constant

In subsonic Fanno flow, as Mach number increases due to friction, velocity increases. From the energy equation $T_0 = T + \frac{u^2}{2C_p} = \text{const}$, since $T_0$ is constant and velocity $u$ increases, the static temperature $T$ must decrease. Correct Answer: A.
Q8. What physical phenomenon occurs if the actual length ($L$) of a constant-area duct exceeds the maximum friction length ($L_{\max}$) calculated for a given subsonic inlet Mach number?
A) Flow reverses direction
B) Flow chokes at the exit ($M=1$), and the upstream mass flow rate automatically decreases
C) Flow becomes supersonic at the exit

If $L > L_{\max}$, the duct cannot sustain the requested mass flow rate. The flow chokes at the exit ($M=1$), and the upstream conditions adjust (mass flow rate decreases) until the new $L_{\max}$ matches the actual duct length. Correct Answer: B.
Q9. Which of the following thermodynamic variables remains constant along a Fanno flow duct?
A) Static pressure ($p$)
B) Stagnation pressure ($p_0$)
C) Stagnation temperature ($T_0$)

Fanno flow is adiabatic ($dq = 0$) with no shaft work ($dw_s = 0$), which directly implies that the stagnation temperature $T_0$ remains constant throughout the duct. Correct Answer: C.

In supersonic Fanno flow, friction causes the Mach number to decrease ($dM < 0$), meaning velocity $u$ decreases. Since $T_0 = T + \frac{u^2}{2C_p}$ is constant, a decrease in velocity $u$ results in an increase in static temperature $T$. Correct Answer: B.

A) $0.6$

C) $1.0$
D) $1.4$
By definition, $L_{\max}$ is the exact duct length required to accelerate a subsonic flow (or decelerate a supersonic flow) from inlet Mach number $M_1$ to the sonic choked condition $M_2 = 1.0$. Correct Answer: C.
Q6. (Grand Mini Test) Assertion (A): Stagnation pressure $p_0$ decreases in Fanno flow even though the process is adiabatic. Reason (R): Friction introduces non-isentropic viscous dissipation, generating entropy which leads to a loss of stagnation pressure.
A) Both A and R are true, and R is the correct explanation of A
B) Both A and R are true, but R is NOT the correct explanation of A
C) A is true, but R is false
D) A is false, but R is true
Adiabatic flow does not imply isentropic flow if irreversible friction is present. Friction generates entropy ($ds > 0$), which is directly tied to stagnation pressure loss via $ds = -R \ln(p_{02}/p_{01})$. Correct Answer: A.
Q7. (Grand Mini Test) Which of the following thermodynamic relations correctly represents entropy change in terms of stagnation pressure ratio across a Fanno flow from state 1 to state 2?
A) $s_2 - s_1 = R \ln\left(\frac{p_{01}}{p_{02}}\right)$
B) $s_2 - s_1 = C_p \ln\left(\frac{p_{01}}{p_{02}}\right)$
C) $s_2 - s_1 = -R \ln\left(\frac{T_{02}}{T_{01}}\right)$
D) $s_2 - s_1 = 0$
Since stagnation temperature is constant ($T_{01} = T_{02}$), the Gibbs relation simplifies to $\Delta s = s_2 - s_1 = -R \ln(p_{02}/p_{01}) = R \ln(p_{01}/p_{02})$. Correct Answer: A.
Q8. (Grand Mini Test) Statement 1: In supersonic Fanno flow, wall friction causes static temperature to increase. Statement 2: In subsonic Fanno flow, wall friction causes static temperature to decrease. Which statement(s) are correct?
A) Both Statement 1 and Statement 2 are true
B) Statement 1 is true, but Statement 2 is false
C) Statement 1 is false, but Statement 2 is true
D) Both Statement 1 and Statement 2 are false
In supersonic Fanno flow, friction decelerates the fluid ($u$ decreases), converting kinetic energy into internal thermal energy, thereby increasing static temperature ($T$). In subsonic flow, friction accelerates the fluid ($u$ increases), decreasing static temperature ($T$). Both statements are correct. Correct Answer: A.
Q9. (Grand Mini Test) If the friction factor $f_D = 0.02$ and hydraulic diameter $D_h = 0.1\text{ m}$, what is the value of the friction parameter per unit length $\frac{4f_D}{D_h}$?
A) $0.2\text{ m}^{-1}$
B) $0.8\text{ m}^{-1}$
C) $2.0\text{ m}^{-1}$
D) $8.0\text{ m}^{-1}$
Calculation: $\frac{4 f_D}{D_h} = \frac{4 \times 0.02}{0.1} = \frac{0.08}{0.1} = 0.8\text{ m}^{-1}$. Correct Answer: B.
Q10. (Grand Mini Test) What is the ultimate fate of any Fanno flow duct of sufficient length, regardless of whether the inlet is subsonic or supersonic?
A) The flow becomes completely isothermal
C) The flow reaches sonic choking ($M = 1.0$)
C) The flow velocity drops to zero (stagnation)
D) The flow becomes perfectly isentropic
Friction acts as a directional driver pushing the Mach number toward $1.0$ from both above and below. If the duct is longer than or equal to $L_{\max}$, the flow will choke at $M = 1.0$. Correct Answer: B.

8. Error Analysis & Common Traps (5 min)

Common student errors and conceptual traps in Fanno flow analysis include:

  • Stagnation Temperature Confusion: Assuming that because friction generates heat, stagnation temperature $T_0$ increases. Students often confuse adiabatic Fanno flow with heated Rayleigh flow. In Fanno flow, $T_0$ is strictly constant ($dT_0 = 0$) because walls are adiabatic ($dq = 0$). Friction redistributes kinetic energy into internal energy, raising static temperature $T$ while total enthalpy remains invariant.
  • Sign Error in Mach Number Variation: Believing friction always decreases velocity. While true for supersonic flow ($M>1$, velocity decreases toward sonic), in subsonic flow ($M<1$), friction actually accelerates the fluid toward $M=1$ to satisfy mass continuity in a constant-area duct.
  • Misinterpreting Choking Length ($L_{\max}$): Assuming that if a duct is longer than $L_{\max}$, the flow stops completely. In reality, the flow chokes at the exit ($M_2 = 1$), and the upstream mass flow rate self-adjusts (decreases) until the new maximum length matches the actual physical duct length.
  • Neglecting Stagnation Pressure Drop: Assuming $p_0$ remains constant because the flow is adiabatic. Students forget that friction is an irreversible viscous dissipation process that generates entropy, causing a continuous drop in stagnation pressure ($dp_0 < 0$).
