Forces of Flight: Lift, Weight, Thrust and Drag

Build a vector-based understanding of aircraft motion. Learn when forces balance, how climb and glide change their components, and why acceleration depends on the resultant force along the flight path.

What you should be able to do

UPSC ASO foundation
  • Name and direct each forceIdentify lift, weight, thrust and drag as vectors with distinct causes and directions.
  • Apply Newton’s three lawsConnect inertia, resultant force and action–reaction pairs to aircraft motion and propulsion.
  • Resolve a flight pathApply force components to level flight, climb, descent and glide using a stated angle convention.
  • Connect force and performanceInterpret excess thrust, excess power, rate of climb, glide angle and turn load factor.

Coverage check: This lesson follows Topic 1.3 L1–L5: four-force balance; all three Newton laws and flight-path geometry; steady and accelerated flight; bank and pull-up loading, stall-speed effects, excess thrust and power; and partial or complete thrust loss, glide range and engine-out performance limits. The 2018 Q7 force statements are included as a tagged practice item.

1. Begin with the four force resultants

A useful first model replaces distributed aerodynamic and gravitational forces with four resultant vectors. It predicts translation; a complete aircraft model also considers moments, rotation and control response.

Four forces in the simplified airplane model
ForceSymbol and usual directionWhat to remember
LiftL; perpendicular to relative airflow / flight path in the simple model.Mostly generated by wings; it is not automatically “straight up.”
WeightW = mg; toward Earth through the centre of gravity.Gravity acts during climb, descent and turns too.
ThrustT; generally forward, approximately along the path in basic cases.Produced by propulsion; its line of action can be angled or vectored.
DragD; opposite motion relative to the air.It opposes air-relative motion, not necessarily ground track.

Direction check: Lift and drag are aerodynamic resultants defined relative to airflow. Weight remains Earth-directed. Thrust direction depends on the propulsion installation.

2. Apply Newton’s three laws and test equilibrium

For translational motion, Newton’s second law is ΣF = ma. Add force vectors, not just magnitudes. If the resultant force is zero, acceleration is zero and velocity is constant; that velocity can be nonzero.

Newton’s three laws in flight
LawRuleAircraft application and exam check
First — inertiaIf the resultant external force is zero, an object remains at rest or moves with constant velocity in an inertial frame.“Constant velocity” means both speed and direction stay fixed; a turning aircraft is accelerating even at constant speed.
Second — resultant forceΣF = ma; resolve the vector equation along chosen axes.The along-path and normal components explain speeding up, slowing down, climbing and turning.
Third — action and reactionIf body A exerts a force on body B, body B exerts an equal and opposite force on A.The pair acts on different bodies, so it does not cancel on one aircraft free-body diagram. A propeller accelerates air aft; the air exerts forward thrust on the propeller and aircraft. A wing turns airflow and experiences the corresponding aerodynamic reaction.
Motion, force balance and the aircraft’s path
TermMeaningWhat it does not guarantee
Equilibrium of forcesThe vector resultant force is zero.It does not mean the aircraft is stationary or level.
Unaccelerated motionVelocity is constant in magnitude and direction.It can be a steady climb or descent.
Constant speedSpeed magnitude stays fixed.Direction may change in a turn; centripetal acceleration is then present.

For straight-and-level, unaccelerated flight in the simple model, vertical and longitudinal components balance separately: L = W and T = D. Equality of one pair balances only that component. A steady attitude also requires relevant moments about the centre of gravity to balance; the equations here focus on translational forces.

Read the net force: If T > D in level flight, the aircraft accelerates forward. If L = W at the same instant, it may gain speed without initially climbing.

3. Straight-and-level flight: the reference case

For straight, level flight at constant speed, flight-path angle γ is zero and acceleration is zero. The force components reduce to two opposing pairs:

  • Vertical: L − W = 0, so L = W.
  • Along the path: T − D = 0, so T = D.
How imbalance changes the reference case
ChangeImmediate tendency in the simple modelCaution
T > DForward acceleration.It does not by itself mean the aircraft climbs.
T < DDeceleration along the path.The aircraft may continue flying while slowing or changing path.
L > WUpward acceleration when the path is level.Actual motion depends on every force component.
L < WDownward acceleration in that same level-path model.A steady straight climb can have L < W.

Attitude is not flight path. Pitch attitude describes aircraft orientation; γ describes velocity direction relative to the horizon. Angle of attack is a third angle.

4. Resolve forces along climb, descent or curve

For motion in a vertical plane, choose axes parallel and perpendicular to the flight path. Let γ be positive for a climb, V speed along the path, and R its radius of curvature. In the basic model, thrust acts along the path and lift is normal to it.

Along path: T − D − W sin γ = m dV/dt

Normal to path: L − W cos γ = mV2/R

For a straight path, R tends to infinity and normal acceleration is zero. If speed is also constant, the equations reduce to T − D = W sin γ and L = W cos γ.

Flight-path angle and weight components
PathSign of γSteady-speed interpretation
Level0°T = D; L = W.
ClimbPositiveT > D; L = W cos γ < W.
DescentNegativeT − D is negative in a steady straight descent with this convention.

Angle convention matters: State that climb is positive before assigning signs. L < W alone does not prove descent; a steady straight climb has L = W cos γ.

5. Connect excess thrust, power and glide

Worked example: a steady climb

An aircraft climbs straight at constant speed through γ = 10° and weighs 24 kN. Use sin 10° ≈ 0.174 and cos 10° ≈ 0.985:

  • Required excess thrust: T − D = W sin γ ≈ 24 × 0.174 = 4.18 kN.
  • Lift for zero normal acceleration: L = W cos γ ≈ 24 × 0.985 = 23.64 kN.

Lift and thrust components together support the climb; lift need not exceed weight in this case.

Excess power and rate of climb

At steady speed, excess power available over that required for level flight supplies the rate of gain of potential energy:

Rate of climb ≈ (Pavailable − Prequired) / W

Watts divided by newtons gives metres per second. If speed is changing, excess power also changes kinetic energy and this simple relation is incomplete.

Glide with no engine thrust

For an ideal steady straight glide, T = 0. With downward glide-angle magnitude |γ|, D = W sin |γ| and L = W cos |γ|. Thus L/D = cot |γ|: a larger lift-to-drag ratio gives a shallower glide in this simplified model.

Compare partial thrust loss, total power loss and engine-out flight

“Thrust loss” does not always mean T = 0. Identify what propulsion remains, then apply the along-path force balance.

First-principles comparison of thrust-loss cases
CaseForce/performance modelWhat follows
Partial thrust reductionIf the aircraft is initially level and available thrust falls below drag, Tavailable − D < 0.It begins to decelerate if it holds the same path. Lift does not vanish instantly; speed and flight path then evolve together.
Complete power loss, single-engine aircraftUse the ideal glide model with T = 0; gravity’s along-path component balances drag in a steady glide.Glide angle and still-air range depend on lift-to-drag ratio. This is a performance model, not an emergency procedure.
One engine inoperative (OEI), multi-engine aircraftRemaining thrust may be positive, while asymmetric thrust and configuration-specific drag affect the result.Do not assume half the engines means half the climb performance. Weight, density altitude, temperature, configuration and remaining-engine performance matter; use that aircraft’s approved OEI data.

For an ideal, still-air glide from height h above the landing area, horizontal distance is approximately h(L/D). For example, h = 2.5 km and L/D = 12 give an idealized range of about 30 km. Wind, turns, speed, configuration and terrain change the reachable area, so this estimate is not a planning or operating instruction.

Safety boundary: These relations explain the physics. Actual engine-failure response, glide speed and landing choice follow the aircraft’s approved flight manual and trained procedures.

6. Constant speed can still require a net force

In a coordinated level turn, speed and altitude may stay constant while velocity direction changes. The banked lift vector has a vertical component that balances weight and a horizontal component that supplies centripetal force.

L cos φ = W;   L sin φ = mV2/R

Load factor: n = L/W = 1/cos φ (coordinated, level turn)

At 60° bank, cos 60° = 0.5, so n = 2: lift magnitude is twice weight in this idealized steady turn. Direction changes, so acceleration is present even though speed is constant.

Manoeuvre loading beyond the steady turn

Load factor n compares aerodynamic lift with aircraft weight and is commonly expressed as a multiple of g. Turns are one source; a pull-up also needs additional upward force to curve the flight path. At the bottom of an ideal vertical pull-up, with negligible tangential acceleration, L − W = mV2/R, so n = 1 + V2/(gR). A faster pull-up or a tighter radius therefore raises the load.

Accelerated stall-speed relation: Vs,n ≈ Vs,1g√n (same weight and configuration, simplified positive-load case)

At n = 2, stall speed is about 1.41 times its 1g value.

Gusts and other manoeuvres can also change load. Aircraft structural load limits are type-specific; do not infer an allowable limit from these equations. Use the approved flight manual and operate within its limitations.

Apply the model to a safety review

When an occurrence report says “level and steady,” check whether it means constant altitude, speed and straight path; these are separate conditions. A turn, gust or speed change can leave a nonzero resultant force.

2018 PYQ lens — Q7: The item treats T > D as acceleration, not deceleration. Its equilibrium wording is a simplified exam model: full force balance also needs vertical or flight-path-normal balance. “Weight greater than lift” indicates a downward resultant only under its simple force assumptions; inspect the trajectory and all components.

Quick recall

1. What conditions give straight-level, unaccelerated flight in the simple model?

Answer: L = W and T = D.

2. Does constant speed always mean zero acceleration?

Answer: No. Direction can change in a turn, so velocity changes and centripetal acceleration is present.

3. In a steady straight climb, must lift exceed weight?

Answer: No. L = W cos γ for this model; thrust supplies the along-path weight component.

4. Which way does weight point in a descent?

Answer: Toward Earth, regardless of aircraft attitude or path.

5. What balances drag in an ideal steady glide?

Answer: The component of weight along the glide path; thrust is zero.

6. Why do a propeller’s action and reaction not cancel on the aircraft?

Answer: The propeller acts on the air and the air acts on the propeller/aircraft. The two forces act on different bodies.

A. Forces, vectors and equilibrium

Q01 · 2018 PYQConsider the following statements regarding forces acting on an airplane in flight:
1. Thrust, drag, lift and weight are the forces on an airplane in flight.
2. The airplane is said to be in a state of equilibrium when thrust and drag are equal and opposite.
3. If weight is greater than lift, the airplane will sink.
4. If thrust is greater than drag, the airplane will decelerate.
Which of the above statements are correct?

Choose one answer
Reveal answer and explanation

Answer A. The first three are accepted in the paper’s simplified force model. Statement 4 reverses Newton’s second law: excess thrust causes forward acceleration. For a complete vector balance, both longitudinal and vertical (or flight-path-normal) components must balance; statement 2 names only the thrust–drag pair.

Q02.In straight-and-level flight at constant speed, which set of force relationships applies in the basic airplane model?

Choose one answer
Reveal answer and explanation

Answer A. With no acceleration and a level straight path, lift balances weight and thrust balances drag.

Q03.A resultant force of zero on an aircraft means that its:

Choose one answer
Reveal answer and explanation

Answer B. Newton’s second law gives zero acceleration. Velocity is therefore constant in magnitude and direction; the aircraft can be moving.

Q04.If thrust exceeds drag while the aircraft continues in the same straight direction, the along-path tendency is to:

Choose one answer
Reveal answer and explanation

Answer B. The net along-path force is positive, so speed increases if the path direction is unchanged.

Q05.Which statement best describes drag in the basic flight-force model?

Choose one answer
Reveal answer and explanation

Answer B. Drag is aerodynamic resistance and opposes air-relative motion; it is not defined as opposite gravity.

Q06.Which force remains directed toward Earth when an aircraft enters a climb or a turn?

Choose one answer
Reveal answer and explanation

Answer C. Weight is gravitational and points toward Earth regardless of aircraft attitude or flight path.

Q07.In the elementary model, lift is usually defined as acting:

Choose one answer
Reveal answer and explanation

Answer A. Lift is normal to the relative airflow in the simple model; during a climb or bank it is not generally vertical.

Q08.An aircraft maintains constant speed in a coordinated turn. Which statement is correct?

Choose one answer
Reveal answer and explanation

Answer B. Velocity includes direction. A turn changes direction and therefore has centripetal acceleration even at constant speed.

B. Flight paths and force resolution

Q09.Let climb angle γ be positive upward. In a straight climb, the component of weight along the path acts:

Choose one answer
Reveal answer and explanation

Answer B. For positive climb angle, gravity has a component down the path opposite the climb, equal in magnitude to W sin γ.

Q10.For a straight, constant-speed climb with thrust aligned to the flight path, which pair is correct?

Choose one answer
Reveal answer and explanation

Answer A. Resolve weight along and normal to the straight path. Constant speed balances the along-path component; zero normal acceleration balances the normal component.

Q11.For the straight, constant-speed climb in Q10, why is lift less than weight when γ is positive?

Choose one answer
Reveal answer and explanation

Answer B. The lift component normal to the path balances W cos γ; thrust’s vertical component helps support the climb.

Q12.For a straight, constant-speed descent with climb-positive γ (< 0) and thrust along the path, the along-path equation implies:

Choose one answer
Reveal answer and explanation

Answer A. The stated convention makes γ negative in descent, so W sin γ and T − D are negative in this steady case.

Q13.A steady ideal glide has T = 0. Which force component balances drag along a straight glide path?

Choose one answer
Reveal answer and explanation

Answer A. In a steady glide, the weight component down the path balances drag; lift balances the normal component.

Q14.In a straight flight path with radius of curvature R tending to infinity, the normal acceleration term V2/R tends to:

Choose one answer
Reveal answer and explanation

Answer B. A straight path has no curvature, so its normal acceleration is zero in this model.

Q15.At constant speed on a straight path, a climb angle of 5° requires what excess thrust for an aircraft weighing 30 kN? Use sin 5° ≈ 0.087.

Choose one answer
Reveal answer and explanation

Answer B. T − D = W sin γ = 30 × 0.087 ≈ 2.61 kN.

Q16.For Q15’s straight, constant-speed climb, estimate lift using cos 5° ≈ 0.996.

Choose one answer
Reveal answer and explanation

Answer A. L = W cos γ = 30 × 0.996 = 29.88 kN.

C. Calculations and performance applications

Q17.A 2,000 kg aircraft flies straight and level. Thrust is 10 kN and drag is 6 kN. What is its initial along-path acceleration?

Choose one answer
Reveal answer and explanation

Answer B. Net thrust = 4,000 N; a = F/m = 4,000/2,000 = 2 m/s². The question asks for the initial along-path acceleration.

Q18.An aircraft has excess power of 720 kW and weight of 24 kN. Under the steady-speed climb approximation, its rate of climb is:

Choose one answer
Reveal answer and explanation

Answer B. Rate of climb ≈ excess power/weight = 720,000/24,000 = 30 m/s.

Q19.An ideal steady glide has L/D = 12. Its glide angle magnitude is closest to:

Choose one answer
Reveal answer and explanation

Answer A. For the simple steady glide, L/D = cot |γ|, so tan |γ| = 1/12 and |γ| ≈ 4.8°.

Q20.Fuel is consumed during straight, level flight. If speed, altitude and configuration are momentarily unchanged, which adjustment to required lift follows from L = W?

Choose one answer
Reveal answer and explanation

Answer B. In level unaccelerated flight lift equals current weight. Consuming fuel reduces weight, so required lift decreases under the stated conditions.

Q21.In a coordinated level turn at 60° bank, the ideal load factor is:

Choose one answer
Reveal answer and explanation

Answer C. n = 1/cos 60° = 2 for a coordinated level turn.

Q22.A straight, constant-speed climb is at 10° with weight 24 kN. Which approximate pair is correct? Use sin 10° = 0.174 and cos 10° = 0.985.

Choose one answer
Reveal answer and explanation

Answer A. T − D = 24 sin 10° ≈ 4.18 kN; L = 24 cos 10° ≈ 23.64 kN.

Q23.Which statement about angle of attack and flight-path angle is correct?

Choose one answer
Reveal answer and explanation

Answer B. Pitch attitude describes aircraft orientation; flight-path angle describes velocity direction. Angle of attack is between a chosen aircraft reference line and relative airflow.

Reason (R): The direction of its velocity changes during the turn.

Choose the correct relationship.

Choose one answer

Answer A. Acceleration is the rate of change of velocity, including direction; the changing direction requires an inward resultant force.

Q26.Match each force to its primary source or direction:

Choose one answer

Answer A. Lift is aerodynamic and normal to airflow; weight is gravity; thrust is produced by propulsion; drag resists air-relative motion.

Q27.A pilot reports “constant speed.” Which additional conclusion is justified without more information?

Choose one answer
Reveal answer and explanation

Answer C. Speed omits velocity direction and altitude; a constant-speed turn still needs centripetal acceleration.

Q28.In a coordinated level turn, which statement is correct when speed is constant?

Choose one answer
Reveal answer and explanation

Answer B. In a steady level turn, thrust and drag can balance along the path, while the horizontal lift component supplies centripetal force and the vertical component balances weight.

Q29.After a hypothetical engine power loss, which is the soundest first-principles statement about a glide?

Choose one answer
Reveal answer and explanation

Answer B. An unpowered aircraft can glide; the ideal force components are D = W sin |γ| and L = W cos |γ|. This is not an emergency checklist.

Q30.An aircraft is in a straight, constant-speed 8° climb. An observer argues, “Lift is less than weight, therefore the aircraft must be descending.” What is the best correction?

Choose one answer
Reveal answer and explanation

Answer B. For a steady straight climb, L = W cos γ and T − D = W sin γ. The initial claim ignores the thrust and path components.

E. Coverage extensions: Newton’s laws, manoeuvre loading and engine-out performance

Q31.Which pair correctly illustrates Newton’s third law for a propeller-driven aircraft?

Choose one answer
Reveal answer and explanation

Answer B. The propeller’s force on the air and the air’s force on the propeller are equal and opposite, but they act on different bodies. They do not cancel on the aircraft’s free-body diagram.

Q32.In an ideal coordinated level turn at a bank angle of 45°, what is the load factor closest to?

Choose one answer
Reveal answer and explanation

Answer C. For a coordinated level turn, n = 1/cos φ. Thus n = 1/cos 45° = √2 ≈ 1.41.

Q33.An aircraft’s 1g stall speed is 80 kt. In the same configuration and at the same weight, estimate the stall speed at load factor n = 2.

Choose one answer
Reveal answer and explanation

Answer B. Under the simplified same-weight, same-configuration relation, Vs,n = Vs,1g√n = 80√2 ≈ 113 kt.

Q34.At the bottom of an ideal vertical pull-up, take V = 90 m/s, R = 900 m and g ≈ 10 m/s². Estimate the load factor.

Choose one answer
Reveal answer and explanation

Answer B. At the bottom, L − W = mV2/R, so n = L/W = 1 + V2/(gR) = 1 + 8100/9000 = 1.9.

Q35.During initially straight-and-level flight, available thrust suddenly falls below drag but remains positive. What is the immediate first-principles tendency if the path is momentarily unchanged?

Choose one answer
Reveal answer and explanation

Answer A. With a temporarily unchanged level path, Tavailable − D < 0 gives negative along-path acceleration. A later speed or path change requires a new force balance; partial thrust loss is not the same as T = 0.

Q36.In an ideal still-air glide, an aircraft is 2.5 km above a landing area and has L/D = 12. Estimate the horizontal distance in the simplified model.

Choose one answer
Reveal answer and explanation

Answer C. Ideal still-air horizontal distance is approximately height × glide ratio: 2.5 × 12 = 30 km. Wind, turns, configuration, speed and terrain change real-world reach.

Q37.A twin-engine aircraft loses one engine. Which statement about its remaining climb performance is soundest?

Choose one answer
Reveal answer and explanation

Answer D. Loss of one engine changes excess thrust/power and can add asymmetric drag and control demands. The result is aircraft-specific and must not be inferred by simply halving the former climb rate.

5. Common exam traps and corrections

Replace memorized slogans with component reasoning
TrapCorrection
“Constant speed means no acceleration.”Only if direction is constant too; a turn changes velocity direction.
“Lift is always vertical.”In the basic model lift is perpendicular to relative airflow / flight path.
“Lift must exceed weight for climb.”In a steady straight climb, L = W cos γ while thrust provides an along-path component.
“T > D makes the aircraft climb.”It produces along-path acceleration in level flight; climb depends on the full force balance and flight path.
“Equal thrust and drag alone prove equilibrium.”That balances only the longitudinal pair; other force components and moments still matter.
“Newton’s third-law forces cancel on the aircraft.”The pair acts on two different bodies, such as propeller/air and air/propeller.
“The 1g stall speed is unchanged in a manoeuvre.”At the same weight and configuration, the simplified accelerated-stall relation is Vs,n ≈ Vs,1g√n.
“Engine failure means the aircraft immediately stops flying.”An aircraft can glide; operational actions and speeds must follow approved procedures.

“Pitch attitude is the flight-path angle.”Aircraft orientation and velocity direction are different; angle of attack is different again.
