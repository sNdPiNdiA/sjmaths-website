Primary and Secondary Flight Controls

Identify what each control surface does, distinguish high-lift and drag devices from trim surfaces, follow a pilot command through different control-system architectures, and reason carefully about failures.

What you should be able to do

UPSC ASO foundation
  • Classify primary controls and connect their principal effects to roll, pitch and yaw.
  • Distinguish flaps, slats, spoilers and speed brakes by location and aerodynamic purpose.
  • Explain trim tabs, balance/servo/anti-servo tabs and combined-purpose surfaces.
  • Trace mechanical, hydraulically powered, electrically signalled and fly-by-wire command paths.
  • Assess jams, disconnects, actuator faults, asymmetry and runaway trim without assuming a universal aircraft response.

Coverage check: This follows the Topic 1.5 L1–L5 outline: primary and secondary control functions; surface deflection and aerodynamic moments; high-lift, trim and combined surfaces; mechanical, hydraulic, electrical and fly-by-wire architectures; then failures, redundancy and safety implications.

1. Primary controls: command the three rotations

On a conventional fixed-wing aircraft, the primary flight controls are the ailerons, elevator (or an equivalent pitch surface) and rudder. Their principal roles are roll, pitch and yaw. “Primary” describes their role in attitude control; it does not mean they are the only surfaces that can influence those motions.

Principal effect for a conventional fixed-wing configuration
ControlTypical locationPrincipal rotationTypical pilot input
AileronsOutboard trailing edge of the wingsRoll about the longitudinal body axisControl wheel / stick left or right
ElevatorMovable part of the horizontal tailPitch about the lateral body axisControl column / stick forward or aft
RudderMovable part of the vertical tailYaw about the vertical body axisRudder pedals
StabilatorAll-moving horizontal tailPitch about the lateral body axisPitch control, with aircraft-specific feel / trim features

Command chain: cockpit input → linkage or signal path → actuator / mechanical transmission → surface deflection → aerodynamic force → moment about the centre of gravity → aircraft angular response. The exact chain varies by aircraft.

2. Deflection creates aerodynamic force and moment

A deflected surface changes the local shape presented to the airflow. The resulting pressure distribution produces a force; its moment about the aircraft’s centre of gravity changes angular motion. Airspeed, density, surface area, lever arm, deflection, geometry and configuration all affect the response.

Dynamic pressure: q̄ = ½ ρV²

Generic aerodynamic moment: M = q̄ S c C

Control-surface hinge moment: H = q̄ Sc cc Ch

Here ρ is air density, V airspeed, S a reference area, c a reference length, and the relevant coefficient is dimensionless. The signed coefficient follows the stated convention.

The equations show why a surface can feel harder to move as aerodynamic loading rises. Real control-force and actuator calculations also depend on hinge geometry, balance features, gearing, friction and system design; this simple relation is for conceptual study.

Worked hinge-moment calculation

Let q̄ = 2,500 N/m², control-surface area Sc = 1.8 m², chord cc = 0.35 m and coefficient Ch = −0.06.

H = 2,500 × 1.8 × 0.35 × (−0.06) = −94.5 N·m. The hinge-moment magnitude is 94.5 N·m. Interpret the negative sign only using the problem’s stated sign convention; it does not universally mean “nose-down” or “restoring.”

Dimension check: (N/m²) × m² × m = N·m, the unit of moment. Deflection angles may be expressed in degrees or radians, but derivatives such as ∂Ch/∂δ must use the matching angular unit.

3. Secondary controls: change lift, drag or handling

Secondary or auxiliary controls modify wing performance, drag, trim or pilot effort. Terminology can differ between references and aircraft manuals; identify a device by its location, motion and function, and follow the aircraft-specific description.

Common devices and their principal purpose
DeviceLocation / movementMain effectHigh-value distinction
Trailing-edge flapTrailing edge; usually moves down, sometimes translatesChanges camber (and on some designs effective area); increases lift and commonly dragHigh-lift device; configuration and setting matter
Leading-edge slatLeading edge; extends to form a slot / altered nose geometryHelps delay separation and support higher angle of attack and liftLeading-edge device; not a trim tab
SpoilerPanel rises from wing upper surfaceDisrupts flow, reduces lift and adds dragMay dump lift on landing or augment roll on some aircraft
Speed brakeDedicated panel or spoiler surface, depending on aircraftPrimarily adds drag to reduce speed or increase descent rateFunction name; spoiler and speed brake can share hardware
Exam contrasts:
• Flap vs slat: trailing-edge camber change vs leading-edge flow/separation support.
• Spoiler vs speed brake: lift destruction plus drag vs primarily drag; one spoiler surface may perform both roles.
• Approach vs landing: flight spoilers / speed brakes and ground lift-dump logic are aircraft-configured; do not assume identical deployment.

Configuration caution: High-lift devices change lift, drag and pitching moments. Their schedules, asymmetry protection and limitations are aircraft-specific; do not infer a safe setting from a generic diagram.

4. Trim tabs and combined-purpose surfaces

Some auxiliary surfaces reduce control force; others combine two control functions in one physical surface. Names describe different aerodynamic or control roles, so avoid treating every small tab as a trim tab.

DeviceTypical roleExam distinction

Balance tabAerodynamically assists movement of its parent surfaceAssists pilot effort; not automatically a trim device

Anti-servo tabOn common stabilators, moves with the surface to increase force / feelDifferent purpose from a force-relieving trim tab

Combined surfaces integrate functions; control laws / linkages mix pilot inputs
Surface nameCombined functionsTypical configuration

Remember: A combined surface is not a new fourth axis. It supplies more than one control function; the aircraft’s mixing system determines how input maps to surface motion.

5. How the command reaches the surface

Flight-control architecture categories can be combined in one aircraft
ArchitectureCommand / power pathWhat to distinguish
MechanicalCables, pulleys, bellcranks, push-pull rods or torque tubes transmit pilot motionPhysical linkage does not necessarily mean the pilot alone supplies all force
Hydraulic power-assisted / poweredHydraulic pressure supplies actuator force; command may remain mechanical, electrical or mixedHydraulic power source is distinct from how the command is sent
Electrically signalled / electro-mechanicalElectrical signal commands an actuator; the actuator may be electric, hydraulic or electrohydraulicElectrical signalling does not imply one universal actuator type
Fly-by-wire (FBW)Pilot input is sensed/processed; computers issue electrical commands to actuators under control lawsMay include feedback, redundancy, mode logic and envelope protections; details vary

Typical fly-by-wire signal path

Pilot input → input sensors → flight-control computers / control laws → electrical command → actuator → surface → position / system feedback. A specific aircraft may use multiple computers, power sources and actuator types, and the actual modes and backup paths must be learned from its approved documentation.

Exam trap: Mechanical, hydraulic and fly-by-wire are not always mutually exclusive labels. A system may use hydraulic actuator power with electrical command signals, or mechanical backup within an otherwise electronically controlled architecture.

6. Failure modes, redundancy and safety review

Reason about the fault path before judging the outcome
Failure / anomalyPossible generic consequenceEvidence / design questions
Jam or restrictionReduced, biased or unavailable surface travelWhich surface, travel direction, linkage, hinge and flight condition?
Disconnect / broken linkageCommand may not reach the surface; surface position may be uncertainWhere did continuity fail? Is another path present?
Actuator / hydraulic / electrical faultSurface movement may be lost, slowed, limited or uncommandedWhich power and command channels are affected? What feedback / alert appeared?
Runaway trimUncommanded continued trim movement changes aircraft balanceTrim command, position, alert, electrical supply and approved response procedures
Asymmetric / uncommanded surfaceUnwanted moment, drag or handling changeSurface position history, actuator state, sensor data and maintenance evidence
Sensor / computer disagreementDegraded, alternate or changed control law on some systemsSystem mode, fault messages, redundant input agreement and aircraft-specific logic

Redundancy uses alternate channels, components, power paths or control capability to reduce the effects of selected failures. It does not prove that a fault is harmless: common-mode faults, damage, jams and combinations of failures must be assessed against the actual system design and certification basis.

Air Safety Officer review sequence

  • Record the time sequence, aircraft configuration, flight phase and crew description.
  • Preserve available recorded indications/data, technical logs, deferred defects, maintenance actions and physical evidence.
  • Identify the affected surface, command path, actuator/power path, feedback and any asymmetry.
  • Separate observed evidence from inferred cause; avoid declaring a system safe or unsafe from a generic diagram.
  • Use the aircraft maintenance/flight manuals and applicable approved procedures for the real aircraft.

Operational boundary: This lesson explains concepts; it is not an abnormal/emergency checklist. Aircraft-specific symptoms, alternate modes and required actions must come from approved aircraft documentation.

Quick recall

  • Aileron → roll; elevator / stabilator → pitch; rudder → yaw (principal conventional mapping).
  • Flap: trailing-edge high-lift device; slat: leading-edge separation-delay aid.
  • Spoiler reduces lift and adds drag; speed brake is primarily for drag, and may use spoiler hardware.
  • Trim reduces sustained force; balance and servo tabs can assist surface motion; anti-servo tabs commonly increase stabilator feel.
  • Elevon = elevator + aileron; ruddervator = rudder + elevator; flaperon = flap + aileron.
  • Electrical command path and actuator power source are separate design questions.
  • For any failure, identify affected surface, command path, power, feedback, redundancy and approved procedures.

A. Primary controls and response

Q01.Which pairing correctly identifies a conventional primary flight control and its principal rotation?

Choose one answer

Reveal answer and explanation

Answer A. Ailerons primarily command roll, elevators pitch, and rudders yaw. The axis named is the line about which the aircraft rotates.

Q02.A pilot moves the control column aft in a conventional airplane. Which response is normally commanded?

Choose one answer

Reveal answer and explanation

Answer B. Aft column movement normally commands nose-up pitch through the elevator or an equivalent pitch-control surface. The precise linkage and surface arrangement vary by aircraft.

Q03.Consider these statements: 1. The rudder primarily controls yaw. 2. The elevator primarily controls pitch. 3. The ailerons primarily control roll. Which are correct for a conventional fixed-wing airplane?

Choose one answer

Reveal answer and explanation

Answer D. All three pairings describe the conventional primary control mapping. Coupling can create secondary responses about other axes.

Q04.Which sequence best represents how a pilot command produces aircraft rotation?

Choose one answer

Reveal answer and explanation

Answer B. The control path moves a surface; airflow produces a force whose moment about the centre of gravity changes angular motion.

Q05.A control surface is deflected while the aircraft is moving through air. What directly produces its aerodynamic effect?

Choose one answer

Reveal answer and explanation

Answer C. The surface changes the local airflow and pressure distribution. The resulting aerodynamic force and moment affect the aircraft.

Q06.Why is “one control surface always affects only one axis” an unsafe generalization?

Choose one answer

Reveal answer and explanation

Answer B. The conventional primary mapping describes the principal effect. Coupling, configuration, control laws and combined surfaces can add other effects.

B. High-lift and drag devices

Q07.Which statement best describes the usual effect of trailing-edge flaps?

Choose one answer

Reveal answer and explanation

Answer A. Flaps change the wing section and lift characteristics; they usually also add drag. The amount and effect depend on flap type and setting.

Q08.A leading-edge slat is extended. What is its main aerodynamic purpose?

Choose one answer

Reveal answer and explanation

Answer B. A slat forms or opens a leading-edge slot that helps maintain more attached flow at higher angles of attack, increasing usable maximum lift on suitable designs.

Q09.Which pairing distinguishes a spoiler from a speed brake most accurately?

Choose one answer

Reveal answer and explanation

Answer B. Spoilers reduce lift and add drag. A speed brake is classified by its drag-producing purpose; spoiler panels may also serve that purpose on some aircraft.

Q10.Why are ground spoilers commonly deployed after touchdown on aircraft so equipped?

Choose one answer

Reveal answer and explanation

Answer B. Spoiling lift after touchdown increases wheel loading and the effectiveness of wheel braking; spoilers also add aerodynamic drag.

Q11.Which statement about speed brakes is correct?

Choose one answer

Reveal answer and explanation

Answer A. Speed brakes are drag devices. Whether they are separate panels or spoiler surfaces, and how they may be used, is aircraft-specific.

Q12.On an aircraft whose spoilers augment roll control, how can spoiler extension contribute to roll?

Choose one answer

Reveal answer and explanation

Answer A. Differential spoiler action can reduce lift on one wing and help produce roll. This is a design feature on some aircraft, not a universal function.

Q13.Match each device to its usual function: 1. Slat 2. Flap 3. Ground spoiler 4. Speed brake; a. increase camber/lift for low-speed configuration b. help delay separation at higher angle of attack c. reduce lift after touchdown d. primarily add drag in flight.

Choose one answer

Reveal answer and explanation

Answer A. Slats support attached flow at higher angle of attack; flaps alter camber and lift; ground spoilers dump lift; speed brakes primarily add drag.

Q14.Which statement is NOT generally correct about flaps and slats?

Choose one answer

Reveal answer and explanation

Answer D. Flap effects depend on design and setting, and increased lift commonly comes with increased drag. There is no universal flap response independent of configuration.

C. Trim and combined surfaces

Q15.What is the primary purpose of a conventional trim system?

Choose one answer

Reveal answer and explanation

Answer A. Trim changes the balance of forces or the control-system neutral condition so the pilot need not continuously hold a control force. It does not replace normal control.

Q16.A balance tab is designed mainly to:

Choose one answer

Reveal answer and explanation

Answer A. A balance tab uses aerodynamic force on the tab to assist the parent surface. Tab direction and linkage depend on the particular design.

Q17.How does a servo tab commonly help move a larger control surface?

Choose one answer

Reveal answer and explanation

Answer A. A servo tab can use aerodynamic force to move the main control surface, reducing the force required through the control path.

Q18.An anti-servo tab on an all-moving stabilator commonly:

Choose one answer

Reveal answer and explanation

Answer A. An anti-servo tab moves in the same direction as the stabilator on typical installations, increasing control force and feel. The design is aircraft-specific.

Q19.What does an adjustable horizontal stabilizer do when used for trim?

Choose one answer

Reveal answer and explanation

Answer A. An adjustable stabilizer changes the tailplane incidence and associated pitching moment; it is not simply a small trim tab.

Q20.Assertion (A): A trim tab can reduce the continuous force needed to hold a selected flight condition. Reason (R): A tab changes local aerodynamic forces and can establish a balancing moment.

Choose one answer

Reveal answer and explanation

Answer A. Both are true. The aerodynamic effect of the tab can create a balancing moment that reduces the sustained force needed at the main control.

Q21.Which statement correctly defines an elevon?

Choose one answer

Reveal answer and explanation

Answer A. Elevons combine pitch and roll control functions. Their mixing and movement depend on aircraft design.

Q22.A V-tail aircraft uses ruddervators. These surfaces combine:

Choose one answer

Reveal answer and explanation

Answer A. Ruddervators combine pitch and yaw control functions, with control inputs mixed to produce the required response.

D. Control-system architecture

Q23.Which architecture normally carries pilot movement through cables, bellcranks or push-pull rods toward a control surface?

Choose one answer

Reveal answer and explanation

Answer A. A mechanical system transmits input through physical linkages. Power assistance may be added while retaining mechanical paths.

Q24.What is the main role of hydraulic power in a powered flight-control system?

Choose one answer

Reveal answer and explanation

Answer A. Hydraulic pressure can power an actuator that moves a control surface. The command path and backup design vary by aircraft.

Q25.“Electrically signalled” controls necessarily mean the surface is moved by an electric motor alone. Is this statement correct?

Choose one answer

Reveal answer and explanation

Answer B. Signal transmission and actuator power are separate design choices. An electrical command may ultimately drive different actuator types.

Q26.Which sequence best describes a typical fly-by-wire command path?

Choose one answer

Reveal answer and explanation

Answer A. Fly-by-wire uses electrical signalling and flight-control computers between pilot input and actuators. Specific architectures differ.

Q27.What is the purpose of redundancy in a flight-control system?

Choose one answer

Reveal answer and explanation

Answer A. Redundant channels/components can preserve function after some failures. Redundancy does not mean immunity to common-mode faults or every possible failure.

Q28.Which distinction between a control command and an actuator is correct?

Choose one answer

Reveal answer and explanation

Answer A. The command path determines a desired movement; an actuator converts supplied energy into mechanical movement. Feedback and monitoring may verify response.

Q29.Why does mode awareness matter on an aircraft with multiple flight-control laws or modes?

Choose one answer

Reveal answer and explanation

Answer A. Control laws and available protections may change with system mode or failures; crews need to recognize the actual mode and approved procedures.

E. Failures and safety

Q30.A safety report notes that an elevator was restricted during a ground check. What is the best generic conclusion?

Choose one answer

Reveal answer and explanation

Answer A. A restriction can reduce or bias control. It is not possible to infer controllability or operational action without aircraft-specific inspection and approved procedures.

Q31.A broken control linkage is found during an investigation. Which effect is most technically defensible?

Choose one answer

Reveal answer and explanation

Answer A. A disconnect can interrupt command transmission, but the surface position and remaining authority depend on architecture, actuator arrangement and failure location.

Q32.A hydraulic actuator failure is reported. Which assessment should come first?

Choose one answer

Reveal answer and explanation

Answer A. Effects depend on system segregation, actuator arrangement and backup capability. Records and approved aircraft information are needed to assess it.

Q33.What best describes runaway trim?

Choose one answer

Reveal answer and explanation

Answer A. Runaway trim is unwanted continued trim-system movement. Recognition, response and available cut-outs are aircraft-specific and belong to the approved flight manual.

Q34.Why is an asymmetric control-surface position potentially hazardous?

Choose one answer

Reveal answer and explanation

Answer A. Asymmetry can generate an unintended moment and drag. Consequences vary with surface, speed, aircraft and system design.

Q35.During a control-system occurrence review, which evidence set is most useful to preserve first?

Choose one answer

Reveal answer and explanation

Answer A. A defensible review preserves time-linked operational and maintenance evidence, recorded data where available, and physical findings before drawing conclusions.

Q36.A control surface has area S = 1.8 m², characteristic chord c = 0.35 m, dynamic pressure q̄ = 2,500 N/m², and hinge-moment coefficient Cₕ = −0.06. Using H = q̄ScCₕ, what is the signed hinge moment?

Choose one answer

Reveal answer and explanation

Answer A. H = 2,500 × 1.8 × 0.35 × (−0.06) = −94.5 N·m. The sign depends on the stated coefficient convention; magnitude is 94.5 N·m.

Q37.On an aircraft fitted with a spring tab, which statement best describes its usual role?

Choose one answer

Reveal answer and explanation

Answer A. A spring tab can use spring coupling and aerodynamic loading to assist control movement when hinge loads are high. Its linkage and behavior vary by aircraft; it is not automatically a trim tab.

Q38.A trailing-edge surface is called a flaperon. Which description is most accurate?

Choose one answer

Reveal answer and explanation

Answer B. A flaperon is a combined flap/aileron surface. It can contribute to lift configuration changes while providing roll control through aircraft-specific surface mixing.

8. Active recall cards

What are the three conventional primary controls?

Ailerons for roll, elevator / stabilator for pitch, and rudder for yaw.

Flap versus slat?

Flap is at the trailing edge and changes camber / lift; slat is at the leading edge and helps delay separation at higher angle of attack.

Spoiler versus speed brake?

A spoiler spoils lift and adds drag; a speed brake is primarily intended to add drag. A spoiler may serve as a speed brake.

Trim tab versus balance tab?

A trim tab relieves sustained control force; a balance tab aerodynamically assists its parent surface. Aircraft linkages can vary.

What does a typical anti-servo tab do?

On common stabilator installations it moves with the all-moving surface and increases control force / feel.

Define elevon, ruddervator and flaperon.

Elevon = elevator + aileron; ruddervator = rudder + elevator; flaperon = flap + aileron.

What does fly-by-wire describe?

An electrically signalled, computer-mediated command path to actuators; it does not mean that every aircraft uses the same actuator or backup arrangement.

What should a safety reviewer never infer from “jam” alone?

The exact remaining control or safe response. Identify the affected system, evidence, aircraft configuration and approved procedures.
