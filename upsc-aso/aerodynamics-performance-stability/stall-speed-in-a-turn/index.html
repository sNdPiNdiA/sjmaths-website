Stall speed in a turn

Textbook-grade UPSC Air Safety Officer deep-dive: Comprehensive theoretical foundations, analytical derivations, engineering reference data, DGCA/ICAO airworthiness integration, 14 practice MCQs, and timed mini-test.

How This Topic Is Useful for Air Safety Officers & Aircraft Operations
DGCA CAR & ICAO Mandate

In aviation operations and UPSC ASO assessments, this parameter is critical across 4 core operational pillars:

  • 1. Aircraft Systems & Flight Deck Monitoring: Angle of Attack (AoA) sensors, inertial reference systems, and Electronic Flight Instrument Systems (EFIS) compute real-time variable stall speed cues.
  • 2. Flight Safety & Dispatch Envelopes: Traffic pattern entry speeds and base-to-final turn margins must incorporate elevated stall speeds to prevent accelerated stalls.
  • 3. DGCA CAR & ICAO Airworthiness Compliance: Compliance with DGCA CAR Section 2 and ICAO Annex 8 mandates clear stall warning systems effective in all flight configurations and bank angles.
  • 4. Air Safety Investigation & Incident Prevention: ASO accident analysts examine FDR data for uncoordinated low-altitude steep turns exceeding critical load factors prior to loss of control.

Pillar 1: Formal Technical Definition & Nomenclature

Stall speed in a turn is the increased true airspeed at which an aircraft stalls when subjected to an elevated load factor during banked flight, governed by the equation $$V_{s_n} = V_{s} \sqrt{n}$$
  • Physical Meaning: The aircraft requires a higher stall speed because turning generates centrifugal acceleration, which increases the apparent weight and wing loading.
  • Governing Law: Newton's second law combined with aerodynamic lift requirements dictates that lift must equal weight multiplied by the secant of the bank angle.
  • Continuum Boundary: Valid under standard continuum aerodynamic assumptions up to high subsonic Mach numbers where compressibility effects alter maximum lift coefficients.

Nomenclature & Symbols

  • $V_{s_n}$ = Stall speed in a coordinated turn (knots / m/s)
  • $V_{s}$ = 1G straight and level stall speed in given configuration (knots / m/s)
  • $n$ = Load factor, equal to $\frac{1}{\cos \phi}$ in steady level turns (Dimensionless)
  • $\phi$ = Bank angle of the aircraft (Degrees / Radians)
  • $L$ = Aerodynamic lift force generated by the wings (Newtons / lbs)
  • $W$ = Total aircraft weight (Newtons / lbs)

Pillar 2: Microscopic Physics & Continuum Mechanics

1. Lift Vector Tilting: When an aircraft rolls into a bank angle $\phi$, the total lift vector tilts off the vertical axis.
2. Load Factor Increase: To maintain altitude, vertical lift must equal weight, forcing the total lift to increase by a factor of $n = \frac{1}{\cos \phi}$.
3. Stagnation & Separation: The wings operate at a higher angle of attack to generate this increased lift, causing boundary layer separation to occur earlier at a higher airspeed.

Pillar 3: Mathematical Derivations & Governing Laws

Step 1: Governing Relation

Equate vertical lift component to aircraft weight in a steady, level turn.

$$L \cos \phi = W$$
Step 2: Boundary / Thermodynamic Conditions

Express total lift $L$ and load factor $n$ using standard aerodynamic lift equation.

$$n = \frac{L}{W} = \frac{1}{\cos \phi}, \quad L = \frac{1}{2} \rho V^2 S C_{L_{max}}$$
Step 3: Integration & Substitution

Substitute the increased lift required at maximum lift coefficient into the speed equation.

$$\frac{1}{2} \rho V_{s_n}^2 S C_{L_{max}} = n W = n \left(\frac{1}{2} \rho V_{s}^2 S C_{L_{max}}\right)$$
Step 4: Final Working Law

Cancel common terms and take the square root to derive the turned stall speed formula.

$$V_{s_n} = V_{s} \sqrt{n} = \frac{V_{s}}{\sqrt{\cos \phi}}$$

Pillar 4: Dimensional Analysis, SI/CGS Units & Conversion Factors

  • Fundamental Dimensions: Velocity has the fundamental dimensional representation of $[L T^{-1}]$, derived from length divided by time.
  • SI Unit: Meters per second ($m/s$), representing spatial displacement per unit time.
  • CGS Unit: Centimeters per second ($cm/s$), converting at a factor of $1 \, m/s = 100 \, cm/s$.
  • Aeronautical & Imperial Equivalents: Knots ($kts$) are standard in aviation, where $1 \, kts = 0.5144 \, m/s$; imperial calculations often use feet per second ($fps$).

Pillar 6: Aircraft Airworthiness, Systems & DGCA CAR / ICAO Compliance

  • Regulatory Standards (DGCA CAR & ICAO): DGCA CAR Section 2 Series 'F' Part III and ICAO Annex 8 require aircraft to demonstrate predictable stall recovery and adequate stall warning margins.
  • Critical Aircraft Systems: Stick pushers, stick shakers, and stall warning computers rely on continuous bank angle and load factor inputs to dynamically adjust warning thresholds.
  • Aviation Incident Case Study: Low-altitude loss of control in transport category aircraft during base-to-final turns caused by failure to account for increased stall speed during uncoordinated maneuvers.

Pillar 7: High-Frequency UPSC Examiner Traps & Misconceptions

Trap 1: Stall Speed Depends on Weight in Turns

❌ Examiner Trap: Assuming heavier aircraft have exponentially higher percentage increases in stall speed during turns.

✅ UPSC Truth: The percentage increase in stall speed depends solely on the load factor ($n = 1/\cos\phi$), making it independent of aircraft weight.

Trap 2: Stall Speed Varies with Altitude in Knots CAS vs TAS

❌ Examiner Trap: Believing calibrated stall speed changes significantly with bank angle.

✅ UPSC Truth: Indicated and calibrated stall speeds increase with $\sqrt{n}$, whereas true stall speed increases even further due to density altitude effects.

Trap 3: Bank Angle Limits at Low Altitude

❌ Examiner Trap: Assuming a 60-degree bank stall speed is double the 1G stall speed.

✅ UPSC Truth: At a 60-degree bank, load factor is 2.0, but stall speed increases by the square root of 2.0, which is approximately $1.41$ ($41\%$ increase).

Trap 4: Accelerated Stall Confusion

❌ Examiner Trap: Confusing accelerated stalls exclusively with abrupt pull-ups.

✅ UPSC Truth: Any maneuver generating high load factors, including steep turns, rapid pitch changes, or violent rudder inputs, induces an accelerated stall.

Pillar 8: Step-by-Step Worked Aircraft Numerical Problem

Realistic Aircraft Scenario

An aircraft has a certified 1G straight-and-level stall speed ($V_s$) of $60$ knots CAS. Calculate the stall speed when the aircraft is placed in a steady, coordinated turn with a bank angle of $60^\circ$.

Given Flight Parameters:
1G stall speed $V_s = 60$ knots; Bank angle $\phi = 60^\circ$
Governing Formula:
$$V_{s_n} = V_s \sqrt{\frac{1}{\cos \phi}}$$
Step-by-Step Analytical Solution:
Step 1: Compute load factor $n = \frac{1}{\cos(60^\circ)} = \frac{1}{0.5} = 2.0$. Step 2: Calculate the stall speed multiplier $\sqrt{n} = \sqrt{2.0} \approx 1.414$. Step 3: Multiply by base stall speed $V_{s_n} = 60 \times 1.414 = 84.84$ knots.
Final Answer: $84.84 knots CAS$
Air Safety Officer Operational Takeaway:

Pilots must recognize that executing a 60-degree banked turn raises the stall speed by over $40\%$, requiring a significant speed buffer during maneuvering flight.

Question 1 • conceptual
What is the primary aerodynamic reason for the increase in stall speed during a coordinated turn?
A Reduction of wing surface area due to bank
B Increased load factor requiring higher angle of attack
C Decrease in air density at higher bank angles
D Centrifugal force pushing air molecules downward
Solution & Technical Rationale:
Turning tilts the lift vector, requiring higher total lift (and load factor) to maintain altitude, which necessitates a higher angle of attack and increases stall speed.
Question 2 • conceptual
Which of the following bank angles results in a load factor of exactly 2.0 and a stall speed multiplier of approximately 1.41?
A $30^\circ$
B $45^\circ$
C $60^\circ$
D $75^\circ$
Solution & Technical Rationale:
At a bank angle of $60^\circ$, $\cos(60^\circ) = 0.5$, yielding a load factor $n = 1/0.5 = 2.0$ and a stall speed multiplier of $\sqrt{2} \approx 1.41$.
Question 3 • numerical
An aircraft stalls at 70 knots in wings-level flight. What is its stall speed in a steady 45-degree banked turn?
A 77.0 knots
B 83.3 knots
C 98.9 knots
D 140.0 knots
Solution & Technical Rationale:
For $45^\circ$, $n = 1 / \cos(45^\circ) = 1 / 0.7071 \approx 1.414$. Multiplying by 70 gives $70 \times \sqrt{1.414} = 70 \times 1.189 = 83.3$ knots.
Question 4 • statement-based
Consider the following statements regarding stall speed in a turn: 1. Stall speed increases with the square root of the load factor. 2. Weight affects the absolute stall speed, but the percentage increase in a turn depends only on the bank angle. 3. Indicated stall speed decreases at altitude due to compressibility. Which of the statements given above are correct?
A 1 and 2 only
B 2 and 3 only
C 1 and 3 only
D 1, 2, and 3
Solution & Technical Rationale:
Statements 1 and 2 are correct. Statement 3 is false because calibrated and indicated stall speeds remain relatively constant with altitude while true stall speed increases.
Question 5 • assertion-reasoning
Assertion (A): An accelerated stall can occur at airspeeds well above the normal 1G stall speed. Reason (R): Abrupt control inputs or steep turns increase the load factor, raising the stall speed above current flight speed.
A Both (A) and (R) are true and (R) is the correct explanation of (A)
B Both (A) and (R) are true but (R) is not the correct explanation of (A)
C (A) is true but (R) is false
D (A) is false but (R) is true
Solution & Technical Rationale:
Both are true; high load factor increases stall speed such that if airspeed is below $V_{s_n}$, an accelerated stall occurs.
Question 6 • conceptual
If an aircraft maintains altitude in a 75-degree banked turn, what is the approximate load factor?
A 2.0
B 3.0
C 3.86
D 5.76
Solution & Technical Rationale:
Load factor $n = 1 / \cos(75^\circ) = 1 / 0.2588 \approx 3.86$.
Question 7 • numerical
An aircraft has a 1G stall speed of 80 knots. If the pilot pulls back sharply on the control yoke, imposing a load factor of 4.0, what is the new stall speed?
A 120 knots
B 160 knots
C 200 knots
D 320 knots
Solution & Technical Rationale:
$V_{s_n} = 80 \times \sqrt{4.0} = 80 \times 2.0 = 160$ knots.
Question 8 • statement-based
Which DGCA airworthiness requirement governs stall warning characteristics for civil transport aircraft?
A DGCA CAR Section 2, Series 'F'
B DGCA CAR Section 4, Series 'A'
C DGCA CAR Section 8, Series 'B'
D DGCA CAR Section 3, Series 'C'
Solution & Technical Rationale:
DGCA CAR Section 2 sets forth airworthiness standards including stall warning and maneuverability margins.
Question 9 • conceptual
During a slip or skid in a turn, how is the stall speed affected compared to a coordinated turn at the same bank angle?
A It is lower because centrifugal force assists lift
B It is higher because uncoordinated flight disrupts airflow over one wing earlier
C It is completely unaffected by coordination
D It drops to zero due to asymmetric yaw
Solution & Technical Rationale:
Uncoordinated turns (slips/skids) frequently trigger an asymmetric stall (spin entry) at even higher speeds due to adverse yaw and unequal wing loading.
Question 10 • conceptual
What happens to the stalling angle of attack ($\alpha_{crit}$) when an aircraft is placed in a steep turn?
A It increases significantly
B It decreases significantly
C It remains essentially constant
D It drops to zero
Solution & Technical Rationale:
The critical angle of attack at which airflow separates is an aerodynamic property of the airfoil and remains virtually constant regardless of load factor or bank angle.
Question 11 • numerical
An aircraft stall speed multiplier is measured at 1.5. What was the bank angle of the turn?
A $33.5^\circ$
B $48.2^\circ$
C $58.2^\circ$
D $68.4^\circ$
Solution & Technical Rationale:
If multiplier is $1.5$, then $n = 1.5^2 = 2.25$. Since $n = 1/\cos\phi$, $\cos\phi = 1/2.25 = 0.4444$. $\phi = \arccos(0.4444) \approx 58.6^\circ$.
Question 12 • conceptual
Why do base-to-final turns present a high risk for fatal stall/spin accidents?
A Low altitude prevents recovery, and overshooting final prompts pilots to increase bank angle and apply back pressure, raising stall speed.
B Engines automatically lose power during turns
C Pitot tubes freeze due to centrifugal cooling
D Air density increases rapidly near the runway threshold
Solution & Technical Rationale:
Overshooting the final approach centerline often induces tight, uncoordinated corrective turns with elevated load factors at low altitude, where the increased stall speed exceeds the aircraft's airspeed.

The stall speed of an aircraft in a 60-degree banked level turn compared to its 1G stall speed is increased by:
A 20%
B 30%
C 41%
D 100%
Rationale:
At 60 degrees, load factor $n = 2.0$. The stall speed multiplier is $\sqrt{2} \approx 1.414$, representing a 41% increase.
Question 2 of 10 +3 / -1
If the gross weight of an aircraft is doubled, what happens to the stall speed in a 45-degree banked turn?
A It increases by a factor of 2
B It increases by a factor of $\sqrt{2}$
C It remains at the same percentage increase above the new 1G stall speed
D It decreases because of higher momentum
Rationale:
While the absolute 1G stall speed increases with weight, the percentage increase in a 45-degree turn depends strictly on the load factor ($n = 1.41$), independent of weight.
Question 3 of 10 +3 / -1
What is the load factor in a steady level turn with a bank angle of $30^\circ$?
A 1.05
B 1.15
C 1.41
D 2.00
Rationale:
$n = 1 / \cos(30^\circ) = 1 / 0.866 = 1.154$.
Question 4 of 10 +3 / -1
An accelerated stall occurs when:
A The aircraft exceeds Mach 1
B The critical angle of attack is exceeded at an airspeed higher than 1G stall speed due to high load factor
C Engines experience compressor stall during rapid acceleration
D Flaps are retracted too quickly on takeoff
Rationale:
Accelerated stalls occur at higher airspeeds when excessive load factor forces the wing past its critical angle of attack.
Question 5 of 10 +3 / -1
Which instrument provides the most direct warning of an impending stall regardless of bank angle or load factor?
A Altimeter
B Angle of Attack (AoA) indicator
C Vertical Speed Indicator
D Turn Coordinator
Rationale:
An AoA indicator measures the exact local angle between the relative wind and chord line, making it independent of weight, density, or load factor.
Question 6 of 10 +3 / -1
Calculate the stall speed in a $45^\circ$ banked turn if $V_s = 50$ knots.
A 55 knots
B 59.4 knots
C 70.7 knots
D 75.2 knots
Rationale:
$V_{s_n} = 50 \times \sqrt{1.414} = 50 \times 1.189 = 59.45$ knots.
Question 7 of 10 +3 / -1
The primary hazard of an uncoordinated stall during a turn is:
A Engine flameout
B Rapid loss of altitude without wing drop
C Uncommanded spin entry due to asymmetric wing stalling
D Pitot-static system blockage
Rationale:
An uncoordinated turn means one wing stalls before the other, resulting in an immediate wing drop and potential spin entry.
Question 8 of 10 +3 / -1
According to ICAO and DGCA airworthiness standards, stall warning must begin:
A Exactly at $V_s$
B At least 5% to 10% above stall speed in all operational configurations
C Only when the stick pusher activates
D At speeds exceeding $2\times V_s$
Rationale:
Airworthiness regulations require adequate warning margin prior to reaching the stall speed to allow pilot recovery.
Question 9 of 10 +3 / -1
If an aircraft's stall speed in straight and level flight is 60 knots, what bank angle produces a stall speed of 120 knots?
A $45^\circ$
B $60^\circ$
C $75.5^\circ$
D $83.6^\circ$
Rationale:
Multiplier is $120/60 = 2.0$. Thus $\sqrt{n} = 2.0 \implies n = 4.0$. $\cos\phi = 1/4.0 = 0.25 \implies \phi = \arccos(0.25) \approx 75.5^\circ$ (Wait, $\arccos(0.25)$ is $75.5^\circ$, let's check: $\cos(75.5^\circ) = 0.25$, so $n=4$, $\sqrt{4}=2$, correct).
Question 10 of 10 +3 / -1
In a steady level coordinated turn, as the bank angle increases, the required lift:
A Decreases because centrifugal force helps
B Increases, requiring a higher angle of attack
C Remains constant while thrust increases
D Drops to zero at 90 degrees
Rationale:
To balance weight vertically while banking, total lift must increase ($L = W / \cos\phi$), requiring a higher angle of attack.
