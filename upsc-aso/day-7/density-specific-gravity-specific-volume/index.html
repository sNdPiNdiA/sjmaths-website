Density, specific gravity, specific volume

Day 7 of 129 Fluid Mechanics & Machinery Phase 1: Foundations 60-90 min Zero-to-Hero Master Cycle

1. Understand (From Scratch, 5–10 min)

In fluid mechanics, understanding the core characteristics of a substance begins with how its matter is distributed and compared. Let us demystify three fundamental properties: Density, Specific Gravity, and Specific Volume.

1. Density ($\rho$)

Intuitive Analogy: Imagine two identical cargo crates. One is filled entirely with feather pillows, while the other is filled with solid iron bars. Although they occupy the exact same physical space (volume), the crate containing iron is incomparably heavier because its atoms are packed tightly together. This level of compactness is what we call density.

Definition: Mass density is defined as the mass of a substance contained within a unit volume. It is a point function in fluid mechanics, assuming the fluid behaves as a continuum.

SI Unit: Kilogram per cubic meter ($\text{kg/m}^3$)

Physical Meaning: It measures how much matter ($m$) is crammed into a given space ($V$). Mathematically:

$$\rho = \frac{m}{V}$$

2. Specific Gravity ($SG$ or $S$)

Intuitive Analogy: If you dive into a swimming pool of pure fresh water, you float with ease. If you dive into a pool of pure liquid mercury, you would sit high on top of it like a block of wood because mercury is much "heavier" than water for the same volume. Specific Gravity is the comparison tool we use to quantify this relative heaviness.

Definition: Specific gravity (also known as relative density) is the dimensionless ratio of the density of a fluid to the density of a standard reference fluid (usually pure water at $4^\circ\text{C}$ for liquids, and dry air at standard temperature and pressure for gases).

SI Unit: Dimensionless (no units, as it is a ratio of identical units).

Physical Meaning: If a hydraulic fluid has an $SG = 0.85$, it is $0.85$ times as dense as water (meaning it will float on water). If mercury has $SG = 13.6$, it is $13.6$ times denser than water.

3. Specific Volume ($v$)

Intuitive Analogy: Imagine you are a thermodynamicist looking at the fluid from the perspective of a single unit of mass (e.g., $1\text{ kg}$). How much elbow room (volume) does that $1\text{ kg}$ of fluid demand? This "elbow room" per unit mass is the specific volume.

Definition: Specific volume is the volume occupied by a unit mass of a fluid. It is the absolute mathematical reciprocal of density.

SI Unit: Cubic meter per kilogram ($\text{m}^3\text{/kg}$)

Physical Meaning: Highly compressed gases have tiny specific volumes, while highly expanded, low-density gases at high altitudes have extremely large specific volumes.

2. Learn the Concept (Complete Theoretical Foundation, 15–20 min)

To master UPSC-level fluid mechanics, we must progress beyond basic definitions and explore the rigorous mathematical, thermodynamic, and physical behaviors of these properties.

1. Density and the Continuum Hypothesis

In classical fluid mechanics, we do not track individual molecules. Instead, we assume the fluid is a continuous distribution of matter called a continuum. We define density at a point by considering a small volume $\Delta V$ containing mass $\Delta m$ surrounding that point:

$$ \rho = \lim_{\Delta V \to \Delta V_c} \frac{\Delta m}{\Delta V} $$

Here, $\Delta V_c$ is the limiting volume (typically around $10^{-9}\text{ mm}^3$ for standard atmospheric air). If $\Delta V$ becomes smaller than $\Delta V_c$, molecular fluctuations dominate, and the definition of density breaks down as shown below:

```text Density (\rho) ^ | /\ /\ | /\ / \/ \ <-- Molecular Chaos (Non-continuum Zone) | / \_/ |----------------------------- [Continuum Zone: \rho = constant] | +------------------------------------------------------------> Volume (\Delta V) ^ \Delta V_c (Limiting Volume) ```

2. Mathematical Relationships & Equations of State

A. Behavior of Gases (Compressible Fluids)

For an ideal gas, density is highly sensitive to changes in pressure and temperature. This behavior is governed by the Ideal Gas Equation of State:

$$ P = \rho R T \implies \rho = \frac{P}{R T} $$

Where $P$ is absolute pressure ($\text{Pa}$), $T$ is absolute temperature ($\text{K}$), and $R$ is the specific gas constant ($\text{J/kg}\cdot\text{K}$). For dry air, $R \approx 287\text{ J/kg}\cdot\text{K}$.

B. Behavior of Liquids (Incompressible Assumption & Bulk Modulus)

Liquids are generally treated as incompressible, meaning their density is assumed constant. However, under extreme pressures (such as water hammer in high-pressure conduits or deep-sea conditions), liquids do compress. This compressibility is quantified by the Bulk Modulus of Elasticity ($K$):

$$ K = - V \left(\frac{dP}{dV}\right) $$

Since the mass of a closed system is constant ($m = \rho V = \text{const}$), taking the differential yields:

$$ d(\rho V) = 0 \implies \rho dV + V d\rho = 0 \implies \frac{dV}{V} = -\frac{d\rho}{\rho} $$

Substituting this back into the Bulk Modulus equation gives the fundamental relation governing liquid density variation:

$$ K = \rho \left(\frac{dP}{d\rho}\right) \implies \frac{d\rho}{\rho} = \frac{dP}{K} $$

3. Specific Weight ($\gamma$) and Its Altitude Variations

Specific weight (also called weight density) is weight per unit volume:

$$ \gamma = \frac{W}{V} = \frac{m g}{V} = \rho g $$

SI Unit: $\text{N/m}^3$. Unlike density ($\rho$), which is an intrinsic mass property, specific weight ($\gamma$) is a local property that depends on the gravitational acceleration $g$, which varies with latitude and altitude:

$$ g(h) \approx g_0 \left( \frac{R_e}{R_e + h} \right)^2 $$

Where $R_e$ is the Earth's radius and $h$ is altitude.

4. Specific Gravity ($S$) Mathematical Definition

For liquids:

$$ S_{\text{liquid}} = \frac{\rho_{\text{liquid}}}{\rho_{\text{water at } 4^\circ\text{C}}} = \frac{\gamma_{\text{liquid}}}{\gamma_{\text{water at } 4^\circ\text{C}}} $$

At $4^\circ\text{C}$ ($39.2^\circ\text{F}$), pure water reaches its maximum density:

$$ \rho_{\text{water, max}} \approx 1000\text{ kg/m}^3 \quad \left(999.97\text{ kg/m}^3\right) $$ $$ \gamma_{\text{water, max}} \approx 9810\text{ N/m}^3 $$

For gases:

$$ S_{\text{gas}} = \frac{\rho_{\text{gas}}}{\rho_{\text{air at STP}}} $$

3. Memorize Important Facts (10 min)

  • Density ($\rho$): $\rho = \frac{m}{V} = \frac{1}{v}$, Unit: $\text{kg/m}^3$, Dimensions: $[M L^{-3} T^0]$
  • Specific Volume ($v$): $v = \frac{V}{m} = \frac{1}{\rho}$, Unit: $\text{m}^3\text{/kg}$, Dimensions: $[M^{-1} L^3 T^0]$
  • Specific Weight ($\gamma$): $\gamma = \rho g$, Unit: $\text{N/m}^3$, Dimensions: $[M L^{-2} T^{-2}]$
  • Specific Gravity ($S$): $S = \frac{\rho_{\text{fluid}}}{\rho_{\text{ref}}}$, Unit: Dimensionless ($1$), Dimensions: $[M^0 L^0 T^0]$
  • Standard Constants to Memorize:
    • $\rho_{\text{water}} \text{ at } 4^\circ\text{C} = 1000\text{ kg/m}^3 = 1\text{ g/cm}^3$
    • $\rho_{\text{mercury}} = 13600\text{ kg/m}^3 \implies S_{\text{Hg}} = 13.6$
    • $\rho_{\text{air}} \text{ at Sea Level (ISA)} = 1.225\text{ kg/m}^3$
    • $g_{\text{standard}} = 9.80665\text{ m/s}^2$
  • Gas Density Equation: $\rho = \frac{P}{RT}$ (Ensure $P$ is absolute pressure in $\text{Pa}$ and $T$ is absolute temperature in $\text{K}$).

4. Aviation Application (5–10 min)

In aerospace engineering and aviation safety, density is arguably the most critical parameter governing flight. Here is why:

1. Aerodynamic Lift & Drag Equations

An aircraft's wings generate lift ($L$) and drag ($D$) by interacting directly with the surrounding fluid molecules. The governing equations are:

$$ L = \frac{1}{2} \rho V_{\infty}^2 S_{\text{wing}} C_L \quad \text{and} \quad D = \frac{1}{2} \rho V_{\infty}^2 S_{\text{wing}} C_D $$

Where $\rho$ is the ambient air density, $V_{\infty}$ is the true airspeed, $S_{\text{wing}}$ is the wing surface area, and $C_L, C_D$ are the lift and drag coefficients. If density ($\rho$) decreases (e.g., at high altitudes or on hot days), the lift produced at a given speed drops linearly. To compensate, the aircraft must fly faster ($V_{\infty}$) or increase its angle of attack, which increases drag and fuel consumption.

2. The Hazard of "Density Altitude"

Density Altitude is defined as pressure altitude corrected for non-standard temperature variations. It is the altitude at which the aircraft "feels" like it is flying based on the density of the air.

  • On a hot day, the air expands, lowering its density ($\rho = \frac{P}{RT}$). The air density at a sea-level airport might equal the standard density at $5,000\text{ ft}$.
  • The aircraft's performance behaves as if it were taking off from a high-altitude runway. Safety Hazards: Significantly longer takeoff ground rolls, diminished rate of climb, and reduced engine thrust (less oxygen density for combustion).

3. Aircraft Fuel Density and Mass-Volume Conversions

Aviation turbine fuels (Jet A-1) are metered into the aircraft by mass (kg or lbs) rather than volume (liters or gallons). This is because the energy content of the fuel is directly proportional to its mass, not its volume.

However, fuel trucks fill the tanks by volume. Because the specific gravity ($SG$) of Jet A-1 varies with temperature (ranging from $0.775$ to $0.840$), the Air Safety Officer must use the fuel's exact specific gravity to verify that the mass of fuel loaded matches the flight plan calculations:

$$ m_{\text{fuel}} = V_{\text{fuel}} \times SG \times \rho_{\text{water}} $$

An error in this specific gravity conversion can lead to fuel starvation incidents, famously illustrated by the Gimli Glider incident of 1983.

5. UPSC ASO Focus (10 min)

  • Frequently Asked Exam Facts:
    • Maximum Density of Water: Occurs at $3.98^\circ\text{C}$ ($277.13\text{ K}$) due to the unique structural collapse of hydrogen-bonded ice-like clusters into a denser liquid packing arrangement. Above this temperature, thermal expansion dominates; below it, the open lattice structure of water precursors dominates.
    • Dimensional Analysis: UPSC frequently tests the dimensional formulae of fluid properties. Be prepared to identify combinations that represent kinematic viscosity ($\nu = \mu/\rho$, $[L^2 T^{-1}]$), dynamic viscosity ($\mu$, $[M L^{-1} T^{-1}]$), or specific weight ($\gamma$, $[M L^{-2} T^{-2}]$).
    • Compressibility Relation: $\beta = \frac{1}{K} = -\frac{1}{V}\frac{\partial V}{\partial P} = \frac{1}{\rho}\frac{\partial \rho}{\partial P}$.
  • Conceptual Traps:
    • The "Specific Gravity" Reference Trap: Students often forget that Specific Gravity is dimensionless. Do not write units like $\text{g/cm}^3$ or $\text{kg/m}^3$ for $SG$. Always multiply $SG$ by reference density ($1000\text{ kg/m}^3$ in SI) to get the actual density before plugging it into hydrostatic equations ($P = \rho g h$).
    • Temperature Scale in Gas Laws: When calculating air density using $\rho = \frac{P}{RT}$, using temperature in Celsius ($^\circ\text{C}$) instead of Kelvin ($\text{K}$) is the most common mathematical pitfall. Always convert: $T(\text{K}) = T(^\circ\text{C}) + 273.15$.
    • Gauge vs. Absolute Pressure: In $\rho = \frac{P}{RT}$, $P$ must be the absolute pressure ($P_{\text{abs}} = P_{\text{gauge}} + P_{\text{atm}}$). Standard $P_{\text{atm}} = 101,325\text{ Pa}$ or $101.325\text{ kPa}$.

6. Practice Questions (Exhaustive UPSC ASO MCQs)

Test your fundamental knowledge and analytical skills with these 10 targeted practice questions.

Q1. A vessel contains dry air at an absolute pressure of $200\text{ kPa}$ and a temperature of $27^\circ\text{C}$. Taking the specific gas constant of air as $287\text{ J/(kg}\cdot\text{K)}$, determine the density of the air.
A) $2.58\text{ kg/m}^3$
B) $2.32\text{ kg/m}^3$
C) $1.85\text{ kg/m}^3$
D) $1.16\text{ kg/m}^3$
Step-by-step Solution:
1. Identify given values: $$P = 200\text{ kPa} = 200 \times 10^3\text{ Pa}$$ $$T = 27^\circ\text{C} = 27 + 273.15 = 300.15\text{ K}$$ $$R = 287\text{ J/(kg}\cdot\text{K)}$$ 2. Use the ideal gas equation of state: $$\rho = \frac{P}{RT}$$ 3. Calculate: $$\rho = \frac{200 \times 10^3}{287 \times 300.15} \approx \frac{200000}{86143} \approx 2.322\text{ kg/m}^3$$ Hence, the correct option is B.
Q2. An aircraft fuel tank contains $15,000\text{ liters}$ of Jet A-1 fuel at $15^\circ\text{C}$ where its specific gravity is $0.812$. If the temperature rises to $45^\circ\text{C}$ during flight, causing the fuel to expand such that its specific gravity drops to $0.790$, what is the mass of fuel in the tank at $45^\circ\text{C}$ (neglecting structural tank expansion and leakage)?
A) $11,850\text{ kg}$
B) $12,180\text{ kg}$
C) $15,000\text{ kg}$
D) There is insufficient information because the new volume is not given.
Step-by-step Solution:
This is a classic conceptual trap designed to test your understanding of mass conservation. 1. The mass of the fuel in a closed, non-leaking tank does not change with temperature, even though its volume and density (specific gravity) do. 2. Therefore, the mass of fuel at $45^\circ\text{C}$ is identical to the mass of fuel at $15^\circ\text{C}$ when it was initially loaded. 3. Calculate the mass at $15^\circ\text{C}$: $$V = 15,000\text{ L} = 15\text{ m}^3$$ $$\rho = S \times \rho_{\text{water}} = 0.812 \times 1000\text{ kg/m}^3 = 812\text{ kg/m}^3$$ $$\text{Mass } (m) = \rho \times V = 812 \times 15 = 12,180\text{ kg}$$ Thus, the mass remains $12,180\text{ kg}$ at any temperature (assuming no boil-off or leaks). Correct option is B.
Q3. If the bulk modulus of water is $2.2\text{ GPa}$, calculate the percentage increase in the density of water when the pressure increases from atmospheric pressure ($0.1\text{ MPa}$) to $22\text{ MPa}$ at a constant temperature.
A) $0.01\%$
B) $0.10\%$
C) $1.00\%$
D) $10.0\%$
Step-by-step Solution:
1. Use the compressibility relation: $$\frac{d\rho}{\rho} = \frac{dP}{K}$$ 2. Identify the pressure change: $$dP = P_2 - P_1 = 22\text{ MPa} - 0.1\text{ MPa} = 21.9\text{ MPa} = 21.9 \times 10^6\text{ Pa}$$ 3. Identify Bulk Modulus: $$K = 2.2\text{ GPa} = 2.2 \times 10^9\text{ Pa}$$ 4. Calculate the fractional density change: $$\frac{d\rho}{\rho} = \frac{21.9 \times 10^6}{2.2 \times 10^9} \approx 0.00995$$ 5. Convert to percentage: $$\% \text{ increase} = 0.00995 \times 100\% \approx 0.995\% \approx 1.00\%$$ Hence, the density increases by approximately $1.00\%$. Correct option is C.
Q4. A liquid has a specific volume of $1.25 \times 10^{-3}\text{ m}^3\text{/kg}$. What is its specific gravity?
A) $0.80$
B) $1.25$
C) $8.00$
D) $0.08$
Step-by-step Solution:
1. Specific volume $v = \frac{1}{\rho}$ 2. Find the density: $$\rho = \frac{1}{v} = \frac{1}{1.25 \times 10^{-3}} = \frac{1000}{1.25} = 800\text{ kg/m}^3$$ 3. Find specific gravity ($S$): $$S = \frac{\rho}{\rho_{\text{water}}} = \frac{800}{1000} = 0.80$$ Hence, the correct option is A.
Q5. At standard atmospheric conditions, the density of air is $1.225\text{ kg/m}^3$. Calculate the specific volume of standard air.
A) $0.816\text{ m}^3\text{/kg}$
B) $1.225\text{ m}^3\text{/kg}$
C) $1.000\text{ m}^3\text{/kg}$
D) $0.122\text{ m}^3\text{/kg}$
Step-by-step Solution:
1. Specific volume ($v$) is defined as: $$v = \frac{1}{\rho}$$ 2. Calculate using $\rho = 1.225\text{ kg/m}^3$: $$v = \frac{1}{1.225} \approx 0.8163\text{ m}^3\text{/kg}$$ Hence, the correct option is A.
Q6. An engine oil has a dynamic viscosity of $0.29\text{ Pa}\cdot\text{s}$ and a specific gravity of $0.87$. Determine its kinematic viscosity ($\nu = \mu / \rho$).
A) $3.33 \times 10^{-4}\text{ m}^2\text{/s}$
B) $2.52 \times 10^{-4}\text{ m}^2\text{/s}$
C) $3.33 \times 10^{-5}\text{ m}^2\text{/s}$
D) $2.52 \times 10^{-3}\text{ m}^2\text{/s}$
Step-by-step Solution:
1. Given: $$\mu = 0.29\text{ Pa}\cdot\text{s}$$ $$S = 0.87 \implies \rho = S \times 1000 = 870\text{ kg/m}^3$$ 2. Formula for kinematic viscosity: $$\nu = \frac{\mu}{\rho}$$ 3. Calculation: $$\nu = \frac{0.29}{870} \approx 3.333 \times 10^{-4}\text{ m}^2\text{/s}$$ Hence, the correct option is A.
Q7. What is the specific weight of a liquid on a planet where the local gravitational acceleration is $3.71\text{ m/s}^2$, given that the liquid has a specific gravity of $0.85$?
A) $3153.5\text{ N/m}^3$
B) $8338.5\text{ N/m}^3$
C) $315.3\text{ N/m}^3$
D) $850.0\text{ N/m}^3$
Step-by-step Solution:
1. Find the density of the liquid: $$\rho = S \times \rho_{\text{water, ref}} = 0.85 \times 1000\text{ kg/m}^3 = 850\text{ kg/m}^3$$ 2. Specific weight ($\gamma$) is defined as: $$\gamma = \rho g_{\text{local}}$$ 3. Using $g_{\text{local}} = 3.71\text{ m/s}^2$: $$\gamma = 850 \times 3.71 = 3153.5\text{ N/m}^3$$ Hence, the correct option is A.
Q8. Which of the following statements represents the correct relationship for the dimensional formula of specific weight?
A) $[M L^{-3} T^0]$
B) $[M L^{-1} T^{-2}]$
C) $[M L^{-2} T^{-2}]$
D) $[M L^2 T^{-2}]$
Step-by-step Solution:
1. Specific weight ($\gamma$) is weight per unit volume: $$\gamma = \frac{\text{Force}}{\text{Volume}}$$ 2. Force has dimensions $[M L T^{-2}]$ 3. Volume has dimensions $[L^3]$ 4. Thus: $$[\gamma] = \frac{[M L T^{-2}]}{[L^3]} = [M L^{-2} T^{-2}]$$ Hence, the correct option is C.
Q9. If a block of wood with a volume of $0.05\text{ m}^3$ floats in water with $60\%$ of its volume submerged, determine the density of the wood.
A) $400\text{ kg/m}^3$
B) $600\text{ kg/m}^3$
C) $1000\text{ kg/m}^3$
D) $1667\text{ kg/m}^3$
Step-by-step Solution:
1. According to Archimedes' principle, for a floating body: $$\text{Weight of body} = \text{Buoyant force}$$ $$\rho_{\text{body}} V_{\text{body}} g = \rho_{\text{water}} V_{\text{submerged}} g$$ 2. Simplifying gives: $$\rho_{\text{body}} = \rho_{\text{water}} \left(\frac{V_{\text{submerged}}}{V_{\text{body}}}\right)$$ 3. Given $\frac{V_{\text{submerged}}}{V_{\text{body}}} = 0.60$ and $\rho_{\text{water}} = 1000\text{ kg/m}^3$: $$\rho_{\text{body}} = 1000 \times 0.60 = 600\text{ kg/m}^3$$ Hence, the correct option is B.
Q10. At a constant pressure of $101.3\text{ kPa}$, air is heated from $20^\circ\text{C}$ to $80^\circ\text{C}$. What is the ratio of the final density to the initial density of the air?
A) $0.25$
B) $0.83$
C) $1.20$
D) $4.00$
Step-by-step Solution:
1. Ideal Gas Law relates density and temperature: $$\rho = \frac{P}{RT}$$ 2. Since pressure ($P$) and gas constant ($R$) are constant: $$\rho \propto \frac{1}{T} \implies \frac{\rho_{\text{final}}}{\rho_{\text{initial}}} = \frac{T_{\text{initial}}}{T_{\text{final}}}$$ 3. Convert temperatures to Kelvin: $$T_{\text{initial}} = 20 + 273.15 = 293.15\text{ K}$$ $$T_{\text{final}} = 80 + 273.15 = 353.15\text{ K}$$ 4. Calculate ratio: $$\frac{\rho_{\text{final}}}{\rho_{\text{initial}}} = \frac{293.15}{353.15} \approx 0.83$$ Hence, the correct option is B.

Q1. Consider the following statements regarding the mass density of a fluid:
1. Mass density of a liquid is virtually independent of pressure variations under standard engineering conditions.
2. Mass density is defined only when the system volume is significantly larger than the limiting volume ($\Delta V_c$) associated with the molecular continuum.
Which of the statements given above is/are correct?
A) 1 only
B) 2 only
C) Both 1 and 2
D) Neither 1 nor 2
Detailed Explanation:
- Statement 1 is correct: Under standard atmospheric/engineering conditions, liquids have high bulk moduli, making their density practically invariant with pressure (assumed incompressible).
- Statement 2 is correct: According to the continuum hypothesis, if the volume of interest is smaller than the limiting volume $\Delta V_c$, molecular fluctuations prevent a stable, differentiable mathematical limit for density. Thus, density is physically defined only within the continuum regime.
Correct option is C.
Q2. Assertion (A): The specific weight of a given fluid mass is not a thermodynamic property of the state of the system.
Reason (R): Specific weight depends on local gravitational acceleration, which is an external field effect rather than an intrinsic thermodynamic state variable.
A) Both A and R are true and R is the correct explanation of A
B) Both A and R are true but R is NOT the correct explanation of A
C) A is true but R is false
D) A is false but R is true
Detailed Explanation:
- Thermodynamic state properties (like density, specific volume, temperature) depend purely on the internal state of the matter. - Specific weight ($\gamma = \rho g$) depends directly on $g$, which is determined by geographical position and altitude, making it an extrinsic parameter of the force environment rather than a pure thermodynamic thermodynamic variable. Both statements are true, and the reason explains the assertion correctly. Correct option is A.
Q3. A deep-sea research submarine operates at a depth where the pressure is $40\text{ MPa}$. If the bulk modulus of seawater is $2.3\text{ GPa}$ and its density at the surface is $1025\text{ kg/m}^3$, determine the density of seawater at this depth.
A) $1007\text{ kg/m}^3$
B) $1043\text{ kg/m}^3$
C) $1068\text{ kg/m}^3$
D) $1025\text{ kg/m}^3$
Detailed Explanation:
Using the equation: $$\frac{d\rho}{\rho} \approx \frac{dP}{K}$$ Given: $$dP = 40\text{ MPa} - 0.1\text{ MPa} \approx 40 \times 10^6\text{ Pa}$$ $$K = 2.3\text{ GPa} = 2.3 \times 10^9\text{ Pa}$$ $$\rho_{\text{surf}} = 1025\text{ kg/m}^3$$ Calculate density change $d\rho$: $$d\rho = \rho_{\text{surf}} \times \left(\frac{dP}{K}\right) = 1025 \times \frac{40 \times 10^6}{2.3 \times 10^9} \approx 1025 \times 0.01739 \approx 17.82\text{ kg/m}^3$$ New Density: $$\rho = \rho_{\text{surf}} + d\rho = 1025 + 17.82 = 1042.82\text{ kg/m}^3$$ Thus, the correct option is B.
Q4. Consider the following statements regarding Specific Gravity (SG) and Relative Density:
1. For gases, the standard reference fluid used to compute specific gravity is water at $4^\circ\text{C}$ to maintain consistency with liquid properties.
2. Specific gravity varies with temperature because the densities of both the substance and reference fluids change dynamically with temperature.
Which of the statements given above is/are correct?
A) 1 only
B) 2 only
C) Both 1 and 2

- Statement 1 is incorrect: The standard reference fluid for gases is dry air at STP (or sometimes hydrogen), not water. Water is used for liquids.
- Statement 2 is correct: Because thermal expansion coefficients vary between different substances, the ratio of their densities changes as temperature shifts. Hence, $SG$ depends on temperature and must be reported alongside its reference temperature.

Q6. Assertion (A): Aircraft climb performance is severely degraded when taking off from high-altitude airports on exceptionally warm days.
Reason (R): Increasing air temperature at constant pressure decreases the local specific volume of air, thereby reducing wing lift.
A) Both A and R are true and R is the correct explanation of A
B) Both A and R are true but R is NOT the correct explanation of A
C) A is true but R is false
D) A is false but R is true
Detailed Explanation:
- Assertion (A) is true: Climb performance is dependent on thrust and lift, both of which degrade under high density altitude (warm, high altitude environments).
- Reason (R) is false: Warm air *expands*, meaning its volume per unit mass increases. Therefore, specific volume ($v = 1/\rho$) *increases*, not decreases. Density ($\rho$) decreases, which is the direct cause of the lift reduction ($L = \frac{1}{2} \rho V^2 S C_L$).
Correct option is C.
Q7. A container of dimensions $2\text{ m} \times 2\text{ m} \times 3\text{ m}$ is completely filled with a fluid whose density varies linearly from $\rho = 800\text{ kg/m}^3$ at the top surface to $\rho = 1200\text{ kg/m}^3$ at the bottom surface. Calculate the total mass of fluid in the container.
A) $12,000\text{ kg}$
B) $9,600\text{ kg}$
C) $14,400\text{ kg}$
D) $10,000\text{ kg}$
Detailed Explanation:
1. The density variation is linear with depth ($z$), where $z=0$ is the top and $z=3\text{ m}$ is the bottom: $$\rho(z) = \rho_{\text{top}} + \left(\frac{\rho_{\text{bottom}} - \rho_{\text{top}}}{H}\right)z = 800 + \left(\frac{1200 - 800}{3}\right)z = 800 + \frac{400}{3}z$$ 2. The horizontal cross-sectional area of the container is: $$A = 2\text{ m} \times 2\text{ m} = 4\text{ m}^2$$ 3. The differential mass of a layer of thickness $dz$ is: $$dm = \rho(z) A dz$$ 4. Integrate from $z = 0$ to $z = 3$: $$M = \int_0^3 \left(800 + \frac{400}{3}z\right)(4) dz = 4 \left[ 800z + \frac{200}{3}z^2 \right]_0^3$$ $$M = 4 \left( 800(3) + \frac{200}{3}(9) \right) = 4 (2400 + 600) = 4(3000) = 12,000\text{ kg}$$ (Alternatively, since the density variation is linear, the average density is simply the mean value: $\rho_{\text{avg}} = \frac{800+1200}{2} = 1000\text{ kg/m}^3$. Total volume $V = 2 \times 2 \times 3 = 12\text{ m}^3$. Mass $M = \rho_{\text{avg}} \times V = 1000 \times 12 = 12,000\text{ kg}$.) Correct option is A.
Q8. Under isothermal conditions, the density of a gas is directly proportional to its absolute pressure. Under isentropic (adiabatic reversible) conditions, how does density ($\rho$) relate to absolute pressure ($P$) where $\gamma = C_p/C_v$?
A) $\rho \propto P^{\gamma}$
B) $\rho \propto P^{1/\gamma}$
C) $\rho \propto P^{\gamma - 1}$
D) $\rho \propto P^{-\gamma}$
Detailed Explanation:
1. For an isentropic process of an ideal gas, the pressure-volume relation is: $$P V^{\gamma} = \text{constant}$$ 2. Since $V = m/\rho$, this can be written as: $$P \left(\frac{m}{\rho}\right)^{\gamma} = \text{constant} \implies \frac{P}{\rho^{\gamma}} = \text{constant}$$ 3. Rearranging this relationship to solve for density: $$\rho^{\gamma} \propto P \implies \rho \propto P^{1/\gamma}$$ Correct option is B.
Q9. An aircraft flying at sea level in standard ISA conditions ($\rho = 1.225\text{ kg/m}^3$) produces a certain lift force. If the aircraft climbs to an altitude where the density is $0.408\text{ kg/m}^3$, what must be the ratio of its new airspeed ($V_2$) to its initial airspeed ($V_1$) to maintain the exact same lift force at the same angle of attack?
A) $0.33$
B) $1.73$
C) $3.00$
D) $1.41$
Detailed Explanation:
1. Lift Equation: $L = \frac{1}{2} \rho V^2 S C_L$. 2. To keep lift constant ($L_1 = L_2$) at constant $S$ and $C_L$: $$\rho_1 V_1^2 = \rho_2 V_2^2$$ 3. Solve for the velocity ratio: $$\frac{V_2}{V_1} = \sqrt{\frac{\rho_1}{\rho_2}}$$ 4. Calculate: $$\frac{V_2}{V_1} = \sqrt{\frac{1.225}{0.408}} = \sqrt{3.002} \approx 1.732$$ Thus, the true airspeed must increase by approximately $1.73$ times to maintain the same lift at this higher altitude. Correct option is B.
Q10. Assertion (A): Hydrometers, which measure the specific gravity of liquids, sink deeper into high-density fluids than into low-density fluids.
Reason (R): According to Archimedes' principle, a floating body must displace a volume of fluid whose weight equals the weight of the floating body.
A) Both A and R are true and R is the correct explanation of A
B) Both A and R are true but R is NOT the correct explanation of A
C) A is true but R is false
D) A is false but R is true
Detailed Explanation:
- Assertion (A) is false: Hydrometers sink deeper into *low-density* fluids, not high-density fluids. In a lower density fluid, more volume of fluid must be displaced to balance the weight of the hydrometer.
- Reason (R) is true: This is the precise formulation of the principle of flotation (Archimedes' principle). Since $W_{\text{hydrometer}} = \rho_{\text{fluid}} V_{\text{submerged}} g$, if $\rho_{\text{fluid}}$ is lower, $V_{\text{submerged}}$ must be larger (sinks deeper).
Correct option is D (A is false, R is true).

8. Error Analysis & Common Traps (5 min)

To secure a top rank in the UPSC ASO exam, you must eliminate these three high-frequency mistakes:

1. Hydrology and Pressure Manometry Density Pitfalls

In manometry questions, the density of water is often substituted as $1$ instead of $1000\text{ kg/m}^3$ when performing calculations in SI units.
For instance, if given a fluid with $SG = 13.6$ (mercury), the correct density to use in $P = \rho g h$ is $13600\text{ kg/m}^3$, not $13.6$. This subtle oversight can lead to pressure calculations being off by a factor of 1000.

2. The Specific Weight latitude/altitude trap

A classic UPSC trick is to ask if "specific weight" is a constant property of a fluid sample. No, it is not! Density ($\rho$) is an invariant mass property, but specific weight ($\gamma = \rho g$) changes depending on where the measurement is made (e.g., at the Equator vs. the Poles, or on Earth vs. Mars) because of gravitational variations ($g$).

3. Forgetting Absolute Scales in Gas Calculations

In aeronautical calculations, air density is computed using temperatures in Celsius or pressures in Gauge. Look at this incorrect computation:
$$\text{Incorrect: } \rho = \frac{100\text{ kPa}}{287 \times 20^\circ\text{C}} = 0.017\text{ kg/m}^3$$
$$\text{Correct: } \rho = \frac{100,000\text{ Pa}}{287 \times (20 + 273.15)\text{ K}} = 1.189\text{ kg/m}^3$$ Always convert $T$ to Kelvin and $P$ to Pascals (Absolute).
