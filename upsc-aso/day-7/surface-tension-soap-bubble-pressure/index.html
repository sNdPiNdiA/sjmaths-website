Surface tension – soap bubble pressure

Day 7 of 129 Fluid Mechanics & Machinery Phase 1: Foundations 60-90 min Zero-to-Hero Master Cycle

1. Understand (From Scratch, 5–10 min)

To understand the mechanics of a soap bubble, we must first break down the fundamental phenomenon of surface tension. Imagine a volume of liquid, such as water, at a molecular scale. Molecules deep within the bulk liquid are surrounded by other molecules on all sides. They experience equal attractive cohesive forces in every direction, resulting in a net vector force of zero.

However, a molecule at the free surface (the liquid-gas interface) experiences a different physical reality. It is attracted by molecules below and beside it, but there are virtually no liquid molecules above it to balance this downward pull. This creates a net inward cohesive force pulling the surface molecules toward the bulk. To minimize this high-energy state, the liquid naturally minimizes its surface area, behaving as if it were covered by a stretched elastic membrane. This force per unit length acting parallel to the surface is defined as surface tension ($\sigma$).

Intuitive Analogy: Think of a soap bubble as an incredibly thin, spherical rubber balloon. The elastic rubber sheet represents the liquid soap film, which constantly wants to contract to its smallest possible size. The only reason the balloon does not collapse into a single point is because the air trapped inside is compressed, exerting an outward pressure that perfectly balances the inward elastic squeezing force.

Physical Definitions and Units

  • Surface Tension ($\sigma$): The force acting perpendicular to a line of unit length drawn on the liquid surface. $$ \sigma = \frac{\text{Force } (F)}{\text{Length } (L)} $$ The SI unit of surface tension is Newton per meter ($\text{N/m}$) or, equivalently in terms of energy per unit area, Joule per square meter ($\text{J/m}^2$). Its dimensional formula is $[M T^{-2}]$ (or $[M L^0 T^{-2}]$).
  • Soap Bubble: A soap bubble is unique because it is a hollow spherical shell consisting of a very thin liquid soap film. It has two distinct liquid-gas interfaces: an inner interface enclosing the trapped air core and an outer interface in contact with the ambient atmosphere.
  • Excess Pressure ($\Delta P$): Due to the surface tension forces acting along both interfaces, the pressure inside the bubble ($P_i$) must be strictly greater than the ambient pressure outside ($P_o$). The difference is the excess pressure: $$ \Delta P = P_i - P_o $$ In UPSC/GATE examinations, this $\Delta P$ is mathematically identical to the internal gauge pressure if the ambient atmosphere is taken as the reference pressure ($P_o = 0\text{ gauge}$).

2. Learn the Concept (Complete Theoretical Foundation, 15–20 min)

Let us mathematically derive the governing equations for the excess pressure inside a soap bubble, comparing it rigorously with a liquid droplet and a liquid jet to build a complete analytical foundation.

1. Thermodynamic Derivation via Virtual Work Principle

Consider a spherical soap bubble of radius $R$ with surface tension $\sigma$. Suppose we experimentally increase the radius of this bubble by a tiny, virtual increment $dR$ under isothermal conditions. This expansion involves two competing work terms:

  1. Work Done by Excess Pressure ($dW_p$): The excess pressure $\Delta P$ acts outward across the surface area of the sphere. The outward force is $F = \Delta P \cdot A = \Delta P \cdot 4\pi R^2$. The work done during the radial expansion $dR$ is: $$ dW_p = \text{Force} \times \text{displacement} = (\Delta P \cdot 4\pi R^2) \cdot dR $$
  2. Work Done in Increasing Interfacial Energy ($dW_\sigma$): A soap bubble has two surfaces (inner and outer). Total surface area of a single sphere is $A_{single} = 4\pi R^2$. Total active surface area of the soap bubble film is: $$ A_{total} = 2 \times 4\pi R^2 = 8\pi R^2 $$ When the radius increases from $R$ to $R + dR$, the change in total surface area is: $$ dA_{total} = \frac{d}{dR}(8\pi R^2) dR = 16\pi R dR $$ The energy required to create this new interface is: $$ dW_\sigma = \sigma \cdot dA_{total} = 16\pi R \sigma dR $$

By the principle of virtual work, for the bubble to remain in static equilibrium, the work done by the internal pressure must equal the energy stored in the expanded film ($dW_p = dW_\sigma$):

$$ \Delta P \cdot 4\pi R^2 dR = 16\pi R \sigma dR $$

Solving for $\Delta P$ yields the celebrated Young-Laplace Relation for a Soap Bubble:

$$ \Delta P = P_i - P_o = \frac{4\sigma}{R} = \frac{8\sigma}{D} $$ where $R$ is the radius of the bubble and $D$ is its diameter.
---

2. Mechanical Equilibrium Derivation (Free Body Diagram)

Alternatively, we can derive this by slicing the soap bubble into two equal hemispheres. Let us analyze the static force balance acting along the cutting plane of one hemisphere:

  • Bursting Force (due to Excess Pressure): The excess pressure $\Delta P$ acts uniformly on the inner curved surface. The net force tending to push the two hemispheres apart is equal to the pressure multiplied by the projected area of the hemisphere (which is a flat circle of area $\pi R^2$): $$ F_{pressure} = \Delta P \cdot \pi R^2 $$
  • Restoring Force (due to Surface Tension): Surface tension acts along the cut edge to hold the two hemispheres together. Because the film is extremely thin, both the inner perimeter and outer perimeter can be approximated as having radius $R$. Thus, the total length along which the surface tension acts is twice the circumference: $$ L = 2 \times (2\pi R) = 4\pi R $$ The total holding force is: $$ F_{tension} = \sigma \cdot L = 4\pi R \sigma $$

Equating these forces for static equilibrium ($F_{pressure} = F_{tension}$):

$$ \Delta P \cdot \pi R^2 = 4\pi R \sigma \implies \Delta P = \frac{4\sigma}{R} $$ ---

4. Advanced Thermodynamic Behavior: Two-Bubble Instability

Consider two soap bubbles of different radii, $R_1$ (smaller) and $R_2$ (larger), connected by a closed valve. What happens when the valve is opened?

According to our governing equation:

$$ \Delta P_1 = \frac{4\sigma}{R_1} \quad \text{and} \quad \Delta P_2 = \frac{4\sigma}{R_2} $$

Since $R_1 < R_2$, it mathematically follows that:

$$ \Delta P_1 > \Delta P_2 $$

Fluid always flows from a region of higher pressure to a region of lower pressure. Therefore, air flows from the smaller bubble to the larger bubble. The smaller bubble shrinks further ($R_1 \to 0$), driving its internal pressure even higher, while the larger bubble expands. This runaway process is a classic demonstration of thermodynamic instability in systems with surface-tension-dominated pressure states.

3. Memorize Important Facts (10 min)

  • Core Formula 1 & Units: Soap Bubble Excess Pressure: $\Delta P = \frac{4\sigma}{R} = \frac{8\sigma}{D}$ (SI unit: $\text{Pa}$ or $\text{N/m}^2$).
  • Core Formula 2 & Units: Liquid Droplet Excess Pressure: $\Delta P = \frac{2\sigma}{R} = \frac{4\sigma}{D}$ (SI unit: $\text{Pa}$).
  • Core Formula 3 & Units: Liquid Jet Excess Pressure: $\Delta P = \frac{\sigma}{R} = \frac{2\sigma}{D}$ (SI unit: $\text{Pa}$).
  • Standard Constant / Dimension: Surface Tension $\sigma$: Dimension $[M T^{-2}]$, SI unit $\text{N/m}$ or $\text{J/m}^2$.
  • Key Law / Principle: Young-Laplace Law states that the pressure difference across a curved interface is directly proportional to the surface tension and the mean curvature ($H = \frac{1}{R_1} + \frac{1}{R_2}$).
  • Work Formula (Soap Bubble): Total thermodynamic work required to form a soap bubble of radius $R$ from a flat film is $W = 8\pi R^2 \sigma$. For a droplet, $W = 4\pi R^2 \sigma$.

4. Aviation Application (5–10 min)

While soap bubbles seem academic, the physics of surface tension and interfacial pressure differences plays a direct, critical role in several aerospace and aviation systems:

1. Gas Turbine Combustors & Fuel Atomization

In aircraft jet engines, liquid aviation fuel (such as Jet-A) must be mixed with high-velocity air for combustion. To ensure rapid, efficient, and complete combustion, the fuel injector must atomize the bulk liquid fuel into an incredibly fine spray of tiny droplets. This process is governed by the Weber Number ($We$), which is a dimensionless parameter comparing inertial forces to surface tension forces:

$$ We = \frac{\rho_g v_{rel}^2 d_p}{\sigma} $$

Where $\rho_g$ is gas density, $v_{rel}$ is the relative velocity between fuel and air, $d_p$ is droplet diameter, and $\sigma$ is the fuel's surface tension. If the surface tension is too high, the fuel resists deformation and breaking, resulting in larger droplets. Larger droplets evaporate slowly, causing delayed combustion, localized hot spots on turbine blades, soot formation, and elevated emissions. Understanding the internal pressure $\Delta P = \frac{2\sigma}{R}$ of these droplets allows combustion engineers to model the critical breakup conditions ($We_{crit} \approx 12$) where aerodynamic shear overcomes internal cohesive pressures.

2. Raindrop Deformation & Aircraft Aerodynamics

When an aircraft flies through heavy rain, high-speed airflow shears the falling raindrops. The aerodynamic pressure on the front of the raindrop must overcome the internal excess pressure ($\Delta P = 2\sigma / R$) holding the drop in a spherical shape. The droplet flattens into a disk and eventually bursts when aerodynamic forces dominate. This distortion alters the optical characteristics of the cockpit windshield and modifies the boundary layer behavior on the wing leading edges, reducing the maximum lift coefficient ($C_{L,max}$) and accelerating stalls.

3. Microfluidic Spacecraft Life Support Systems

In microgravity environments (like the International Space Station), gravity-driven phase separation fails. Engineers rely heavily on surface tension forces and capillary pressures to separate water from air bubbles in life support loops and to manage fuel inside liquid propellant tanks (using surface-tension-based PMDs—Propellant Management Devices).

5. UPSC ASO Focus (10 min)

  • Frequently Asked Exam Facts:
    • The ratio of excess pressure for a liquid jet, a liquid droplet, and a soap bubble of the same radius $R$ under identical surface tension conditions is strictly: $$ \Delta P_{jet} : \Delta P_{droplet} : \Delta P_{bubble} = 1 : 2 : 4 $$
    • Surface tension of pure water at $20^\circ\text{C}$ is approximately $0.073\text{ N/m}$. Soap solutions drastically lower this to around $0.025 - 0.030\text{ N/m}$ by disrupting hydrogen bonds, allowing stable, high-surface-area bubbles to form.
    • Surface tension is a strongly temperature-dependent property. It decreases linearly with temperature according to Eötvös' rule and becomes exactly zero at the critical temperature of the fluid ($T = T_c$), where the phase boundary vanishes.
  • Conceptual Traps:
    • The Radius vs. Diameter Trap: UPSC questions frequently provide the diameter of the bubble but students reflexively use the radius formula $\frac{4\sigma}{R}$ without dividing the diameter by 2, leading to answers off by a factor of 2. Always write: $\Delta P = \frac{8\sigma}{D}$ if given diameter!
    • Interface Count Trap: Look closely at the wording of the question: "water droplet" or "bubble falling through water" (1 interface, $\Delta P = 2\sigma/R$) versus "soap bubble in air" (2 interfaces, $\Delta P = 4\sigma/R$).
    • Gauge vs. Absolute Pressure: $\Delta P$ is the excess pressure over ambient. If a question asks for the "absolute pressure" inside a bubble, you must add atmospheric pressure: $P_{abs} = P_{atm} + \frac{4\sigma}{R}$.

6. Practice Questions (Exhaustive UPSC ASO MCQs)

Q1. A soap bubble of diameter $4\text{ mm}$ is formed in air using a soap solution with a surface tension of $0.03\text{ N/m}$. What is the excess pressure inside this bubble?
A) $15\text{ Pa}$
B) $30\text{ Pa}$
C) $60\text{ Pa}$
D) $120\text{ Pa}$
Given:
Diameter, $D = 4\text{ mm} = 4 \times 10^{-3}\text{ m} \implies$ Radius, $R = 2 \times 10^{-3}\text{ m}$.
Surface tension, $\sigma = 0.03\text{ N/m}$.
For a soap bubble (two interfaces):
$$ \Delta P = \frac{4\sigma}{R} = \frac{4 \times 0.03}{2 \times 10^{-3}} = \frac{0.12}{0.002} = 60\text{ Pa} $$ Alternatively, using diameter:
$$ \Delta P = \frac{8\sigma}{D} = \frac{8 \times 0.03}{4 \times 10^{-3}} = 60\text{ Pa} $$
Q2. Calculate the work done in blowing a spherical soap bubble from an initial radius of $1\text{ cm}$ to a final radius of $2\text{ cm}$ in a vacuum. (Take surface tension of soap solution $\sigma = 0.03\text{ N/m}$).
A) $72\pi \times 10^{-6}\text{ J}$
B) $144\pi \times 10^{-6}\text{ J}$
C) $36\pi \times 10^{-6}\text{ J}$
D) $18\pi \times 10^{-6}\text{ J}$
The total active surface area of a soap bubble of radius $R$ is $A = 2 \times (4\pi R^2) = 8\pi R^2$.
Work done ($W$) is the change in surface free energy:
$$ W = \sigma \cdot \Delta A = \sigma \cdot 8\pi (R_2^2 - R_1^2) $$ Given: $R_1 = 0.01\text{ m}$, $R_2 = 0.02\text{ m}$, $\sigma = 0.03\text{ N/m}$.
$$ R_1^2 = 10^{-4}\text{ m}^2, \quad R_2^2 = 4 \times 10^{-4}\text{ m}^2 $$ $$ \Delta A = 8\pi (4 \times 10^{-4} - 10^{-4}) = 8\pi (3 \times 10^{-4}) = 24\pi \times 10^{-4}\text{ m}^2 $$ $$ W = 0.03 \times 24\pi \times 10^{-4} = 7.2\pi \times 10^{-5}\text{ J} = 72\pi \times 10^{-6}\text{ J} $$
Q3. Two soap bubbles of radii $R_1$ and $R_2$ ($R_1 > R_2$) are kept in contact. Which of the following statements correctly describes their physical behavior?
A) Air flows from the larger bubble to the smaller bubble until their sizes equalize.
B) No air flows between them because both are soap bubbles of the same liquid.
C) Air flows from the smaller bubble to the larger bubble, causing the smaller bubble to collapse completely.
D) The pressure inside both bubbles remains equalized because surface tension adjusts.
The excess pressure inside a soap bubble is inversely proportional to its radius ($\Delta P = 4\sigma / R$).
Since $R_1 > R_2$, the pressure inside the smaller bubble is higher ($\Delta P_2 > \Delta P_1$).
Air flows from high pressure to low pressure, so it flows from the smaller bubble ($R_2$) to the larger bubble ($R_1$). As the smaller bubble loses air, its radius $R_2$ decreases further, which causes its pressure to increase even more, accelerating the flow until the smaller bubble completely empties into the larger one.
Q4. A spherical liquid droplet, a cylindrical liquid jet, and a spherical soap bubble are all formed from the same liquid with surface tension $\sigma$. If all three configurations have the exact same radius $R$, the ratio of their excess pressures is:
A) $1 : 2 : 4$ (Jet : Droplet : Bubble)
B) $4 : 2 : 1$ (Jet : Droplet : Bubble)
C) $2 : 1 : 4$ (Jet : Droplet : Bubble)
D) $1 : 1 : 2$ (Jet : Droplet : Bubble)
Let's write down the excess pressure relations for the same radius $R$:
1. Liquid Jet: $\Delta P_{jet} = \frac{\sigma}{R}$
2. Liquid Droplet: $\Delta P_{droplet} = \frac{2\sigma}{R}$
3. Soap Bubble: $\Delta P_{bubble} = \frac{4\sigma}{R}$

Taking the ratio:
$$ \Delta P_{jet} : \Delta P_{droplet} : \Delta P_{bubble} = \frac{\sigma}{R} : \frac{2\sigma}{R} : \frac{4\sigma}{R} = 1 : 2 : 4 $$
Q5. In an aircraft gas turbine engine fuel injector spray test, fuel droplets of Jet-A with surface tension $\sigma = 0.025\text{ N/m}$ are created. If the measured excess pressure inside a droplet is $1000\text{ Pa}$, what is the diameter of the droplet?
A) $0.05\text{ mm}$
B) $0.10\text{ mm}$
C) $0.20\text{ mm}$
D) $0.40\text{ mm}$
Note: A fuel droplet is a solid sphere of liquid surrounded by air, meaning it has only one interface.
Formula for excess pressure of a droplet:
$$ \Delta P = \frac{2\sigma}{R} = \frac{4\sigma}{D} $$ Given: $\Delta P = 1000\text{ Pa}$, $\sigma = 0.025\text{ N/m}$.
$$ D = \frac{4\sigma}{\Delta P} = \frac{4 \times 0.025}{1000} = \frac{0.10}{1000} = 10^{-4}\text{ m} = 0.1\text{ mm} $$
Q6. Which of the following parameters has the identical dimensions as that of surface tension ($\sigma$)?
A) Dynamic Viscosity
B) Spring Constant
C) Pressure
D) Kinematic Viscosity
Let's find the dimensional formulas:
- Surface Tension ($\sigma = \text{Force}/\text{Length}$): $\frac{[M L T^{-2}]}{[L]} = [M T^{-2}]$
- Spring Constant ($k = \text{Force}/\text{Displacement}$): $\frac{[M L T^{-2}]}{[L]} = [M T^{-2}]$
- Dynamic Viscosity ($\mu$): $[M L^{-1} T^{-1}]$
- Pressure ($P$): $[M L^{-1} T^{-2}]$
- Kinematic Viscosity ($\nu$): $[L^2 T^{-1}]$

Therefore, the dimensions of surface tension match perfectly with those of a spring constant.
Q7. What is the equivalent SI unit of surface tension expressed in terms of energy?
A) $\text{J/m}$
B) $\text{J}\cdot\text{m}$
C) $\text{J/m}^2$
D) $\text{W/m}^2$
Surface tension can be defined as surface energy per unit area:
$$ \sigma = \frac{\text{Energy}}{\text{Area}} \implies \text{Unit} = \frac{\text{Joule (J)}}{\text{meter}^2 (\text{m}^2)} = \text{J/m}^2 $$ Alternatively, using force per unit length:
$$ \text{N/m} = \frac{\text{N} \cdot \text{m}}{\text{m} \cdot \text{m}} = \frac{\text{J}}{\text{m}^2} $$
Q8. If the air pressure inside a soap bubble of radius $R$ is $P_i$ and the atmospheric pressure is $P_{atm}$, what is the absolute pressure inside the bubble?
A) $P_i = P_{atm} + \frac{2\sigma}{R}$
B) $P_i = P_{atm} - \frac{4\sigma}{R}$
C) $P_i = P_{atm} + \frac{4\sigma}{R}$
D) $P_i = \frac{4\sigma}{R}$
The excess pressure inside a soap bubble is given by:
$$ \Delta P = P_{absolute, internal} - P_{ambient} = \frac{4\sigma}{R} $$ Since $P_{ambient} = P_{atm}$:
$$ P_{absolute, internal} = P_{atm} + \frac{4\sigma}{R} $$
Q9. Soap is added to water to wash dirty clothes. What physical effect does the soap have on the water to improve cleaning?
A) It increases the surface tension of water, helping it form bigger droplets.
B) It decreases the surface tension of water, allowing it to easily wet and penetrate fabrics.
C) It increases the viscosity of water to carry dirt away.
D) It chemically neutralizes the cotton fibers of fabrics.
Pure water has a relatively high surface tension ($\approx 0.073\text{ N/m}$), which prevents it from spreading easily into the tiny pores of fabric fibers (high contact angle). Adding soap introduces surfactant molecules that disrupt the cohesive hydrogen bonding of water, decreasing its surface tension (to $\approx 0.025 - 0.030\text{ N/m}$). This allows the water to easily wet the fabric, penetrate the pores, and dissolve oil and dirt.
Q10. Two soap bubbles of radii $R_1$ and $R_2$ coalesce isothermally in a vacuum to form a single larger soap bubble of radius $R_f$. The relation for the final radius is:
A) $R_f = R_1 + R_2$
B) $R_f = \sqrt{R_1^2 + R_2^2}$
C) $R_f = (R_1^3 + R_2^3)^{1/3}$
D) $R_f = \frac{R_1 R_2}{R_1 + R_2}$
Under isothermal conditions in a vacuum, the temperature $T$ is constant and the external pressure is zero ($P_{atm} = 0$).
The internal pressure inside the bubbles is solely due to excess pressure:
$$ P_1 = \frac{4\sigma}{R_1}, \quad P_2 = \frac{4\sigma}{R_2}, \quad P_f = \frac{4\sigma}{R_f} $$ Since the number of moles of air ($n$) is conserved ($n_1 + n_2 = n_f$), the ideal gas law ($PV = nRT$) gives:
$$ P_1 V_1 + P_2 V_2 = P_f V_f $$ Substituting volume $V = \frac{4}{3}\pi R^3$:
$$ \left(\frac{4\sigma}{R_1}\right) \left(\frac{4}{3}\pi R_1^3\right) + \left(\frac{4\sigma}{R_2}\right) \left(\frac{4}{3}\pi R_2^3\right) = \left(\frac{4\sigma}{R_f}\right) \left(\frac{4}{3}\pi R_f^3\right) $$ Simplifying by dividing out common terms ($ \frac{16}{3}\pi \sigma $):
$$ R_1^2 + R_2^2 = R_f^2 \implies R_f = \sqrt{R_1^2 + R_2^2} $$

Q1. (Statement-Based) Consider the following statements regarding surface tension:
Statement I: The surface tension of a liquid is entirely due to cohesive forces between molecules of the liquid.
Statement II: With an increase in temperature, the cohesive forces decrease, causing the surface tension of the liquid to decrease.
Choose the correct option:
A) Both Statement I and Statement II are correct, and Statement II is the correct explanation of Statement I.
B) Both Statement I and Statement II are correct, but Statement II is NOT the correct explanation of Statement I.
C) Statement I is correct but Statement II is incorrect.
D) Statement I is incorrect but Statement II is correct.
Statement I is correct: Surface tension is a direct manifestation of cohesive molecular forces pull the surface molecules inward.
Statement II is correct: As temperature increases, the kinetic energy of the molecules increases, weakening the cohesive intermolecular bonds. Hence, surface tension decreases, which explains the physical mechanism behind Statement I.

The Assertion is true: smaller connected bubbles have higher internal pressure, meaning air flows out of them to larger bubbles, indicating they are unstable when connected. More importantly, the Reason is completely false because the excess pressure is inversely proportional to the radius ($\Delta P = 4\sigma / R$), not directly proportional.

Q4. A student blows a soap bubble of radius $R$ in an atmosphere of constant ambient pressure $P_0$. What is the total work done ($W_{total}$) in terms of surface tension $\sigma$, radius $R$, and ambient pressure $P_0$? (Consider expansion from zero radius).
A) $W_{total} = 8\pi R^2 \sigma + \frac{4}{3}\pi R^3 P_0$
B) $W_{total} = 4\pi R^2 \sigma + \frac{4}{3}\pi R^3 P_0$
C) $W_{total} = 8\pi R^2 \sigma$
D) $W_{total} = 8\pi R^2 \sigma - \frac{4}{3}\pi R^3 P_0$
To blow a soap bubble of radius $R$ in an ambient atmosphere of pressure $P_0$, work must be done against two distinct forces:
1. Work done to create the double interface of the soap bubble:
$$ W_{surface} = \sigma \cdot A = \sigma \cdot (8\pi R^2) = 8\pi R^2 \sigma $$ 2. Work done to push back the surrounding atmosphere to occupy a volume $V = \frac{4}{3}\pi R^3$:
$$ W_{volume} = P_0 \cdot \Delta V = P_0 \cdot \frac{4}{3}\pi R^3 $$ Total work is the sum of both:
$$ W_{total} = 8\pi R^2 \sigma + \frac{4}{3}\pi R^3 P_0 $$
Q5. In aerospace combustion dynamics, the dimensionless parameter governing droplet deformation and aerodynamic breakup in fuel sprays is the Weber number ($We$). If a fuel droplet of density $\rho_L$ and surface tension $\sigma$ is exposed to air of density $\rho_g$ moving at relative velocity $v_{rel}$, the Weber number is defined as:
A) $We = \frac{\sigma}{\rho_L v_{rel}^2 D}$
B) $We = \frac{\rho_g v_{rel}^2 D}{\sigma}$
C) $We = \frac{\rho_L v_{rel}^2 D}{\sigma}$
D) $We = \frac{\mu v_{rel}}{\sigma}$
The Weber number compares the disruptive aerodynamic inertial force of the surrounding gas phase ($\rho_g v_{rel}^2$) to the restoring surface tension pressure forces stabilizing the droplet ($\sigma / D$):
$$ We = \frac{\text{Inertial Force}}{\text{Surface Tension Force}} = \frac{\rho_g v_{rel}^2 D}{\sigma} $$ Hence, option B is correct. (Option C uses fuel density $\rho_L$, which represents internal liquid droplet inertia, not the aerodynamic shearing force).
Q6. A soap bubble of radius $R = 2.5\text{ cm}$ is blown inside a vacuum chamber. If the surface tension of the soap solution is $0.02\text{ N/m}$, what is the absolute pressure inside the bubble when the chamber is completely evacuated?
A) $0\text{ Pa}$
B) $1.6\text{ Pa}$
C) $3.2\text{ Pa}$
D) $6.4\text{ Pa}$
In a complete vacuum, the external pressure $P_o = 0\text{ Pa}$ (absolute).
The absolute pressure inside the soap bubble is:
$$ P_i = P_o + \Delta P = 0 + \frac{4\sigma}{R} $$ Given: $R = 2.5\text{ cm} = 0.025\text{ m}$ and $\sigma = 0.02\text{ N/m}$.
$$ P_i = \frac{4 \times 0.02}{0.025} = \frac{0.08}{0.025} = 3.2\text{ Pa} $$
Q7. What is the excess pressure inside a soap bubble of diameter $D$ if the surface tension of the soap solution is $\sigma$?
A) $\frac{2\sigma}{D}$
B) $\frac{4\sigma}{D}$
C) $\frac{8\sigma}{D}$
D) $\frac{\sigma}{2D}$
This is a classic UPSC trap. The formula in terms of radius is $\Delta P = \frac{4\sigma}{R}$.
Since radius $R = D/2$, substituting this gives:
$$ \Delta P = \frac{4\sigma}{D/2} = \frac{8\sigma}{D} $$ Therefore, the excess pressure is $\frac{8\sigma}{D}$.
Q8. (Assertion-Reasoning)
Assertion (A): When an organic impurity (such as a surfactant) is dissolved in water, the surface tension of the water decreases.
Reason (R): Surfactant molecules concentrate at the surface of the liquid, disrupting the cohesive hydrogen bonds between adjacent water molecules.
Choose the correct option:
A) Both A and R are true, and R is the correct explanation of A.
B) Both A and R are true, but R is NOT the correct explanation of A.
C) A is true, but R is false.
D) A is false, but R is true.
Surfactants have a hydrophilic head and a hydrophobic tail. They concentrate at the surface interface, inserting themselves between water molecules. This weakens the strong intermolecular cohesive hydrogen forces of the water surface layer, reducing its surface tension. Both A and R are true, and R is the direct physical explanation.
Q9. A spherical soap bubble is expanding isothermally. If its radius doubles, how does the excess pressure inside the bubble change?
A) It remains unchanged.
B) It is halved.
C) It doubles.
D) It is reduced to one-fourth.
The excess pressure inside a soap bubble is given by:
$$ \Delta P = \frac{4\sigma}{R} $$ If the radius $R$ is doubled ($R' = 2R$), and assuming surface tension $\sigma$ remains constant during isothermal expansion:
$$ \Delta P' = \frac{4\sigma}{2R} = \frac{1}{2} \left(\frac{4\sigma}{R}\right) = \frac{\Delta P}{2} $$ Hence, the excess pressure is halved.
Q10. A cylindrical liquid jet of diameter $D$ has a surface tension $\sigma$. What is the excess pressure inside this jet?
A) $\frac{\sigma}{2D}$
B) $\frac{\sigma}{D}$
C) $\frac{2\sigma}{D}$
D) $\frac{4\sigma}{D}$
The excess pressure of a cylindrical liquid jet in terms of radius is:
$$ \Delta P = \frac{\sigma}{R} $$ Since $R = D/2$, substituting this gives:
$$ \Delta P = \frac{\sigma}{D/2} = \frac{2\sigma}{D} $$ Therefore, option C is correct.

8. Error Analysis & Common Traps (5 min)

To score high in the UPSC Air Safety Officer exam, you must systematically avoid these frequent student pitfalls:

  1. The $2\sigma$ vs $4\sigma$ Confusion: Always identify the number of active interfaces!
    • A droplet of water falling in air has only one interface (liquid-gas) $\implies \Delta P = \frac{2\sigma}{R}$.
    • An air bubble submerged inside a cup of water has only one interface (gas-liquid) $\implies \Delta P = \frac{2\sigma}{R}$.
    • A soap bubble blown in air has two interfaces (inner gas-liquid and outer liquid-gas) $\implies \Delta P = \frac{4\sigma}{R}$.
  2. The Diameter vs Radius Conversion: Standard questions will state "A bubble of $5\text{ mm}$ diameter...". If you use $R = 5\text{ mm}$ instead of $2.5\text{ mm}$, your pressure calculation will be exactly half of the correct value. Always convert to SI units ($m$) first.
  3. Surface Tension Units: Watch out for units like $\text{mN/m}$ (milli-Newtons per meter) or $\text{dynes/cm}$. $$ 1\text{ mN/m} = 10^{-3}\text{ N/m} $$ $$ 1\text{ dyne/cm} = 10^{-3}\text{ N/m} $$ Always perform calculations in standard SI ($\text{N/m}$) to avoid scale errors in Pascals.
  4. Thermodynamic Work Errors: When calculating the work done to blow a soap bubble, the active surface area is doubled. $$ \text{Work (Droplet)} = 4\pi R^2 \sigma $$ $$ \text{Work (Soap Bubble)} = 8\pi R^2 \sigma $$ Failing to double the surface area for a soap bubble is a major source of lost points.
