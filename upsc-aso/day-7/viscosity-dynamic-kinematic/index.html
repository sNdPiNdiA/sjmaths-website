Viscosity (dynamic, kinematic)

Day 7 of 129 Fluid Mechanics & Machinery Phase 1: Foundations 60-90 min Zero-to-Hero Master Cycle

1. Understand (From Scratch, 5–10 min)

Viscosity is fundamentally the "internal friction" of a fluid. Just as solid friction resists the sliding of one dry surface over another, viscosity resists the relative motion between adjacent layers of fluid. Imagine pouring water versus pouring honey: honey flows slowly and clings heavily to surfaces because it possesses a much higher viscosity than water.

To grasp this scientifically, let us establish two distinct viewpoints: Dynamic Viscosity ($μ$) and Kinematic Viscosity ($ν$).

1. Dynamic (Absolute) Viscosity ($μ$)

Dynamic viscosity is the measure of a fluid's internal resistance to shear flow. When a fluid is sheared between two parallel plates, the force required to maintain a constant relative velocity is directly proportional to the dynamic viscosity. It represents the actual physical "stickiness" or cohesive strength of the fluid.

  • SI Unit: Pascal-second ($Pa·s$) or Kilogram per meter-second ($kg·m-1·s-1$).
  • CGS Unit: Poise ($P$), where $1 Pa·s = 10 Poise$.

2. Kinematic Viscosity ($ν$)

Kinematic viscosity is the ratio of dynamic viscosity to the fluid density ($ρ$):

$$ν = μρ$$

It represents the fluid's resistance to shear flow under the influence of gravity. It measures how fast momentum diffuses through the fluid relative to its mass. Think of it as "molecular momentum diffusivity".

  • SI Unit: Square meters per second ($m^2/s$).
  • CGS Unit: Stoke ($St$), where $1 Stoke = 10-4 m^2/s = 1 cm^2/s$.

UPSC Standard Terminology

  • No-Slip Condition: The physical boundary condition stating that at a solid boundary, the fluid parameters (velocity, temperature) take the exact value of the boundary surface. Thus, fluid velocity $u = 0$ relative to a stationary wall.
  • Velocity Gradient ($du/dy$): The rate at which velocity changes as you move perpendicularly away from the boundary surface. This is also called the shear strain rate ($\dot{γ}$).

2. Learn the Concept (Complete Theoretical Foundation, 15–20 min)

Let us mathematically derive the governing principles of viscosity starting from zero prior knowledge.

1. Newton's Law of Viscosity

Consider a fluid element trapped between two large, parallel flat plates separated by a tiny distance $y = h$. The bottom plate is stationary, while the top plate is pulled to the right at a constant velocity $u = U$.

Due to the no-slip condition, the fluid layer in contact with the bottom plate has $u = 0$, and the layer in contact with the top plate has $u = U$. For a laminar, parallel flow, the velocity profile $u(y)$ varies linearly from $0$ to $U$:

$$u(y) = Uhy$$

To maintain the motion of the upper plate, a continuous shearing force $F$ must be applied. Experimentally, Sir Isaac Newton observed that this force $F$ is: 1. Directly proportional to the plate area $A$. 2. Directly proportional to the velocity $U$. 3. Inversely proportional to the distance $h$.

Expressing this in terms of shear stress ($τ = F/A$):

$$τ ∝ Uh \implies τ ∝ dudy$$

Introducing the constant of proportionality, Dynamic Viscosity ($μ$), we write Newton's Law of Viscosity:

$$ τ = μ dudy $$

Where:

  • $τ$ is the shear stress ($N/m^2$ or $Pa$).
  • $μ$ is the dynamic viscosity ($Pa·s$).
  • $dudy$ is the velocity gradient or shear deformation rate ($s-1$).

2. Geometric Derivation of Shear Strain Rate

Let a fluid particle deform by an angle $dθ$ over an interval $dt$. The top edge of the fluid element moves a distance $du · dt$ relative to its bottom edge over a height $dy$. From trigonometry:

$$\tan(dθ) ≈ dθ = du · dtdy$$

Dividing by $dt$ yields the rate of shear strain:

$$dθdt = dudy$$

Thus, Newton's law states that shear stress is directly proportional to the rate of shear strain.

3. Temperature Dependence of Viscosity

The mechanisms of viscosity differ fundamentally between liquids and gases:

A. Liquids

In liquids, viscosity is dominated by intermolecular cohesive forces. As temperature $T$ increases, these cohesive forces weaken due to energetic molecular vibrations. Consequently, the dynamic viscosity of liquids decreases with an increase in temperature.

This behavior is modeled by Andrade's Equation:

$$μ(T) = A eBT$$

Where $A$ and $B$ are empirical constants and $T$ is absolute temperature in Kelvin.

B. Gases

In gases, molecules are widely spaced, making cohesive forces negligible. Instead, viscosity arises from the molecular momentum transfer during chaotic molecular collisions. As temperature $T$ increases, the thermal kinetic energy increases, leading to more frequent and violent molecular collisions. Thus, the dynamic viscosity of gases increases with temperature.

This is modeled by Sutherland's Law:

$$μ(T) = μ_0 (TT_0)3/2 T_0 + ST + S$$

Where $S$ is Sutherland's constant (for air, $S ≈ 110.4 K$), and $μ_0$ is the viscosity at reference temperature $T_0$. For simplified analyses, we use the approximation:

$$μ ∝ T^n \quad (where n ≈ 0.6 to 1.0)$$

4. Pressure Dependence of Viscosity

  • Liquids: Dynamic viscosity $μ$ is virtually independent of pressure for moderate ranges. However, under extremely high pressures (thousands of atmospheres), $μ$ increases exponentially due to the physical compaction of molecules.
  • Gases: According to kinetic theory, dynamic viscosity $μ$ is independent of pressure. However, kinematic viscosity $ν = μρ$ is highly pressure-dependent because density $ρ$ is directly proportional to pressure ($P = ρ RT$). Therefore, for gases, as pressure increases, $ν$ decreases.

3. Memorize Important Facts (10 min)

  • Core Formula 1 & Units (Dynamic Viscosity): $τ = μ dudy \implies μ = τdu/dy \quad [Unit: Pa·s = 10 Poise = 1000 cP]$
  • Core Formula 2 & Units (Kinematic Viscosity): $ν = μρ \quad [Unit: m^2/s = 10^4 Stokes = 10^6 cSt]$
  • Dimensional Analysis: $$Dynamic Viscosity [μ] = [M^1 L-1 T-1]$$ $$Kinematic Viscosity [ν] = [M^0 L^2 T-1]$$
  • Key Temperature Trends: $$Liquids: T \uparrow \implies μ \downarrow, \, ν \downarrow$$ $$Gases: T \uparrow \implies μ \uparrow, \, ν \uparrow \quad (since ρ ∝ 1/T at constant P)$$
  • Sutherland's Constant for Air: $S ≈ 110.4 K$
  • Reference Value for Water at $20^\circC$: $μ ≈ 1.0 × 10-3 Pa·s = 1.0 cP$
  • Reference Value for Air at $20^\circC$: $μ ≈ 1.8 × 10-5 Pa·s$, $ν ≈ 1.5 × 10-5 m^2/s$

4. Aviation Application (5–10 min)

Viscosity dictates critical parameters in aeronautical engineering and aviation operations:

1. Boundary Layer and Skin Friction Drag

When air flows over an aircraft wing, viscosity creates a thin region adjacent to the wing surface called the boundary layer, where velocity goes from zero (at the wing skin) to the free-stream velocity ($V_∞$). The shear stress at the wall ($τ_w$) causes skin friction drag, which accounts for up to $50%$ of the total drag on modern transport aircraft:

$$τ_w = μ ( dudy )y=0$$

The transition from low-drag laminar flow to high-drag turbulent flow is governed by the dimensionless Reynolds Number ($Re$), which is the ratio of inertial forces to viscous forces:

$$Re = ρ V Lμ = V Lν$$

Higher kinematic viscosity $ν$ helps damp out small turbulent perturbations, delaying boundary layer transition and helping maintain laminar flow over the airfoil.

2. Viscosity Variation with Altitude in ISA

Under the International Standard Atmosphere (ISA), the air temperature decreases linearly in the troposphere at a lapse rate of $-6.5^\circC/km$ up to $11 km$ ($36,089 ft$). According to Sutherland's Law, as temperature decreases with altitude, the dynamic viscosity ($μ$) of air decreases. However, because air density ($ρ$) drops off exponentially with altitude, the kinematic viscosity ($ν = μ/ρ$) actually increases rapidly as altitude increases. This means that at high cruise altitudes, aircraft operate in lower Reynolds number regimes than at sea level for the same velocity.

3. Aircraft Hydraulic Systems

Aviation hydraulic systems (operating flight controls, landing gear, and brakes) must function reliably across extreme temperatures (from $+135^\circC$ on the ground in hot deserts to $-54^\circC$ at high cruising altitudes). If the fluid's viscosity drops too low at high temperatures, internal leakage and pump wear occur. If it becomes too high at low temperatures, the system becomes sluggish and unresponsive.

Military and civil aviation standard fluids (such as MIL-H-5606 or synthetic fire-resistant fluids like Skydrol) are engineered with a high Viscosity Index (VI), meaning their dynamic viscosity is highly resistant to temperature fluctuations.

5. UPSC ASO Focus (10 min)

  • Dynamic Viscosity vs Pressure: While UPSC often tests standard assumptions, remember that for *gases*, $μ$ is independent of pressure ($P$) based on ideal kinetic theory, but *kinematic* viscosity ($ν = μ/ρ$) is inversely proportional to $P$ because $ρ = P/RT$.
  • Unit Conversions: UPSC routinely sets traps by combining CGS and SI units. Always convert: $$1 Poise = 0.1 N·s/m^2$$ $$1 Stoke = 10-4 m^2/s$$
  • Non-Newtonian Fluid Rheology: UPSC ASO exams frequently ask about different fluid classes modeled by the power-law equation: $$τ = A (dudy)^n + B$$
    • $n = 1, B = 0$: Newtonian Fluid (water, air).
    • $n > 1, B = 0$: Dilatant (shear-thickening, e.g., starch in water).
    • $n < 1, B = 0$: Pseudoplastic (shear-thinning, e.g., blood, paper pulp).
    • $B > 0$: Bingham Plastic (yield stress required, e.g., toothpaste, sewage sludge).

Q8. Under ideal gas assumptions, if the absolute pressure of a gas is doubled isothermally, how do its dynamic viscosity ($μ$) and kinematic viscosity ($ν$) change?

8. Error Analysis & Common Traps (5 min)

1. Dynamic vs Kinematic Viscosity Temperature Trends for Gases

Typical Mistake: Thinking that because gas density drops as temperature increases, kinematic viscosity ($ν = μ/ρ$) must decrease.

Correct Concept: For gases, $μ$ increases with temperature ($μ ∝ T0.7$), and density decreases ($ρ ∝ 1/T$ at constant pressure). Thus, $ν$ increases sharply with temperature ($ν ∝ T1.7$ to $T^2$).

2. Specific Gravity to Density Conversion Error

Typical Mistake: Using specific gravity ($S$) directly as density in $ν = μ/ρ$. For example, for an oil with $S = 0.85$ and $μ = 0.1 Pa·s$, calculating $ν = 0.1 / 0.85$.

Correct Concept: Always convert specific gravity to absolute density: $ρ = S × ρwater = 0.85 × 1000 kg/m^3 = 850 kg/m^3$. Thus, $ν = 0.1 / 850 = 1.176 × 10-4 m^2/s$.

3. Poise and Stokes SI Conversion Traps

Typical Mistake: Multiplying Poise by 10 to get $Pa·s$ (e.g. thinking $10 Poise = 100 Pa·s$).

Correct Concept: Remember that $1 Pa·s = 10 Poise$. Therefore, to convert Poise to SI, you must **divide** by 10 ($1 Poise = 0.1 Pa·s$). Similarly, $1 Stoke = 10-4 m^2/s$ (not $10-6$).

4. Concentric Cylinder Viscometer Clearance Gap

Typical Mistake: Using the difference in diameter ($Douter - Dinner$) directly as the clearance gap $h$ when calculating velocity gradient $du/dy = U/h$.

Correct Concept: The radial clearance gap $h$ is half of the diametrical clearance: $h = Douter - Dinner2$.
