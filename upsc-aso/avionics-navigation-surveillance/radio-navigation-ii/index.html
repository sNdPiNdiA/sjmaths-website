ILS: Localizer, Glide Slope & Marker Beacon Architecture

VHF / UHF Guidance

1. Formal Technical Definition

Instrument Landing System (ILS): A ground-based radio navigation system that provides precision lateral (Localizer) and vertical (Glide Slope) guidance to an aircraft approaching and landing on a runway, supplemented by VHF marker beacons (or DME) to indicate distance along the approach path. The system operates on the principle of Amplitude Modulation (AM) depth comparison at 90 Hz and 150 Hz audio frequencies.

2. Basic Concept & Physical Mechanism

The ILS relies on electromagnetic wave interference and spatial radiation pattern modulation. For the Localizer (LLZ), situated at the stop-end of the runway, a carrier frequency in the VHF band (108.10 MHz to 111.95 MHz, odd tenths) is modulated by two distinct audio tones: 90 Hz and 150 Hz. Two separate directional antennas radiate overlapping lobes. The left lobe (when viewed from the approach end) is predominantly modulated at 90 Hz, while the right lobe is modulated at 150 Hz. Along the extended runway centerline, the modulation depths of the 90 Hz and 150 Hz signals are exactly equal (Difference in Depth of Modulation, DDM = 0).

For the Glide Slope (G/S), located beside the touchdown zone, transmissions occur in the UHF band (329.15 MHz to 335.00 MHz) paired with the Localizer channel. It creates a vertical descent plane typically set at 3.0° above the horizontal. The upper lobe is modulated at 90 Hz and the lower lobe at 150 Hz using captured radiation and sideband techniques.

Marker Beacons operate on a fixed carrier frequency of 75 MHz, modulated by specific audio tones (Outer Marker: 400 Hz dash keying; Middle Marker: 1300 Hz dot/dash keying; Inner Marker: 3000 Hz dot keying) and broadcast vertically in an elliptical radiation cone to provide distance checkpoints.

3. Mathematical Derivation & Equations

// Combined AM Modulated Signal Equation for Localizer/Glide Slope:

$$E(t) = E_c \left[ 1 + m_1 \cos(\omega_1 t) + m_2 \cos(\omega_2 t) \right] \cos(\omega_c t)$$

Where $E_c$ is carrier amplitude, $\omega_c = 2\pi f_c$ (carrier frequency), $m_1, m_2$ are modulation indices for $\omega_1 = 2\pi(90\text{ Hz})$ and $\omega_2 = 2\pi(150\text{ Hz})$.

// Difference in Depth of Modulation (DDM):

$$\text{DDM} = |m_{90} - m_{150}|$$

The airborne receiver detects DDM to drive the Course Deviation Indicator (CDI). Full scale deflection (FSD) occurs at specific DDM thresholds (e.g., Localizer course width DDM = 0.155 at runway threshold, corresponding to $\pm 2.5^\circ$ angular displacement).

6. Aircraft Application & Air Safety Officer Connection

In modern commercial transport aircraft, dual ILS receivers feed Flight Control Computers (FCC) and Autoland systems. Air Safety Officers investigating ILS approach deviations must examine receiver flag behaviors, antenna masking, and ground-based multipath interference caused by vehicle or aircraft movement in ILS critical and sensitive areas.

DGCA CAR Section 2, Series I, Part VII Compliance: Ground and airborne ILS equipment must satisfy ICAO Annex 10 volume 1 standards. Air Traffic Services (ATS) must enforce strict ground holding points and prohibit vehicle transit through ILS critical areas when instrument meteorological conditions (IMC) prevail or when Cat II/III operations are in progress to prevent beam bends and false glide path captures.

7. UPSC Traps & Common Misconceptions

Trap 1: UPSC setters frequently confuse the modulation frequencies. Remember: 90 Hz is ALWAYS on the Left (Localizer) and Top (Glide Slope), while 150 Hz is on the Right (Localizer) and Bottom (Glide Slope).

Trap 2: Assuming ILS localizer frequencies share the entire VOR band. Localizer frequencies occupy only the odd tenths of the 108.0–112.0 MHz sub-band (e.g., 108.1, 108.3), whereas VOR occupies both odd and even tenths.

8. Step-by-Step Worked Numerical

// Problem Statement:

An aircraft on an ILS localizer approach measures a 90 Hz tone modulation index $m_{90} = 0.22$ and a 150 Hz tone modulation index $m_{150} = 0.14$. Calculate the Difference in Depth of Modulation (DDM) and determine whether the aircraft is to the left or right of the runway centerline.

Given: $m_{90} = 0.22$, $m_{150} = 0.14$

Formula: $\text{DDM} = |m_{90} - m_{150}|$

Calculation:

$$\text{DDM} = |0.22 - 0.14| = 0.08$$

Operational Interpretation:

Since $m_{90} > m_{150}$, the 90 Hz tone dominates. Because 90 Hz corresponds to the left lobe of the localizer antenna pattern, the aircraft is displaced to the LEFT of the runway centerline, and the Course Deviation Indicator (CDI) needle will deflect to the RIGHT to command a right turn.

Subtopic 2 of 3

DME Substitution for Marker Beacons and Locator NDBs

UHF Ranging / Slant Range

1. Formal Technical Definition

DME Substitution: The authorized operational procedure of utilizing Distance Measuring Equipment (DME) slant-range distance information in lieu of Outer Markers (OM), Middle Markers (MM), compass locators, or step-down fixes during instrument approach procedures, provided the DME facility is co-located with the ILS facility or properly charted.

2. Basic Concept & Physical Mechanism

Traditional ILS approaches relied on VHF marker beacons pointing vertically upwards to confirm specific distance fixes along the final approach track. However, marker beacon reception is prone to propagation anomalies and provides only discrete binary triggers (passing or not passing). Distance Measuring Equipment (DME), operating in the 960–1215 MHz UHF band through interrogator-transponder pulse pair exchange, provides continuous distance readout in nautical miles (NM) and tenths of NM.

In DME substitution, pilots or Flight Management Systems (FMS) map DME slant range directly to the required threshold distances (e.g., OM fix at 5.0 NM from threshold). Crucially, the airborne DME measures slant range ($R_s$), which includes the hypotenuse of the aircraft's altitude above ground level ($h$) and horizontal ground distance ($d$).

3. Mathematical Derivation & Equations

// Slant Range vs Horizontal Ground Distance Correction:

$$R_s = \sqrt{d^2 + h^2}$$

Where $R_s$ = DME Slant Range, $d$ = Horizontal ground distance from facility, $h$ = Aircraft height above the transponder antenna elevation.

// When close to the station (e.g. at altitude $h$ when $d$ is small), slant range error becomes significant (Slant Range Error = $h$ in feet converted to nautical miles):

$$\text{Error} \approx \frac{h (\text{in feet})}{6076.12}$$

6. Aircraft Application & Air Safety Officer Connection

Flight decks utilize DME distance as a primary cross-check for ILS approach fixes. In safety investigations involving controlled flight into terrain (CFIT) during non-precision or ILS back-course approaches, ASO officers verify whether DME distance was correctly interpreted, accounting for slant range error overhead the station (where DME reads aircraft altitude in NM rather than zero).

DGCA Airworthiness & Operations Directive: Instrument approach charts must explicitly state "DME Required" or "DME Substitution Authorized for OM". Flight crews must verify DME receiver lock-on to the correct paired ILS frequency before commencing descent.

7. UPSC Traps & Common Misconceptions

Trap 1: Believing DME measures true horizontal ground distance. DME always measures slant range. Directly overhead a co-located VOR/DME at 6,000 feet AGL, the DME will indicate approximately 1.0 NM (6,000 / 6,076), not 0.0 NM.

Trap 2: Assuming DME can substitute for an ILS localizer or glide slope. DME substitutes for marker beacons and distance fixes, never for angular lateral/vertical guidance.

8. Step-by-Step Worked Numerical

// Problem Statement:

An aircraft on an ILS approach with a co-located DME is at an altitude of 3,000 feet AGL. The airborne DME indicates a slant range of 5.50 Nautical Miles (NM). Calculate the true horizontal ground distance ($d$) from the runway threshold antenna.

Given: Slant Range $R_s = 5.50\text{ NM}$, Altitude $h = 3,000\text{ ft}$

Convert altitude to Nautical Miles ($1\text{ NM} = 6,076.12\text{ ft}$):

$$h (\text{in NM}) = \frac{3000}{6076.12} = 0.4937\text{ NM}$$

Formula: $R_s^2 = d^2 + h^2 \implies d = \sqrt{R_s^2 - h^2}$

Calculation:

$$d = \sqrt{(5.50)^2 - (0.4937)^2} = \sqrt{30.25 - 0.2437} = \sqrt{30.0063} = 5.478\text{ NM}$$

Operational Interpretation:

The true horizontal distance is 5.48 NM. While the slant range error is relatively minor at 5.5 NM (~0.02 NM), it becomes significant at lower altitudes close to touchdown, requiring flight crews and ASO incident investigators to account for altitude geometry in slant-range error analysis.

ILS Approach Categories: Concepts, RVR, and Decision Heights

Cat I / II / III Operations

1. Formal Technical Definition

Precision Approach Categories (ICAO Annex 14 / PANS-OPS): A standardized classification of instrument landing capabilities based on the lowest operating weather minima—specifically Decision Height (DH) and Runway Visual Range (RVR)—categorized into CAT I, CAT II, CAT III A, CAT III B, and CAT III C, requiring certified airborne autopilot/flight director systems and ground facility integrity monitoring.

2. Basic Concept & Physical Mechanism

As meteorological visibility deteriorates due to fog, low cloud base, or precipitation, human visual reference becomes insufficient to safely align and flare an aircraft during landing. ILS Approach Categories progressively replace human visual cues with automated electronic guidance and monitoring redundancy.

CAT I: Provides guidance down to a Decision Height (DH) not less than 200 feet and Runway Visual Range (RVR) not less than 550 meters (or visibility 800 meters). Flown manually or via Flight Director.

CAT II: Provides precision guidance down to a DH not less than 100 feet and RVR not less than 300 meters. Requires dual redundant autopilots or fail-passive/fail-operational autoland systems.

CAT III: Subdivided into IIIa (DH below 50 ft or no DH, RVR not less than 175 m), IIIb (DH below 50 ft or no DH, RVR down to 50 m), and IIIc (No DH, no RVR limits, requiring fully automated landing and rollout).

3. Mathematical Derivation & Equations

// Time to Decision Height Calculation during Final Approach Descent:

$$t_{\text{DH}} = \frac{H_{\text{initial}} - \text{DH}}{R_v}$$

Where $H_{\text{initial}}$ is initial approach fix altitude, $\text{DH}$ is decision height, and $R_v$ is vertical descent rate (typically $\approx 700\text{ to } 800\text{ ft/min}$ for a 3.0° glide path at 140 knots ground speed).

// Vertical Descent Rate formula ($V_v$):

$$V_v = V_g \times \tan(\theta)$$

Where $V_g$ = ground speed, $\theta = 3.0^\circ$ glide slope angle.

6. Aircraft Application & Air Safety Officer Connection

Air Safety Officers evaluating low-visibility operations (LVOP) must review operator certification under DGCA CAR Ops, aircraft maintenance logs for autoland redundancy, and aerodrome lighting (ALCMS, centerline lights, touchdown zone lights). Runway incursions during low visibility represent one of the highest aviation safety risks.

DGCA CAR Section 8 Compliance: Operators must hold specific approval for CAT II/III operations. Flight crews must undergo recurrent simulator training, and aerodromes must maintain CAT II/III ILS ground transmitter integrity with automatic switchover to standby transmitters upon primary failure.

7. UPSC Traps & Common Misconceptions

Trap 1: Confusing Decision Height (DH) with Minimum Descent Altitude (MDA). DH is used in precision approaches (ILS/GLS) and is timed/sensed relative to threshold elevation, whereas MDA is used in non-precision approaches as a hard floor below which descent is prohibited without visual contact.

Trap 2: Assuming CAT IIIc is widely operational globally. While technically defined in ICAO manuals, CAT IIIc (zero RVR landing and taxi without external visual cues) is rarely implemented commercially due to ground taxiway guidance limitations.

8. Step-by-Step Worked Numerical

// Problem Statement:

An aircraft conducts a CAT II ILS approach on a standard 3.0° glide path at a ground speed of 135 knots. The Decision Height (DH) is set to 100 feet above the runway threshold elevation. Calculate the approximate time remaining from reaching DH to touchdown on the runway, and calculate the vertical descent rate.

Given: DH = 100 ft, Ground Speed $V_g = 135\text{ knots}$, Glide path angle $\theta = 3.0^\circ$

Convert ground speed to feet per second ($1\text{ knot} = 1.6878\text{ ft/s}$):

$$V_g (\text{ft/s}) = 135 \times 1.6878 = 227.85\text{ ft/s}$$

Calculate vertical descent rate ($V_v$):

$$V_v = V_g (\text{ft/s}) \times \sin(3.0^\circ) = 227.85 \times 0.05234 = 11.92\text{ ft/s} = 715.3\text{ ft/min}$$

Calculate time from 100 ft DH to touchdown ($t$):

$$t = \frac{\text{DH}}{V_v (\text{ft/s})} = \frac{100}{11.92} = 8.39\text{ seconds}$$

Operational Interpretation:

From the Decision Height of 100 ft, the flight crew or autoland system has approximately 8.4 seconds before touchdown. This brief window underscores why fail-operational automation or immediate go-around execution is mandatory if visual reference is not established at DH.

Case Study 1: False Glide Slope Capture

Boeing 747 Accident / Incident Analysis (ILS Harmonic Capture)

Historically, aircraft conducting ILS approaches have occasionally captured a false glide slope (harmonics occurring above the primary 3.0° glide path, such as at 9° or 12°). In several incidents, flight crews initiated descents from high altitudes believing they were on the true glide slope, resulting in near-CFIT events.

Safety Lesson: Mandatory cross-checking of outer marker crossing altitudes and DME distance vs. altitude check tables on approach plates. Modern avionics incorporate glide slope lock-out circuitry below certain altitudes to prevent false lobe capture.

DGCA CAR Reference: CAR Section 2 Series I Part VII mandates flight inspection periodicity and ground transmitter integrity monitoring to suppress spurious sideband radiation.

Case Study 2: Low-Visibility Runway Incursion

CAT III Ground Operations & Critical Area Protection

During low-visibility operations (RVR < 550 m), an aircraft landing or taxiing can reflect ILS localizer and glide slope signals if it enters the ILS critical area or sensitive area. Vehicle movements by ground maintenance staff have historically distorted the DDM balance, causing autopilot hard-overs during final flare.

Safety Lesson: Strict enforcement of surface movement radar (SMR), stop bars, and automated holding point lights. Air Traffic Control (ATC) must halt all vehicle traffic near ILS critical areas prior to aircraft passing the Outer Marker in CAT II/III conditions.

DGCA CAR Reference: CAR Section 8 Operations / Aerodrome Standards mandate rigorous low-visibility procedures (LVPs) and joint ATS-Operator coordination.

UPSC Interview Corner: Model Answers for Air Safety Officer Aspirants

Q1: As an Air Safety Officer, what immediate action would you recommend if multiple flight crews report localizer beam bends at a major international airport during instrument meteorological conditions (IMC)? ▼

Model Answer: "First, I would coordinate immediately with Air Traffic Services (ATS) and the Communications, Navigation, and Surveillance (CNS) department to restrict vehicle movement and aircraft taxiing within the ILS critical and sensitive areas. Multipath reflections caused by large aircraft or ground vehicles parked near the localizer or glide slope antennae frequently induce beam bends. Second, I would dispatch a CNS engineering team to perform ground field strength and DDM integrity checks, and review flight inspection logs. If beam integrity cannot be verified, the precision approach procedure must be temporarily suspended or downgraded to a non-precision approach until calibration is restored."

Q2: Explain the engineering rationale behind requiring fail-operational autoland systems for CAT III operations compared to fail-passive systems for CAT II. ▼

Model Answer: "In CAT II operations, the Decision Height is 100 feet or higher, and RVR is at least 300 meters. If a single autopilot (fail-passive system) experiences a sudden failure or hard-over just above DH, the human pilot has sufficient visibility and altitude to disengage the autopilot, take manual control, and execute a safe go-around. Conversely, in CAT III operations (especially CAT IIIb/c), visibility is below 175 meters or zero, and DH is extremely low or non-existent. In the event of an autopilot failure, the human pilot cannot see the runway to safely recover manually. Therefore, a *fail-operational* system with redundant channels ensures that even if one autopilot fails, the remaining operational channel continues to guide the aircraft safely to touchdown and rollout without disruption."
