Learning map

What this chapter is teaching

This chapter connects weighted averages, mixtures, percentages, tables and charts. The central question is: when quantities are combined, which values should count more?

CombineFind a mean using every quantity and its size.
WeightGive larger groups or more important parts their correct influence.
UpdateRevise an average when new data enters or leaves.
VisualiseChoose charts that show totals, parts or percentages clearly.
Study tip: Before calculating, ask what the weights are: number of items, volume, marks ratio, frequency or total size.
Coverage check: Notes 1–9 cover the methods needed for Exercises 10.1–10.5 and the end-of-chapter questions. For chart-based questions, read the original figure for exact approximate values; the supplied text did not include those images.

1Ordinary mean and combined mean

The mean is total value divided by total number of values.

Mean = total of all values / number of values

When two groups have different sizes, do not average their averages directly. First reconstruct each group total.

Combined mean: If group A has mean a and n items, and group B has mean b and m items, then
Combined mean = (n × a + m × b) / (n + m)
For Exercise 10.1 Q1, the expression is (30 × 72 + 25 × 76) / (30 + 25).
Common trap: (72 + 76) / 2 is correct only when the two groups have equal numbers of students.

2Weighted average: the main tool

A weighted average gives value xi a weight wi. The weight may be a quantity, frequency, volume, number of people or a prescribed importance ratio.

Weighted average = Σ(wi × xi) / Σwi
  • Equal weights give the ordinary mean.
  • Weights do not have to add to 100; only their relative sizes matter.
  • Doubling every weight does not change the weighted average because the common factor cancels.
  • Adding the same number to every weight usually changes the weighted average because the old balance changes.
Quick check: A weighted average must lie between the smallest and largest value being averaged.

3Mixtures, concentration and purity

Convert each concentration into an amount of the substance being measured, add those amounts, then divide by the total mixture quantity.

Mixture concentration = Σ(quantity × concentration) / Σquantity
  • For equal quantities, the concentration is the ordinary mean of the concentrations.
  • For different volumes, volume is the weight. Do not average percentages without using the volumes.
  • For gold purity, k varṇa means k/16 of the piece is gold. Combine the gold amounts, then divide by total units and convert back to varṇa.
  • For salt and sugar in one mixture, track salt and sugar separately; one percentage cannot describe both.
Exercise patterns: fertilizer Q2 uses equal weights; gold Q3 uses weights 9, 5 and 17; kashayam Q5 uses volumes 100, 200 and 300 mL; salinity questions use litres as weights.

4Marks, profit and ratios

First make values comparable, then apply the weights.

  • For marks out of different totals, convert each result to a percentage before applying the ratio.
  • If the ratio is 3:4:5, use weights 3, 4 and 5. Dividing by 12 gives the weighted average percentage.
  • For profit percentage on total sales, combine profit amounts and sales amounts: total profit ÷ total sales × 100.
  • For ratings with weights, find each metric’s average first, then combine the metric averages with their prescribed weights.
Exercise 10.2 reminder: 35 out of 50, 44 out of 60 and 80 out of 100 must not be combined as if they were already percentages.

5How an average changes

Keep the old total visible instead of starting from the old average alone.

New mean = (old number × old mean + added value) / (old number + 1)
  • If the new value is above the old mean, the mean rises.
  • If the new value is below the old mean, the mean falls.
  • If the new value equals the old mean, the mean stays unchanged.
  • When values are removed, subtract their total and subtract their count.

This method applies to the stork distance, run rate, gold purchases, share prices and changing langur groups.

6Dilution and possible target values

When a solution is diluted with pure water, the amount of spice or salt stays the same while the total volume increases.

New concentration = amount of solute / new total volume
  • To reduce 8% spice to three-fourths of its original concentration, the target is 6%.
  • A mixture of two concentrations must have a concentration between them, including the endpoints.
  • It cannot have a concentration lower than both sources unless a still-purer source is added.
  • Set up an equation using solute amount before and after mixing; do not subtract percentages directly.

7Unknown counts and frequency tables

Use a variable for each unknown group size and let the total count provide a second equation.

Overall mean = Σ(value × frequency) / Σfrequency
  • For the langur table, let x and y be the male and female counts. Then x + y = 60.
  • The overall-weight equation is (16.5x + 13.8y) / 60 = 14.925.
  • For ratings, multiply each rating by its frequency before adding.
  • After a person or animal enters or leaves, update both the total and the count.
Reasoning before calculation: If a group’s mean is above the overall mean, that group must pull the combined mean upward; this helps check whether an answer is sensible.
Practice10.3 Q6–Q8End Q2, Q14–Q15

8Tables, stacked bars and 100% stacked bars

  • Use an ordinary stacked bar chart when comparing actual totals and the parts that make each total.
  • Use a 100% stacked bar chart when comparing composition or shares, even when totals are different.
  • Stacked bar lengths answer “how much in total?” and segment lengths answer “how much in each part?”
  • 100% stacked bars answer “what fraction or percentage of each total?” but hide differences in total size.
  • For a table, label the categories, keep one scale, and make each segment proportional to its value.
Chart-reading checklist: Read the title, units, scale, legend, total bar length and segment boundaries before making an inference.
Practice10.410.5End Q7–Q13

9Percentages, shares and inferences

  • A percentage describes a share, not necessarily a count. To compare counts, the total population is also needed.
  • To find a combined national percentage, use each region’s count as a weight; percentages alone are not enough.
  • If all groups decrease, their shares may increase, decrease or stay equal depending on their relative rates of decrease.
  • A 100% stacked chart can compare fractions of time or ratings, but it cannot tell which athlete finished first without total times.
  • When a graph is approximate, report an approximate value and explain what was read.
Practice10.4End Q7–Q13, Q16

10Final solving routine

1. Name the valuesWrite what each number measures and its unit.
2. Find the weightsLook for counts, volumes, frequencies or ratios.
3. Build the totalUse value × weight, and keep separate substances separate.
4. Check the resultCheck units, range, direction and whether the chart answers the question.
One-line summary: Combine totals first, divide by total weight, then choose a visualisation that matches the question.

Exercise Set 10.1 — Solutions

Combined averages, equal quantities, purity and concentration.

1The average score of students on a test in Section A is 72 and that of students in Section B is 76. What is the combined average of both the sections given that Section A has 30 students and Section B has 25 students?
Answer / method
Combined average = (30×72 + 25×76) / (30+25) = 4060/55 ≈ 73.82.
2A farmer mixes three equal quantities of fertilisers. The first one contains 1/10 nitrogen, the second contains 9/50 nitrogen, and the third contains 3/60 nitrogen. What is the fraction of nitrogen in the mixture?
Answer / method
The quantities are equal, so use the ordinary mean: (1/10 + 9/50 + 3/60) / 3 = 11/100.
3(Śrīdharācārya, Pāṭīgaṇita, c. 750 CE) In ancient India, Varṇa was the measure of gold purity. A purity of 16 varṇa meant pure gold; in general, a purity of k varṇa meant that the gold-alloy was k/16 gold and the rest impurities. (Now the term used is karat; 16 Varna = 24 karat.) Suppose a goldsmith melts together three pieces of gold: 9 units at 12 varṇa, 5 units at 10 varṇa, and 17 units at 11 varṇa. Find the purity in varṇa of the combined gold.
Answer / method
Purity = (9×12 + 5×10 + 17×11) / (9+5+17) = 345/31 ≈ 11.13 varṇa.
4The average rainfall per day in the months of May, June, and July in a certain location are 3.5 mm, 10 mm and 8.7 mm respectively. Write an expression that gives their combined average.
Answer / method
Combined average = (3.5 + 10 + 8.7) / 3 = 7.4 mm per day.
5Calculate the concentration of spice mix in these two scenarios. (i) A 100 mL kashayam/kadha with 5% spice mix, a 200 mL one with 10% spice mix, and a 300 mL one with 15% spice mix are combined. (ii) A 300 mL kashayam/kadha with 5% spice mix, a 200 mL one with 10% spice mix, and a 100 mL one with 15% spice mix are mixed.
Answer / method
(i) (100×5 + 200×10 + 300×15) / 600 = 70/6 ≈ 11.67%; (ii) (300×5 + 200×10 + 100×15) / 600 = 50/6 ≈ 8.33%.

Exercise Set 10.2 — Solutions

Weighted marks and converting scores to percentages.

1Savitri's marks in Kashmiri are as follows: 35 out of 50 in internal tests, 44 out of 60 in the project, and 80 out of 100 in the final exam. The annual percentage score is calculated by combining the internals, project, and final exam in the ratio 3:4:5. Which of the following expression(s) gives her annual score (as a percentage) in Kashmiri?
  • (i) (35 × 3 + 44 × 4 + 80 × 5) / (3 + 4 + 5)
  • (ii) [ (35/100) × 3 + (44/100) × 4 + (80/100) × 5 ] / (3 + 4 + 5)
  • (iii) [ (35/50) × 3 + (44/60) × 4 + (80/100) × 5 ] / (3 + 4 + 5) × 100
  • (iv) [ ((35/50) × 100) × 3 + ((44/60) × 100) × 4 + ((80/100) × 100) × 5 ] / (3 + 4 + 5)
Answer / method
Expressions (iii) and (iv) are correct. Each score must first be converted to a percentage, then weighted in the ratio 3:4:5.

Exercise Set 10.3 — Solutions

Updating means, mixtures, dilution, weighted ratings and frequency tables.

1A stationery shop owner made ₹8000 selling books, of which 30% is the profit amount, and ₹1000 selling book covers, of which 50% is the profit amount. What is the percentage of profit on the total sales?
Answer / method
Total profit = 30% of 8000 + 50% of 1000 = 2400 + 500 = ₹2900. Profit percentage = 2900/9000 × 100 ≈ 32.22%.
2A white stork's migration is tracked. The average daily distance travelled, calculated over 20 days, is 44.5 km. On the 21st day, it flew 55 km. What is the average daily distance travelled over these 21 days? Make a guess before you calculate.
Answer / method
Old total distance = 20×44.5 = 890 km. New total = 890+55 = 945 km. Average over 21 days = 945/21 = 45 km.
3A 600 mL solution with 5% salt is mixed with a 300 mL solution with 8% sugar. What are the concentrations of salt and sugar in the mixture?
  • (i) Salt: 5%, Sugar: 8%
  • (ii) Salt: 13%, Sugar: 3%
  • (iii) Salt: 6%, Sugar: 6%
  • (iv) Salt: 5.55%, Sugar: 8.88%
  • (v) Salt: 3.33%, Sugar: 2.67%
  • (vi) Salt: 4.1%, Sugar: 7.08%
Answer / method
Salt = 600×5% = 30 mL and sugar = 300×8% = 24 mL. In 900 mL, salt = 3.33% and sugar = 2.67%. Correct option: (v).
4At a panipuri (golgappa) stall, the concentration of spice in the pani (spiced water) was 8%. Many customers complained that it was too spicy. What quantity of regular water should be mixed into the 10 litres of pani so that the spice level is reduced to (3/4)th of the original concentration?
Answer / method
Original spice = 10×8% = 0.8 L. Target concentration = 6%. Required total volume = 0.8/0.06 = 13 1/3 L, so add 13 1/3 − 10 = 3 1/3 L of water.
5A physical fitness evaluation is being undertaken. The final marks are calculated by combining the marks for strength, flexibility, and agility in the ratio 4:5:6. Rashi has scored 60, 65, and 70. Keerthi has scored 55, 65, and 75 respectively. (i) Find out whose total is more without calculating. (ii) What are their final marks?
Answer / method
Keerthi has the higher weighted result: her advantage in agility outweighs her disadvantage in strength. Rashi = (60×4+65×5+70×6)/15 = 65 2/3. Keerthi = (55×4+65×5+75×6)/15 = 66 1/3.

1In cricket, the run rate is the average number of runs scored per over. In a T20 match, a team scored 6 runs in the first over making the run rate 6. (i) In the second over they scored 12 runs. What is the run rate now? (ii) After Over 19, their run rate was 6. What is the run rate after 20 overs given the team made 12 runs in the last over?
Answer / method
(i) After 2 overs, total runs = 6+12 = 18, so run rate = 18/2 = 9. (ii) After 19 overs, runs = 19×6 = 114. Add 12 runs: 126/20 = 6.3 runs per over.
2Five friends who play badminton surveyed the city and collected the price of shuttlecocks across brands and types (Nylon and Feather). Suppose these are aᵧ, aₛ, aₖ, aₚ, ag respectively. Write an expression that gives the combined average. (ii) Suppose an, af are the average prices of the nylon shuttlecocks and the feather shuttlecocks, respectively. Write an expression that gives the average price of a shuttlecock. The five personal averages must be weighted by the numbers of prices: (6aᵧ + 4aₛ + 5aₖ + 4aₚ + 4a_g)/23. There are 13 nylon and 10 feather prices, so the type-based average is (13aₙ + 10a_f)/23. They are equal when both methods use exactly the same 23 observations.
3Shreyas holds 25 shares of a company at an average price of ₹150 and Vaishnavi holds 5 shares of the same company at an average price of ₹150. Answer / method
Mean depth = (4×9 + 5×7 + 6×7 + 7×3 + 8×2)/(4+5+6+7+8) = 150/30 = 5 hastas.

13Small-group project: Make a group of 3–4 students. Choose one scenario to design a custom rating scheme by assigning appropriate weights: (a) shopping experience at a cloth store, (b) travel experience in a bus, (c) tourism experience of a nearby tourist spot, (d) clinic/hospital experience. (i) Discuss and arrive at 4–6 aspects to rate. For each aspect chosen, justify why it matters for an overall experience. (ii) Discuss and decide the relative weights and justify. (iii) Collect or imagine ratings from at least 10 people on a scale of 1–5 (5 being very good). (iv) Compute each individual rating. Compute the overall average rating across individuals. (v) Make a 100% stacked bar chart using your data. (vi) Write a short note on the observations and inferences, what your rating system captures well and what it misses.
Answer / method
Open project. Choose 4–6 aspects, justify their weights, collect at least 10 ratings, calculate each weighted rating and the overall mean, then draw a 100% stacked chart. State what the scheme captures and what it may miss.
14Whole class project: Each student shares the average age of their family and the number of family members. Discuss among the class and come up with a way to find out the average age of all the families of the class.
Answer / method
Use a weighted average by family size: combined average age = Σ(family average age × family members) / Σ(family members). Weighting by family size prevents a small family and a large family from having the same influence on the average age of all people.
*15Given some data with corresponding weights, what would happen to the weighted average if all the weights are increased by a constant value, say 1? If required, experiment with some data. What do you observe? Justify your answer using algebra.
Answer / method
Adding 1 to every weight generally changes the result: new average = [Σ(wᵢxᵢ)+Σxᵢ]/[Σwᵢ+n]. It stays unchanged only in special cases, such as when the old weighted average already equals the ordinary mean.
*16A farm has some cows, sheep, and chickens. Last year the cows made up 60%, the sheep 25%, and the chickens 15%. There was a decrease in the number of all three animals' population over the year. Choose the possibilities for the change in their respective shares of the population — (i) % of cows decreased, % of sheep decreased, % of chickens decreased. (ii) % of cows increased, % of sheep increased, % of chickens increased. (iii) % of cows remained the same, % of sheep remained the same, % of chickens remained the same. (iv) % of cows decreased, % of sheep increased, % of chickens remained the same. (v) % of cows increased, % of sheep increased, % of chickens decreased.
Answer / method
All three shares cannot decrease together and all three cannot increase together because shares must total 100%. Possible choices are (iii), (iv) and (v), depending on the relative rates of decrease of the three animal populations.
