1Ordinary mean and combined mean (Average of Averages)

In earlier grades, we saw how the average (arithmetic mean) conveys the ‘centre’ of given data:

Mean = total of all values / number of values

When combining multiple data sets, a fundamental question arises: can we simply average the individual averages? Let us examine the textbook examples.

Example 1: Trainees at a Badminton Academy

In a badminton academy, there is a group of 11 trainees — 8 seniors and 3 juniors. Their individual heights and average heights are given below:

GroupHeights (in cm)CountAverage Height
Seniors165, 169, 164, 167, 170, 159, 164, 1668165.5 cm
Juniors146, 149, 1533149.33 cm

Two students calculate the overall average height of the whole group in two different ways:

Method 1 (Simple average of averages):
(165.5 + 149.33) / 2 = 314.83 / 2 = 157.415 cm

Method 2 (Sum of all individual heights ÷ 11):
(165 + 169 + 164 + 167 + 170 + 159 + 164 + 166 + 146 + 149 + 153) / 11 = 1772 / 11 ≈ 161.09 cm

Whose calculation is correct? Method 2 gives the correct average height of the whole group because the mean is the sum of all 11 heights divided by 11.

Think and Reflect — Why did Method 1 not work?
Method 1 does not work because it treats the senior and junior groups equally (giving each a weight of 50%), but there are 8 seniors and only 3 juniors! The seniors heavily outnumber the juniors, so the group average must be pulled much closer to the seniors' average (165.5 cm).
Shreyas's Calculation:
[(165.5 × 8) + (149.33 × 3)] / (8 + 3) = (1324 + 448) / 11 = 1772 / 11 ≈ 161.09 cm
Notice why this works: 165.5 × 8 = 1324 cm recovers the sum of heights of all 8 seniors, and 149.33 × 3 ≈ 448 cm recovers the sum of heights of all 3 juniors. Dividing the combined total by (8 + 3) gives the exact combined mean!

Generalisation & Algebraic Derivation: Combined Mean of Two Collections

Suppose we have two collections of data:

  1. Collection 1 has n values: x1, x2, …, xn with average a.
    By definition: (x1 + x2 + … + xn) / n = a &implies; Sum1 = a × n.
  2. Collection 2 has m values: y1, y2, …, ym with average b.
    By definition: (y1 + y2 + … + ym) / m = b &implies; Sum2 = b × m.
  3. Combined Average:
    Combined Mean = (Sum1 + Sum2) / (n + m) = (a × n + b × m) / (n + m)

Theorem — When does simple average of averages work?
For 3 collections with averages a, b, c and sizes p, q, r:
Combined Mean = (a×p + b×q + c×r) / (p + q + r)
When all three collections have equal size (i.e. p = q = r = 5 weekdays):
(a×p + b×p + c×p) / (p + p + p) = [(a + b + c)×p] / (3p) = (a + b + c) / 3
Rule: When the sizes of all collections are equal, we can simply add their averages and divide by the number of collections. If collection sizes differ, we MUST use the weighted mean.

2Weighted average: the main tool

The weighted average (or weighted mean) is a generalisation of the arithmetic mean where each value is multiplied by a measure of how important, frequent, or large it is:

Weighted Mean x̄ = (w1x1 + w2x2 + … + wnxn) / (w1 + w2 + … + wn) = Σ(wi × xi) / Σwi

Here, the prefix ‘weighted’ does not necessarily refer to physical weight, but abstract weights (sizes, volumes, counts, credits, frequencies, or priority ratios).

Historical Origins in Ancient India:
  • Brahmagupta in his seminal treatise Brāhmasphuṭasiddhānta (c. 628 CE) formulated this exact principle to find the mean depth x̄ of an irregularly shaped pit: if the pit has depth x1 for length w1, depth x2 for length w2, …, then the average depth is given by the weighted mean formula.
  • Śrīdharācārya (c. 750 CE) in Pāṭīga logic;ita used the weighted arithmetic mean to calculate the purity of mixed gold-alloys (the ancient Indian varṇa system, identical to modern karat calculations).
  • By the 1700s, the concept gained widespread prominence across Europe, becoming the cornerstone of modern statistics.
Equal weightsWhen w1 = w2 = … = wn = w, the weights cancel out: wΣxi / (n×w) = Σxi / n, giving the ordinary arithmetic mean.
Doubling weightsMultiplying all weights by a constant k > 0 leaves the weighted average completely unchanged: Σ(kwixi) / Σ(kwi) = Σ(wixi) / Σwi.
Adding a constantAdding a constant (e.g. +1) to every weight alters their relative ratios, changing the weighted average (unless all xi are already equal to the mean).
Bounding propertyThe weighted average must always lie strictly between the smallest and largest values: min(xi) ≤ x̄ ≤ max(xi).

3Mixtures, concentration and purity

When substances with different concentrations are blended, the mixture concentration is determined by the total quantity of solute divided by the total volume of mixture:

Concentration of Mixture = quantity of active ingredient in mixture / total quantity of mixture

Example 3: Mixing Equal Quantities of Lemonade

Two glasses of equal quantities of lemonade are prepared. One glass has 10% jaggery and the other has 20% jaggery. If we mix both glasses, what is the concentration of jaggery in the mixture?

Mathematical Derivation:
Let y be the quantity of lemonade in each glass.
Amount of jaggery in first glass = (10/100) × y = 0.1y
Amount of jaggery in second glass = (20/100) × y = 0.2y
Total mixture volume = y + y = 2y

Concentration = (0.1y + 0.2y) / (y + y) = 0.3y / 2y = 0.15 = 15%
Insight: Because both glasses had equal quantities, the concentration of jaggery in the combined mixture is exactly midway (15%) between 10% and 20%.

Example 4: Mixing Unequal Quantities of Lemonade

A bowl of 500 mL lemonade has 10% jaggery. A glass of 200 mL lemonade has 20% jaggery. If we mix the bowl and the glass, what is the concentration of jaggery in the mixture?

Can you estimate beforehand? Is it 15%, more, or less?
Since the bowl has a larger volume (500 mL vs 200 mL), the final concentration must be pulled closer to 10% (i.e. less than midway).

Concentration = (500 × 0.10 + 200 × 0.20) / (500 + 200) = (50 + 40) / 700 = 90 / 700 ≈ 0.1286 ≈ 12.86% (≈ 13%)
The final concentration is not the simple average (15%), but closer to 10% because the 10% mixture contributed over 71% of the total volume.

Example 5: Brass Alloy — Finding the Weight of Batch C

Brass is an alloy of copper and zinc. Batch A weighs 200 kg (70% copper). Batch B weighs 120 kg (50% copper). Batch C is 45% copper. When all three batches are combined, the resulting alloy is 55% copper. What is the weight of Batch C?

Step-by-step Solution:
  1. Let y be the weight (in kg) of Batch C.
  2. Quantity of copper in Batch A = 200 × 0.70 = 140 kg
  3. Quantity of copper in Batch B = 120 × 0.50 = 60 kg
  4. Quantity of copper in Batch C = y × 0.45 = 0.45y kg
  5. Total weight of mixture = 200 + 120 + y = (320 + y) kg
  6. Set up the concentration equation:
    0.55 = (140 + 60 + 0.45y) / (320 + y) = (200 + 0.45y) / (320 + y)
  7. Cross-multiply and solve for y:
    0.55 × (320 + y) = 200 + 0.45y
    176 + 0.55y = 200 + 0.45y
    0.55y − 0.45y = 200 − 176
    0.10y = 24 &implies; y = 24 / 0.10 = 240 kg
Answer: Batch C weighs 240 kg.

4Marks, profit and custom weights (Rating Systems)

The weighted mean is indispensable whenever different parts of data require different levels of importance or weightage (prescribed assessment ratios, store margins, or customer rating rubrics).

Example 6: Rehmat's Annual Score in Mathematics

Rehmat’s marks in Maths are as follows: 60% in internal tests, 64% in the project, and 73% in the final exam. The annual percentage score is calculated by combining internals, project, and final exam in the ratio 3 : 2 : 5. What is Rehmat’s annual score in Maths?

Equal Weightage vs. Prescribed Ratio:
  • If all modes had equal weightage, the arithmetic mean would be:
    (60 + 64 + 73) / 3 = 197 / 3 ≈ 65.67%
  • The ratio 3 : 2 : 5 indicates that internals get 3 parts, project gets 2 parts, and the final exam gets 5 parts of importance out of a total of (3 + 2 + 5 = 10) parts. This is equivalent to having 10 test scores: 60, 60, 60, 64, 64, 73, 73, 73, 73, 73.
  • Arithmetic Mean with relative weights:
    Annual Score = (60 × 3 + 64 × 2 + 73 × 5) / (3 + 2 + 5) = (180 + 128 + 365) / 10 = 673 / 10 = 67.3%
  • Expressed as weighted percentages:
    [(60/100)×3 + (64/100)×2 + (73/100)×5] / (3 + 2 + 5) = (673/100) / 10 = 0.673 = 67.3%

Real-Life Situations for Custom Weights

1. Hotel / Restaurant RatingsA customer experience depends on Food, Ambience, and Service. If assigned weights are Food : Service : Ambience = 4 : 3 : 2, and a customer rates Food = 5, Service = 3, Ambience = 4, then:
Combined rating = (5×4 + 3×3 + 4×2) / (4 + 3 + 2) = (20 + 9 + 8) / 9 = 37 / 9 ≈ 4.11 / 5.
2. Product Review RecencyA store selling portable furniture frequently upgrades its designs. To calculate the overall store rating, recent reviews (e.g. last 3 months) can be assigned twice the weightage of older reviews so current quality is accurately reflected.
Crucial Reminder for Exercise 10.2: When marks are given out of different maximum scores (e.g. 35/50, 44/60, 80/100), you must first convert each score into a percentage (or fraction of 1) before applying the weights. Combining raw marks directly without normalisation is incorrect!

5How an average changes

Keep the old total visible instead of starting from the old average alone.

New mean = (old number × old mean + added value) / (old number + 1)
  • If the new value is above the old mean, the mean rises.
  • If the new value is below the old mean, the mean falls.
  • If the new value equals the old mean, the mean stays unchanged.
  • When values are removed, subtract their total and subtract their count.

This method applies to the stork distance, run rate, gold purchases, share prices and changing langur groups.

6Dilution and possible target values

When a solution is diluted with pure water, the amount of spice or salt stays the same while the total volume increases.

New concentration = amount of solute / new total volume
  • To reduce 8% spice to three-fourths of its original concentration, the target is 6%.
  • A mixture of two concentrations must have a concentration between them, including the endpoints.
  • It cannot have a concentration lower than both sources unless a still-purer source is added.
  • Set up an equation using solute amount before and after mixing; do not subtract percentages directly.

9Percentages, shares and inferences (What Can We Infer?)

A critical statistical skill is knowing what can and cannot be inferred from percentage-based visualisations.

Example 8: Flower Blooms across Seasons (Fatima vs. Naveen)

A 100% stacked bar chart shows the proportion of flowers blooming across seasons:

  • Fatima's Garden: Summer = 50%, Monsoon = 30%, Winter = 20% (Total = 100%)
  • Naveen's Garden: Summer = 40%, Monsoon = 35%, Winter = 25% (Total = 100%)

Evaluating Statements:

Statement (i): In Fatima’s garden, there were more blooms in the summer than in the monsoon.
True — This compares seasons within the same garden. Since both percentages refer to the same total, 50% of the total is definitely greater than 30% of the total.

Statement (ii): In summer, Fatima’s garden had more blooms than Naveen’s garden.
Cannot be inferred — This compares across two different gardens. A 100% chart does not state total flowers! If Naveen had 1,000 flowers (40% = 400) and Fatima had 100 flowers (50% = 50), Naveen had far more flowers.

Statement (iii): In winter, Fatima’s garden had fewer blooms than Naveen’s garden.
Cannot be inferred — Same logic as (ii). Percentage share cannot determine absolute count without knowing group totals.

Statement (iv): The total number of flowers, across seasons, is the same in both gardens.
False / Cannot be inferred — Any two totals can be normalised to a 100% bar.
Concrete Proof: Case 1 vs. Case 2
• Case 1: Fatima has 210 blooms (Summer: 105) and Naveen has 120 blooms (Summer: 48). Here Fatima has more summer blooms.
• Case 2: Fatima has 180 blooms (Summer: 90) and Naveen has 240 blooms (Summer: 96). Here Fatima has fewer summer blooms!
Both cases produce the exact same 100% stacked bar chart, proving absolute comparisons across groups are impossible.
Think and Reflect — Conversions between Charts:
  • Stacked Bar → 100% Stacked Bar: Always possible. Calculate the total length of each bar and find each segment’s percentage.
  • 100% Stacked Bar → Stacked Bar: Impossible without external data. You cannot reconstruct absolute numbers without knowing the grand total.

10Chapter Summary — Key Takeaways

  • The weighted average (weighted mean) of x1, x2, …, xn with weights w1, w2, …, wn is given by:
    x̄ = (w1x1 + w2x2 + … + wnxn) / (w1 + w2 + … + wn)
  • An average of averages can be computed accurately using the weighted mean. The weighted mean is also used to calculate the new mean when values are added to or removed from data.
  • The weighted average is essential in calculating the concentration of mixtures and alloys.
  • Whenever different parts of data have different group sizes or levels of importance, the weighted mean must be used by assigning appropriate weights (e.g. rating systems, multi-component exam scoring).
  • Stacked bar charts are preferred over cluster bar charts when the total of each cluster is also a matter of primary interest.
  • 100% stacked bar charts, like pie charts, compare proportions/shares and not absolute values.
  • Given a stacked bar chart, a corresponding 100% stacked bar chart can always be made, but not vice versa.

Combined averages, equal quantities, purity and concentration.

1The average score of students on a test in Section A is 72 and that of students in Section B is 76. What is the combined average of both the sections given that Section A has 30 students and Section B has 25 students?
Answer / method

Step-by-step Solution:

  1. Given:
    • Section A: Number of students n = 30, Average score a = 72
    • Section B: Number of students m = 25, Average score b = 76
  2. Calculate total scores for each section:
    • Total score in Section A = 30 × 72 = 2,160
    • Total score in Section B = 25 × 76 = 1,900
  3. Combined total score:
    Total score = 2,160 + 1,900 = 4,060
  4. Total number of students:
    Total students = 30 + 25 = 55
  5. Combined Average:
    Combined Mean = (n×a + m×b) / (n + m) = 4060 / 55 = 812 / 11 ≈ 73.82
Answer: The combined average score of both sections is 73.82 (or 73 9/11).
2A farmer mixes three equal quantities of fertilisers. The first one contains 1/10 nitrogen, the second contains 9/50 nitrogen, and the third contains 3/60 nitrogen. What is the fraction of nitrogen in the mixture?
Answer / method

Step-by-step Solution:

  1. Simplify the given nitrogen fractions:
    • First fertiliser = 1/10
    • Second fertiliser = 9/50
    • Third fertiliser = 3/60 = 1/20
  2. Use the Equal Quantities Principle:
    Since the farmer mixes three equal quantities, the combined concentration is the ordinary arithmetic mean of the three fractions:
    Combined Fraction = (1/10 + 9/50 + 1/20) / 3
  3. Find a common denominator (LCM of 10, 50, 20 is 100):
    • 1/10 = 10/100
    • 9/50 = 18/100
    • 1/20 = 5/100
    Sum of fractions = (10 + 18 + 5) / 100 = 33/100
  4. Divide by 3:
    (33 / 100) ÷ 3 = 33 / (100 × 3) = 11 / 100
Answer: The fraction of nitrogen in the mixture is 11/100 (or 11%).
3(Śrīdharācārya, Pāṭīgaṇita, c. 750 CE) In ancient India, Varṇa was the measure of gold purity. A purity of 16 varṇa meant pure gold; in general, a purity of k varṇa meant that the gold-alloy was k/16 gold and the rest impurities. (Now the term used is karat; 16 Varna = 24 karat.) Suppose a goldsmith melts together three pieces of gold: 9 units at 12 varṇa, 5 units at 10 varṇa, and 17 units at 11 varṇa. Find the purity in varṇa of the combined gold.
Answer / method

Step-by-step Solution:

  1. Given:
    • Piece 1: Weight w1 = 9 units, Purity x1 = 12 varṇa
    • Piece 2: Weight w2 = 5 units, Purity x2 = 10 varṇa
    • Piece 3: Weight w3 = 17 units, Purity x3 = 11 varṇa
  2. Total weight of melted gold:
    W = 9 + 5 + 17 = 31 units
  3. Weighted sum of purity:
    Σ(wi × xi) = (9 × 12) + (5 × 10) + (17 × 11) = 108 + 50 + 187 = 345
  4. Combined Purity in varṇa:
    Combined Purity = Σ(wi × xi) / Σwi = 345 / 31 = 11 4/31 ≈ 11.13 varṇa
Answer: The purity of the combined gold is 345/31 ≈ 11.13 varṇa (equivalent to ≈ 16.69 karat).
4The average rainfall per day in the months of May, June, and July in a certain location are 3.5 mm, 10 mm and 8.7 mm respectively. Write an expression that gives their combined average.
Answer / method

Step-by-step Solution:

  1. Method A — Accounting for exact number of days in each month:
    • May has 31 days (Daily average = 3.5 mm &implies; Total rain = 3.5 × 31 mm)
    • June has 30 days (Daily average = 10 mm &implies; Total rain = 10 × 30 mm)
    • July has 31 days (Daily average = 8.7 mm &implies; Total rain = 8.7 × 31 mm)

    Combined Daily Average = (3.5 × 31 + 10 × 30 + 8.7 × 31) / (31 + 30 + 31) = (108.5 + 300 + 269.7) / 92 = 678.2 / 92 ≈ 7.37 mm/day
  2. Method B — If each month is treated as an equal unit:
    Combined Average = (3.5 + 10 + 8.7) / 3 = 22.2 / 3 = 7.4 mm/day

5Calculate the concentration of spice mix in these two scenarios. (i) A 100 mL kashayam/kadha with 5% spice mix, a 200 mL one with 10% spice mix, and a 300 mL one with 15% spice mix are combined. (ii) A 300 mL kashayam/kadha with 5% spice mix, a 200 mL one with 10% spice mix, and a 100 mL one with 15% spice mix are mixed.
Answer / method

Step-by-step Solution:

  1. Scenario (i):
    • Spice from 100 mL @ 5% = 100 × 0.05 = 5 mL
    • Spice from 200 mL @ 10% = 200 × 0.10 = 20 mL
    • Spice from 300 mL @ 15% = 300 × 0.15 = 45 mL
    • Total spice = 5 + 20 + 45 = 70 mL
    • Total volume = 100 + 200 + 300 = 600 mL
    Concentration = (70 / 600) × 100% = 70 / 6 % = 35 / 3 % ≈ 11.67%
  2. Scenario (ii):
    • Spice from 300 mL @ 5% = 300 × 0.05 = 15 mL
    • Spice from 200 mL @ 10% = 200 × 0.10 = 20 mL
    • Spice from 100 mL @ 15% = 100 × 0.15 = 15 mL
    • Total spice = 15 + 20 + 15 = 50 mL
    • Total volume = 300 + 200 + 100 = 600 mL
    Concentration = (50 / 600) × 100% = 50 / 6 % = 25 / 3 % ≈ 8.33%
Key Takeaway: In scenario (i), the largest volume (300 mL) had 15% spice, pulling the concentration up to 11.67%. In scenario (ii), the largest volume (300 mL) had 5% spice, pulling the concentration down to 8.33%.

Weighted marks and converting scores to percentages.

1Savitri's marks in Kashmiri are as follows: 35 out of 50 in internal tests, 44 out of 60 in the project, and 80 out of 100 in the final exam. The annual percentage score is calculated by combining the internals, project, and final exam in the ratio 3:4:5. Which of the following expression(s) gives her annual score (as a percentage) in Kashmiri?
  • (i) (35 × 3 + 44 × 4 + 80 × 5) / (3 + 4 + 5)
  • (ii) [ (35/100) × 3 + (44/100) × 4 + (80/100) × 5 ] / (3 + 4 + 5)
  • (iii) [ (35/50) × 3 + (44/60) × 4 + (80/100) × 5 ] / (3 + 4 + 5) × 100
  • (iv) [ ((35/50) × 100) × 3 + ((44/60) × 100) × 4 + ((80/100) × 100) × 5 ] / (3 + 4 + 5)
Answer / method

Step-by-step Analysis:

  1. Normalise raw marks to percentages:
    • Internal tests: 35 out of 50 = (35/50) × 100 = 70%
    • Project: 44 out of 60 = (44/60) × 100 = 73.33%
    • Final exam: 80 out of 100 = (80/100) × 100 = 80%
  2. Evaluate each expression:
    • (i) is INCORRECT: It averages raw marks (35, 44, 80) directly. Because they are out of different maximums (50, 60, 100), combining raw scores creates false weightage.
    • (ii) is INCORRECT: It divides 35 and 44 by 100 instead of their actual maximum totals of 50 and 60.
    • (iii) is CORRECT: It calculates the fractional scores (35/50, 44/60, 80/100), weights them by 3, 4, 5, divides by 12, and multiplies by 100 at the end to get the percentage.
    • (iv) is CORRECT: It converts each component directly into a percentage first [(35/50)×100, (44/60)×100, (80/100)×100], and then computes their weighted average with weights 3, 4, 5.
Correct Expressions: (iii) and (iv) are both mathematically correct and yield approximately 75.28%.

1A stationery shop owner made ₹8000 selling books, of which 30% is the profit amount, and ₹1000 selling book covers, of which 50% is the profit amount. What is the percentage of profit on the total sales?

14Whole class project: Each student shares the average age of their family and the number of family members. Discuss among the class and come up with a way to find out the average age of all the families of the class.
Answer / method

Mathematical Formulation & Discussion:

  1. Why the simple average of family averages FAILS:
    If Student 1 has a family of 3 with average age 25, and Student 2 has a joint family of 10 with average age 45, simply averaging (25 + 45)/2 = 35 gives a misleading result. The 10 individuals in the larger family have been given the same combined weight as the 3 individuals in the smaller family!
  2. Correct Formulation: Weighted Mean by Family Size:
    Let student i's family have ni members with average age ai.
    • Total age of family i = ni × ai.
    • Grand total age of all people across all families = Σ(ni × ai).
    • Grand total number of individuals = Σni.
    True Average Age of All People = Σ(ni × ai) / Σni
  3. Class Procedure:
    • Create a 3-column table on the blackboard: [Student Name | Family Members (ni) | Family Average Age (ai)].
    • Add a 4th column for Product [ni × ai].
    • Sum column 2 to get total people, sum column 4 to get total age, and divide sum(4) by sum(2).
Core Statistical Takeaway: When combining subgroup averages, subgroups MUST be weighted by their sample sizes (ni) to obtain the true population mean.
*15Given some data with corresponding weights, what would happen to the weighted average if all the weights are increased by a constant value, say 1? If required, experiment with some data. What do you observe? Justify your answer using algebra.
Answer / method

Comprehensive Algebraic Proof & Behavior Analysis:

  1. Definitions:
    Let the n data values be x1, x2, …, xn with original weights w1, w2, …, wn.
    • Original weighted mean: x̄w = Σ(wixi) / Σwi
    • Ordinary arithmetic mean: x̄ord = Σxi / n &implies; Σxi = n × x̄ord
  2. Increase all weights by +1 (new weights w'⊂i⊃ = wi + 1):
    • New numerator = Σ[(wi + 1)xi] = Σ(wixi + xi) = Σ(wixi) + Σxi
    • New denominator = Σ(wi + 1) = Σwi + Σ1 = Σwi + n
    New Weighted Mean x̄' = [Σ(wixi) + Σxi] / [Σwi + n]
  3. Proving the Effect:
    Notice that x̄' is the combined mean of the original weighted mean x̄w (with weight Σwi) and the unweighted mean x̄ord (with weight n):
    x̄' = [(Σwi) × x̄w + n × x̄ord] / [Σwi + n]
    • Case 1: If x̄w > x̄ord, adding +1 pulls the new average down towards x̄ord.
    • Case 2: If x̄w < x̄ord, adding +1 pulls the new average up towards x̄ord.
    • Case 3: The weighted average remains unchanged if and only if x̄w = x̄ord (for example, when all original weights were already equal, or by symmetrical coincidence).
Conclusion: Adding 1 to all weights pulls the weighted average closer to the simple unweighted arithmetic mean: x̄' = [Σ(wixi) + Σxi] / [Σwi + n].
