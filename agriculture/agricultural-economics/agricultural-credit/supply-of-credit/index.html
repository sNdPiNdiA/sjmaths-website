Supply of Agricultural Credit in India

An exhaustive academic treatise on the institutional and non-institutional sources of agricultural credit in India, tailored for UP TGT and PGT Agriculture examinations, covering credit agencies, institutional hierarchies, cooperative structures, and rural financing mechanisms.

Introduction to Agricultural Credit and Its Classification

Agricultural credit is the lifeblood of modern commercial farming, enabling farmers to procure critical inputs like high-yielding variety seeds, fertilizers, irrigation equipment, and machinery. Credit is classified based on duration, purpose, and source.

  • Short-Term Credit (Crop Loans): Repayment period up to 15 months, primarily utilized for seasonal agricultural operations such as tillage, sowing, weeding, and purchasing fertilizers.
  • Medium-Term Credit: Repayment period ranges from 15 months to 5 years, required for purchasing pump sets, minor agricultural implements, and breeding cattle.
  • Long-Term Credit: Repayment period exceeds 5 years and can extend up to 20 years, utilized for permanent land improvements, orchard establishment, sinking tubewells, and purchasing heavy tractors and combined harvesters.

Institutional Sources of Agricultural Credit

Institutional credit agencies operate under the regulated framework of the Reserve Bank of India (RBI) and the National Bank for Agriculture and Rural Development (NABARD). They offer low-interest rates, developmental orientation, and structured repayment schedules.

  • Commercial Banks: Play a dominant role in direct agricultural lending, priority sector lending (PSL) mandates, and Kisan Credit Card (KCC) issuance.
  • Regional Rural Banks (RRBs): Established in 1975 based on the recommendations of the Narasimham Working Group, specifically to cater to small and marginal farmers, agricultural laborers, and rural artisans.
  • Cooperative Credit Institutions: Structured as a three-tier system comprising State Cooperative Banks (Apex level at the state), Central Cooperative Banks (District level), and Primary Agricultural Credit Societies (Village level).

Non-Institutional Sources of Agricultural Credit

Non-institutional lenders include moneylenders, traders, commission agents, landlords, relatives, and friends. Historically, they have been the primary source of rural credit due to the ease of access and absence of bureaucratic paperwork, albeit with major disadvantages.

  • Moneylenders: Professional and agricultural moneylenders charge exorbitant interest rates, often leading to perpetual indebtedness and land alienation of smallholders.
  • Traders and Commission Agents: Advance credit against future crop yields, often compelling farmers to sell their produce at distress prices immediately after harvest.
  • Informal Networks: Friends and relatives provide interest-free or low-interest loans during emergencies, though volumes are limited and unpredictable.

Apex Institution: NABARD and Its Role in Credit Supply

The National Bank for Agriculture and Rural Development (NABARD) was established on July 12, 1982, upon the recommendations of the B. Sivaraman Committee, by merging the Agricultural Credit Department and Rural Planning and Credit Cell of RBI, and the Agricultural Refinance and Development Corporation (ARDC).

    NABARD acts as the apex refinancing agency for financing agriculture, small-scale industries, cottage and village industries, and handicrafts.
  • It regulates and inspects Regional Rural Banks (RRBs) and Cooperative Banks.
  • It manages the Rural Infrastructure Development Fund (RIDF) to finance ongoing rural infrastructure projects.

Kisan Credit Card (KCC) Scheme and Priority Sector Lending

Introduced in 1998-99 based on the recommendations of the R. V. Gupta Committee, the Kisan Credit Card (KCC) scheme revolutionised short-term institutional credit delivery by providing adequate and timely credit support to farmers from the banking system.

  • Coverage: Extended to crop husbandry, animal husbandry, and fisheries activities.
  • Interest Subvention: The Government of India provides interest subvention to ensure prompt-paying farmers receive short-term crop loans at an effective rate of 4% per annum.
  • Priority Sector Lending (PSL): Domestic commercial banks are mandated to allocate 18% of their Adjusted Net Bank Credit (ANBC) to agriculture, with sub-targets for small and marginal farmers.

  • Confusing the establishment year of NABARD (1982) with the establishment year of RRBs (1975).

  • Believing that PACS provide long-term credit; PACS primarily provide short-term and medium-term crop loans.

⚡ Chapter Master Summary: Supply of Agricultural Credit in India

Systematic concept review designed for quick retention and last-minute pre-exam revision.

Concept 1

Agricultural Credit Classification by Duration

Agricultural credit is categorized into three distinct operational durations to match the economic lifespan of farming investments.

  • Short-Term Credit: Duration up to 15 months, used for seasonal crop production inputs.
  • Medium-Term Credit: Duration between 15 months and 5 years, used for minor farm equipment and livestock.
  • Long-Term Credit: Duration from 5 to 20 years, used for capital-intensive permanent farm improvements.
Concept 2

Institutional vs Non-Institutional Credit Framework

The Indian rural credit market exhibits dualism between formal institutional agencies and informal moneylenders.

  • Institutional Agency: Regulated by RBI/NABARD, offering low-cost productive loans with formal documentation.
  • Non-Institutional Agency: Unregulated moneylenders and traders charging exorbitant interest rates leading to debt traps.
Concept 3

Cooperative Credit Delivery Mechanism

The cooperative credit structure forms the bedrock of rural financing in India, organized hierarchically.

  • PACS: Grassroots primary society dealing directly with farmers at the village level.
  • CCB: District-level financing institution supervising PACS.
  • SCB: State-level apex institution interfacing with NABARD and RBI.
Concept 4

Kisan Credit Card (KCC) and Refinance Systems

Modern credit delivery relies on flexible credit cards and centralized refinancing mechanisms.

  • KCC Scheme: Launched in 1998-99 to provide hassle-free revolving credit to farmers.
  • NABARD Refinance: Provides financial backing to commercial, cooperative, and regional rural banks for agricultural lending.

📚 Technical Terms Glossary

Agricultural Credit (Agricultural Finance / Rural Credit)
The provision of financial resources to farmers for undertaking agricultural production and capital investments.
NABARD (National Bank for Agriculture and Rural Development)
Apex development bank in India providing refinancing for agriculture and rural development, established in 1982.
PACS (Primary Agricultural Credit Society)
The grassroots cooperative credit institution operating at the village level to disburse short-term loans.
RRB (Regional Rural Bank)
State-sponsored, regionally focused commercial banks established in 1975 to cater to rural smallholders.
KCC (Kisan Credit Card)
A credit scheme introduced in 1998-99 to provide timely and adequate credit to farmers for cultivation and other needs.
Priority Sector Lending (PSL Mandate)
RBI guidelines requiring banks to allocate a specified percentage of credit to core sectors like agriculture.
Short-Term Loan (Crop Loan / Production Credit)
Credit repayable within 15 months, primarily utilized for seasonal agricultural operations.
Long-Term Credit (Investment Credit)
Credit with a repayment period exceeding 5 years for permanent capital improvements.
B. Sivaraman Committee (Committee to Review Arrangements for Institutional Credit for Agriculture and Rural Development)
The committee whose recommendations led to the establishment of NABARD.
R.V. Gupta Committee (High-Level Committee on Agricultural Credit through Commercial Banks)
The committee that recommended the formulation of the Kisan Credit Card (KCC) scheme.

⚖️ Concepts Often Confused in Examinations

Short-Term Credit VS Long-Term Credit

Short-term credit is for seasonal crop production (up to 15 months), whereas long-term credit is for capital investments and land development (over 5 years).

PACS VS SCB

PACS is at the village grassroots level dealing with individual farmers, while SCB is the apex state-level cooperative bank.
