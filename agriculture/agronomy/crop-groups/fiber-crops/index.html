Agronomy of Fiber Crops: Cotton, Jute, Mesta, and Sunnhemp

Botanical Taxonomy and Classification of Fiber Crops

Fiber crops are cultivated primarily for their textile, cordage, and industrial fibrous materials derived from seed hairs, bast tissues, or surface leaves. Understanding their botanical classification is vital for competitive agronomy examinations.

  • Cotton (Kapas): Belongs to the family Malvaceae. Cultivated species include Gossypium hirsutum (American upland cotton, 2n=4x=52), Gossypium barbadense (Egyptian or Sea Island cotton, 2n=4x=52), Gossypium arboreum (Tree cotton, 2n=2x=26), and Gossypium herbaceum (Levant cotton, 2n=2x=26).
  • Jute (Pat): Belongs to the family Tiliaceae (or Malvaceae under APG IV). Two primary cultivated species are Corchorus capsularis (White jute, round pods, 2n=2x=14) and Corchorus olitorius (Tossa jute, elongated pods, 2n=2x=14).
  • Mesta: Also known as kenaf or roselle, belonging to Malvaceae. Major species are Hibiscus cannabinus (Kenaf) and Hibiscus sabdariffa (Roselle).
  • Sunnhemp: Belongs to Fabaceae (Leguminosae) and is scientifically known as Crotalaria juncea (2n=2x=16), serving both as a fiber and a green manure crop.

Climate, Soil, and Agro-Climatic Requirements

Fiber crops exhibit distinct climatic and edaphic adaptations across the diverse agro-climatic zones of India, especially the Indo-Gangetic plains and central cotton-growing tracts.

  • Cotton: Thrives in warm, sunny climates with a frost-free period of at least 200 days. Requires a temperature range of 21°C to 30°C during growth and abundant sunshine during boll maturation. Prefers deep, well-drained alluvial soils of the northern plains and heavy black vertisols of central India.
  • Jute: Requires a warm and humid climate with heavy rainfall (1000 to 2000 mm) and high relative humidity (70% to 90%). Temperature ranges between 24°C and 38°C are optimal. Grows best on fertile alluvial soils of river basins that receive annual silt deposits.
  • Soil pH and Drainage: Waterlogging is highly detrimental to cotton, causing boll shedding and root rot. Jute tolerates temporary waterlogging during the monsoon season. Optimal soil pH for cotton is 6.0 to 8.0, whereas jute prefers slightly acidic to neutral soils (pH 6.0 to 7.5).

Cultural Practices: Tillage, Sowing Geometry, and Seed Rate

Establishment of an optimal plant population is critical for maximizing the economic yield of fiber crops.

  • Land Preparation: Fine tilth is required for cotton and jute. Deep ploughing followed by harrowing and planking ensures proper moisture conservation and weed eradication.
  • Seed Rate and Spacing:
    • Desi Cotton (G. arboreum): Seed rate 12-15 kg/ha; Spacing 45-60 cm × 15-30 cm.
    • American Cotton (G. hirsutum): Seed rate 15-20 kg/ha; Spacing 67-75 cm × 30-45 cm.
    • Hybrid Cotton: Seed rate 1.5-2.0 kg/ha (dibbling); Spacing 90-100 cm × 45-60 cm.
    • Jute (C. capsularis & C. olitorius): Line sowing seed rate 6-8 kg/ha; Broadcasting seed rate 10-12 kg/ha; Spacing 30 cm × 5-7 cm.
  • Seed Treatment: Cotton seeds are acid-delinted using concentrated sulfuric acid (100 ml per kg of seed for 2-3 minutes) to remove fuzz, followed by washing and treating with Trichoderma viride (10 g/kg) and Azotobacter. Jute seeds are treated with carbendazim at 2 g/kg of seed to prevent seed-borne fungal infections.

Nutrient Management and Fertilizer Recommendations

Nutrient uptake in fiber crops is heavy, requiring balanced application of nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium (NPK) along with secondary and micronutrients.

  • Cotton Fertilizer Dose: Recommended N:P:K for rainfed hybrid cotton is 80:40:40 kg/ha, while irrigated hybrids require up to 120-150:60:60 kg/ha. Nitrogen is applied in splits (basal, square formation, and flowering stages) to prevent excessive vegetative growth.
  • Jute Fertilizer Dose: Typically requires 60 to 80 kg N, 30 kg P₂O₅, and 30 kg K₂O per hectare. Nitrogen is applied in two equal splits—half as basal and half after the first weeding and thinning (at 20-25 days after sowing).
  • Micronutrient Deficiencies: Boron deficiency in cotton leads to flower bud shedding and twisted bolls; foliar spray of boron (Solubor at 0.2%) during square formation is recommended. Zinc deficiency causes interveinal chlorosis.

Crop Protection: Major Pests, Diseases, and Management

Fiber crops face severe biotic stresses that can drastically reduce commercial lint and fiber yields.

  • Insect Pests of Cotton:
    • Pink Bollworm (Pectinophora gossypiella): Larvae feed on developing seeds and fibers inside the boll. Managed using pheromone traps (5/acre), release of Trichogramma, and transgenic Bt cotton hybrids.
    • American Bollworm (Helicoverpa armigera): Polyphagous pest damaging squares, flowers, and bolls.
    • Jassids (Amrasca devastans): Sucking pest causing hopper burn (curling and yellowing of leaves).
  • Diseases of Cotton:
    • Bacterial Blight (Xanthomonas citri pv. malvacearum): Angular leaf spots and black arm symptoms. Managed by seed treatment with copper oxychloride and streptomycin.
    • Fusarium Wilt (Fusarium oxysporum f. sp. vasinfectum): Vascular wilt leading to yellowing and defoliation.
  • Diseases of Jute:
    • Stem Rot and Root Rot (Macrophomina phaseolina): Causes bark rotting and seedling blight. Controlled by seed treatment with carbendazim and avoiding water stagnation.
    • Anthracnose (Colletotrichum corchori): Lesions on stems and leaves.

Harvesting, Extraction Technology, and Storage

Post-harvest processing distinguishes fiber crops from cereal and pulse production, requiring specialized extraction and grading techniques.

  • Cotton Harvesting: Done in multiple pickings as bolls open fully. Avoid picking damp cotton or harvesting after sunset to maintain lint quality (cleanliness and color grade). Average yield ranges from 15-25 quintals/ha of seed cotton (kapas).
  • Jute Harvesting: Harvested at the small pod/flower stage (around 120-135 days after sowing) for superior fiber strength and quality. Plants are cut close to the ground or pulled out.
  • Retting Process in Jute: Retting is a biological process where bundles of harvested jute stems are submerged in slow-moving or stagnant retting water (34°C optimal). Microorganisms (pectinolytic bacteria like Clostridium and Bacillus species) dissolve plant gums and pectins, loosening the fiber from the woody core (xylem).
  • Stripping and Storage: Fibers are extracted manually or mechanically by stripping, washed in clean water, dried in the sun for 2-3 days, and baled. Safe moisture content for jute storage is 12% to 14%.

⚠️ Common Mistakes & Exam Pitfalls
  • Error: Confusing the chromosome number of American upland cotton with desi cotton. Correct Fact: American cotton (Gossypium hirsutum) is tetraploid (2n=4x=52), while desi cotton (G. arboreum) is diploid (2n=2x=26).
  • Error: Assuming jute retting is purely chemical. Correct Fact: Jute retting is a biochemical and microbiological process driven by anaerobic and facultative aerobic pectinolytic bacteria.

⚡ Chapter Master Summary: Agronomy of Fiber Crops: Cotton, Jute, Mesta, and Sunnhemp

Systematic concept review designed for quick retention and last-minute pre-exam revision.

Concept 1

Taxonomic Diversity and Ploidy Levels in Cotton

Cotton exhibits diverse ploidy levels across its cultivated species, impacting hybridization and breeding programs.

  • New World Cottons: Tetraploid species Gossypium hirsutum and Gossypium barbadense possessing 52 chromosomes (2n=4x=52).
  • Old World Cottons: Diploid species Gossypium arboreum and Gossypium herbaceum possessing 26 chromosomes (2n=2x=26).
Concept 2

Fiber Extraction Mechanics in Bast Fiber Crops

Bast fibers are located in the phloem of stem tissues and require specialized separation techniques.

  • Retting: Microbial degradation of pectin and hemicellulose cementing fiber strands to woody xylem core.
  • Stripping: Manual or mechanical removal of loosened fibers from the stalk followed by washing and sun drying.
Concept 3

Transgenic Technology in Cotton Production

Modern cotton agronomy relies heavily on genetic engineering to combat lepidopteran insect complexes.

  • Bt Cotton: Expresses crystal endotoxin genes (Cry1Ac and Cry2Ab) from Bacillus thuringiensis targeting bollworms.
  • Refugia Strategy: Cultivation of non-Bt cotton around transgenic fields to prevent the rapid development of insect resistance.
Concept 4

Agronomic Management of Jute Crops

Jute cultivation demands specific moisture and management protocols for optimal bast fiber yield.

  • Seed Rate: 6-8 kg/ha for line sowing which ensures uniform plant stands and facilitates intercultural operations.
  • Harvesting Stage: 120-135 days after sowing at small pod stage to balance fiber strength and yield.

📚 Technical Terms Glossary

Lint (Seed Hair Fiber)
Long staple fibers attached to cotton seeds after ginning, utilized in textile manufacturing.
Fuzz (Short Residual Fibers)
Short, persistent hairs remaining on cotton seeds after commercial ginning, removed via acid delinting.
Ginning Percentage (Lint-to-Seed Cotton Ratio)
The percentage weight of lint obtained from a given weight of seed cotton (kapas), typically ranging from 30% to 40%.
Retting (Microbial Bark Separation)
The biological process of soaking bast fiber plant bundles in water to dissolve adhesive gums via microbial action.
Square (Floral Bud)
The pyramid-shaped floral bud of the cotton plant enclosed by bracts prior to anthesis.
Hopper Burn (Jassid Damage Symptom)
Yellowing and marginal necrosis of cotton leaves caused by the feeding toxins of leafhoppers (Amrasca devastans).
Bast Fiber (Phloem Fiber)
Tough, flexible fibers obtained from the phloem or inner bark of dicotyledonous stems such as jute and mesta.
Refugia (Insect Resistance Management Zone)
Non-Bt crop plantings maintained alongside transgenic crops to preserve susceptible insect populations.
Ribboning (Mechanical Fiber Extraction)
The process of stripping outer bark ribbons containing bast fibers directly from green stems before retting.
Bt Cotton (Genetically Modified Bacillus thuringiensis Cotton)
Transgenic cotton cultivars engineered with bacterial insecticidal protein genes for internal bollworm control.
Toping (Terminal Bud Removal)
Pruning of the main stem apex in crops like sunnhemp or cotton to encourage lateral branching and boll development.

🎯 Must-Remember High-Frequency Facts

  • Chromosome number of American cotton (Gossypium hirsutum): 2n=52.
  • Chromosome number of Jute (Corchorus capsularis/olitorius): 2n=14.
  • Jute retting temperature optimum: 34°C.
  • Safe storage moisture limit for jute fiber: 12-14%.

📌 Quick Recap Bullet Points

  • Cotton belongs to Malvaceae with distinct diploid and tetraploid cultivated species.
  • Jute (Corchorus species) is the leading bast fiber crop requiring warm, humid conditions and retting for extraction.
  • Acid delinting eliminates seed fuzz and enhances germination uniformity in cotton.
  • Bt cotton relies on Cry toxin genes to provide durable protection against lepidopteran pests.

⚖️ Concepts Often Confused in Examinations

White Jute VS Tossa Jute

White jute (Corchorus capsularis) has round pods and tolerates waterlogging better, whereas Tossa jute (Corchorus olitorius) has cylindrical pods and superior fiber quality.

Desi Cotton VS American Cotton

Desi cotton comprises diploid species (2n=26) with short staples, while American cotton comprises tetraploid species (2n=52) with medium to long staple length.

Question 1 of 18 Conceptual MCQ

Which of the following cultivated cotton species is known as Sea Island or Egyptian cotton?

Explanation: Gossypium barbadense is referred to as Egyptian or Sea Island cotton, known for its extra-long and fine staple fibers.

Question 2 of 18 Conceptual MCQ

What is the diploid chromosome number (2n) of cultivated jute species (Corchorus capsularis and Corchorus olitorius)?

Explanation: Both cultivated jute species have a somatic chromosome number of 2n = 14.

Question 3 of 18 Conceptual MCQ

Which chemical is standard for acid delinting of cotton seeds prior to sowing?

Explanation: Concentrated sulfuric acid (H2SO4) is used for rapid acid delinting of cotton seeds to remove fuzzy hairs.

Question 4 of 18 Conceptual MCQ

The biological process of jute retting is primarily mediated by which group of microorganisms?

Explanation: Jute retting is driven by anaerobic and facultative aerobic pectinolytic bacteria such as Clostridium and Bacillus species that break down pectins.

Question 5 of 18 Conceptual MCQ

What is the optimal temperature range for efficient jute retting?

Explanation: An optimum water temperature of around 34°C facilitates rapid and complete microbial retting of jute bundles.

Question 6 of 18 Conceptual MCQ

Which insect pest causes 'hopper burn' symptom in cotton crops?

Explanation: Jassids (Amrasca devastans) cause hopper burn, characterized by yellowing, curling, and drying of leaf margins.

Question 7 of 18 Conceptual MCQ

What is the primary economic part of the cotton plant?

Explanation: Cotton lint consists of epidermal seed hairs attached to the seed coat, harvested after boll dehiscence.

Question 8 of 18 Conceptual MCQ

Which gene is introduced into Bt cotton to impart resistance against lepidopteran bollworms?

Explanation: Cry genes (such as Cry1Ac) derived from Bacillus thuringiensis are engineered into Bt cotton to produce crystal proteins toxic to bollworms.

Question 9 of 18 Conceptual MCQ

Which cultivated cotton species is diploid (2n = 2x = 26)?

Explanation: Gossypium arboreum is a diploid Old World cotton species with 2n = 26 chromosomes.

Question 10 of 18 Conceptual MCQ

What is the recommended seed rate for line-sown jute cultivation?

Explanation: Line sowing of jute requires 6 to 8 kg of seeds per hectare to maintain optimal plant density and allow weeding.

Question 11 of 18 Conceptual MCQ

What is the safe moisture percentage for storage of jute fiber to prevent fungal deterioration?

Explanation: Jute fiber should be stored at 12% to 14% moisture content to prevent microbial decay and loss of luster.

Question 12 of 18 Conceptual MCQ

Sunnhemp (Crotalaria juncea) belongs to which plant family?

Explanation: Sunnhemp belongs to the family Fabaceae (Leguminosae) and forms root nodules fixing atmospheric nitrogen.

Question 13 of 18 Conceptual MCQ

Which stage of cotton growth is most critical for irrigation to prevent flower bud shedding?

Explanation: Square formation and flowering represent the critical irrigation stages in cotton where water deficit causes severe yield reduction.

Question 14 of 18 Conceptual MCQ

What is the ginning percentage in most commercial cotton cultivars?

Explanation: Ginning percentage typically ranges between 30% and 40%, indicating the proportion of lint recovered from kapas.

Question 15 of 18 Conceptual MCQ

Which of the following is a distinguishing characteristic of Tossa jute (Corchorus olitorius)?

Explanation: Corchorus olitorius (Tossa jute) features elongated, cylindrical capsules, whereas C. capsularis features round capsules.

Question 16 of 18 Conceptual MCQ

What is the purpose of maintaining a 'refugia' crop in Bt cotton cultivation?

Explanation: Refugia fields of non-Bt cotton allow susceptible insects to survive and mate with any resistant insects, delaying resistance evolution.

Question 17 of 18 Conceptual MCQ

Which disease of jute causes blackening of stems, bark rotting, and seedling blight?

Explanation: Macrophomina phaseolina causes stem and root rot (rhizoctonia root rot) in jute, leading to bark shredding and plant death.

Question 18 of 18 Conceptual MCQ

What is the botanical name of Kenaf or Mesta commonly cultivated for coarse fiber?

Explanation: Hibiscus cannabinus is the botanical name for kenaf or mesta, widely grown for cordage and paper pulp.

Test Question 2 of 10 TGT Standard

Match the fiber crop with its respective plant family: A. Cotton -> 1. Tiliaceae; B. Jute -> 2. Malvaceae; C. Sunnhemp -> 3. Fabaceae.

Solution & Rationale: Cotton belongs to Malvaceae (A-2), Jute belongs to Tiliaceae/Malvaceae (B-1), and Sunnhemp belongs to Fabaceae (C-3).

Test Question 3 of 10 TGT Standard

What is the recommended seed rate for hybrid cotton per hectare when planted through dibbling?

Solution & Rationale: Hybrid cotton utilizes high-cost F1 seeds planted at wide spacing via dibbling, requiring a low seed rate of 1.5 to 2.0 kg/ha.

Test Question 4 of 10 PGT Standard

Which agronomic practice in cotton involves removing the terminal growing point of the main stem to check excessive vegetative growth and enhance boll development?

Solution & Rationale: Topping involves pinching or cutting the main stem apex to redirect photosynthates from vegetative shoots toward developing bolls.

Test Question 5 of 10 PGT Standard

In jute agronomy, what is the consequence of delaying harvest beyond the small pod/flower stage?

Solution & Rationale: Late harvesting of jute leads to lignification of fiber cells, making the fiber coarse, harsh, and stiff with reduced tensile strength.

Test Question 6 of 10 TGT Standard

Which pest of cotton is characterized by internal feeding inside the developing bolls, leaving characteristic exit holes?

Solution & Rationale: Pink bollworm (Pectinophora gossypiella) larvae tunnel into developing bolls, feeding on seeds and lint, leaving round exit holes.

Test Question 7 of 10 TGT Standard

What is the primary function of acid delinting in cotton seed preparation?

Solution & Rationale: Acid delinting removes short fuzz hairs, making seeds smooth and free-flowing for mechanical planters and precise seed drills.

Test Question 8 of 10 PGT Standard

Identify the correct diploid Old World cotton species cultivated in India.

Solution & Rationale: Gossypium arboreum and Gossypium herbaceum are diploid Old World cotton species (2n=26) native to Asia and Africa.

Test Question 9 of 10 PGT Standard

What is the optimal stage and water condition for jute retting?

Solution & Rationale: Jute harvested at flowering/small pod stage is retted in slow-moving or stagnant water at approximately 34°C for optimal bacterial action.

Test Question 10 of 10 PGT Standard

Which micronutrient deficiency in cotton leads to flower bud shedding, deformation of bolls, and corky stem splitting?

Solution & Rationale: Boron deficiency in cotton causes sterility, shedding of floral buds (squares), and internal necrosis or boll deformation.
