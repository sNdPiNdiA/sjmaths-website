Introduction and Botanical Taxonomy of Commercial Flowers

Floriculture encompasses the cultivation, processing, and marketing of ornamental flowers, foliage plants, bedding plants, and turfgrasses. In the context of Indian horticulture and the UP TGT/PGT Agriculture syllabi, commercial floriculture is a high-value, labor-intensive sector characterized by high economic returns per unit area compared to traditional field crops.

  • Rose (Rosa hybrida): Family Rosaceae, Origin: Asia, Chromosome number: 2n = 28 (diploid to tetraploid cultivars). Propagated commercially via T-budding or shield budding on rootstocks like Rosa indica var. odorata.
  • Gladiolus (Gladiolus grandiflorus): Family Iridaceae, Origin: South Africa, Chromosome number: 2n = 30. Propagated through corms and cormels.
  • Chrysanthemum (Chrysanthemum morifolium): Family Asteraceae (Compositae), Origin: China/Japan, Chromosome number: 2n = 54 (hexaploid). Propagated vegetatively via terminal stem cuttings or root suckers.
  • Marigold (Tagetes erecta - African Marigold; Tagetes patula - French Marigold): Family Asteraceae, Origin: Central and South America, Chromosome number: 2n = 24 (African) and 2n = 48 (French). Propagated via seeds.
  • Carnation (Dianthus caryophyllus): Family Caryophyllaceae, Origin: Mediterranean region, Chromosome number: 2n = 30. Propagated using terminal stem cuttings.

Agro-Climatic Requirements, Soil, and Land Preparation

Successful commercial floriculture requires strict adherence to environmental parameters, particularly photoperiod, temperature, and soil physical-chemical properties.

  • Soil Requirements: Most commercial flowers thrive in well-drained, sandy loam to clay loam soils rich in organic matter. The ideal pH range is 6.0 to 7.5. Water stagnation is highly detrimental and leads to root rot diseases.
  • Temperature Thresholds: Roses prefer day temperatures of 25°C to 30°C and night temperatures of 15°C to 18°C. Carnations require cooler regimes (15°C to 24°C). Chrysanthemums are sensitive to day length; being short-day plants, they require specific photoperiod manipulation for year-round flower production.
  • Land Preparation: Deep ploughing followed by harrowing and leveling is essential. Farm Yard Manure (FYM) @ 25 to 30 t/ha must be thoroughly incorporated into the soil at least 3 to 4 weeks prior to planting to improve soil structure, microbial activity, and moisture retention capacity.

Propagation Techniques, Spacing, and Planting Geometry

Planting density and geometry dictate light interception, canopy microclimate, and ultimate flower yield and quality.

  • Rose: Spacing is typically maintained at 60 cm × 30 cm or 45 cm × 45 cm in open fields, and higher density planting (30 cm × 15 cm or 20 cm rows) under protected cultivation structures (polyhouses). Bud union must always remain 2 to 5 cm above the soil surface.
  • Gladiolus: Corms of diameter 4.5 to 5.0 cm are selected. Spacing is maintained at 40 cm × 20 cm at a planting depth of 5 to 7 cm, requiring a corm rate of approximately 1.5 to 2.0 lakh corms per hectare.
  • Chrysanthemum: Rooted terminal cuttings are planted at a spacing of 30 cm × 30 cm during June–July. Pinching (removal of growing tips) is a critical cultural operation performed 4 weeks after planting to encourage lateral branching and increase flower production.
  • Marigold: Seed rate is 1.0 to 1.5 kg/ha for African marigold and 400 to 500 g/ha for French marigold. Spacing is kept at 40 cm × 40 cm for African and 30 cm × 30 cm for French types.

Nutrient Management and Irrigation Scheduling

Flower crops have a high nutrient and water demand, especially during vegetative growth and flush development.

  • Fertilizer Recommendation (Rose): A general recommendation is 120:60:60 kg N:P:K per hectare per year, split into multiple applications coinciding with pruning flushes. Micronutrients like iron (Fe) and manganese (Mn) foliar sprays prevent interveinal chlorosis.
  • Fertilizer Recommendation (Chrysanthemum): Requires heavy feeding of nitrogen and potassium. Standard dosage is 200:100:100 kg N:P:K per hectare.
  • Irrigation Management: Critical irrigation stages include active vegetative growth, flower bud initiation, and flowering. Drip irrigation combined with fertigation is the most efficient method, maintaining soil moisture tension between 0.2 to 0.3 bar. Overhead irrigation should be minimized to prevent fungal spore germination on foliage and flowers.

Crop Protection: Major Pests, Diseases, and Physiological Disorders

Phytosanitary management is vital to meet export quality standards and prevent catastrophic yield losses.

  • Insect Pests: Red Spider Mite (Tetranychus urticae) causes bronzing and webbing on rose and carnation leaves (controlled by abamectin or propargite). Thrips (Frankliniella occidentalis) feed on floral buds, causing silvering and distortion (controlled by spinosad or fipronil). Aphids (Aphis gossypii) suck sap and transmit viral diseases.
  • Fungal Diseases: Powdery Mildew (Podosphaera pannosa) creates white powdery patches on young rose leaves and buds (managed by wettable sulfur or triadimefon). Black Spot (Diplocarpon rosae) causes circular black spots leading to defoliation (managed by chlorothalonil or mancozeb). Fusarium Wilt (Fusarium oxysporum) affects carnation and chrysanthemum.
  • Physiological Disorders: Blindness in roses (failure to produce flower buds due to low light or low temperatures), Bullhead in roses (deformed, flat-topped flowers caused by sudden temperature drops or genetic instability), and Calyx splitting in carnations (high temperature fluctuations and excessive nitrogen).

Harvesting Indices, Post-Harvest Handling, and Yield

Post-harvest losses in floriculture are exceptionally high (up to 30-40%) without proper cold chain management and chemical preservation.

  • Harvesting Stage: Roses are harvested at the tight bud stage when the first petal is just unfurling (outer petals showing color). Gladiolus spikes are harvested when the bottom 1-2 florets show color. Chrysanthemum standard types are harvested when outer petals are fully expanded, while spray types are harvested when 50% of buds show color. Marigold flowers are hand-picked fully open in the early morning.
  • Pulse Treatments and Vase Life Solutions: Pulse treatment with Silver Thiosulfate (STS) @ 0.5 to 1.0 mM for 10-20 minutes prevents ethylene-induced senescence in carnations and roses. Vase solutions typically contain 2% sucrose (energy source) and 200 ppm 8-Hydroxyquinoline Citrate (8-HQC) as a germicide to prevent vascular blockage.
  • Storage and Yield: Cut flowers are stored at 2°C to 4°C with 90-95% relative humidity. Average flower yield ranges from 1.5 to 2.5 lakh cut stems per hectare per year for roses and carnations, and 12 to 15 tonnes per hectare for loose marigold flowers.

⚡ High-Yield Exam Points (UP TGT / PGT Focus)
  • Rose rootstock predominantly used in India for commercial budding is Rosa indica var. odorata.
  • Gladiolus propagation is achieved through underground modified stems known as corms; cormels are used for multiplication.
  • Chrysanthemum is a classic qualitative short-day plant (SDP) requiring dark period manipulation for blooming.
  • Pinching in carnation and chrysanthemum is done to eliminate apical dominance and promote lateral side shoots.
  • Silver Thiosulfate (STS) is widely used as an anti-ethylene agent in carnation and rose vase life extension.
  • Bullhead in roses (flat-topped, deformed flowers) is a physiological disorder triggered by sudden temperature fluctuations and nutrient imbalance.
  • Calyx splitting in carnation is primarily caused by low night temperatures followed by high nitrogen fertilization.
  • African marigold (Tagetes erecta) has a chromosome number of 2n = 24, whereas French marigold (Tagetes patula) has 2n = 48.
  • Pest responsible for silvering and necrosis of flower buds in roses and carnations is Thrips (Frankliniella occidentalis).
  • Optimum cold storage temperature for most tropical and subtropical cut flowers is maintained strictly between 2°C and 4°C.
⚠️ Common Mistakes & Exam Pitfalls
  • Confusing corms (Gladiolus) with bulbs (Tuberose or Onion) or tubers (Dahlia). Gladiolus propagates via corms, while Tuberose propagates via single-bulb divisions.
  • Assuming marigold is propagated vegetatively; marigold is strictly a seed-propagated annual flower crop.
  • Forgetting that Chrysanthemum morifolium is a hexaploid species with a somatic chromosome number of 2n = 54.
  • Confusing pinching with disbudding: Pinching is the removal of shoot tips for lateral branching, whereas disbudding is the removal of lateral buds to obtain a single large exhibition flower.

⚡ Chapter Master Summary: Comprehensive Study Material on Commercial Floriculture

Systematic concept review designed for quick retention and last-minute pre-exam revision.

Concept 1

Botanical Classification and Propagation Systems

Commercial flower production relies heavily on correct taxonomic identification and precise vegetative or sexual propagation techniques.

  • Asexual Propagation: Utilized for high-value uniform cut flowers such as roses (T-budding), carnations, and chrysanthemums (terminal cuttings).
  • Underground Structures: Corms serve as primary propagation units for Gladiolus, requiring vernalization or rest period management.
Concept 2

Environmental Control and Photoperiodism

Photoperiodic response dictates flower bud differentiation in many floricultural crops, determining market timing and greenhouse operational schedules.

  • Short-Day Plants (SDP): Chrysanthemum requires nights longer than critical dark periods (or black cloth treatment) to initiate flowering.
  • Long-Day Plants (LDP): Carnations and certain summer annuals respond favorably to extended photoperiods.
Concept 3

Cultural Operations: Pinching and Disbudding

Canopy architecture management is critical for optimizing flower yield and stem length in commercial floriculture.

  • Pinching: Removal of apical meristem to break apical dominance and stimulate axillary bud growth.
  • Disbudding: Selective removal of side buds or main terminal buds depending on whether spray or standard flower market standards are desired.
Concept 4

Post-Harvest Physiology and Senescence Control

Extending vase life involves managing respiration, water relations, and ethylene sensitivity post-harvest.

  • Ethylene Action Inhibitors: Silver Thiosulfate (STS) blocks ethylene receptors, dramatically preventing shattering and petal fall in carnations.
  • Bactericides: 8-Hydroxyquinoline Citrate (8-HQC) prevents microbial vascular blockage in cut flower stems.

📚 Technical Terms Glossary

Floriculture (Branch of Horticulture)
The branch of horticulture concerned with the cultivation, processing, and marketing of flowering and ornamental plants.
Pinching (Apical Bud Removal)
The removal of the terminal growing tip of a shoot to break apical dominance and promote lateral branching.
Disbudding (Lateral Bud Removal)
The practice of removing lateral flower buds to encourage the development of one large, exhibition-quality terminal bloom.
Corm (Modified Underground Stem)
A short, swollen underground stem storage organ surrounded by dry scale leaves, characteristic of Gladiolus.
Blindness (Physiological Flower Failure)
A physiological disorder in roses characterized by the failure of flower shoots to develop terminal flower buds.
Bullhead (Deformed Rose Flower)
A deformed, flat-topped rose flower resulting from extreme temperature fluctuations or abnormal cell division.
Calyx Splitting (Carnation Malformation)
The bursting of the calyx tube in carnation flowers, caused by low night temperatures and excess nitrogen application.
Pulsing (High Concentration Chemical Treatment)
Treating freshly harvested cut flowers with high concentrations of sugar and germicides/ethylene inhibitors for a short duration.
Silver Thiosulfate (STS Solution)
An anti-ethylene chemical compound used as a standard pulsing agent for carnations and roses.
Rooging (Off-type Elimination)
The removal of diseased, damaged, or genetically off-type plants from a seed crop or propagation bed.
Hardening (Acclimatization Process)
Gradual exposure of nursery-grown seedlings or rooted cuttings to outdoor field conditions prior to transplanting.

⚖️ Concepts Often Confused in Examinations

Corm VS Bulb

Corm is a solid swollen stem base (Gladiolus), whereas a bulb consists of modified fleshy leaf scales attached to a short stem plate (Tuberose).

Pinching VS Pruning

Pinching is the removal of herbaceous shoot tips during early growth, while pruning involves cutting back woody stems annually to stimulate new flowering flushes.

Question 1 of 20 Conceptual MCQ

Which rootstock is most commonly used for commercial rose budding in India?

Explanation: Rosa indica var. odorata is widely used as a standard rootstock in India due to its excellent compatibility, ease of propagation through cuttings, and adaptability to diverse soil conditions.

Question 2 of 20 Conceptual MCQ

What is the somatic chromosome number (2n) of commercial Chrysanthemum (Chrysanthemum morifolium)?

Explanation: Commercial chrysanthemum (Chrysanthemum morifolium) is a hexaploid species with a basic chromosome number x = 9, resulting in a somatic chromosome number of 2n = 6x = 54.

Question 3 of 20 Conceptual MCQ

Which of the following flowers is commercially propagated through underground corms?

Explanation: Gladiolus is commercially propagated using corms and cormels. In contrast, roses and carnations utilize stem cuttings or budding, and chrysanthemums use terminal stem cuttings.

Question 4 of 20 Conceptual MCQ

What is the primary cause of the physiological disorder known as 'Bullhead' in roses?

Explanation: Bullhead in roses is characterized by a flat-topped, malformed center caused by sudden drops in temperature, low light intensity, or severe root restriction during bud development.

Question 5 of 20 Conceptual MCQ

Which chemical is standardly used as an anti-ethylene pulsing agent for carnations?

Explanation: Silver Thiosulfate (STS) is widely used to pulse carnations and roses to inhibit ethylene action, thereby preventing petal shattering and sleepiness in cut flowers.

Question 6 of 20 Conceptual MCQ

African marigold belongs to which botanical family?

Explanation: African marigold (Tagetes erecta) belongs to the family Asteraceae (formerly Compositae), characterized by its composite flower head (capitulum).

Question 7 of 20 Conceptual MCQ

What is the somatic chromosome number of African marigold (Tagetes erecta)?

Explanation: African marigold (Tagetes erecta) is a diploid species with 2n = 24, whereas French marigold (Tagetes patula) is a tetraploid with 2n = 48.

Question 8 of 20 Conceptual MCQ

What is the primary purpose of 'pinching' in chrysanthemum cultivation?

Explanation: Pinching removes the apical growing tip, breaking apical dominance and encouraging the growth of numerous lateral shoots, which significantly increases flower yield per plant.

Question 9 of 20 Conceptual MCQ

Calyx splitting is a major economic physiological disorder in which of the following cut flower crops?

Explanation: Calyx splitting is a severe disorder in carnations (Dianthus caryophyllus) triggered by temperature fluctuations, high nitrogen levels, and boron deficiency during calyx development.

Question 10 of 20 Conceptual MCQ

At what stage should Gladiolus spikes be harvested for distant markets?

Explanation: Gladiolus spikes are harvested when the bottom one or two florets show color but have not yet opened. This ensures optimal opening of upper florets after harvest during transport and storage.

Question 11 of 20 Conceptual MCQ

What is the recommended germicide concentration commonly added to vase water to prevent vascular blockage?

Explanation: 8-Hydroxyquinoline Citrate (8-HQC) at 200 ppm is a standard antimicrobial compound added to vase water to prevent microbial proliferation and subsequent vascular blockage in cut stems.

Question 12 of 20 Conceptual MCQ

Which insect pest is primarily responsible for causing silvering and distortion of flower buds in roses?

Explanation: Thrips (such as Frankliniella occidentalis) feed by rasping plant tissue and sucking cell sap, causing characteristic silvery-white patches and deformed, unmarketable flower buds.

Question 13 of 20 Conceptual MCQ

What type of photoperiodic response does Chrysanthemum exhibit?

Explanation: Chrysanthemums are classic qualitative short-day plants (SDP), requiring dark periods exceeding a critical length to initiate and develop flower buds.

Question 14 of 20 Conceptual MCQ

What is the ideal soil pH range for successful commercial floriculture production?

Explanation: Most commercial flower crops thrive best in well-drained loamy soils with a pH range of 6.0 to 7.5, where nutrient availability is optimal and root diseases are minimized.

Question 15 of 20 Conceptual MCQ

Which propagation method is universally adopted for commercial rose plantations?

Explanation: Shield budding or T-budding is the standard commercial method for roses, joining a superior scion variety onto a robust, disease-resistant rootstock.

Question 16 of 20 Conceptual MCQ

What is the typical recommended spacing for standard cut roses grown in open fields?

Explanation: Open-field standard cut roses are typically planted at a spacing of 60 cm × 30 cm or 45 cm × 45 cm to ensure proper sunlight interception and air circulation.

Question 17 of 20 Conceptual MCQ

Which fungus causes Powdery Mildew in roses?

Explanation: Powdery mildew in roses is caused by the ectoparasitic fungus Podosphaera pannosa, which coats foliage and buds with white, powdery conidial spores.

Question 18 of 20 Conceptual MCQ

What is the primary function of sucrose in vase solutions for cut flowers?

Explanation: Sucrose added to vase solutions serves as an essential carbohydrate energy source to sustain cellular respiration and enable tight flower buds to open fully.

Question 19 of 20 Conceptual MCQ

Which of the following is an example of a bulbous ornamental plant cultivated for cut flowers?

Explanation: Tuberose (Polianthes tuberosa) is a classic bulbous ornamental crop propagated via true bulbs, whereas Gladiolus uses corms.

Question 20 of 20 Conceptual MCQ

What is the optimum cold storage temperature range for prolonging the post-harvest shelf life of tropical cut flowers?

Explanation: Maintaining cut flowers at 2°C to 4°C significantly slows down respiration, transpiration, and ethylene production, thereby doubling or tripling their vase life.

Test Question 1 of 10 TGT Standard

In commercial rose cultivation, what is the agronomic significance of ensuring the bud union remains 2 to 5 cm above the soil line?

Solution & Rationale: Keeping the bud union above ground prevents the scion from developing its own roots and checks excessive rootstock sucker growth, maintaining the desired cultivar characteristics.

Test Question 2 of 10 PGT Standard

Why is disbudding practiced extensively in standard chrysanthemum and carnation production?

Solution & Rationale: Disbudding involves removing lateral flower buds so that all plant assimilates are channeled into developing one large, premium terminal flower.

Test Question 3 of 10 PGT Standard

Which of the following physiological disorders in carnation is directly correlated with low night temperatures followed by excessive nitrogen fertilization?

Solution & Rationale: Low night temperatures induce abnormal petal proliferation inside the bud, which, when combined with high nitrogen vigor, causes the calyx tube to split open prematurely.

Test Question 4 of 10 PGT Standard

What is the exact botanical role of Silver Thiosulfate (STS) in cut flower preservation?

Solution & Rationale: STS releases silver ions that permanently block ethylene receptor sites in plant tissues, effectively stopping ethylene-induced senescence, abscission, and sleepiness.

Test Question 5 of 10 TGT Standard

What distinguishes Tagetes erecta from Tagetes patula in commercial floriculture?

Solution & Rationale: Tagetes erecta represents African marigold (diploid, 2n=24, large flowers), while Tagetes patula represents French marigold (tetraploid, 2n=48, dwarf habit with smaller flowers).

Test Question 6 of 10 TGT Standard

In greenhouse rose production, what is the primary agronomic hazard of overhead sprinkler irrigation?

Solution & Rationale: Overhead irrigation wets foliage and flowers, providing the free moisture film required for fungal pathogen germination and infection (e.g., powdery mildew and botrytis blight).

Test Question 7 of 10 PGT Standard

What is the primary objective of 'curing' gladiolus corms immediately after harvest?

Solution & Rationale: Curing corms at 25°C to 30°C for a short period dries out moist tissue, heals mechanical harvest wounds, and prevents fungal/bacterial pathogen entry during storage.

Test Question 8 of 10 PGT Standard

How does photoperiodic manipulation affect year-round production in Chrysanthemum morifolium?

Solution & Rationale: Being a short-day plant, chrysanthemums are kept vegetative using night-break lighting during long days, and forced into bloom using black cloth shading during short summer days.

Test Question 9 of 10 TGT Standard

What is the primary reason for including 8-Hydroxyquinoline Citrate (8-HQC) in floral preservative solutions?

Solution & Rationale: Bacteria multiplying in vase water produce slime and enzymes that clog xylem vessels, causing water stress. 8-HQC acts as a powerful germicide preventing vascular blockage.

Test Question 10 of 10 TGT Standard

Which of the following describes the correct harvesting maturity stage for standard cut roses?

Solution & Rationale: Standard cut roses are harvested when the bud is firm, sepals are reflexed, and outer petals are just beginning to unfurl, ensuring maximum post-harvest longevity and proper opening.
