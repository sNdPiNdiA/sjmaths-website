Chemical Properties of Soils: Soil Reaction, Cation Exchange Capacity, Base Saturation, and Redox Potentials

Fundamentals of Soil Reaction and Soil pH

Soil reaction is one of the master variables governing chemical, biological, and physical properties of soils. It refers to the degree of acidity or alkalinity of the soil mass, quantitatively expressed as the negative logarithm of hydrogen ion activity (pH = -\log10[H^+]).

  • Active Acidity: Refers to the concentration of hydrogen (H^+) and aluminum (Al3+) ions present in the soil solution at any given moment. It is measured directly using a glass electrode pH meter.
  • Reserve (Potential) Acidity: Represents the quantity of exchangeable H^+ and Al3+ ions held electrostatically on the colloidal exchange complex (clays and humus). It is vastly larger than active acidity and acts as a buffer.
  • Buffering Capacity: The ability of a soil to resist sudden changes in pH upon the addition of acidic or basic amendments. Fine-textured soils rich in 2:1 layer silicates and organic matter possess high buffering capacities, whereas coarse-textured sandy soils have low buffering capacities.

Cation Exchange Capacity (CEC) and Anion Exchange Capacity (AEC)

Cation Exchange Capacity (CEC) is the capacity of a soil to adsorb and exchange positively charged ions (Ca2+, Mg2+, K^+, Na^+, NH_4^+, H^+, Al3+). It is expressed in centimoles of charge per kilogram of dry soil (cmol(p^+)/kg or traditionally meq/100g).

  • Origin of Charges: Isomorphic substitution within clay mineral crystal lattices generates permanent negative charges (e.g., substitution of Mg2+ for Al3+ in octahedral sheets, or Al3+ for Si4+ in tetrahedral sheets). pH-dependent charges arise from the dissociation of functional groups (carboxyl and phenolic hydroxyls) on soil organic matter and exposed edges of silicate clays.
  • CEC Values of Colloids: Humus (100-300\;cmol/kg), Vermiculite (100-150\;cmol/kg), Smectite (80-120\;cmol/kg), Illite (20-40\;cmol/kg), Kaolinite (3-15\;cmol/kg).
  • Anion Exchange Capacity (AEC): The capacity of a soil to hold and exchange negatively charged anions (NO_3^-, Cl^-, SO_42-, H_2PO_4^-). It is prominent in highly weathered tropical soils (Oxisols and Ultisols) rich in sesquioxides (iron and aluminum oxides/hydroxides) under acidic pH conditions where protonation creates positive charges.

Base Saturation Percentage and Exchangeable Sodium Percentage (ESP)

Base saturation is a vital chemical fertility index that denotes the proportion of the soil CEC occupied by basic cations (Ca2+, Mg2+, K^+, Na^+) rather than acidic cations (H^+, Al3+).

  • Formula: Base Saturation (%) = ( Sum of Basic CationsCEC ) × 100.
  • Agronomic Significance: Soils with high base saturation (>80%) are typically neutral to alkaline and fertile. Soils with low base saturation (<50%) are acidic, heavily leached, and prone to aluminum toxicity and calcium/magnesium deficiencies.
  • Exchangeable Sodium Percentage (ESP): Measures the sodicity hazard of soils, calculated as ESP = ( Exchangeable Na^+CEC ) × 100. Soils with an ESP > 15 are classified as sodic (alkaline) soils, characterized by structural dispersion and poor hydraulic conductivity.

Oxidation-Reduction (Redox) Potential (Eh) in Submerged Soils

Redox potential (Eh), measured in millivolts (mV), reflects the intensity of oxidation or reduction reactions in soils, governed by the availability of electron acceptors.

  • Aerobic Soils: High Eh (+400 mV to +700 mV). Oxygen serves as the primary electron acceptor. Organic matter decomposes rapidly; nitrogen exists as nitrate (NO_3^-); iron is present in oxidized ferric (Fe3+) forms.
  • Submerged (Paddy) Soils: Low Eh (dropping below +300 mV down to -300 mV). Oxygen is rapidly depleted by microorganisms. Sequential reduction of alternative electron acceptors occurs: NO_3^- is reduced to N_2 gas (denitrification), Mn4+ is reduced to soluble Mn2+, Fe3+ is reduced to mobile Fe2+ (causing gleying), and sulfates are reduced to hydrogen sulfide (H_2S).
  • Nutrient Availability Dynamics: Submergence increases the availability of phosphorus due to the dissolution of iron phosphate complexes upon reduction of Fe3+ to Fe2+, but can lead to zinc deficiency due to the precipitation of zinc as frankinite or zinc sulfide.

Soil Salinity and Alkalinity Chemistry

Salt-affected soils are categorized based on three chemical parameters: Electrical Conductivity of the saturation extract (EC_e), soil pH, and Exchangeable Sodium Percentage (ESP).

  • Saline Soils: EC_e > 4\;dS/m, pH < 8.5, ESP < 15. Dominated by neutral soluble salts of chlorides and sulfates of sodium, calcium, and magnesium. White alkali crusts are common.
  • Sodic (Sodified/Alkaline) Soils: EC_e < 4\;dS/m, pH > 8.5, ESP > 15. Dominated by sodium carbonate and bicarbonate, leading to high hydroxyl ion concentrations and dispersion of soil colloids (black alkali).
  • Saline-Sodic Soils: EC_e > 4\;dS/m, pH < 8.5, ESP > 15. Properties are intermediate; if leached without calcium amendments, they convert into sodic soils. Reclamation requires gypsum (CaSO_4 · 2H_2O) for sodic soils to replace exchangeable sodium with calcium.

⚡ High-Yield Exam Points (UP TGT / PGT Focus)
  • Soil pH is defined as the negative logarithm of hydrogen ion activity: pH = -\log10[H^+].
  • Humus possesses the highest CEC among all soil colloids ranging between 100 to 300 cmol(p+)/kg.
  • Kaolinite is a 1:1 type expanding/non-expanding clay mineral with a low CEC of 3 to 15 cmol(p+)/kg.
  • Sodic soils are reclaimed using gypsum (CaSO_4 · 2H_2O), which supplies divalent calcium ions to replace sodium on the exchange complex.
  • Electrical Conductivity (EC) of saturation extract for saline soils is greater than 4 dS/m at 25°C.
  • Base saturation percentage is calculated as the sum of basic cations divided by CEC multiplied by 100.
  • Active acidity refers to hydrogen ions in the soil solution, whereas reserve acidity is held on exchange sites.
  • Submerged paddy soils exhibit reduced redox potentials (Eh), causing reduction of insoluble Fe3+ to soluble Fe2+.
⚠️ Common Mistakes & Exam Pitfalls
  • Confusing saline soils with sodic soils: Saline soils have pH < 8.5 and EC > 4 dS/m, whereas sodic soils have pH > 8.5 and ESP > 15.
  • Assuming kaolinite has a high CEC: Kaolinite has a very low CEC (3-15 cmol/kg) due to minimal isomorphic substitution and fixed 1:1 lattice structure.
  • Believing that active acidity is larger than reserve acidity: Reserve acidity is thousands of times larger than active acidity in mineral soils.

⚡ Chapter Master Summary: Chemical Properties of Soils: Soil Reaction, Cation Exchange Capacity, Base Saturation, and Redox Potentials

Systematic concept review designed for quick retention and last-minute pre-exam revision.

Concept 1

Soil Reaction and pH Buffering

Soil reaction dictates chemical solubility and microbial activity. pH values between 6.5 and 7.5 are optimal for most crops as macronutrients are most available.

  • Active Acidity: Instantaneous H^+ activity measured in soil solution.
  • Buffering Range: Carbonates buffer high pH, while alumino-silicates and organic matter buffer low pH.
Concept 2

Mechanisms of Cation Exchange

Cation exchange is stoichiometric, reversible, and rapid. Lyotropic series dictates the adsorption affinity of cations: Al3+ > Ca2+ > Mg2+ > K^+ > Na^+.

  • CEC: Total capacity of soil to hold exchangeable cations expressed in cmol(p^+)/kg.
  • Isomorphic Substitution: Replacement of ions in crystal lattices without changing crystal structure, generating permanent charge.
Concept 3

Base Saturation and Soil Fertility

Base saturation indicates soil leaching status and inherent nutrient reserve. High base saturation ensures adequate calcium, magnesium, and potassium availability.

  • Base Saturation: Ratio of basic cations to total CEC expressed as a percentage.
  • Leaching Effect: High rainfall leaches basic cations, replacing them with acidic H^+ and Al3+ ions.
Concept 4

Redox Potential in Waterlogged Soils

Submergence induces anaerobic respiration by microbes, transforming chemical equilibria from oxidizing to reducing environments.

  • Eh Value: Decreases sharply upon flooding due to oxygen exhaustion.
  • Denitrification: Nitrate acts as an electron acceptor, reducing to gaseous nitrogen (N_2 and N_2O).
Concept 5

Reclamation Chemistry of Salt-Affected Soils

Chemical amendments are mandatory for sodic soil reclamation to neutralize alkalinity and replace exchangeable sodium.

  • Gypsum Requirement: Calculated based on exchangeable sodium to be replaced.
  • Leaching Requirement: Fraction of irrigation water required to leach excess soluble salts below the root zone in saline soils.

📚 Technical Terms Glossary

Soil pH (pH = -log[H+])
Negative logarithm of hydrogen ion activity indicating soil acidity or alkalinity.
Cation Exchange Capacity (CEC)
Total sum of exchangeable cations a soil can adsorb, expressed in cmol(p+)/kg.
Base Saturation (BS %)
Percentage of soil CEC occupied by basic cations (Ca, Mg, K, Na).
Exchangeable Sodium Percentage (ESP)
Percentage of CEC occupied by sodium ions; ESP > 15 indicates sodic soil.
Electrical Conductivity (ECe)
Measure of ionic concentration in soil water extract, expressed in dS/m at 25°C.
Redox Potential (Eh)
Electrochemical potential measuring oxidation-reduction status of soil in millivolts.
Active Acidity (Solution H+ activity)
Concentration of hydrogen ions present directly in the soil aqueous phase.
Reserve Acidity (Adsorbed H+ and Al3+)
Potential acidity held on exchange sites of soil colloids.
Isomorphic Substitution (Crystal lattice substitution)
Replacement of one ion by another of similar size in clay mineral layers without structural disruption.
Anion Exchange Capacity (AEC)
Capacity of positively charged soil colloids to adsorb and exchange anions.
Lyotropic Series (Cation replacement order)
Ranking of cations by their adsorption affinity onto exchange sites.

🎯 Must-Remember High-Frequency Facts

  • Gypsum (CaSO_4 · 2H_2O) is the standard chemical amendment for sodic soils.
  • The lyotropic adsorption series for divalent/monovalent cations is Al3+ > Ca2+ > Mg2+ > K^+ > Na^+.
  • Permanent charges on clay minerals arise from isomorphic substitution.
  • Saline-sodic soils possess both EC_e > 4\;dS/m and ESP > 15.

📌 Quick Recap Bullet Points

  • Soil pH controls nutrient solubility, with 6.5-7.5 being optimal.
  • Humus has the highest CEC (100-300\;cmol/kg), while kaolinite has the lowest (3-15\;cmol/kg).
  • Saline soils have EC_e > 4\;dS/m, pH < 8.5, and ESP < 15.
  • Sodic soils have EC_e < 4\;dS/m, pH > 8.5, and ESP > 15, requiring gypsum for reclamation.
  • Flooding reduces soil redox potential (Eh), converting Fe3+ to soluble Fe2+.

⚖️ Concepts Often Confused in Examinations

Saline Soil VS Sodic Soil

Saline soils have high neutral salts and pH < 8.5, whereas sodic soils have high sodium carbonate/bicarbonate and pH > 8.5.

Active Acidity VS Reserve Acidity

Active acidity is in soil solution (measured by pH meter), whereas reserve acidity is bound to colloids and buffers active acidity.

Question 1 of 18 Conceptual MCQ

What is the primary source of permanent negative charge in 2:1 layer silicate clay minerals?

Explanation: Permanent negative charges on 2:1 silicate clays (e.g., smectite, vermiculite) originate from isomorphic substitution, such as Mg2+ replacing Al3+ in the octahedral sheet.

Question 2 of 18 Conceptual MCQ

Which of the following soil colloids exhibits the highest Cation Exchange Capacity (CEC)?

Explanation: Humus possesses the highest CEC, ranging between 100 to 300 cmol(p+)/kg due to numerous exposed pH-dependent functional groups like carboxyl and phenolic hydroxyls.

Question 3 of 18 Conceptual MCQ

What is the defining chemical threshold for classifying a soil as a saline soil according to standard soil science?

Explanation: Saline soils are characterized by an Electrical Conductivity of saturation extract (EC_e) greater than 4 dS/m, pH less than 8.5, and Exchangeable Sodium Percentage (ESP) less than 15.

Question 4 of 18 Conceptual MCQ

Which chemical amendment is universally recommended for the reclamation of sodic (alkaline) soils?

Explanation: Gypsum supplies Ca2+ ions which replace exchangeable Na^+ on the soil exchange complex, allowing sodium to be leached out as sodium sulfate.

Question 5 of 18 Conceptual MCQ

In the lyotropic series of cation adsorption, which cation is held most strongly by soil colloids?

Explanation: The lyotropic series of adsorption affinity is Al3+ > Ca2+ > Mg2+ > K^+ > Na^+. Trivalent aluminum and divalent calcium are held much more tightly than monovalent sodium.

Question 6 of 18 Conceptual MCQ

What happens to the redox potential (Eh) of a soil when it is permanently submerged under water for paddy cultivation?

Explanation: Flooding creates anaerobic conditions where oxygen is depleted, leading to a sharp drop in redox potential (Eh) down to low positive or negative values.

Question 7 of 18 Conceptual MCQ

Active acidity in soils is best described as:

Explanation: Active acidity represents the concentration of free H^+ (and associated Al3+) ions in the soil solution, measured directly by a pH meter.

Question 8 of 18 Conceptual MCQ

What is the typical CEC range (in cmol(p+)/kg) of Kaolinite clay?

Explanation: Kaolinite is a 1:1 layer silicate with hydrogen bonding between layers, restricting expansion and resulting in a low CEC of 3 to 15 cmol(p+)/kg.

Question 9 of 18 Conceptual MCQ

How is Base Saturation Percentage calculated?

Explanation: Base Saturation % = (Sum of exchangeable basic cations [Ca2+, Mg2+, K^+, Na^+] / CEC) × 100.

Question 10 of 18 Conceptual MCQ

Which iron transformation occurs when a soil becomes severely reduced due to waterlogging?

Explanation: Under reduced (anaerobic) conditions, Fe3+ acts as an electron acceptor and is reduced to mobile, soluble Fe2+, causing characteristic gray/gleyed soil colors.

Question 11 of 18 Conceptual MCQ

What is the primary characteristic of a sodic soil in terms of physical structure?

Explanation: High exchangeable sodium causes the dispersion of soil colloids, destroying soil structure, causing surface crusting, and drastically reducing water infiltration.

Question 12 of 18 Conceptual MCQ

What causes pH-dependent (variable) charges on soil colloids?

Explanation: pH-dependent charges arise from the ionization (dissociation) of hydrogen ions from hydroxyl and carboxyl groups on organic matter and broken edges of silicate clays as pH increases.

Question 13 of 18 Conceptual MCQ

Which of the following clay minerals is classified as a 2:1 expanding type with a high CEC?

Explanation: Smectite (montmorillonite) is a 2:1 expanding layer silicate clay with extensive isomorphic substitution, giving it a high CEC of 80 to 120 cmol(p+)/kg.

Question 14 of 18 Conceptual MCQ

What is the term for the ability of a soil to resist changes in pH when acids or bases are added?

Explanation: Soil buffering capacity refers to the soil's ability to resist pH fluctuations, largely governed by reserve acidity, CEC, and calcium carbonate reserves.

Question 15 of 18 Conceptual MCQ

Under extremely acidic soil conditions (pH < 4.5), which toxic ion predominantly limits plant root growth?

Explanation: At pH below 5.0, aluminum hydroxide minerals dissolve, releasing toxic trivalent aluminum (Al3+) ions into the soil solution which inhibit root elongation.

Question 16 of 18 Conceptual MCQ

What is the typical pH range of sodic (alkaline) soils?

Explanation: Sodic soils typically exhibit high pH values ranging from 8.5 to 10.0 due to the presence of sodium carbonate and bicarbonate hydrolysis.

Question 17 of 18 Conceptual MCQ

Anion Exchange Capacity (AEC) is most pronounced in which type of soils?

Explanation: AEC is prominent in highly weathered tropical soils (Oxisols, Ultisols) containing iron and aluminum oxides (sesquioxides) that develop positive charges at low pH.

Question 18 of 18 Conceptual MCQ

What is the primary electron acceptor reduced immediately after oxygen is depleted in a flooded soil?

Explanation: In the sequential reduction of electron acceptors in flooded soils, nitrate (NO_3^-) is reduced to nitrogen gas via denitrification immediately after oxygen (O_2) is exhausted.

Test Question 1 of 10 PGT Standard

Calculate the Exchangeable Sodium Percentage (ESP) of a soil having a Cation Exchange Capacity (CEC) of 25 cmol(p+)/kg and an exchangeable sodium content of 5.0 cmol(p+)/kg.

Solution & Rationale: ESP = (Exchangeable Na+ / CEC) × 100 = (5.0 / 25) × 100 = 20%. Since ESP > 15, this soil is classified as sodic.

Test Question 2 of 10 TGT Standard

Which of the following lists soil colloids in correct increasing order of their Cation Exchange Capacity (CEC)?

Solution & Rationale: Kaolinite (3-15) < Illite (20-40) < Smectite (80-120) < Humus (100-300 cmol/kg).

Test Question 3 of 10 PGT Standard

What is the primary chemical reaction responsible for the high pH (>8.5) of sodic soils?

Solution & Rationale: Sodium carbonate (Na_2CO_3) hydrolyzes in water to form sodium hydroxide and bicarbonate, releasing OH^- ions which elevate soil pH.

Test Question 4 of 10 TGT Standard

In acid soil management, why is agricultural limestone applied?

Solution & Rationale: Liming neutralizes H^+ and Al3+ ions, raises base saturation, eliminates aluminum toxicity, and provides essential calcium.

Test Question 5 of 10 TGT Standard

Which soil property is directly measured by the Electrical Conductivity (EC) of a saturated soil paste extract?

Solution & Rationale: Electrical conductivity measures the ability of a soil solution extract to conduct electrical current, which is directly proportional to the total concentration of dissolved soluble salts.

Test Question 6 of 10 PGT Standard

What is the theoretical gypsum requirement concept when reclaiming sodic soils?

Solution & Rationale: Gypsum requirement is calculated to neutralize soluble sodium carbonate and replace excess exchangeable sodium on the exchange complex with calcium.

Test Question 7 of 10 PGT Standard

Why does phosphorus availability often increase when a submerged paddy soil becomes reduced?

Solution & Rationale: Under reduced conditions, insoluble ferric iron (Fe3+) in iron phosphate complexes is reduced to soluble ferrous (Fe2+), releasing phosphate ions into the soil solution.

Test Question 8 of 10 PGT Standard

What distinguishes saline-sodic soils from purely saline soils?

Solution & Rationale: Both possess high EC (>4 dS/m), but saline-sodic soils also have an Exchangeable Sodium Percentage (ESP) > 15, making them susceptible to sodification if leached improperly.

Test Question 9 of 10 PGT Standard

Which layer silicate clay mineral possesses a 2:1 expanding crystal lattice structure with significant isomorphic substitution in the tetrahedral sheet?

Solution & Rationale: Illite (hydrous mica) is a 2:1 expanding/semi-expanding clay characterized by potassium ions holding silicate layers together and substitution of aluminum for silicon in tetrahedral sheets.

Test Question 10 of 10 TGT Standard

What is the primary effect of high active acidity on plant nutrient availability?

Solution & Rationale: In strongly acidic soils (low pH), phosphorus is strongly fixed by soluble iron and aluminum oxides, rendering it unavailable to plants.
