Introduction to Soil Fertility and Soil Productivity

Soil fertility is defined as the inherent capacity of a soil to supply essential plant nutrients in adequate amounts and in proper proportions for the growth of plants, provided other factors such as light, temperature, water, and physical condition of soil are favorable. It is a dynamic property of soil that can be managed and improved through agronomic interventions.

  • Soil Productivity: A broader term that refers to the capacity of a soil to produce a specified crop or sequence of crops under a specified system of management. All productive soils are fertile, but all fertile soils may not be productive due to limiting factors like waterlogging, acidity, or toxic salinity.
  • Essential Plant Nutrients: Arnon and Stout (1939) established criteria of essentiality: (1) A plant must be unable to complete its life cycle in the absence of the element; (2) The function of the element must be specific and non-replaceable by another element; (3) The element must be directly involved in plant nutrition and metabolism.
  • Macronutrients vs Micronutrients: Essential elements are broadly divided into macronutrients (required in larger amounts, e.g., N, P, K, Ca, Mg, S) and micronutrients or trace elements (required in trace amounts, e.g., Fe, Mn, Cu, Zn, B, Mo, Cl, Ni).

Classification and Functions of Essential Plant Nutrients

Out of 17 essential plant nutrients recognized currently, carbon (C), hydrogen (H), and oxygen (O) are derived from air and water, acting as structural components. The remaining 14 are derived from the soil solid phase. They are systematically categorized based on their physiological roles and quantitative requirements.

  • Primary Nutrients: Nitrogen (N), Phosphorus (P), and Potassium (K) are referred to as fertilizer elements or primary macro-nutrients because they are most frequently deficient in cultivated soils.
  • Secondary Nutrients: Calcium (Ca), Magnesium (Mg), and Sulfur (S) play vital roles in cell wall stability, enzymatic activation, and amino acid synthesis.
  • Mobile vs Immobile Nutrients: Highly mobile nutrients (N, P, K, Mg) show deficiency symptoms on older leaves first because the plant translocates them to growing meristemic tissues. Immobile nutrients (Ca, S, Fe, Mn, Cu, B) display deficiency symptoms on younger developing leaves.
  • Beneficial Elements: Elements like Sodium (Na), Silicon (Si), Cobalt (Co), and Vanadium (V) are beneficial for specific plant species (e.g., Silicon in Oryza sativa for lodging resistance).

Soil Organic Matter and Soil Humus

Soil organic matter (SOM) is the lifeblood of soil fertility. It encompasses all organic components of the soil including living biomass, fresh plant residues, and well-decomposed amorphous colloidal substances known as humus.

  • C:N Ratio: The carbon-to-nitrogen ratio of soil organic matter typically ranges around 10:1 to 12:1 in stable humus. A wide C:N ratio (>30:1) leads to microbial immobilization of available soil nitrogen, whereas a narrow ratio (<20:1) promotes net mineralization and release of plant-available nitrate and ammonium.
  • Cation Exchange Capacity (CEC): Humus possesses a very high CEC (150–300 cmol(p+)/kg) due to the presence of carboxylic and phenolic functional groups, which greatly enhances the nutrient retention capacity of sandy and weathered soils.
  • Soil Aggregation: Polysaccharides, glomalin, and fungal hyphae associated with organic matter decomposition stabilize soil aggregates, improving soil aeration, infiltration, and root penetration.

Nutrient Dynamics, Fixation, and Losses in Soils

Nutrients applied through fertilizers undergo complex physical, chemical, and biological transformations within the soil matrix, affecting their use efficiency and availability to crop plants.

  • Nitrogen Transformations: Urea undergoes enzymatic hydrolysis by urease enzyme to form ammonium carbonate, which volatilizes as ammonia gas under alkaline soil conditions. Ammonium (NH_4^+) is subsequently oxidized by autotrophic bacteria (Nitrosomonas and Nitrobacter) to nitrate (NO_3^-) via nitrification. Nitrate is highly prone to leaching loss in coarse-textured soils and denitrification losses under waterlogged anaerobic conditions.
  • Phosphorus Fixation: Phosphorus reacts with iron and aluminum oxides in acidic soils to form insoluble iron and aluminum phosphates, whereas in alkaline calcareous soils, it precipitates as insoluble calcium phosphates, resulting in low phosphorus use efficiency (PUE, typically 15-20%).
  • Potassium Dynamics: Soil potassium exists in four distinct pools: water-soluble, exchangeable, non-exchangeable (fixed in 2:1 expanding clay interlayers like illite), and mineral lattice potassium.

Soil Testing, Fertilizer Recommendation, and Nutrient Management

Rational fertilizer use relies on accurate soil testing, plant tissue analysis, and scientific nutrient budgeting to optimize crop yield and prevent environmental pollution.

  • Soil Sampling: Composite soil samples are collected randomly from a depth of 0–15 cm for field crops using a soil auger or V-shaped notch method, ensuring representation of the entire management unit.
  • Fertilizer Calculations: Nutrient requirements are calculated using target yield equations or state-specific recommendations. For example, applying urea (CO(NH_2)_2, containing 46% N) to meet a nitrogen recommendation of 120 kg N/ha requires: Urea dose = 120 × 10046 = 260.8 kg/ha.
  • Integrated Nutrient Management (INM): The simultaneous use of chemical fertilizers, organic manures (FYM, vermicompost), green manuring (Sesbania rostrata), and biofertilizers (Rhizobium, Azotobacter, Phosphat solubilizing bacteria) to sustain long-term soil health and productivity.

⚡ High-Yield Exam Points (UP TGT / PGT Focus)
  • Arnon and Stout (1939) proposed the criteria of essentiality for plant nutrients, originally consisting of 16 elements, later updated to 17 with the inclusion of Nickel (Ni) by Brown et al. in 1987.
  • Urea contains 46% Nitrogen, whereas Anhydrous Ammonia contains the highest nitrogen concentration at 82%.
  • Single Superphosphate (SSP) contains 16% P2O5, 12% Sulfur, and 19% Calcium, making it ideal for sulfur-deficient soils.
  • C:N ratio of 10:1 to 12:1 is characteristic of well-humified soil organic matter.
  • Denitrification in waterlogged soils is mediated by anaerobic bacteria such as Pseudomonas and Thiobacillus, converting nitrate into gaseous N2O and N2.
  • Molybdenum (Mo) is the only micronutrient whose availability increases with an increase in soil pH (liming).
  • Zinc deficiency in paddy soils causes 'Khaira disease', which was first discovered by Y.L. Nene at Pantnagar in 1966.
  • Boron deficiency causes 'heart rot' in sugarbeet and 'brown heart' in cauliflower.
  • Cation Exchange Capacity (CEC) is highest in organic humus and 2:1 expanding clay minerals like montmorillonite.
  • Critical limit of available Olsen-P in soils is generally taken as 10 kg P/ha.
⚠️ Common Mistakes & Exam Pitfalls
  • Confusing soil fertility with soil productivity. (Correction: Fertility is the nutrient supply capacity, whereas productivity is the overall crop yield output under specific management.)
  • Believing that all essential elements are derived from soil. (Correction: C, H, and O are obtained from air and water.)
  • Assuming mobile nutrient deficiency appears on younger leaves. (Correction: Highly mobile nutrients like N, P, K show deficiency symptoms on older leaves first.)

⚡ Chapter Master Summary: Soil Fertility and Nutrient Management

Systematic concept review designed for quick retention and last-minute pre-exam revision.

Concept 1

Criteria of Essentiality

The framework established by Arnon and Stout governing plant nutrient essentiality.

  • Arnon & Stout Criteria: Specificity, completion of life cycle, and direct metabolic involvement.
  • Total Nutrients: Exactly 17 recognized essential elements.
Concept 2

Nutrient Mobility and Deficiency Symptoms

Understanding internal translocation of mineral ions within the plant vascular system.

  • Mobile Elements: N, P, K, Mg show chlorosis and necrosis on older leaves.
  • Immobile Elements: Ca, B, Fe show terminal bud death and interveinal chlorosis on young leaves.
Concept 3

Soil Organic Matter and Humus Dynamics

The transformation of organic residues into stable colloidal humus.

  • C:N Ratio: Optimum ratio around 10:1; wide ratio causes temporary nitrogen immobilization.
  • Humus CEC: High buffering capacity and cation exchange capacity (150-300 cmol/kg).
Concept 4

Fertilizer Nutrient Calculations

Quantitative agronomic mathematics for fertilizer application.

  • Urea Conversion: 100 kg urea supplies 46 kg actual N.
  • DAP Conversion: Supplies 18% N and 46% P2O5.

📚 Technical Terms Glossary

Soil Fertility (Inherent Nutrient Capacity)
The capacity of a soil to supply essential nutrients in adequate amounts and balanced proportions.
Soil Productivity (Crop Yield Capacity)
The capacity of soil to produce crops under specified management systems.
C:N Ratio (Carbon to Nitrogen Ratio)
The proportion of organic carbon to total nitrogen in soil organic matter or residues.
Cation Exchange Capacity (CEC)
The total capacity of a soil to hold exchangeable cations, expressed in cmol(p+)/kg.
Nitrification (Biological Oxidation of Ammonia)
Microbial conversion of ammonium ions to nitrate via nitrite by Nitrosomonas and Nitrobacter.
Denitrification (Reduction of Nitrate to Nitrogen Gas)
Microbial reduction of soil nitrate under anaerobic conditions resulting in nitrogen gas loss.
Mineralization (Organic to Inorganic Conversion)
Microbial breakdown of organic compounds releasing inorganic plant-available ions.
Immobilization (Inorganic to Organic Assimilation)
Conversion of inorganic available nutrients into microbial biomass or organic forms.
Khaira Disease (Zinc Deficiency in Oryza sativa)
A physiological disorder in rice caused by zinc deficiency, characterized by rusty brown spots on leaves.
Blossom-End Rot (Calcium Deficiency in Solanaceous Crops)
Physiological breakdown of fruit tissue at the distal end due to localized calcium deficiency.
Green Manuring (In-situ Incorporation of Biomass)
Ploughing under of soft green plant tissues (e.g., Sesbania aculeata) to enrich soil N and organic matter.

  • Molybdenum availability increases in alkaline soils.
  • Optimum C:N ratio for soil humus is 10:1.

📌 Quick Recap Bullet Points

  • Soil fertility is dynamic, whereas soil productivity is comprehensive and yield-oriented.
  • Arnon and Stout established the 17 essential plant nutrient criteria.
  • Mobile nutrients show deficiency on older leaves; immobile nutrients show deficiency on younger leaves.
  • Urea contains 46% N; DAP contains 18% N and 46% P2O5.
  • Zinc deficiency causes Khaira disease in paddy.

⚖️ Concepts Often Confused in Examinations

Soil Fertility VS Soil Productivity

Fertility is strictly about nutrient supply potential; productivity includes all environmental and management factors influencing total yield.

Mineralization VS Immobilization

Mineralization releases inorganic nutrients from organic matter, whereas immobilization ties up inorganic nutrients into microbial cells.

Question 1 of 20 Conceptual MCQ

Who proposed the criteria of essentiality for plant nutrients in 1939?

Explanation: D.I. Arnon and P.L. Stout (1939) formulated the criteria of essentiality for plant mineral nutrition.

Question 2 of 20 Conceptual MCQ

How many essential plant nutrients are currently recognized globally in agricultural science?

Explanation: There are 17 essential plant nutrients recognized, including carbon, hydrogen, oxygen, macronutrients, and micronutrients including nickel.

Question 3 of 20 Conceptual MCQ

Which of the following elements is classified as a secondary macronutrient?

Explanation: Calcium, magnesium, and sulfur are secondary macronutrients. Nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium are primary macronutrients.

Question 4 of 20 Conceptual MCQ

Deficiency symptoms of mobile nutrients such as Nitrogen and Potassium first appear on:

Explanation: Mobile nutrients are translocated to young growing organs during deficiency, causing deficiency symptoms to manifest first on older leaves.

Question 5 of 20 Conceptual MCQ

What is the nitrogen percentage present in commercial grade Urea?

Explanation: Urea [CO(NH2)2] contains 46% nitrogen by weight.

Question 6 of 20 Conceptual MCQ

Khaira disease in paddy (rice) is caused by the deficiency of:

Explanation: Khaira disease in rice is caused by Zinc deficiency and is successfully corrected by spraying Zinc Sulfate (ZnSO4).

Question 7 of 20 Conceptual MCQ

What is the optimal C:N ratio of stable soil organic matter (humus)?

Explanation: The C:N ratio of stable soil humus typically ranges between 10:1 and 12:1.

Question 8 of 20 Conceptual MCQ

Which element's availability in soil increases with an increase in pH (liming)?

Explanation: Molybdenum availability increases as soil pH increases, unlike most micronutrients which become less available under alkaline conditions.

Question 9 of 20 Conceptual MCQ

Single Superphosphate (SSP) contains what percentage of Sulfur?

Explanation: SSP contains 16% P2O5, 12% Sulfur, and 19% Calcium.

Question 10 of 20 Conceptual MCQ

Which bacteria are responsible for the first step of biological nitrification in soils?

Explanation: Nitrosomonas oxidizes ammonium to nitrite, while Nitrobacter subsequently oxidizes nitrite to nitrate.

Question 11 of 20 Conceptual MCQ

Blossom-end rot in tomato and capsicum is a physiological disorder caused by deficiency of:

Explanation: Blossom-end rot is caused by localized calcium deficiency in developing fruit tissues.

Question 12 of 20 Conceptual MCQ

The conversion of inorganic nutrient ions into organic forms by soil microorganisms is called:

Explanation: Immobilization is the microbial assimilation of inorganic ions into organic microbial tissues, making them temporarily unavailable to plants.

Question 13 of 20 Conceptual MCQ

Which of the following is an immobile nutrient in plants?

Explanation: Calcium is an immobile nutrient; hence its deficiency symptoms appear on younger leaves and growing shoot tips.

Question 14 of 20 Conceptual MCQ

What is the primary form of phosphorus absorbed by plant roots from the soil solution?

Explanation: Plants absorb phosphorus predominantly as primary orthophosphate (H2PO4-) in acidic to neutral soils and secondary orthophosphate (HPO42-) in alkaline soils.

Question 15 of 20 Conceptual MCQ

The capacity of soil to hold exchangeable cations is known as:

Explanation: Cation Exchange Capacity (CEC) measures the total quantity of exchangeable cations a soil can adsorb.

Question 16 of 20 Conceptual MCQ

Which micronutrient is a structural component of the enzyme urease and nitrate reductase?

Explanation: Nickel is an essential component of urease, necessary for urea metabolism in higher plants.

Question 17 of 20 Conceptual MCQ

Under waterlogged anaerobic soil conditions, nitrate is lost into the atmosphere as nitrogen gas through:

Explanation: Denitrification is the biological reduction of nitrate to gaseous nitrogen (N2O and N2) by anaerobic bacteria in waterlogged soils.

Question 18 of 20 Conceptual MCQ

Which fertilizer has the highest nitrogen content among solid nitrogenous fertilizers?

Explanation: Anhydrous ammonia contains 82% nitrogen, the highest among all nitrogenous fertilizers.

Question 19 of 20 Conceptual MCQ

Heart rot in sugarbeet is caused by the deficiency of:

Explanation: Boron deficiency causes heart rot in sugarbeet and brown heart in cauliflower.

Question 20 of 20 Conceptual MCQ

The inherent capacity of a soil to supply plant nutrients in adequate amounts is termed:

Explanation: Soil fertility refers specifically to the nutrient-supplying capacity of the soil.

Calculate the quantity of Urea required to supply 60 kg of Nitrogen per hectare for a wheat crop.

Test Question 2 of 10 PGT Standard

Which of the following clay minerals possesses the highest Cation Exchange Capacity (CEC)?

Solution & Rationale: Montmorillonite (a 2:1 expanding clay) has a high CEC (80-150 cmol/kg) compared to illite (20-40) and kaolinite (3-15).

Test Question 3 of 10 PGT Standard

What is the primary mechanism of phosphorus fixation in acidic soils?

Solution & Rationale: In acidic soils, phosphate ions react with soluble iron and aluminum to form insoluble iron and aluminum phosphates.

Test Question 4 of 10 PGT Standard

Which of the following elements is strictly required for symbiotic nitrogen fixation in legumes as part of the nitrogenase enzyme complex?

Solution & Rationale: Molybdenum and Iron are crucial structural components of the nitrogenase enzyme complex responsible for biological nitrogen fixation.

Test Question 5 of 10 TGT Standard

A soil sample test indicates available phosphorus as 8 kg P/ha. According to standard rating limits, this soil is categorized as:

Solution & Rationale: Available P below 10 kg P/ha is rated as Low, 10-25 kg P/ha as Medium, and above 25 kg P/ha as High.

Test Question 6 of 10 PGT Standard

Which fertilizer leaves an acidic residual effect in agricultural soils upon continuous application?

Solution & Rationale: Ammonium sulfate leaves a strong acid residual effect, requiring approximately 1.1 kg of CaCO3 to neutralize the acidity caused by 1 kg of fertilizer.

Test Question 7 of 10 TGT Standard

Which form of potassium in soil is directly available for immediate plant uptake?

Solution & Rationale: Plants take up potassium from the water-soluble pool in soil solution and the exchangeable pool adsorbed on clay-humus complexes.

Test Question 8 of 10 PGT Standard

What is the critical deficiency limit of Zinc in soil extracted with DTPA solution?

Solution & Rationale: The critical limit for DTPA-extractable soil zinc is generally accepted as 0.6 ppm (mg/kg).

Test Question 9 of 10 TGT Standard

Which green manuring crop is exceptionally tolerant to waterlogged and saline-sodic soil conditions?

Solution & Rationale: Sesbania aculeata (Dhaincha) is highly tolerant to waterlogging, alkalinity, and salinity, making it ideal for green manuring.

Test Question 10 of 10 PGT Standard

The ratio of Carbon to Nitrogen (C:N) in microbial biomass typically averages around:

Solution & Rationale: Soil microbial biomass has an average C:N ratio of approximately 8:1.
