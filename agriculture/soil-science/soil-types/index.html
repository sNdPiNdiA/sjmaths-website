Soil Types of India: Classification, Distribution, and Agronomic Properties

An authoritative academic guide covering the classification, formation, distribution, physical-chemical properties, and agronomic management of major soil orders in India, specifically tailored for UP TGT and PGT Agriculture examinations.

Pedogenesis and Major Soil Orders of India

Pedogenesis, the process of soil formation, is governed by five classic factors formulated by Hans Jenny: parent material, climate, organisms, relief, and time. In the Indian subcontinent, the vast physiographic diversity translates into complex soil-forming environments. The Indian Council of Agricultural Research (ICAR) and the National Bureau of Soil Survey and Land Use Planning (NBSS&LUP), aligning with the United States Department of Agriculture (USDA) Soil Taxonomy, classify Indian soils into eight major soil orders: Inceptisols, Entisols, Alfisols, Vertisols, Aridisols, Ultisols, Mollisols, and Histosols.

  • Inceptisols: Representing the most widely distributed soil order in India (covering approximately 39.7% of the geographical area), these are young soils exhibiting moderate profile development with cambic subsurface horizons.
  • Entisols: Constituting about 28% of the total land area, these are recent, poorly developed mineral soils lacking diagnostic subsurface horizons, commonly found in floodplains and arid shifting sand dunes.
  • Alfisols: Comprising roughly 13.5% of Indian soils, these are fertile, clay-enriched sub-surface horizon (argillic) soils developed under forest or savanna vegetation in humid to sub-humid climates.

Alluvial Soils (Fluvisols / Inceptisols and Entisols)

Alluvial soils are deposited by fluvial action of major river systems including the Indo-Gangetic-Brahmaputra plains, as well as coastal deltas. They represent the agricultural backbone of India, covering nearly 43% of the total land area. Chemically, they are rich in potassium, lime, and phosphorus, but deficient in nitrogen and organic matter. Texturally, they range from sandy loam to clay loam.

  • Bangar: Older alluvial soil situated above the flood level, characterized by higher clay content, lower permeability, and the presence of calcium carbonate nodules known as kankar.
  • Khadar: Newer alluvial soil found in floodplains, inundated annually, highly fertile, fine-textured, and completely devoid of kankar nodules.
  • Crop Suitability: Ideal for intensive cultivation of Triticum aestivum (wheat), Oryza sativa (rice), Zea mays (maize), and various pulses under assured irrigation.

Black Soils (Vertisols)

Derived primarily from basaltic trap rock formations through weathering under semi-arid to sub-humid conditions, black soils are widely known as regur soils or black cotton soils. They cover approximately 16.6% of India's total area, predominantly concentrated in the Deccan plateau states of Maharashtra, Madhya Pradesh, Gujarat, and parts of Uttar Pradesh (Bundelkhand region).

  • Physical Characteristics: High smectite (montmorillonite) clay mineral content imparts pronounced shrink-swell properties. They become sticky and plastic when wet, and develop deep, wide desiccation cracks upon drying, facilitating self-mulching.
  • Chemical Profile: High cation exchange capacity (CEC), rich in calcium, magnesium, and iron, but deficient in nitrogen, phosphorus, and zinc.
  • Agronomic Management: Tillage operations must be meticulously timed; working when too wet causes puddling and compaction, while working when too dry requires excessive draft power.

Red and Laterite Soils (Alfisols and Ultisols)

Red soils develop over crystalline and metamorphic rocks in areas of low rainfall and high temperature, predominantly across the peninsular plateau, Tamil Nadu, Karnataka, and southern districts of Uttar Pradesh (Jhansi, Lalitpur, Mirzapur). Their characteristic red coloration is derived from ferric oxide coatings on mineral particles rather than high clay mineral concentration.

  • Laterite Soils: Formed under conditions of high temperature and torrential alternating wet and dry spells, leading to intense leaching of silica and accumulation of iron and aluminum oxides (gibbsite and goethite).
  • Nutrient Status: Highly acidic, extremely deficient in nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium, and calcium, requiring heavy liming and balanced fertilization for economic crop production.
  • Crop Adaptation: Suitable for drought-tolerant crops like Arachis hypogaea (groundnut), Eleusine coracana (finger millet), and plantation crops like Anacardium occidentale (cashew).

Problem Soils: Saline, Sodic, and Acid Soils

Abiotic stresses associated with chemical degradation severely limit agricultural productivity across millions of hectares in India. Reclamation and management require precise chemical amendments and hydrological interventions.

  • Saline Soils (Solanchaks): Characterized by electrical conductivity (EC) > 4 dS/m, exchangeable sodium percentage (ESP) < 15, and pH < 8.5. Dominated by neutral soluble salts (chlorides and sulfates of sodium, calcium, and magnesium). Reclaimed primarily through sub-surface drainage and leaching.
  • Sodic Soils (Alkali / Solonetz): Characterized by EC < 4 dS/m, ESP > 15, and high pH (8.5 to 10.0). Dominated by sodium carbonate and bicarbonate. Reclaimed using chemical amendments like agricultural gypsum ( ext{CaSO}_4 \cdot 2 ext{H}_2 ext{O}) to replace exchangeable sodium with calcium.
  • Acid Soils: Characterized by pH < 6.5, aluminum and manganese toxicity, and phosphorus fixation. Reclaimed by surface application of agricultural lime ( ext{CaCO}_3 or ext{CaMg(CO}_3)_2).

Memory Trick / Mnemonic
Bangar vs Khadar (Older vs Newer Alluvium)
Khadar is 'Kool & Fresh', Bangar is 'Barely Growing / Old'.

Khadar refers to newer alluvial deposits that are replenished annually (hence fresh and highly fertile). Bangar refers to older alluvial deposits situated at higher terraces containing gravel and kankar nodules.

Memory Trick / Mnemonic
Order of Soil Moisture Constants
Max Field Pointers Always Wilt.

Max (Maximum Water Holding Capacity) > Field (Field Capacity, -0.33 bar) > Pointers (Permanent Wilting Point, -15 bars) > Wilt (Oven Dry Soil, -10,000 bars).

⚡ High-Yield Exam Points (UP TGT / PGT Focus)
  • Alluvial soils cover the largest geographical area in India, accounting for approximately 43% of total land area.
  • Vertisols (Black soils) possess high shrink-swell capacity due to dominance of 2:1 expanding crystal lattice clay mineral smectite (montmorillonite).
  • Kankar nodules found in Bangar soil consist of calcium carbonate ( ext{CaCO}_3) concretions.
  • Agricultural gypsum ( ext{CaSO}_4 \cdot 2 ext{H}_2 ext{O}) is the universal chemical amendment used for the reclamation of sodic (alkali) soils.
  • Agricultural lime ( ext{CaCO}_3) is applied to neutralize soil acidity and precipitate toxic aluminum in acid soils.
  • Laterite soils are formed under conditions of high leaching and are rich in hydrated oxides of iron (limonite) and aluminum (bauxite).
  • The Cation Exchange Capacity (CEC) is highest in Vertisols and lowest in Entisols and coarse sandy soils.
  • Saline soils have an electrical conductivity (EC) greater than 4 dS/m at 25°C, an exchangeable sodium percentage (ESP) less than 15, and pH less than 8.5.
⚠️ Common Mistakes & Exam Pitfalls
  • Confusing Saline soils with Sodic soils: Saline soils have high soluble salts with pH < 8.5, whereas sodic soils have high exchangeable sodium with pH > 8.5.
  • Assuming Black soils are rich in nitrogen: Although black soils are fertile and rich in lime and iron, they are universally deficient in nitrogen and phosphorus.
  • Believing Red soils derive their color from high clay content: Red color is caused by iron oxide coatings on soil particles, not necessarily high clay fraction.

Concept 1

Pedogenic Regimes and Soil Taxonomy

Pedogenesis involves transformative mineral and organic reactions. USDA Soil Taxonomy categorizes soils based on diagnostic horizons.

  • Epipedons: Diagnostic surface horizons like mollic, umbric, ochric, and histic.
  • Endopedons: Diagnostic subsurface horizons including cambic, argillic, natric, and oxic.
Concept 2

Alluvial Soils and Fluvial Stratification

Alluvial soils are azonal soils formed by sediment deposition. They support intense cropping systems in the Indo-Gangetic plains.

  • Khadar: Low-lying, annually flooded, highly fertile younger alluvium.
  • Bangar: Elevated older alluvium containing calcium carbonate concretions.
Concept 3

Vertisols and Self-Mulching Mechanics

Black cotton soils exhibit deep cracking during dry periods, allowing surface soil to fall into cracks, creating inversion mixing known as self-mulching.

  • Smectite: 2:1 swelling clay mineral responsible for high plasticity and cohesion.
Concept 4

Reclamation Dynamics of Problem Soils

Problem soils impose chemical and physical barriers to root proliferation, requiring specific chemical amendments.

  • Gypsum Requirement: Calculated to replace exchangeable sodium on the soil exchange complex.
  • Leaching Requirement: Fraction of irrigation water required to maintain root zone salinity below crop tolerance thresholds.

📚 Technical Terms Glossary

Pedogenesis (Soil Genesis)
The evolutionary process of soil formation involving additions, losses, transpositions, and transformations.
Bangar (Older Alluvium)
Older alluvial soil deposits situated above flood levels containing calcium carbonate nodules.
Khadar (Newer Alluvium)
Recent alluvial soil deposits replenished annually by river floods, highly fertile and fine-textured.
Regur (Black Cotton Soil)
Dark-colored, clay-rich basaltic soil belonging to the Vertisol order possessing high shrink-swell properties.
Laterization (Ferrallitization)
A weathering process characterized by intensive leaching of silica, leaving behind sesquioxides of iron and aluminum.
Saline Soil (Solonchak)
Soil containing excess neutral soluble salts with electrical conductivity > 4 dS/m and pH < 8.5.
Sodic Soil (Alkali Soil / Solonetz)
Soil dominated by exchangeable sodium with ESP > 15 and high pH ranging between 8.5 and 10.0.
ESP (Exchangeable Sodium Percentage)
The proportion of cation exchange capacity of a soil occupied by sodium ions, expressed as a percentage.
CEC (Cation Exchange Capacity)
The total capacity of a soil to hold exchangeable cations, expressed in cmol(p+)/kg.
Self-Mulching (Natural Tilth Formation)
The natural process in shrink-swell clay soils where surface soil falls into deep cracks, creating a granular mulch layer.
Kankar (Calcium Carbonate Concretions)
Hard nodules of calcium carbonate found accumulated in sub-surface layers of older alluvial soils.
Leaching Requirement (LR Formula)
The fraction of applied irrigation water that must pass through the root zone to prevent toxic salt accumulation.

🎯 Must-Remember High-Frequency Facts

  • EC threshold for saline soils: > 4 dS/m at 25°C.
  • ESP threshold for sodic soils: > 15.
  • Dominant clay mineral in black soils: Smectite (Montmorillonite).
  • Chemical formula of agricultural gypsum used in sodic soil reclamation: ext{CaSO}_4 \cdot 2 ext{H}_2 ext{O}.

📌 Quick Recap Bullet Points

  • Alluvial soils cover the maximum geographical area in India (43%), followed by Red soils and Black soils.
  • Vertisols (Black soils) are characterized by expanding 2:1 clay minerals and exhibit self-mulching properties.
  • Sodic soils are reclaimed using agricultural gypsum ( ext{CaSO}_4 \cdot 2 ext{H}_2 ext{O}), while saline soils are reclaimed via leaching.
  • Laterite soils are formed in heavy rainfall regions due to intense leaching of silica and accumulation of iron and aluminum oxides.

⚖️ Concepts Often Confused in Examinations

Saline Soils VS Sodic Soils

Saline soils have high neutral soluble salts and pH < 8.5; Sodic soils have high exchangeable sodium, ESP > 15, and pH between 8.5 and 10.0.

Bangar VS Khadar

Bangar is older alluvium with kankar nodules above flood levels; Khadar is newer alluvium deposited annually in floodplains.

Question 1 of 19 Conceptual MCQ

Which of the following soil orders covers the largest geographical area in India?

Explanation: Inceptisols are the most widely distributed soil order in India, covering nearly 39.7% of the total geographical area.

Question 2 of 19 Conceptual MCQ

What is the primary clay mineral responsible for the shrink-swell behavior of black cotton soils (Vertisols)?

Explanation: Smectite (montmorillonite) is an expanding 2:1 crystal lattice clay mineral that causes high swelling when wet and deep cracking when dry.

Question 3 of 19 Conceptual MCQ

What is the upper limit of Electrical Conductivity (EC) for classifying a soil as saline, assuming normal crop growth thresholds?

Explanation: Saline soils are defined by an electrical conductivity (EC) greater than 4 dS/m at 25°C, an exchangeable sodium percentage (ESP) less than 15, and pH less than 8.5.

Question 4 of 19 Conceptual MCQ

Which chemical amendment is universally recommended for the reclamation of sodic (alkali) soils?

Explanation: Agricultural gypsum ( ext{CaSO}_4 \cdot 2 ext{H}_2 ext{O}) supplies calcium ions ( ext{Ca}^{2+}) which replace exchangeable sodium on the exchange complex, facilitating leaching.

Question 5 of 19 Conceptual MCQ

What are calcium carbonate nodules found in older alluvial (Bangar) soils called?

Explanation: Kankar refers to concretionary calcium carbonate nodules commonly found in the subsoil profiles of Bangar (older alluvial) deposits.

Question 6 of 19 Conceptual MCQ

Red color in red soils is primarily attributed to the presence of:

Explanation: The characteristic red color of red soils is caused by the diffusion of ferric oxide coatings on soil particles, rather than high clay mineral concentration.

Question 7 of 19 Conceptual MCQ

Which soil order is characterized by extreme weathering, leaching of silica, and accumulation of iron and aluminum oxides in humid tropical climates?

Explanation: Laterite soils (classified under Oxisols or Ultisols) form under high temperature and heavy rainfall through intense leaching, leaving sesquioxides.

Question 8 of 19 Conceptual MCQ

What is the Exchangeable Sodium Percentage (ESP) threshold that defines a soil as sodic (alkali)?

Explanation: Sodic soils are characterized by an Exchangeable Sodium Percentage (ESP) greater than 15, high pH (8.5–10.0), and low electrical conductivity.

Question 9 of 19 Conceptual MCQ

Which term describes newer alluvial soils that are replenished annually by river floods?

Explanation: Khadar represents newer alluvial soil deposited annually by floods, making it highly fertile and fine-textured.

Question 10 of 19 Conceptual MCQ

In acid soils, plant growth is severely inhibited primarily due to the toxicity of which elements?

Explanation: Under acidic conditions (pH < 5.5), aluminum and manganese become soluble in toxic concentrations, restricting root elongation.

Question 11 of 19 Conceptual MCQ

Which of the following describes the self-mulching property of Vertisols?

Explanation: Black cotton soils develop wide cracks upon drying, allowing surface aggregates to crumble and fall inward, creating a natural self-mulching effect.

Question 12 of 19 Conceptual MCQ

What is the ideal pH range for optimum availability of most plant nutrients in mineral soils?

Explanation: A soil pH between 6.5 and 7.5 ensures maximum availability of macro and micronutrients, avoiding toxicities or fixation.

Question 13 of 19 Conceptual MCQ

Which soil group is predominantly found in arid and semi-arid regions of Western Rajasthan?

Explanation: Aridisols are desert soils developed under arid and semi-arid conditions with low organic matter and high soluble salt accumulation.

Question 14 of 19 Conceptual MCQ

What is the primary objective of applying agricultural lime to acidic soils?

Explanation: Agricultural lime ( ext{CaCO}_3) neutralizes soil acidity by consuming hydrogen ions and precipitating toxic aluminum as insoluble aluminum hydroxide.

Question 15 of 19 Conceptual MCQ

Which classification system is currently adopted by the National Bureau of Soil Survey and Land Use Planning (NBSS&LUP) in India?

Explanation: NBSS&LUP utilizes the comprehensive USDA Soil Taxonomy system for mapping and classifying Indian soils.

Question 16 of 19 Conceptual MCQ

What is the typical organic carbon content percentage in virgin Indian tropical soils?

Explanation: Due to high ambient temperatures and rapid microbial decomposition, most Indian agricultural soils have low organic carbon content (< 0.5%).

Question 17 of 19 Conceptual MCQ

Which factor is considered an active factor in soil formation according to Hans Jenny's equation (s = f(cl, o, r, p, t))?

Explanation: Climate (cl) and organisms (o) are classified as active factors of pedogenesis, while parent material, relief, and time are passive factors.

Question 18 of 19 Conceptual MCQ

What is the primary management strategy for managing soil salinity in irrigated agriculture?

Explanation: Saline soils are reclaimed by applying excess irrigation water to leach soluble salts below the root zone, provided adequate sub-surface drainage is present.

Question 19 of 19 Conceptual MCQ

In which Indian state is the maximum area under saline and sodic (alkali) soils found?

Explanation: Uttar Pradesh has the largest area under sodic (alkali) soils (locally known as usar lands), particularly in districts like Rae Bareli, Sultanpur, and Aligarh.

Test Question 1 of 10 PGR Standard

A soil sample has an EC of 2.5 dS/m, an ESP of 18, and a pH of 9.2. How should this soil be classified?

Solution & Rationale: Since EC < 4 dS/m, ESP > 15, and pH > 8.5, the soil is classified as a Sodic (Alkali) soil.

Test Question 2 of 10 PGT Standard

Calculate the approximate Gypsum Requirement (GR) in tonnes/hectare for a sodic soil if the exchangeable sodium to be replaced is 2.5 meq/100g of soil, assuming a soil depth of 15 cm and bulk density of 1.33 g/cm³ (Purity of gypsum = 100%).

Solution & Rationale: GR (t/ha) = Exchangeable Na (meq/100g) × 1.72 × Depth (cm) × Bulk Density (g/cm³). Thus, 2.5 × 1.72 × 15 × 1.33 ≈ 8.6 tonnes/hectare.

Test Question 3 of 10 TGT Standard

Which diagnostic subsurface horizon is characterized by the accumulation of silicate clay minerals in Alfisols?

Solution & Rationale: The argillic horizon is a diagnostic subsurface horizon characterized by illuvial accumulation of silicate clay minerals.

Test Question 4 of 10 TGT Standard

What is the primary reason why laterite soils are infertile and acidic?

Solution & Rationale: Intensive leaching under high rainfall removes basic cations and silica, leaving behind insoluble sesquioxides and creating acidic, nutrient-deficient conditions.

Test Question 5 of 10 PGR Standard

Which of the following orders includes soils with high organic matter content forming in water-saturated environments?

Solution & Rationale: Histosols are organic soils (peats and mucks) formed in wetlands and water-saturated environments due to restricted decomposition.

Test Question 6 of 10 PGR Standard

What is the primary effect of applying gypsum ( ext{CaSO}_4 \cdot 2 ext{H}_2 ext{O}) to sodic soils on soil permeability?

Solution & Rationale: Calcium ions replace sodium on the exchange complex, causing clay flocculation and stabilizing soil aggregates, thereby improving water infiltration.

Test Question 7 of 10 TGT Standard

In the USDA textural triangle, a soil containing 40% sand, 40% silt, and 20% clay belongs to which textural class?

Solution & Rationale: A mixture of 40% sand, 40% silt, and 20% clay falls squarely into the Loam textural class.

Test Question 8 of 10 TGT Standard

Why are black soils difficult to cultivate immediately after heavy monsoon showers?

Solution & Rationale: High smectite clay content makes black soils highly plastic and sticky when wet, making tillage operations exceedingly difficult.

Test Question 9 of 10 PGR Standard

Which term denotes the movement of soil particles suspended in water from upper to lower horizons?

Solution & Rationale: Lessivage specifically refers to the mechanical migration of clay suspensions from the eluvial (E) horizon to the illuvial (B) horizon.

Test Question 10 of 10 TGT Standard

What is the typical bulk density range of mineral agricultural soils?

Solution & Rationale: Normal bulk density of mineral soils ranges between 1.1 and 1.6 g/cm³, while organic soils range from 0.2 to 0.5 g/cm³.
