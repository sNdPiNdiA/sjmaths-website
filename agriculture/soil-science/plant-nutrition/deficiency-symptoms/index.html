Plant Nutrient Deficiency Symptoms in Agronomic Crops

Master the morphological symptomology of essential plant nutrient deficiencies for UP TGT and PGT Agriculture examinations, covering mobility classifications, physiological roles, and diagnostic agronomic keys.

UP PGT Agriculture Tracker →

Principles of Nutrient Mobility and Deficiency Manifestation

Understanding the physiological mobility of essential plant nutrients within the vascular system (xylem and phloem) is foundational for accurate diagnostic field agronomy. Plant nutrients are categorized based on their ability to translocate from older, mature vegetative tissues to actively growing meristematic sinks under deficient supply conditions.

  • Mobile Nutrients: Elements such as Nitrogen (N), Phosphorus (P), Potassium (K), Magnesium (Mg), and Molybdenum (Mo) are phloem-mobile. When soil availability is restricted, plants remobilize these ions from older lower leaves to younger upper leaves, causing deficiency symptoms to appear first on older, basal foliage.
  • Immobile Nutrients: Elements such as Calcium (Ca), Boron (B), Iron (Fe), Manganese (Mn), Zinc (Zn), Copper (Cu), and Sulfur (S) have restricted phloem mobility. They cannot be easily translocated from mature tissues; hence, deficiency symptoms manifest primarily on younger, emerging leaves and growing meristematic apices.
  • Diagnostic Significance: Agronomists must systematically inspect the canopy vertical profile (basal vs. terminal leaves) during field diagnosis to correctly differentiate between macronutrient and micronutrient disorders.

Macronutrient Deficiency Symptoms in Major Cereals and Pulses

Macronutrients (N, P, K, Ca, Mg, S) are required in large quantities by staple crops such as Triticum aestivum (wheat), Oryza sativa (rice), and Zea mays (maize). Their deficiencies produce distinct, highly recognizable visual symptoms.

  • Nitrogen (N): General chlorosis starting from older leaves, leading to yellowing (V-shaped yellowing in maize starting from the tip), stunted growth, and premature senescence. In Oryza sativa, tillering is severely reduced.
  • Phosphorus (P): Dark green to bluish-green foliage with characteristic purpling or bronze discoloration on leaves and stems due to anthocyanin accumulation, particularly noticeable in young seedlings of Zea mays and Solanum tuberosum.
  • Potassium (K): Marginal chlorosis followed by necrosis (scorching and firing) along the leaf edges of older leaves, weak stalks leading to lodging, and shriveled grains in cereals.
  • Calcium (Ca): Hooking or tip-blighting of young unfolding leaves, blossom-end rot in Solanum lycopersicum, and death of apical meristems.
  • Magnesium (Mg): Interveinal chlorosis in older leaves where tissue between veins turns yellow while veins remain green (striated chlorosis in monocots).
  • Sulfur (S): Uniform yellowing of entire young leaves (similar to nitrogen deficiency, but appearing first on younger foliage due to immobility).

Micronutrient Deficiency Disorders and Physiological Syndromes

Micronutrients are vital enzymatic cofactors and electron carriers. Their deficiencies cause profound physiological disruptions and characteristic morphological deformities across economically important field and horticultural crops.

  • Zinc (Zn): Khaira disease in Oryza sativa marked by pale green or yellow chlorotic spots on lower leaves that later turn brown/bronze with stunted growth and reduced internodal elongation (rosetting in fruit trees and maize white bud).
  • Iron (Fe): Sharp interveinal chlorosis on young, newly emerging leaves where even minor veins remain green, progressing to complete ivory-white bleaching under severe stress.
  • Boron (B): Heart rot of Beta vulgaris (sugar beet), hollow heart in Brassica oleracea var. botrytis (cauliflower), failure of seed/grain set, and brittleness of terminal shoots.
  • Molybdenum (Mo): Whiptail disorder in Brassica oleracea var. botrytis where leaf blades fail to expand fully, leaving narrow, whip-like midribs. Essential for nitrate reductase activity.
  • Manganese (Mn): Grey speck of Avena sativa (oats) and marsh spot of Pisum sativum, characterized by greyish-brown necrotic spots and interveinal chlorosis.
  • Copper (Cu): Reclamation disease or dieback in citrus and cereals, causing twisted, white-tipped, withered young leaves and failure of ear head emergence.

Agronomic Mitigation, Soil Testing, and Corrective Fertilization

Correct management of nutritional disorders requires integrated soil testing, foliar diagnostics, and targeted fertilizer application protocols aligned with Indian Council of Agricultural Research (ICAR) recommendations.

  • Soil pH Management: Micronutrient availability is highly governed by soil reaction (pH). Iron, manganese, zinc, and copper availability increase under acidic conditions (pH 5.5-6.5), whereas molybdenum and phosphorus become more available near neutral to slightly alkaline pH (6.5-7.5).
  • Foliar Sprays for Rapid Correction: For acute micronutrient deficiencies during critical crop growth stages, foliar application is preferred over soil application. Typical corrective sprays include 0.5% Zinc Sulfate ( ext{ZnSO}_4) + 0.25% lime for Khaira disease in rice, and 0.2% Borax for boron deficiency in cruciferous vegetables.
  • Soil Amendments: Application of agricultural lime to acidic soils corrects calcium and magnesium deficiencies while mitigating heavy metal toxicity. Gypsum ( ext{CaSO}_4 \cdot 2 ext{H}_2 ext{O}) is applied to sodic soils to supply calcium and displace sodium ions.
  • Integrated Nutrient Management (INM): Combining chemical fertilizers with farmyard manure (FYM), vermicompost, and biofertilizers (e.g., Azotobacter, Azospirillum, and Phosphate Solubilizing Bacteria) ensures sustained micronutrient availability and prevents induced deficiencies.

⚡ High-Yield Exam Points (UP TGT / PGT Focus)
  • Khaira disease of rice is caused by the deficiency of Zinc (Zn) and was first reported by Dr. Y.L. Nene at Pantnagar in 1966.
  • Whiptail in cauliflower is a classic physiological disorder caused by Molybdenum (Mo) deficiency under acidic soil conditions.
  • Blossom-end rot in tomato and capsicum is induced by local Calcium (Ca) deficiency coupled with irregular soil moisture regimes.
  • Heart rot of sugar beet and hollow heart of cauliflower are direct consequences of Boron (B) deficiency.
  • Interveinal chlorosis with green veins on young leaves is the hallmark diagnostic symptom of Iron (Fe) deficiency.
  • Potassium deficiency manifests as marginal scorching ('firing') on older leaves, reducing resistance to lodging and diseases.
  • Sulfur deficiency causes uniform chlorosis of younger leaves, mimicking nitrogen deficiency but initiating in the upper canopy.
  • The critical limit of available zinc in Indian soils as per DTPA extraction method is generally 0.60 mg kg⁻¹ (ppm).
⚠️ Common Mistakes & Exam Pitfalls
  • Confusing Iron (Fe) and Nitrogen (N) chlorosis: Iron chlorosis occurs on younger leaves with sharp interveinal green networks, whereas Nitrogen chlorosis starts uniformly on older leaves.
  • Assuming all chlorosis is due to nitrogen shortage: Yellowing can also stem from sulfur, iron, zinc, magnesium, or waterlogging stress.
  • Applying soil fertilizers for acute micronutrient stress during grain filling: Foliar spray is scientifically mandated for rapid uptake when root absorption is compromised.

⚡ Chapter Master Summary: Plant Nutrient Deficiency Symptoms in Agronomic Crops

Systematic concept review designed for quick retention and last-minute pre-exam revision.

Concept 1

Nutrient Mobility and Canopy Stratification

Nutrient translocation dictates the spatial distribution of deficiency symptoms across the plant canopy.

  • Phloem Mobile: N, P, K, Mg, Mo symptoms appear on basal leaves.
  • Phloem Immobile: Ca, B, Fe, Zn, Cu, S symptoms appear on apical leaves.
Concept 2

Macronutrient Physiological Functions

Macronutrients form the structural and biochemical backbone of crop plants.

  • Nitrogen: Constituent of amino acids, proteins, and chlorophyll; drives vegetative growth.
  • Phosphorus: Energy currency (ATP/ADP), nucleic acids, and root development.
  • Potassium: Osmoregulation, stomatal conductance, and enzyme activation.
Concept 3

Micronutrient Enzymatic Roles

Micronutrients act as prosthetic groups and redox catalysts in plant metabolism.

  • Zinc: Auxin (IAA) biosynthesis and carbonic anhydrase activity.
  • Iron: Cytochromes, chlorophyll synthesis, and electron transport chains.
  • Boron: Sugar translocation, cell wall synthesis, and pollen tube germination.
Concept 4

Diagnostic Physiological Disorders

Specific crop abnormalities serve as visual bio-indicators for precise nutrient deficiencies.

  • Khaira Disease: Zinc deficiency in paddy nurseries and transplanted fields.
  • Whiptail: Molybdenum deficiency in cruciferous vegetables.
  • Blossom-End Rot: Calcium deficiency in solanaceous crops.

📚 Technical Terms Glossary

Chlorosis (Chlorosis)
The yellowing of normally green plant tissue due to the reduced formation or destruction of chlorophyll pigments.
Necrosis (Necrosis)
Localized death of living plant tissues, resulting in brown or black dead spots, patches, or marginal scorching.
Interveinal Chlorosis (Interveinal Chlorosis)
Yellowing of leaf tissue located between the veins while the veins themselves retain their green coloration.
Khaira Disease (Zinc Deficiency in Oryza sativa)
A pathological physiological disorder in rice characterized by chlorotic patches turning brown with stunted growth due to zinc deficiency.
Whiptail (Molybdenum Deficiency Disorder)
A physiological deformity in cauliflower where leaf blades fail to develop normally, leaving only narrow whip-like midribs.
Blossom-End Rot (Calcium Deficiency Disorder)
Water-soaked sunken lesions appearing at the distal end of tomato and pepper fruits due to localized calcium deficiency.
Rosetting (Zinc Deficiency Growth Habit)
Shortening of internodes causing leaves to cluster in a rosette formation at shoot tips.
Anthocyanescence (Anthocyanin Accumulation)
Development of red, purple, or blue pigmentation in leaves and stems, often triggered by phosphorus deficiency.
Mobility Index (Phloem Translocation Capacity)
The physiological ability of a nutrient element to move from older to younger plant organs during shortage.
DTPA Extractant (Diethylenetriaminepentaacetic Acid)
Standard chemical extracting solution used in soil laboratories to determine available micronutrient (Fe, Mn, Zn, Cu) status.

🎯 Must-Remember High-Frequency Facts

  • Khaira disease in rice: Zn deficiency (Dr. Y.L. Nene, 1966).
  • Whiptail in cauliflower: Mo deficiency.
  • Blossom-end rot in tomato: Ca deficiency.
  • Hollow heart in potato/cauliflower: B deficiency.

📌 Quick Recap Bullet Points

  • Deficiency symptoms of mobile nutrients appear on older leaves; immobile nutrients affect younger leaves first.
  • Nitrogen deficiency causes generalized V-shaped yellowing; Phosphorus causes dark green leaves with purpling.
  • Zinc deficiency leads to Khaira disease in rice and rosetting in orchards.
  • Boron deficiency causes heart rot in sugar beet and sterility in cereals.

⚖️ Concepts Often Confused in Examinations

Nitrogen Deficiency VS Sulfur Deficiency

Nitrogen deficiency symptoms start on older leaves, whereas sulfur deficiency symptoms start on younger leaves due to lower mobility.

Question 1 of 20 Conceptual MCQ

Which of the following essential plant nutrients is classified as phloem-immobile, causing deficiency symptoms to appear first on younger terminal leaves?

Explanation: Calcium (Ca) is an immobile nutrient involved in cell wall pectin synthesis (calcium pectate). Because it cannot be translocated through the phloem from older tissues, deficiency symptoms manifest first on actively growing apical buds and young leaves.

Question 2 of 20 Conceptual MCQ

Khaira disease of rice, characterized by chlorotic spots turning brown and stunted plant growth, is caused by the deficiency of:

Explanation: Khaira disease is a classic nutritional disorder in paddy caused by Zinc (Zn) deficiency, successfully managed by spraying 0.5% zinc sulfate and 0.25% lime.

Question 3 of 20 Conceptual MCQ

Whiptail disorder in cauliflower is specifically induced by the deficiency of which micronutrient?

Explanation: Molybdenum (Mo) deficiency causes whiptail in cauliflower, where leaf blades fail to expand, resulting in narrow, whip-like structures.

Question 4 of 20 Conceptual MCQ

Interveinal chlorosis on younger leaves where veins remain distinctly green is the classic diagnostic symptom of:

Explanation: Iron (Fe) deficiency produces sharp interveinal chlorosis on young leaves because iron is immobile and required for chlorophyll biosynthesis enzymes.

Question 5 of 20 Conceptual MCQ

Blossom-end rot in solanaceous crops like tomatoes is primarily associated with the deficiency of:

Explanation: Blossom-end rot is caused by Calcium (Ca) deficiency in developing fruit tissues, leading to water-soaked dark sunken lesions at the distal end.

Question 6 of 20 Conceptual MCQ

Which macronutrient deficiency typically results in dark green to bluish-green foliage with abnormal purpling of stems and leaves due to anthocyanin accumulation?

Explanation: Phosphorus (P) deficiency restricts protein synthesis while allowing carbohydrate accumulation, which shunts intermediates into anthocyanin pigment production, causing purpling.

Question 7 of 20 Conceptual MCQ

Marginal chlorosis followed by necrosis ('scorching' or 'firing') of older basal leaves is the characteristic deficiency symptom of:

Explanation: Potassium (K) is mobile and highly concentrated in meristematic and active tissues; its deficiency causes marginal scorching and firing starting from older leaves.

Question 8 of 20 Conceptual MCQ

Heart rot of sugar beet and hollow heart in cauliflower are physiological disorders caused by the deficiency of:

Explanation: Boron (B) deficiency disrupts cell division and lignification, resulting in heart rot in root crops and hollow stems in crucifers.

Question 9 of 20 Conceptual MCQ

Which of the following sets contains ONLY phloem-mobile nutrients?

Explanation: Nitrogen (N), Phosphorus (P), Potassium (K), and Magnesium (Mg) are highly mobile in the phloem and translocate from older to younger leaves during deficiency.

Question 10 of 20 Conceptual MCQ

Grey speck of oat (Avena sativa) is a physiological disorder caused by the deficiency of:

Explanation: Grey speck is caused by Manganese (Mn) deficiency, impairing photosynthesis and cellular redox reactions.

Question 11 of 20 Conceptual MCQ

Reclamation disease or 'white tip' in cereals is associated with the deficiency of:

Explanation: Copper (Cu) deficiency causes reclamation disease, characterized by withered, white-tipped young leaves and failure of grain setting.

Question 12 of 20 Conceptual MCQ

Which soil extraction chemical is universally recommended by SAUs and ICAR for determining available Zinc, Iron, Manganese, and Copper in Indian soils?

Explanation: DTPA extraction method is standard for estimating plant-available micronutrient cations (Zn, Fe, Mn, Cu) in soils.

Question 13 of 20 Conceptual MCQ

Uniform yellowing of entire young leaves (resembling nitrogen deficiency but occurring on the upper canopy) points toward a deficiency of:

Explanation: Sulfur (S) is relatively immobile in plants; therefore, its deficiency causes uniform chlorosis across young terminal leaves.

Question 14 of 20 Conceptual MCQ

What is the primary biochemical role of Zinc in plant metabolic systems?

Explanation: Zinc acts as an activator for various enzymes and is essential for the synthesis of tryptophan, a precursor for auxin (IAA) production.

Question 15 of 20 Conceptual MCQ

Under alkaline soil conditions (pH > 8.0), the availability of which micronutrients is severely restricted, often inducing chlorosis?

Explanation: Iron and Zinc solubility decreases sharply in high-pH calcareous soils, leading to acute iron chlorosis and zinc deficiency.

Question 16 of 20 Conceptual MCQ

Which physiological symptom is most indicative of Magnesium deficiency in monocot crops like maize and rice?

Explanation: Magnesium is the core atom of the chlorophyll molecule. Its deficiency causes interveinal chlorosis (striated yellowing) on older basal leaves.

Question 17 of 20 Conceptual MCQ

The critical deficiency limit of available Zinc (DTPA-extractable) in Indian soils is generally accepted as:

Explanation: According to ICAR soil science standards, a DTPA-extractable zinc level below 0.60 mg kg⁻¹ (ppm) is considered deficient for cereal production.

Question 18 of 20 Conceptual MCQ

Why are foliar sprays preferred over soil applications for correcting acute micronutrient deficiencies during advanced crop reproductive stages?

Explanation: Foliar sprays bypass soil fixation complexes (such as precipitation at high pH) and provide immediate uptake through leaf stomata and cuticles.

Question 19 of 20 Conceptual MCQ

Which of the following disorders is caused by Boron deficiency during the reproductive stage of wheat or paddy?

Explanation: Boron deficiency impairs pollen germination and tube elongation, leading to poor grain setting, sterility, and chaffy panicles.

Question 20 of 20 Conceptual MCQ

In Integrated Nutrient Management (INM), the application of Farmyard Manure (FYM) helps prevent micronutrient deficiencies primarily by:

Explanation: Organic manures decompose to form natural chelates that bind micronutrient cations, keeping them soluble and available for plant root uptake while preventing fixation.

Test Question 2 of 10 PGT Standard

Assertion (A): Deficiency symptoms of mobile nutrients like Nitrogen and Potassium appear first on older, lower leaves. Reason (R): Plants remobilize mobile nutrients from mature tissues to actively growing meristematic sinks during deficiency.

Solution & Rationale: Both statements are scientifically accurate. Phloem mobility allows plants to translocate mobile nutrients from older to younger tissues, causing deficiency symptoms to appear first on basal leaves.

Test Question 3 of 10 TGT Standard

Which of the following micronutrients plays an essential role as a structural component of the nitrate reductase enzyme and nitrogen fixation in legumes?

Solution & Rationale: Molybdenum (Mo) is a critical component of nitrate reductase and nitrogenase enzymes, making it indispensable for nitrogen metabolism and symbiotic ext{N}_2 fixation.

Test Question 4 of 10 PGT Standard

A farmer observes interveinal chlorosis on newly emerged terminal leaves of maize plants grown in calcareous soil (pH 8.2). Soil application of NPK fertilizers does not cure the problem. Which corrective measure should be recommended?

Solution & Rationale: High pH calcareous soils cause iron precipitation. Symptoms on young leaves indicate iron deficiency, which is rapidly corrected by a foliar spray of ext{FeSO}_4.

Test Question 5 of 10 PGT Standard

Which element is unique because its deficiency causes symptoms on both old and young leaves simultaneously or exhibits intermediate mobility characteristics in certain plant species?

Solution & Rationale: Zinc exhibits intermediate phloem mobility in many agronomic crops, often displaying deficiency symptoms on middle to younger leaves as rosetting or interveinal chlorosis.

Test Question 6 of 10 TGT Standard

The chemical formula of the common agricultural compound used to correct Zinc deficiency in paddy nurseries is:

Solution & Rationale: Zinc sulfate heptahydrate ( ext{ZnSO}_4 \cdot 7 ext{H}_2 ext{O}) is the standard fertilizer grade salt used for soil and foliar application to correct zinc deficiency.

Test Question 7 of 10 TGT Standard

What is the primary consequence of Boron deficiency on reproductive plant parts?

Solution & Rationale: Boron is essential for sugar translocation and pollen tube growth; its deficiency leads to empty grains, hollow fruit cavities, and reproductive sterility.

Test Question 8 of 10 PGT Standard

Which physiological disorder in potato tubers is directly linked to Boron deficiency, resulting in dark, necrotic cavities inside the tuber flesh?

Solution & Rationale: Hollow heart in potato and cauliflower is caused by localized boron deficiency during rapid cell expansion phases.

Test Question 9 of 10 PGT Standard

In diagnostic plant tissue analysis, what is the general physiological significance of determining total nutrient concentrations at critical growth stages?

Solution & Rationale: Plant tissue analysis helps identify hidden hunger—where crop yield is limited by suboptimal nutrient levels before visible deficiency symptoms manifest.

Test Question 10 of 10 TGT Standard

Which of the following secondary macronutrients is a constituent of amino acids methionine, cysteine, and cystine?

Solution & Rationale: Sulfur is an essential constituent of sulfur-containing amino acids (methionine and cysteine), which are building blocks for plant proteins.
