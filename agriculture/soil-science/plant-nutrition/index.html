Plant Nutrition: Essential Elements, Functions, Deficiency Symptoms, and Fertilizer Management

Criteria of Essentiality and Classification of Plant Nutrients

Plant nutrition deals with the supply and absorption of chemical compounds required for plant growth, metabolism, and reproduction. Out of more than 90 naturally occurring elements found in plants, only 17 are universally recognized as essential for higher plants.

  • Arnon and Stout (1939) Criteria of Essentiality: (1) A plant must be unable to complete its life cycle (seed to seed) in the absence of the element. (2) The function of the element must be specific and cannot be replaced by another element. (3) The element must be directly involved in plant metabolism (nutrition or enzymatic activation). D.I. Nicholas (1961) added the concept of functional or beneficial elements.
  • Macronutrients (Primary and Secondary): Required in concentrations greater than 1.0 mg g-1 of dry matter. Primary macronutrients include Nitrogen (N), Phosphorus (P), and Potassium (K). Secondary macronutrients include Calcium (Ca), Magnesium (Mg), and Sulfur (S).
  • Micronutrients (Trace Elements): Required in concentrations less than 1.0 mg g-1 (or < 100 ppm) of dry matter. These include Iron (Fe), Manganese (Mn), Zinc (Zn), Copper (Cu), Boron (B), Molybdenum (Mo), Chlorine (Cl), and Nickel (Ni - added as the 17th essential element by Brown et al. in 1987).
  • Beneficial Elements: Elements required by certain plant species or under specific conditions, including Sodium (Na), Silicon (Si), Cobalt (Co), and Vanadium (V).

Physiological Roles and Biochemical Functions of Essential Nutrients

Each essential element plays a precise biochemical role in plant enzymatic pathways, structural integrity, and energy transfer mechanisms.

  • Nitrogen (N): Constituent of proteins, amino acids, nucleic acids, coenzymes, and chlorophyll. Promotes vegetative growth and imparts dark green color.
  • Phosphorus (P): The 'Energy Currency' of the plant. Crucial component of Adenosine Triphosphate (ATP), Ribonucleic Acid (RNA), Deoxyribonucleic Acid (DNA), and phospholipids. Essential for root development, flowering, and early maturity.
  • Potassium (K): The 'Quality Element' and osmotic regulator. Serves as an activator for over 60 enzymes. Regulates stomatal opening and closing via turgor pressure changes, enhances disease resistance, and facilitates translocation of carbohydrates.
  • Calcium (Ca): Structural component of the middle lamellae (calcium pectate) in cell walls. Essential for cell division, elongation, and signaling as a second messenger.
  • Magnesium (Mg): The central metallic atom of the chlorophyll porphyrin ring. Acts as a bridge for phosphate transfer reactions and activates numerous carbohydrate-metabolizing enzymes.
  • Sulfur (S): Found in sulfur-containing amino acids (methionine, cysteine, cystine) and vitamins (biotin, thiamine). Essential for oil synthesis in oilseed crops.
  • Micronutrients: Iron acts in electron transport systems (cytochromes, ferredoxin) and chlorophyll synthesis. Zinc is required for tryptophan synthesis, the precursor of Indole-3-Acetic Acid (IAA, auxin). Boron aids in sugar translocation, pollen tube germination, and pollination. Molybdenum is vital for nitrogenase and nitrate reductase enzyme function during biological nitrogen fixation and nitrate assimilation.

Deficiency Symptoms and Mobility in Plants

Nutrient mobility within the plant vascular system dictates where deficiency symptoms first appear on the plant canopy.

  • Mobile Elements (N, P, K, Mg, Mo): Symptoms first appear on older, lower leaves because these nutrients are translocated to actively growing meristematic tissues under deficiency stress. For instance, Nitrogen and Phosphorus deficiency causes chlorosis and purpling of older leaves, respectively.
  • Immobile Elements (Ca, Fe, Mn, Zn, Cu, B, S): Symptoms first appear on younger, upper leaves and growing shoot tips because they cannot be remobilized from older plant parts. Calcium deficiency leads to tip hook and blossom-end rot; Boron deficiency causes hollow stem and barren cobs.
  • Chlorosis vs. Necrosis: Chlorosis refers to the yellowing of leaves due to the breakdown or failure of chlorophyll synthesis (typical of N, Mg, Fe, Mn, Zn deficiency). Necrosis refers to the localized death of plant tissue, often resulting from advanced stages of nutrient starvation or toxicity.
  • Visual Diagnosis: Interveinal chlorosis is characteristic of Magnesium and Iron deficiencies. In Mg deficiency, veins remain green while interveinal areas turn yellow on older leaves; in Fe deficiency, the same pattern appears on younger leaves.

Quantitative Nutrient Management and Fertilizer Calculations

Applying the correct quantity of chemical fertilizers based on soil test recommendations is critical for agronomic efficiency and environmental sustainability.

  • Fertilizer Grades and Nutrient Composition: Urea contains 46% N; Di-ammonium Phosphate (DAP) contains 18% N and 46% P2O5; Muriate of Potash (MOP) contains 60% K2O; Single Super Phosphate (SSP) contains 16% P2O5, 12% S, and 19-20% Ca.
  • Calculation of Fertilizer Quantity: To calculate the physical weight of fertilizer required, use the standard formula:
    Weight of Fertilizer = (Target Nutrient Dose / Percentage of Nutrient in Fertilizer) × 100
  • Numerical Example: Calculate the quantity of Urea required to supply 120 kg N per hectare.
    Urea Required = (120 / 46) × 100 = 260.87 kg ha-1.
  • Complex Fertilizer Adjustment: When supplying N and P using DAP and Urea for a recommended dose of 120:60:40 N:P:K kg ha-1: First calculate DAP required for 60 kg P2O5:
    DAP = (60 / 46) × 100 = 130.43 kg ha-1.
    This DAP supplies: 130.43 × (18/100) = 23.48 kg N.
    Remaining N to be supplied via Urea: 120 - 23.48 = 96.52 kg N.
    Urea Required = (96.52 / 46) × 100 = 209.83 kg ha-1.

Soil Fertility, Nutrient Availability, and Integrated Nutrient Management (INM)

Soil fertility is the inherent capacity of a soil to supply plant nutrients in adequate amounts and proportions, whereas soil productivity is the capacity of a soil to produce crops under specified management systems.

  • Factors Affecting Nutrient Availability: Soil pH is the master variable. Phosphorus availability is highest at a neutral pH (6.5 to 7.5); in acidic soils, P is fixed by Iron and Aluminum oxides, while in alkaline soils, it is fixed by Calcium carbonates. Micronutrient availability (except Mo) increases under acidic conditions.
  • Cation Exchange Capacity (CEC): The capacity of a soil to hold and exchange cations. Humus and 2:1 expanding clay minerals (like montmorillonite) possess high CEC, retaining essential cations such as Ca2+, Mg2+, K+, and NH4+ against leaching losses.
  • Integrated Nutrient Management (INM): The maintenance or adjustment of soil fertility and plant nutrient supply to an optimum level for sustaining crop productivity through the combined use of chemical fertilizers, organic manures (FYM, vermicompost, green manure), biofertilizers (Rhizobium, Azotobacter, Azospirillum, Phosphate Solubilizing Bacteria like Bacillus megaterium), and crop residues.

⚡ High-Yield Exam Points (UP TGT / PGT Focus)
  • Arnon and Stout proposed the criteria of essential plant nutrients in 1939.
  • Nickel (Ni) was discovered as the 17th essential plant nutrient by Brown et al. in 1987.
  • Molybdenum (Mo) is required in the lowest quantity among all essential micronutrients.
  • Urea contains the highest percentage of nitrogen (46%) among solid nitrogenous fertilizers.
  • Chlorosis in older leaves accompanied by reddish-purple coloration is diagnostic of Phosphorus deficiency.
  • Blossom-end rot in tomato and tip burn in leafy vegetables are physiological disorders caused by Calcium deficiency.
  • Hollow stem of cauliflower and boron toxicity/deficiency are linked to Boron (B) availability.
  • Zinc deficiency causes 'Khaira disease' in paddy (rice), correctable by spraying Zinc Sulfate (ZnSO4) and lime.
  • Cation Exchange Capacity (CEC) is measured in centimoles of charge per kilogram of soil (cmol(p+) kg-1).
  • Nitrate reductase and nitrogenase enzymes specifically require Molybdenum (Mo) for their activation.
⚠️ Common Mistakes & Exam Pitfalls
  • Confusing mobile and immobile nutrient deficiency symptoms; remember that deficiency of mobile elements appears on older leaves, while immobile element deficiency appears on younger leaves.
  • Assuming SSP only contains phosphorus; SSP also contains 12% sulfur and 19-20% calcium, making it an excellent multi-nutrient carrier.
  • Calculating fertilizer dose directly from raw fertilizer weight without accounting for the actual percentage concentration of the nutrient.
  • Believing that soil pH has no impact on micronutrient availability; actually, availability of Fe, Mn, Zn, and Cu increases as soil becomes more acidic.

⚡ Chapter Master Summary: Plant Nutrition: Essential Elements, Functions, Deficiency Symptoms, and Fertilizer Management

Systematic concept review designed for quick retention and last-minute pre-exam revision.

Concept 1

Criteria of Essentiality

Essential elements are indispensable for plant survival, fulfilling specific biochemical roles that cannot be substituted by any other element.

  • Arnon and Stout (1939): Established the three classical criteria of essentiality.
  • 17 Essential Elements: Divided into macronutrients and micronutrients based on plant tissue concentration thresholds.
Concept 2

Macronutrient Functions and Deficiencies

Primary macronutrients (N, P, K) govern vegetative vigor, energy transfer, and quality attributes, while secondary nutrients (Ca, Mg, S) provide structural support and enzyme activation.

  • Nitrogen: Essential for vegetative growth and amino acid synthesis; deficiency causes general chlorosis of older leaves.
  • Phosphorus: Vital for root growth and energy storage (ATP); deficiency results in stunted growth and purpling.
Concept 3

Micronutrient Roles in Plant Metabolism

Micronutrients act primarily as enzymatic activators and electron carriers in trace quantities.

  • Zinc (Zn): Required for auxin synthesis; deficiency causes khaira disease in rice and little leaf in fruit trees.
  • Boron (B): Regulates carbohydrate translocation and reproductive success; deficiency causes barren cobs and hollow stems.
Concept 4

Nutrient Mobility and Visual Symptomatology

The phloem mobility of an element dictates the anatomical location of deficiency symptom expression on the plant canopy.

  • Mobile Nutrients (N, P, K, Mg): Symptoms manifest on older leaves first due to internal redistribution.
  • Immobile Nutrients (Ca, Fe, B): Symptoms manifest on younger leaves and growing tips because they cannot be remobilized.
Concept 5

Fertilizer Calculations and INM

Quantitative agronomy relies on precise fertilizer math and integrated nutrient management to preserve soil health.

  • Fertilizer Formula: Weight = (Recommendation / Grade %) × 100.
  • INM Approach: Combines chemical fertilizers, organic manures, and biofertilizers for sustainable high yields.

📚 Technical Terms Glossary

Essential Element (Plant Nutrient Essentiality)
A chemical element required for a plant to complete its normal life cycle and directly involved in its metabolism.
Macronutrient (Primary & Secondary Nutrients)
Nutrients required by plants in large quantities, generally exceeding 1.0 mg g^-1 of dry matter.
Micronutrient (Trace Element)
Nutrients required in minute concentrations (< 1.0 mg g^-1 of dry matter) primarily serving as enzymatic activators.
Chlorosis (Chlorophyll Degradation / Failure)
Yellowing of plant leaves resulting from lack of chlorophyll production due to nutrient deficiency.
Necrosis (Tissue Death)
Death of plant tissues or cells, typically appearing as brown or black necrotic spots on leaf margins or tips.
Cation Exchange Capacity (CEC) (Soil Exchangeable Cation Holding Capacity)
The total capacity of a soil to hold exchangeable cations, vital for nutrient retention.
Khaira Disease (Zinc Deficiency in Oryza sativa)
A physiological disorder in rice characterized by chlorotic patches turning brown, caused by Zinc deficiency.
Blossom-End Rot (Calcium Deficiency Disorder)
Water-soaked dark sunken spots on the blossom end of tomato and pepper fruits due to Calcium deficiency.
Biological Nitrogen Fixation (BNF via Rhizobium symbiosis)
The conversion of atmospheric nitrogen gas into ammonia by specialized prokaryotes possessing nitrogenase.
Integrated Nutrient Management (INM)
The holistic practice of combining organic, inorganic, and biological sources of plant nutrients to sustain soil fertility.
Luxury Consumption (Excessive Nutrient Uptake)
The uptake of a nutrient (such as Potassium) by plants in quantities well above what is needed for optimal growth.
Mobile Element (Phloem-Mobile Nutrient)
An element that can move freely from older to younger plant tissues during deficiency stress.

⚖️ Concepts Often Confused in Examinations

Macronutrients VS Micronutrients

Macronutrients (N, P, K, Ca, Mg, S) are required in concentrations > 1 mg g^-1, whereas micronutrients (Fe, Mn, Zn, Cu, B, Mo, Cl, Ni) are required in < 1 mg g^-1.

Mobile Nutrients VS Immobile Nutrients

Mobile nutrients (N, P, K, Mg) show deficiency symptoms on older leaves; immobile nutrients (Ca, Fe, B) show symptoms on younger growing shoots.

Question 1 of 20 Conceptual MCQ

Who proposed the classical criteria of plant nutrient essentiality in 1939?

Explanation: D.I. Arnon and P.L. Stout proposed the criteria of essentiality in 1939, stating that an element must be directly involved in plant metabolism and necessary for completing the life cycle.

Question 2 of 20 Conceptual MCQ

Which of the following is considered the 17th essential plant nutrient discovered in 1987?

Explanation: Nickel (Ni) was established as the 17th essential plant nutrient by Brown, Welch, and Cary in 1987 due to its essential role in urease activity.

Question 3 of 20 Conceptual MCQ

Which essential nutrient forms the central atom of the chlorophyll porphyrin ring?

Explanation: Magnesium (Mg) is the central metallic ion in the chlorophyll molecule, bonded to four nitrogen atoms in the porphyrin ring.

Question 4 of 20 Conceptual MCQ

Khaira disease in rice, characterized by chlorotic patches turning brown and rusty, is caused by the deficiency of:

Explanation: Khaira disease in rice is caused by Zinc (Zn) deficiency, commonly managed by applying ZnSO4 during nursery raising or main field preparation.

Question 5 of 20 Conceptual MCQ

Deficiency symptoms of mobile nutrients (like Nitrogen and Phosphorus) first appear on:

Explanation: Mobile nutrients are translocated from older to younger tissues during deficiency stress, making deficiency symptoms appear first on older, lower leaves.

Question 6 of 20 Conceptual MCQ

What is the percentage of Nitrogen present in commercial Urea fertilizer?

Explanation: Urea [CO(NH2)2] contains 46% Nitrogen by weight in amide form, making it the most popular nitrogenous fertilizer in India.

Question 7 of 20 Conceptual MCQ

Blossom-end rot in tomato is a physiological disorder directly caused by the deficiency of:

Explanation: Blossom-end rot is caused by Calcium (Ca) deficiency, which impairs cell wall stability and results in water-soaked sunken spots on fruit tips.

Question 8 of 20 Conceptual MCQ

Which of the following elements is classified as an immobile micronutrient in plants?

Explanation: Iron (Fe) is an immobile micronutrient; therefore, iron chlorosis (interveinal yellowing) first appears on younger, upper leaves.

Question 9 of 20 Conceptual MCQ

Molybdenum (Mo) plays a primary biochemical role in which of the following enzymatic processes?

Explanation: Molybdenum is a crucial component of nitrate reductase and nitrogenase enzymes, which facilitate nitrate assimilation and biological nitrogen fixation.

Question 10 of 20 Conceptual MCQ

How much quantity of Urea (in kg) is required to supply 60 kg of actual Nitrogen?

Explanation: Calculation: (60 / 46) × 100 = 130.43 kg of Urea is required to supply 60 kg of elemental Nitrogen.

Question 11 of 20 Conceptual MCQ

Which fertilizer contains 18% Nitrogen and 46% Phosphorus pentoxide (P2O5)?

Explanation: Di-ammonium Phosphate (DAP) contains 18% N and 46% P2O5, providing both nitrogen and water-soluble phosphorus.

Question 12 of 20 Conceptual MCQ

The hollow stem disorder in cauliflower is typically associated with the deficiency of:

Explanation: Boron (B) deficiency leads to hollow stem and browning in cauliflower, impairing internal pith development.

Question 13 of 20 Conceptual MCQ

Which of the following elements is categorized as a beneficial element rather than universally essential?

Explanation: Silicon (Si) is classified as a beneficial element, particularly valuable in strengthening cell walls and imparting lodging and pest resistance in rice.

Question 14 of 20 Conceptual MCQ

Stomatal opening and closing in response to turgor pressure is primarily regulated by which macronutrient?

Explanation: Potassium (K) accumulates in guard cells, lowering water potential and causing water influx, which opens stomata during daylight.

Question 15 of 20 Conceptual MCQ

What is the primary function of Sulfur in plants?

Explanation: Sulfur is a constituent of amino acids (methionine, cysteine) and is vital for oil synthesis and protein quality in oilseed crops.

Question 16 of 20 Conceptual MCQ

Which soil pH range is generally considered optimal for maximum availability of most plant nutrients?

Explanation: A neutral to slightly alkaline pH range (6.5 to 7.5) optimizes the availability of macro and micronutrients, avoiding fixation issues common in extreme pH soils.

Question 17 of 20 Conceptual MCQ

The capacity of a soil to hold and exchange cations is known as:

Explanation: Cation Exchange Capacity (CEC) measures the total quantity of exchangeable cations a soil can retain, representing its chemical fertility potential.

Question 18 of 20 Conceptual MCQ

Which micronutrient is required in the absolute lowest quantity by higher plants?

Explanation: Molybdenum (Mo) is required in the lowest concentration among all essential micronutrients, often measured in parts per billion (ppb).

Question 19 of 20 Conceptual MCQ

What visual symptom is characteristic of Phosphorus deficiency in many agronomic crops?

Explanation: Phosphorus deficiency restricts protein synthesis while allowing carbohydrate accumulation, leading to anthocyanin formation that turns older leaves dark green or purple.

Question 20 of 20 Conceptual MCQ

Single Super Phosphate (SSP) contains what percentage of Sulfur along with 16% P2O5?

Explanation: SSP contains 16% P2O5, 12% Sulfur, and about 19-20% Calcium, making it an excellent source for sulfur-deficient soils.

Calculate the quantity of DAP (Di-ammonium Phosphate) required to supply 60 kg of P2O5 per hectare.

Solution & Rationale: Using the formula: (Recommended P2O5 / Nutrient percentage in DAP) × 100 = (60 / 46) × 100 = 130.43 kg ha^-1.

Test Question 2 of 10 TGT Standard

Which of the following pairs of essential elements are classified as secondary macronutrients?

Solution & Rationale: Calcium, Magnesium, and Sulfur are classified as secondary macronutrients as they are required in quantities intermediate between primary macronutrients and micronutrients.

Test Question 3 of 10 PGR Standard

What is the primary reason why Iron deficiency symptoms appear on younger leaves first?

Solution & Rationale: Iron is an immobile nutrient within plants; once deposited in older tissues, it cannot be remobilized to meet the demands of emerging growing points.

Test Question 4 of 10 TGT Standard

Which biofertilizer is specifically recommended for non-leguminous cereal crops like rice, wheat, and maize to fix atmospheric nitrogen?

Solution & Rationale: Azotobacter (free-living) and Azospirillum (associative) are biofertilizers utilized for non-legume cereal crops to fix nitrogen and secrete growth-promoting substances.

Test Question 5 of 10 PGT Standard

Under acidic soil conditions, which nutrient's availability is severely reduced due to fixation by free Iron and Aluminum oxides?

Solution & Rationale: In acidic soils (pH < 5.5), soluble phosphate ions react with iron and aluminum to form insoluble iron and aluminum phosphates, causing P fixation.

Test Question 6 of 10 PGT Standard

Which element is a key constituent of the enzyme nitrate reductase and is vital for protein synthesis?

Solution & Rationale: Molybdenum is a component of nitrate reductase, the enzyme responsible for reducing nitrates to nitrites during nitrogen assimilation.

Test Question 7 of 10 PGT Standard

Luxury consumption is a well-documented phenomenon most commonly observed with which nutrient in crops?

Solution & Rationale: Crops often absorb Potassium in quantities far exceeding their metabolic requirements when abundant K is available in soil solution, without immediate yield increase.

Test Question 8 of 10 TGT Standard

Which of the following compounds is applied to correct Zinc deficiency in agricultural fields?

Solution & Rationale: Zinc Sulfate heptahydrate or monohydrate is the most common water-soluble fertilizer applied to correct zinc deficiency and khaira disease in paddy.

Test Question 9 of 10 TGT Standard

In what form is Nitrogen primarily absorbed by higher plants from the soil solution under aerobic conditions?

Solution & Rationale: Plants absorb nitrogen primarily as Nitrate (NO3-) in aerated agricultural soils, and as Ammonium (NH4+) in flooded paddy soils.

Test Question 10 of 10 TGT Standard

What is the primary agronomic purpose of Integrated Nutrient Management (INM)?

Solution & Rationale: INM aims to optimize soil health, nutrient use efficiency, and farm profitability by integrating chemical fertilizers, organic manures, and biofertilizers.
