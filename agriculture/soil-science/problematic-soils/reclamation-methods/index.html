Reclamation Methods for Problematic Soils

Classification and Characteristics of Problematic Soils

Problematic soils are characterized by chemical, physical, or biological imbalances that severely restrict root proliferation, water infiltration, and nutrient uptake, leading to drastically reduced crop yields. In Indian agriculture, these soils are broadly categorized into saline, sodic (alkali), and acid soils based on their pH, Electrical Conductivity (EC), and Exchangeable Sodium Percentage (ESP).

  • Saline Soils: Characterized by an Electrical Conductivity (EC) of saturation extract greater than 4 dS m-1 at 25 degrees Celsius, Exchangeable Sodium Percentage (ESP) less than 15, and pH usually less than 8.5. They contain an excess of neutral soluble salts such as chlorides and sulfates of sodium, calcium, and magnesium, creating high osmotic pressure.
  • Sodic / Alkali Soils: Characterized by an EC less than 4 dS m-1, ESP greater than 15, and pH ranging between 8.5 and 10.0. The dominant cation on the exchange complex is sodium, leading to severe dispersion of clay particles, poor structure, and low permeability.
  • Acid Soils: Characterized by a pH below 6.5, caused by the accumulation of exchangeable hydrogen and aluminum ions, coupled with deficiencies in basic cations like calcium, magnesium, and potassium, alongside toxic levels of active aluminum and manganese.

Reclamation and Management of Saline Soils

Saline soils, often referred to as 'White Alkali' due to the surface encrustation of white salts, do not require chemical amendments because they already contain sufficient calcium and magnesium salts. Their reclamation relies primarily on physical removal and leaching of soluble salts through the root zone via adequate drainage.

  • Leaching Requirement (LR): The fraction of irrigation water that must be leached through the root zone to maintain soil salinity at a specified level. It is calculated as LR = (ECiw / ECe), where ECiw is the electrical conductivity of irrigation water and ECe is the electrical conductivity of the saturation extract tolerated by the crop.
  • Drainage Installation: Sub-surface drainage (tile drains or perforated PVC pipes) is critical to lower the shallow water table below the critical depth (usually 1.5 to 2.0 meters) and prevent capillary rise of saline groundwater.
  • Agronomic Practices: Practicing ridge-and-furrow planting helps protect germinating seeds from salt accumulation at the soil surface, as salts tend to accumulate on the ridge tops during evaporation. Mulching with organic crop residues reduces surface evaporation and subsequent salt deposition.
  • Salt-Tolerant Cropping: Cultivating tolerant crops during initial phases, such as barley (Hordeum vulgare), cotton (Gossypium hirsutum), sugar beet (Beta vulgaris), and dhaincha (Sesbania aculeata), aids biological amelioration.

Reclamation and Management of Sodic (Alkali) Soils

Sodic soils, commonly known as 'Black Alkali' or 'Usar' soils, exhibit high ESP and pH due to sodium carbonate and bicarbonate. Reclamation requires chemical amendments that supply soluble calcium to replace exchangeable sodium on the soil exchange complex, followed by leaching of the displaced sodium.

  • Gypsum (Calcium Sulfate Dihydrate, CaSO4.2H2O): The most widely used, economical, and effective amendment. Gypsum application neutralizes alkalinity by releasing calcium ions, which replace sodium on the exchange sites: 2Na+-Clay + CaSO4 -> Ca2+-Clay + Na2SO4. The soluble sodium sulfate is subsequently leached out.
  • Gypsum Requirement (GR): The quantity of pure gypsum required to reduce the ESP of a specific depth of soil to the desired level. Gypsum requirement is typically calculated based on the Exchangeable Sodium to be replaced, where 1 milliequivalent of exchangeable sodium per 100 g of soil requires approximately 1.72 tonnes of 100% pure gypsum per hectare-15 cm soil depth.
  • Alternative Amendments: For calcareous sodic soils or under flooded conditions, elemental sulfur (S), pyrites (FeS2), sulfuric acid (H2SO4), and calcium chloride (CaCl2) are utilized. Pyrites act via microbial oxidation to produce sulfuric acid, which subsequently reacts with calcium carbonate to form gypsum in situ.
  • Green Manuring: Incorporating green manure crops such as Sesbania aculeata (Dhaincha) at 45 to 60 days after sowing adds organic acids, reduces pH, increases carbon dioxide partial pressure, and improves soil physical structure.

Reclamation and Management of Acid Soils

Acid soils, prevalent in high-rainfall regions and lateritic zones, restrict crop growth due to aluminum and manganese toxicity, phosphorus fixation by iron and aluminum oxides, and reduced microbial activity, especially symbiotic nitrogen fixation.

  • Liming Materials: The primary method of ameliorating soil acidity is the application of agricultural lime. Common liming materials include calcitic limestone (CaCO3), dolomitic limestone [CaMg(CO3)2], quicklime (CaO), and slaked lime [Ca(OH)2].
  • Neutralization Reaction: Liming materials dissolve in soil water and react with hydrogen and aluminum ions, raising the soil pH and precipitating toxic aluminum as insoluble aluminum hydroxide: Al3+ + 3OH- -> Al(OH)3.
  • Lime Requirement (LR): Determined by buffer methods (such as Shoemaker, McLean, and Pratt buffer method) to neutralize active and exchangeable acidity to a target pH (typically 6.0 to 6.5). Application is generally recommended 3 to 4 weeks prior to sowing to allow complete chemical reaction.
  • Phosphorus Management: Acid soils fix applied soluble phosphorus as insoluble iron and aluminum phosphates. Band placement of phosphatic fertilizers or co-application with farmyard manure (FYM) reduces P fixation and enhances availability.

Agronomic Package and Nutrient Management in Problematic Soils

Managing crops in remediated problematic soils requires tailored agronomic interventions, precise seed rates, specialized fertilizer schedules, and vigilant plant protection to ensure economic yields.

  • Nutrient Adjustments: Sodic soils typically require 25% higher nitrogen doses than normal soils because nitrogen losses through ammonia volatilization are high under alkaline conditions. Conversely, phosphatic and potassic fertilizers should be applied based on soil test reports, with micronutrients like zinc sulfate (ZnSO4 at 25 kg ha-1) applied basal, as zinc deficiency is ubiquitous in sodic soils.
  • Seed Rate and Spacing: In saline or sodic soils, seed rates are increased by 20% to 25% compared to normal soils to compensate for lower germination percentages. Spacing is kept slightly closer to ensure optimal plant population despite localized mortality.
  • Irrigation Water Quality: Irrigation water quality is evaluated using three primary indices: Electrical Conductivity (EC), Sodium Adsorption Ratio [SAR = Na+ / √((Ca2+ + Mg2+) / 2)], and Residual Sodium Carbonate [RSC = (CO32- + HCO3-) - (Ca2+ + Mg2+)]. High RSC water causes rapid sodication of soil and must be treated with gypsum before application.

⚡ High-Yield Exam Points (UP TGT / PGT Focus)
  • Saline soils have an Electrical Conductivity (EC) > 4 dS m⁻¹, ESP < 15, and pH < 8.5.
  • Sodic soils have an EC < 4 dS m⁻¹, ESP > 15, and pH between 8.5 and 10.0.
  • Gypsum (CaSO₄·2H₂O) is the universal chemical amendment used for reclaiming sodic and alkali soils.
  • Green manure crop Sesbania aculeata (Dhaincha) is highly effective for green manuring in sodic soils due to its high tolerance to alkalinity.
  • Residual Sodium Carbonate (RSC) formula is (CO₃²⁻ + HCO₃⁻) - (Ca²⁺ + Mg²⁺), measured in meq L⁻¹.
  • Sodium Adsorption Ratio (SAR) is calculated as Na⁺ / √((Ca²⁺ + Mg²⁺) / 2) with concentrations in meq L⁻¹.
  • Acid soils are reclaimed by applying agricultural lime (CaCO₃) to neutralize toxic aluminum and hydrogen ions.
  • Nitrogen requirement in sodic soils is 20-25% higher than normal soils due to ammonia volatilization losses.
⚠️ Common Mistakes & Exam Pitfalls
  • Confusing saline soils with sodic soils regarding amendment application: Saline soils require leaching and drainage, whereas sodic soils require chemical amendments like gypsum.
  • Applying gypsum to acid soils: Gypsum contains sulfur and calcium sulfate, which can further lower pH or add unnecessary calcium to soils already requiring basic liming materials.
  • Assuming high electrical conductivity alone defines sodic soils: Sodic soils are characterized by high ESP (>15) and high pH (>8.5), whereas EC is typically below 4 dS m⁻¹.

⚡ Chapter Master Summary: Reclamation Methods for Problematic Soils

Systematic concept review designed for quick retention and last-minute pre-exam revision.

Concept 1

Saline Soil Genesis and Management

Saline soils form through the accumulation of neutral soluble salts under arid and semi-arid climates where evaporation exceeds precipitation. Management focuses on leaching salts out of the root zone.

  • Leaching Requirement: Fraction of water needed to leach salts, calculated as EC_iw / EC_e.
  • Sub-surface Drainage: Essential to maintain the water table below critical depth and prevent secondary salinization.
Concept 2

Sodic Soil Reclamation via Gypsum

Sodic soils possess high exchangeable sodium, causing clay dispersion and impermeability. Gypsum supplies calcium ions to replace sodium on the exchange complex.

  • Gypsum Requirement (GR): Quantity of pure gypsum required to reduce ESP to the desired threshold.
  • Green Manuring: Incorporation of Sesbania aculeata lowers soil pH through organic acid production and CO₂ release.
Concept 3

Acid Soil Amelioration and Liming

Acid soils are characterized by low pH and toxic levels of active aluminum and manganese. Liming is the definitive corrective measure.

  • Liming Materials: Calcitic limestone, dolomitic limestone, and slaked lime neutralize active acidity.
  • Phosphorus Fixation: Mitigated in acid soils through band placement and co-application with organic manures.
Concept 4

Irrigation Water Quality Indices

Assessing irrigation water quality prevents the secondary degradation of normal soils into saline or sodic problematic classes.

  • SAR: Evaluates sodium hazard relative to calcium and magnesium.
  • RSC: Measures carbonate and bicarbonate excess over divalent cations, requiring gypsum neutralization if RSC > 2.5 meq L⁻¹.

📚 Technical Terms Glossary

Saline Soil (Solonchak)
Soil with EC > 4 dS m⁻¹, ESP < 15, and pH < 8.5, dominated by neutral soluble salts.
Sodic Soil (Solonetz / Alkali Soil)
Soil with EC < 4 dS m⁻¹, ESP > 15, and pH between 8.5 and 10.0, dominated by sodium carbonates.
Acid Soil (Ultisol / Oxisol / Acidic Soil)
Soil with pH < 6.5 characterized by exchangeable hydrogen, aluminum toxicity, and P fixation.
Electrical Conductivity (EC (dS m⁻¹))
A measure of the concentration of soluble salts in the soil solution or water extract.
Exchangeable Sodium Percentage (ESP (%))
The percentage of the soil cation exchange capacity occupied by sodium ions.
Sodium Adsorption Ratio (SAR)
A mathematical index expressing the relative activity of sodium ions in exchange reactions with soil.
Residual Sodium Carbonate (RSC (meq L⁻¹))
Index measuring carbonate and bicarbonate hazards in irrigation water relative to calcium and magnesium.
Gypsum Requirement (GR (tonnes ha⁻¹))
Quantity of agricultural gypsum required to neutralize sodium in sodic soils to a target ESP.
Leaching Requirement (LR)
Fraction of irrigation water required to be leached through the root zone to control salinity.
White Alkali (Saline Soil Efflorescence)
Surface salt crust formed by capillary action and evaporation in saline soils.
Black Alkali (Sodic Soil Dispersion)
Dark organic matter dissolved by sodium carbonate spreading across the surface of sodic soils.
Green Manuring (Biological Amelioration)
Growing and incorporating green tissue crops like Sesbania aculeata to improve soil chemical and physical properties.

🎯 Must-Remember High-Frequency Facts

  • Saline soil: EC > 4 dS m⁻¹, ESP < 15, pH < 8.5.
  • Sodic soil: EC < 4 dS m⁻¹, ESP > 15, pH 8.5 - 10.0.
  • Chemical formula of gypsum: CaSO₄·2H₂O.
  • Green manure crop for sodic soils: Sesbania aculeata (Dhaincha).

📌 Quick Recap Bullet Points

  • Saline soils require leaching and drainage; sodic soils require chemical amendments like gypsum; acid soils require liming.
  • Gypsum replaces exchangeable sodium with calcium on the soil exchange complex.
  • Irrigation water quality is assessed using EC, SAR, and RSC indices.
  • Nitrogen fertilizer doses should be increased by 20-25% in sodic soils to compensate for volatilization losses.

⚖️ Concepts Often Confused in Examinations

Saline Soils VS Sodic Soils

Saline soils have high EC (>4) and neutral pH (<8.5) requiring leaching; Sodic soils have high ESP (>15) and high pH (>8.5) requiring gypsum.

SAR VS RSC

SAR measures sodium relative to calcium and magnesium in irrigation water, whereas RSC measures carbonate and bicarbonate excess over calcium and magnesium.

Question 1 of 20 Conceptual MCQ

What is the threshold Electrical Conductivity (EC) value that characterizes saline soils at 25 degrees Celsius?

Explanation: Saline soils are officially defined by the United States Department of Agriculture (USDA) as having an electrical conductivity of the saturation extract greater than 4 dS m⁻¹ at 25 degrees Celsius, an exchangeable sodium percentage less than 15, and a pH generally below 8.5.

Question 2 of 20 Conceptual MCQ

Which chemical amendment is universally recommended for the reclamation of sodic (alkali) soils?

Explanation: Gypsum (calcium sulfate dihydrate) is the most effective, economical, and widely used chemical amendment for sodic soils. It provides divalent calcium ions (Ca²⁺) that replace exchangeable sodium (Na⁺) on the soil exchange complex.

Question 3 of 20 Conceptual MCQ

What is the Exchangeable Sodium Percentage (ESP) threshold characteristic of sodic soils?

Explanation: Sodic soils are characterized by an Exchangeable Sodium Percentage (ESP) greater than 15. This high sodium saturation causes dispersion of soil colloids, leading to poor soil structure and restricted permeability.

Question 4 of 20 Conceptual MCQ

Which green manure crop is exceptionally tolerant to sodic and alkaline soil conditions and is widely used for green manuring?

Explanation: Sesbania aculeata (Dhaincha) is the most preferred green manure crop for sodic soils. It exhibits high tolerance to alkalinity and waterlogging, accumulating substantial organic matter and reducing soil pH upon incorporation.

Question 5 of 20 Conceptual MCQ

How should nitrogen fertilizer doses be adjusted when cultivating crops in sodic soils?

Explanation: In sodic soils, nitrogen fertilizer doses should be increased by 20% to 25% compared to normal soils. High soil pH promotes rapid ammonia volatilization, reducing the nitrogen available to plants.

Question 6 of 20 Conceptual MCQ

What is the primary agronomic method for reclaiming saline soils?

Explanation: Saline soils do not require chemical amendments because they already contain adequate calcium. Reclamation is achieved by leaching excess soluble salts out of the root zone using good quality irrigation water, facilitated by sub-surface drainage.

Question 7 of 20 Conceptual MCQ

Which of the following describes the formula for Sodium Adsorption Ratio (SAR)?

Explanation: The Sodium Adsorption Ratio is expressed as SAR = Na⁺ / √((Ca²⁺ + Mg²⁺) / 2), where ionic concentrations are expressed in milliequivalents per liter (meq L⁻¹).

Question 8 of 20 Conceptual MCQ

What is the primary cause of soil acidity in high-rainfall tropical and subtropical regions?

Explanation: In high-rainfall regions, heavy percolation leaches basic cations like calcium, magnesium, and potassium from the soil profile. This leaves behind exchangeable hydrogen and active aluminum, resulting in low soil pH.

Question 9 of 20 Conceptual MCQ

Which material is used to reclaim acid soils?

Explanation: Agricultural limestone (calcium carbonate) or dolomitic limestone is applied to acid soils to neutralize active hydrogen and aluminum ions, raising the soil pH to optimal agronomic ranges.

Question 10 of 20 Conceptual MCQ

What is the term used to describe the white salt crust formed on the surface of saline soils due to high evaporation?

Explanation: Saline soils are frequently called 'White Alkali' soils because neutral soluble salts accumulate as a white crust on the soil surface during intense periods of surface evaporation.

Question 11 of 20 Conceptual MCQ

What is the primary agricultural hazard associated with irrigation water having high Residual Sodium Carbonate (RSC)?

Explanation: When irrigation water has high RSC (>2.5 meq L⁻¹), calcium and magnesium precipitate out as insoluble carbonates (CaCO₃ and MgCO₃). This increases the relative proportion of sodium in the soil water, leading to rapid sodication and rising ESP.

Question 12 of 20 Conceptual MCQ

Which of the following crops exhibits the highest tolerance to soil salinity?

Explanation: Barley (Hordeum vulgare) is the most salt-tolerant cereal crop, making it an excellent choice for cultivation during the initial stages of saline soil reclamation.

Question 13 of 20 Conceptual MCQ

What is the typical pH range of sodic (alkali) soils?

Explanation: Sodic soils characteristically exhibit high alkalinity with pH values ranging from 8.5 to 10.0, driven by the hydrolysis of sodium carbonate and sodium bicarbonate.

Question 14 of 20 Conceptual MCQ

Why is sub-surface drainage essential in the reclamation of waterlogged saline soils?

Explanation: Sub-surface drainage lowers the shallow saline water table below the critical depth (usually 1.5–2.0 m). This stops capillary rise and allows leached salts to drain out of the root zone rather than reaccumulating at the surface.

Question 15 of 20 Conceptual MCQ

What is the primary mechanism by which pyrites (FeS₂) act as an amendment for sodic soils?

Explanation: Pyrites (FeS₂) undergo microbial oxidation in moist soil to produce sulfuric acid (H₂SO₄). This acid reacts with native calcium carbonate (CaCO₃) present in calcareous sodic soils to form gypsum (CaSO₄·2H₂O) in situ, which then replaces exchangeable sodium.

Question 16 of 20 Conceptual MCQ

What is the Leaching Requirement (LR) formula?

Explanation: Leaching Requirement (LR) is defined mathematically as LR = EC_iw / EC_e, representing the ratio of the electrical conductivity of irrigation water to the electrical conductivity of the saturation extract tolerated by the crop without yield reduction.

Question 17 of 20 Conceptual MCQ

Which nutrient deficiency is most commonly observed in crops grown on calcareous sodic soils?

Explanation: Zinc deficiency is extremely prevalent in sodic soils due to high pH and calcium carbonate presence, which precipitate zinc into unavailable forms. Basal application of zinc sulfate (ZnSO₄) at 25 kg ha⁻¹ is standard agronomic practice.

Question 18 of 20 Conceptual MCQ

Which of the following plant nutrients is fixed in unavailable forms in acid soils due to reaction with iron and aluminum oxides?

Explanation: In acid soils (pH < 6.5), soluble phosphorus reacts with active iron and aluminum oxides to form insoluble iron and aluminum phosphates, causing severe phosphorus fixation.

Question 19 of 20 Conceptual MCQ

What effect does high Exchangeable Sodium Percentage (ESP > 15) have on the physical properties of soil?

Explanation: High ESP (>15) causes the dispersion of clay particles and organic matter. This destroys soil aggregation, seals soil pores, and drastically reduces water infiltration and aeration, creating a hard surface crust upon drying.

Question 20 of 20 Conceptual MCQ

Why are seed rates increased by 20% to 25% when sowing crops in saline or sodic soils?

Explanation: High salinity and alkalinity impose severe osmotic stress and ionic toxicity during germination. Increasing the seed rate by 20% to 25% ensures an adequate final plant stand despite lower germination percentages.

Test Question 1 of 10 PGT Standard

Calculate the approximate pure gypsum requirement (in tonnes ha⁻¹) for a sodic soil if the Exchangeable Sodium to be replaced is 5 milliequivalents per 100 g of soil for a 15 cm soil depth, assuming a soil bulk density of 1.33 g cm⁻³.

Solution & Rationale: 1 meq of exchangeable sodium per 100 g of soil requires approximately 1.72 tonnes of 100% pure gypsum per hectare per 15 cm depth. For 5 meq, the calculation is 5 * 1.72 = 8.6 tonnes per acre, or approx 17.2 tonnes per hectare (using standard conversion factors where 1 meq/100g = 3.44 tonnes per acre-foot or 1.72 tonnes per 15cm layer). Thus, 5 * 1.72 * 2 = 17.2 tonnes ha⁻¹.

Test Question 2 of 10 TGT Standard

Which of the following classifications correctly identifies a soil with Electrical Conductivity (EC) = 5.2 dS m⁻¹, Exchangeable Sodium Percentage (ESP) = 10, and pH = 7.8?

Solution & Rationale: Since EC is greater than 4 dS m⁻¹, ESP is less than 15, and pH is less than 8.5, the soil fits the precise criteria of a Saline Soil.

Test Question 3 of 10 PGT Standard

What is the primary reason why continuous application of high Residual Sodium Carbonate (RSC) irrigation water destroys soil structure?

Solution & Rationale: High RSC in irrigation water precipitates calcium and magnesium out of solution as insoluble carbonates. This increases the relative concentration of sodium, elevating the SAR and ESP of the soil, which subsequently disperses clay colloids and ruins soil structure.

Test Question 4 of 10 TGT Standard

During the reclamation of sodic soils using gypsum, what happens to the displaced sodium ions?

Solution & Rationale: When gypsum (CaSO₄) reacts with sodium-saturated clay, calcium replaces sodium on the exchange complex, forming sodium sulfate (Na₂SO₄), which is highly soluble and easily leached out of the root zone with irrigation water.

Test Question 5 of 10 PGT Standard

Which amendment is most appropriate for reclaiming calcareous sodic soils where direct application of sulfuric acid is uneconomical?

Solution & Rationale: In calcareous sodic soils containing native calcium carbonate, elemental sulfur or pyrites (FeS₂) can be applied. They oxidize biologically to form sulfuric acid, which reacts with native calcium carbonate to generate gypsum in situ.

Test Question 6 of 10 TGT Standard

What is the recommended timing for applying agricultural lime to acid soils before sowing crops?

Solution & Rationale: Agricultural lime requires adequate moisture and time to undergo chemical reactions and neutralize soil acidity. Applying it 3 to 4 weeks prior to sowing ensures the neutralization reaction is largely complete before seeds are planted.

Test Question 7 of 10 PGT Standard

An irrigation water sample has Ca²⁺ = 4 meq L⁻¹, Mg²⁺ = 2 meq L⁻¹, and Na⁺ = 12 meq L⁻¹. What is its Sodium Adsorption Ratio (SAR)?

Solution & Rationale: Using SAR = Na⁺ / √((Ca²⁺ + Mg²⁺) / 2) -> SAR = 12 / √((4 + 2) / 2) = 12 / √(6 / 2) = 12 / √3 = 12 / 1.732 = 6.92 (approx 6.0 in simplified exam options, or strictly calculated closer to 6.9). Let's review calculation: Na=12, (Ca+Mg)/2 = (4+2)/2 = 3. Square root of 3 is 1.732. 12 / 1.732 = 6.92.

Test Question 8 of 10 TGT Standard

Which of the following is a characteristic feature of saline-sodic soils?

Solution & Rationale: Saline-sodic soils exhibit properties of both groups: Electrical Conductivity is greater than 4 dS m⁻¹, Exchangeable Sodium Percentage is greater than 15, and pH is usually around or slightly above 8.5.

Test Question 9 of 10 TGT Standard

What is the primary function of gypsum in correcting sodic soils, chemically speaking?

Solution & Rationale: Gypsum provides soluble calcium ions (Ca²⁺) which have a higher valence and affinity for exchange sites than sodium ions (Na⁺), successfully replacing sodium on the clay exchange complex.

Test Question 10 of 10 PGT Standard

Why is dolomitic limestone preferred over calcitic limestone in certain acid soils?

Solution & Rationale: Dolomitic limestone [CaMg(CO₃)₂] supplies both calcium and magnesium, making it doubly beneficial for acid soils that are frequently deficient in both basic cations.
