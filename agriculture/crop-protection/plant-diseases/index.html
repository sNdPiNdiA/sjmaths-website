1. Fundamentals of Phytopathology and Classification of Plant Diseases

Phytopathology is the scientific study of plant diseases caused by pathogens (infectious agents) and environmental conditions (physiological factors). A plant disease is defined as any harmful deviation from the normal functioning of physiological processes, leading to impaired growth, reduced yield, or death of the plant or its parts.

  • Biotic (Infectious) Diseases: Caused by living organisms such as fungi, bacteria, phytoplasmas, viruses, viroids, nematodes, and parasitic flowering plants. These agents are transmissible from diseased to healthy plants.
  • Abiotic (Non-infectious / Physiological) Diseases: Caused by unfavorable environmental factors such as nutrient deficiencies or toxicities, extreme temperatures, moisture stress, and chemical phytotoxicity. These are non-transmissible.
  • Sign vs. Symptom: A sign is the physical evidence or body of the pathogen itself (e.g., mycelium, spores, bacterial ooze), whereas a symptom is the external or internal reaction or alteration of the plant as a response to the pathogen (e.g., chlorosis, necrosis, wilting, gall formation).

2. Fungal Plant Diseases: Morphology, Reproduction, and Major Crop Pathogens

Fungi constitute the largest group of plant pathogens, accounting for more than 85% of infectious plant diseases. They are eukaryotic, heterotrophic organisms possessing cell walls primarily composed of chitin and glucans.

  • Major Groups: Oomycetes (pseudo-fungi, e.g., Phytophthora infestans), Ascomycetes (e.g., powdery mildews, ergot), and Basidiomycetes (rusts and smuts).
  • Cereal Diseases: Wheat rusts caused by Puccinia graminis tritici (stem rust), Puccinia striiformis (stripe rust), and Puccinia triticina (leaf rust). Rice blast is caused by Magnaporthe oryzae.
  • Pulse and Oilseed Diseases: Pigeonpea wilt caused by Fusarium udum; Mustard white rust caused by Albugo candida.
  • Survival and Dispersal: Fungi disseminate through wind-borne spores (urediniospores, conidia), water-splashed zoospores, or soil-borne structures like chlamydospores, sclerotia, and oospores.

3. Bacterial, Phytoplasmal, Viral, and Nematode Diseases

Non-fungal pathogens cause devastating losses in agricultural crops and require specific vector management and sanitation protocols.

  • Bacterial Pathogens: Prokaryotic, unicellular organisms entering plants through natural openings or wounds. Examples include Xanthomonas oryzae pv. oryzae (Bacterial leaf blight of rice) and Ralstonia solanacearum (Bacterial wilt of solanaceous crops).
  • Phytoplasmas: Wall-less prokaryotes residing in the phloem tissue, vectored exclusively by leafhoppers, planthoppers, and psyllids. Example: Little leaf of brinjal and Sesame phyllody.
  • Plant Viruses: Sub-microscopic nucleoprotein complexes obligate intracellular parasites. Often transmitted by insect vectors such as aphids, whiteflies (Bemisia tabaci), and thrips. Example: Yellow vein mosaic of okra and Tomato leaf curl virus.
  • Plant Parasitic Nematodes: Unicellular or microscopic roundworms possessing a hollow stylet to puncture plant cells. Example: Root-knot nematode (Meloidogyne incognita) causing characteristic root galls.

4. Disease Triangle and Epidemiological Factors in Plant Pathology

The occurrence and severity of an infectious plant disease are governed by the interaction of three primary factors, commonly conceptualized as the Disease Triangle.

  • Susceptible Host: The genetic predisposition of the crop variety to infection by a specific pathogen.
  • Virulent Pathogen: The presence of an aggressive strain of the pathogen with adequate inoculum potential.
  • Favorable Environment: Specific meteorological parameters including high relative humidity, ambient temperature ranges, and leaf wetness duration.
  • Disease Tetrahedron: Modern epidemiology includes a fourth dimension—Time—and human intervention (crop management practices) influencing the rate of disease development in plant populations.

5. Integrated Disease Management (IDM) and Chemical Control Strategies

Integrated Disease Management (IDM) integrates all economic, ecological, and safe control tactics to suppress pathogen populations below the economic injury level (EIL).

  • Regulatory Methods: Plant quarantine acts, domestic and international embargoes, and inspection services to prevent the introduction of exotic plant pathogens.
  • Cultural Practices: Crop rotation with non-host crops, deep summer ploughing to expose resting spores, adjustment of sowing time, rogueing of infected plants, and balanced fertilizer application (avoiding excessive nitrogen).
  • Biological Control: Utilization of antagonistic microorganisms such as Trichoderma viride and Pseudomonas fluorescens to suppress soil-borne fungal pathogens.
  • Chemical Control: Application of protectant fungicides (e.g., Mancozeb, Copper oxychloride) and systemic fungicides (e.g., Carbendazim, Hexaconazole, Propiconazole) at critical economic thresholds.

⚡ High-Yield Exam Points (UP TGT / PGT Focus)
  • Late blight of potato is caused by the oomycete pathogen Phytophthora infestans, responsible for the historic Irish Famine of 1845.
  • Bordeaux mixture, discovered by Millardet in France, is composed of Copper sulfate, Lime, and Water in the standard ratio of 5:5:50.
  • The vector for transmission of Yellow Vein Mosaic Virus (YVMV) in okra is the whitefly Bemisia tabaci.
  • Little leaf of brinjal is a phytoplasmal disease transmitted primarily by the leafhopper Cestius phycitis.
  • Loose smut of wheat (Ustilago tritici) is an internally seed-borne disease controlled effectively by systemic fungicides like carboxin.
  • Ergot of bajra (pearl millet) is caused by Claviceps fusiformis, producing toxic sclerotia known as ergot bodies containing alkaloids.
  • Root-knot nematodes (Meloidogyne spp.) cause characteristic galling on roots and are managed effectively using neem cake or biological antagonist Trichoderma.
  • Tikka disease of groundnut is caused by two distinct fungal species: Cercospora arachidicola (early tikka) and Cercosporidium personatum (late tikka).
⚠️ Common Mistakes & Exam Pitfalls
  • Confusing bacterial leaf blight of rice (caused by Xanthomonas oryzae) with bacterial streak; remember that BLB causes yellowish-white wavy margins along the leaf blade with bacterial ooze.
  • Assuming viruses can be controlled by standard fungicides; viruses are obligate intracellular entities requiring vector management and resistant breeding rather than direct fungicidal sprays.
  • Misidentifying powdery mildew and downy mildew: Powdery mildew is an ectoparasite occurring on the upper leaf surface favored by dry weather, while downy mildew is an obligate oomycete endoparasite requiring high relative humidity and leaf wetness.

Systematic concept review designed for quick retention and last-minute pre-exam revision.

Concept 1

Pathogenicity and Host-Parasite Interactions

Pathogenicity refers to the ability of a pathogen to cause disease, whereas virulence denotes the degree of pathogenicity within a species.

  • Infection Court: The specific site on the host plant where the pathogen establishes initial contact and penetration.
  • Hypersensitive Response: A rapid localized cell death of host tissues surrounding the infection site to restrict the systemic spread of obligate parasites.
Concept 2

Disease Cycle Dynamics

The chain of events involved in disease development including inoculation, penetration, infection, invasion, reproduction, dissemination, and overwintering.

  • Inoculum: Any part of the pathogen that can initiate infection.
  • Primary Inoculum: Overwintering or oversummering propagules initiating the initial disease cycle in a season.
Concept 3

Fungicidal Classification by Mode of Action

Fungicides are classified based on their chemical nature and behavior upon application to the plant surface.

  • Protectant Fungicides: Non-systemic compounds that remain on the plant surface to prevent spore germination (e.g., Mancozeb, Captan).
  • Systemic Fungicides: Absorbed by plant tissues and translocated internally to eradicate established infections (e.g., Carbendazim, Metalaxyl).
Concept 4

Biological Control and Soil Microbiome

Utilization of beneficial microorganisms to antagonize plant pathogens through competition, antibiosis, and mycoparasitism.

  • Trichoderma spp.: Filamentous fungi widely utilized for seed and soil treatment against soil-borne pathogens like Rhizoctonia and Fusarium.
  • Pseudomonas fluorescens: Plant growth-promoting rhizobacteria (PGPR) inducing systemic resistance (ISR) in host crops.

📚 Technical Terms Glossary

Bordeaux Mixture (Copper sulfate + Calcium hydroxide + Water)
A classic broad-spectrum protective fungicide discovered by Alexis Millardet in France.
Chlorosis (Pathological Yellowing)
The yellowing of normally green plant tissue due to chlorophyll destruction or inhibition.
Necrosis (Cellular Death)
Death of plant cells and tissues resulting in brown, black, or dry lesions.
Sclerotium (Resting Fungal Body)
A compact mass of hardened fungal mycelium containing food reserves capable of surviving harsh environmental conditions.
Urediniospore (Dikaryotic Rust Spore)
The repeating, highly infectious asexual spore stage of rust fungi responsible for rapid secondary spread.
Oospore (Sexual Resting Spore)
A thick-walled sexual spore formed by oomycetes, serving as the primary overwintering structure.
Vector (Biological Transmitting Agent)
An organism, typically an insect, that transmits a pathogen from one plant to another.
Systemic Acquired Resistance (SAR)
A broad-spectrum, systemic resistance mechanism induced in plants following localized pathogen infection.
Rogueing (Selective Elimination)
The removal and destruction of diseased, weak, or off-type plants from a crop field to prevent further spread.
Phyllody (Floral Metamorphosis)
A phytoplasmal symptom where floral parts are transformed into leaf-like structures, causing complete sterility.

🎯 Must-Remember High-Frequency Facts

  • Bengal Famine (1943) was triggered by Rice Brown Spot (Bipolaris oryzae).
  • Bordeaux mixture standard preparation ratio is 5:5:50.

📌 Quick Recap Bullet Points

  • Plant diseases are categorized into biotic (pathogenic) and abiotic (physiological) disorders.
  • Fungi represent the major causal agents of plant diseases, reproducing via various spore types.
  • The disease triangle consists of host, pathogen, and environment interacting over time.
  • Integrated Disease Management (IDM) combines cultural, biological, chemical, and regulatory practices.

⚖️ Concepts Often Confused in Examinations

Sign VS Symptom

A sign is the physical presence of the pathogen structure itself, whereas a symptom is the plant's internal or external reaction.

Systemic Fungicide VS Protectant Fungicide

Systemic fungicides are absorbed and translocated inside the plant to cure infections, while protectants remain on the surface to prevent initial entry.

Question 1 of 20 Conceptual MCQ

Which of the following plant diseases is caused by an Oomycete rather than a true fungus?

Explanation: Late blight of potato is caused by Phytophthora infestans, which belongs to the Oomycetes (water molds), possessing cellulose cell walls rather than true fungal chitin.

Question 2 of 20 Conceptual MCQ

Who discovered the famous Bordeaux mixture used for controlling downy mildew of grapes?

Explanation: Alexis Millardet discovered Bordeaux mixture in France in 1885 while observing grapevines treated with copper sulfate and lime to deter pilfering.

Question 3 of 20 Conceptual MCQ

What is the primary vector responsible for transmitting Yellow Vein Mosaic Virus (YVMV) in okra?

Explanation: Yellow vein mosaic virus of okra is a geminivirus vectored exclusively by the sweetpotato whitefly, Bemisia tabaci.

Question 4 of 20 Conceptual MCQ

Which fungal structure serves as the primary overwintering resting spore for loose smut of wheat?

Explanation: Loose smut of wheat is caused by Ustilago tritici, where teliospores survive inside the grain embryo and infect the emerging seedling systemically.

Question 5 of 20 Conceptual MCQ

Little leaf of brinjal is caused by which of the following specialized plant pathogens?

Explanation: Little leaf of brinjal is a classic phytoplasmal disease characterized by extreme reduction in leaf size and plant stunting, vectored by leafhoppers.

Question 6 of 20 Conceptual MCQ

Which of the following is an internally seed-borne fungal disease of wheat?

Explanation: Loose smut of wheat (Ustilago tritici) is internally seed-borne, whereas Karnal bunt and hill bunt are externally seed-borne or soil-borne.

Question 7 of 20 Conceptual MCQ

Tikka disease of groundnut is caused by:

Explanation: Tikka disease of groundnut is caused by two fungal pathogens: Cercospora arachidicola (early leaf spot) and Cercosporidium personatum (late leaf spot).

Question 8 of 20 Conceptual MCQ

The Bengal famine of 1943, leading to massive crop devastation, was caused by which cereal disease?

Explanation: The devastating Bengal famine was caused by severe epiphytotic outbreaks of rice brown spot disease, drastically reducing food grain availability.

Question 9 of 20 Conceptual MCQ

Which chemical is commonly used as a soil fumigant and nematicide in intensive agriculture?

Explanation: Methyl bromide is a potent chemical fumigant utilized for soil sterilization to eliminate nematodes, weed seeds, and soil-borne pathogens.

Question 10 of 20 Conceptual MCQ

What is the standard chemical composition ratio of Copper sulfate and Lime in standard Bordeaux mixture?

Explanation: The standard formulation of Bordeaux mixture is 5:5:50, representing 5 pounds of copper sulfate, 5 pounds of lime, and 50 gallons of water.

Question 11 of 20 Conceptual MCQ

Which antagonistic biological agent is widely used for the management of soil-borne plant pathogenic fungi?

Explanation: Trichoderma viride is a well-known biological control agent that parasitizes and suppresses pathogenic fungi like Rhizoctonia, Pythium, and Fusarium.

Question 12 of 20 Conceptual MCQ

What is the primary symptom of red rot of sugarcane caused by Colletotrichum falcatum?

Explanation: Red rot of sugarcane produces characteristic internal reddening of the stalk pith interspersed with cross-wise white patches, along with a characteristic sour, alcoholic smell.

Question 13 of 20 Conceptual MCQ

Which rust fungus completes its life cycle on two different host species (heteroecious)?

Explanation: Wheat stem rust (Puccinia graminis tritici) is a heteroecious macrocyclic rust requiring wheat (primary host) and barberry (alternate host) to complete its complex five-spore-stage life cycle.

Question 14 of 20 Conceptual MCQ

Green ear disease of pearl millet (bajra) is caused by:

Explanation: Green ear disease (downy mildew) of bajra is caused by Sclerospora graminicola, where floral inflorescences are metamorphosed into leafy, twisted structures.

Question 15 of 20 Conceptual MCQ

Which of the following fungicides is classified as a systemic eradicant?

Explanation: Carbendazim is a systemic benzimidazole fungicide that can be absorbed by plant tissues to eradicate internal or newly established fungal infections.

Question 16 of 20 Conceptual MCQ

What type of symptom is characterized by localized sunken necrotic lesions on stems, fruits, or leaves bearing acervuli?

Explanation: Anthracnose refers to dark, sunken lesions on plant surfaces produced by fungal pathogens that typically form spore-bearing structures called acervuli.

Question 17 of 20 Conceptual MCQ

The disease triangle concept in plant pathology was formally articulated to emphasize the interaction between:

Explanation: The disease triangle comprises three essential components: a susceptible host, a virulent pathogen, and a favorable environment.

Question 18 of 20 Conceptual MCQ

Which plant parasitic nematode causes distinct root galls or root knots in vegetable crops?

Explanation: Meloidogyne incognita is the primary root-knot nematode species inducing characteristic multinucleate giant cells and root galling in solanaceous and cucurbit crops.

Question 19 of 20 Conceptual MCQ

Ergot of pearl millet (bajra) is primarily dangerous to human and animal health because:

Explanation: Ergot sclerotia produced by Claviceps fusiformis contain toxic alkaloids (ergotamine and ergometrine) which cause ergotism, leading to gangrene and neurological disorders.

Question 20 of 20 Conceptual MCQ

Which term describes the total prevention of pathogen entry into an area free from that specific disease?

Explanation: Exclusion refers to regulatory measures like plant quarantine designed to prevent the introduction and establishment of plant pathogens into uninfected geographical regions.

A systemic seed-borne disease of barley and wheat characterized by the replacement of grains with black powdery mass of spores is:

Test Question 2 of 10 TGT Standard

In plant disease epidemiology, what does 'Incubation Period' signify?

Solution & Rationale: The incubation period is the crucial time lapse between initial pathogen contact/penetration and the manifestation of first visual symptoms.

Test Question 3 of 10 PGT Standard

Which of the following is a classic example of an obligate parasite?

Solution & Rationale: Rust fungi like Puccinia graminis are obligate biotrophs that cannot complete their life cycle on artificial nutrient media.

Test Question 4 of 10 PGT Standard

What is the primary mode of action of systemic fungicides like Metalaxyl against Oomycete pathogens?

Solution & Rationale: Metalaxyl specifically inhibits fungal RNA polymerase I, arresting nucleic acid synthesis in oomycetes like Phytophthora and Pythium.

Test Question 5 of 10 TGT Standard

Which insect vector is responsible for the transmission of rice tungro virus?

Solution & Rationale: Green leafhopper (Nephotettix virescens) acts as the primary vector for rice tungro bacilliform and spherical viruses.

Test Question 6 of 10 PGT Standard

The phenomenon where a host plant produces phytoalexins in response to pathogen invasion is an example of:

Solution & Rationale: Phytoalexins are low-molecular-weight antimicrobial compounds synthesized actively by plants following infection or elicitor treatment.

Test Question 7 of 10 TGT Standard

What is the primary symptom associated with citrus tristeza virus (CTV)?

Solution & Rationale: Citrus tristeza causes severe stem pitting, phloem necrosis, and quick decline when trees are grafted on sour orange rootstocks.

Test Question 8 of 10 TGT Standard

Which group of fungicides contains inorganic copper compounds utilized as protectants?

Solution & Rationale: Copper oxychloride and copper sulfate belong to copper-based protectant fungicides effective against bacterial and fungal leaf spots.

Test Question 9 of 10 TGT Standard

What is the causal organism of white rust of crucifers?

Solution & Rationale: White rust of mustard and crucifers is caused by the oomycete pathogen Albugo candida, producing white blister-like pustules.

Test Question 10 of 10 PGT Standard

What does the term 'Hypovirulence' signify in plant pathology?

Solution & Rationale: Hypovirulence refers to reduced pathogenic capacity, often mediated by mycoviruses, utilized successfully in biological control of chestnut blight.
