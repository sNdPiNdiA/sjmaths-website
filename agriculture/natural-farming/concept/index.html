Natural and Organic Farming Systems

Concept of Natural and Organic Farming Systems in Indian Agriculture

1. Introduction and Epistemological Foundation of Natural and Organic Farming

Natural and organic farming represent holistic agricultural paradigms designed to work in complete harmony with natural ecosystems rather than dominating them through synthetic agrochemicals. While modern conventional agriculture relies heavily on fossil-fuel-derived synthetic nitrogenous, phosphatic, and potassic fertilizers, along with broad-spectrum chemical pesticides, natural and organic systems prioritize ecological balance, soil biological vitality, and closed nutrient loops.

  • Organic Farming: A production system that sustains the health of soils, ecosystems, and people. It relies on ecological processes, biodiversity, and cycles adapted to local conditions, rather than the use of inputs with adverse effects.
  • Natural Farming (Zero Budget Natural Farming / Prakritik Krishi): A chemical-free farming system drawing on traditional Indian agro-ecological practices, famously championed by Subhash Palekar, emphasizing microbial activation through indigenous cow dung and urine.
  • Core Objectives: Conservation of natural resources, elimination of chemical residues in the food chain, enhancement of soil organic matter (SOM), and maximization of soil biodiversity including earthworms and beneficial microflora like Azotobacter and Rhizobium species.

2. Core Pillars and Guiding Principles of Organic Agriculture

According to the International Federation of Organic Agriculture Movements (IFOAM), organic agriculture is founded on four interrelated and indivisible ethical principles which serve as the bedrock for competitive exam questions in UP TGT/PGT Agriculture.

  • Principle of Health: Agriculture should sustain and enhance the health of soil, plant, animal, human, and planet as one and indivisible. Healthy soils produce healthy crops that foster health in animals and humans.
  • Principle of Ecology: Farming systems should be modeled on living ecological systems, mimicking their cycles and balances. Management must be tailored to local agro-climatic conditions.
  • Principle of Fairness: Agriculture should build relationships that ensure fairness with regard to the common environment and life opportunities, providing a good quality of life and food sovereignty for all stakeholders.
  • Principle of Care: Management must be precautionary and responsible to protect the health and well-being of current and future generations and the environment.

3. Methodologies of Natural Farming: The Four Pillars of Subhash Palekar Natural Farming (SPNF)

Natural farming eliminates purchased external inputs, especially chemical fertilizers and synthetic pesticides, substituting them with on-farm biological preparations. Understanding these components is vital for agronomy examinations.

  • Jeevamrutha / Jiwamrita: A microbial culture prepared using 200 liters of water, 10 kg local cow dung, 10 liters cow urine, 2 kg jaggery, 2 kg pulse flour (besan), and a handful of live undisturbed forest soil. It multiplies native soil microorganisms exponentially.
  • Beejamrutha / Bijamrita: A seed treatment formulation using cow dung, cow urine, water, lime, and forest soil, applied to seeds, tubers, or rhizomes to protect young seedlings from soil-borne and seed-borne fungal pathogens such as Fusarium oxysporum and Rhizoctonia solani.
  • Whapasa (Soil Moisture-Air Balance): The condition where soil holds equal proportions of moisture and air, created through mulching (Achhadana) and reduced tillage, drastically reducing irrigation requirements.
  • Achhadana (Mulching): Covering topsoil with crop residues, live cover crops, or fallen leaves to suppress weed emergence, conserve soil moisture, regulate soil temperature, and prevent soil erosion.

4. Soil Fertility Management and Biological Nutrient Cycling

Maintaining and building soil organic matter (SOM) is the central tenet of both natural and organic nutrient management. Unlike conventional agriculture which applies immediate soluble ionic nutrients, organic systems rely on slow-release organic complexes mediated by soil macro- and micro-fauna.

  • Composting and Vermicomposting: Aerobic decomposition of organic waste using earthworms such as Eisenia fetida transforms crop residues and animal manure into nutrient-rich vermicompost containing plant-available nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium (NPK), alongside humic substances.
  • Green Manuring and Cover Cropping: Incorporation of fast-growing leguminous green manure crops such as sunnhemp (Crotalaria juncea), dhaincha (Sesbania aculeata), and cluster bean (Cyamopsis tetragonoloba) in situ to fix atmospheric nitrogen via symbiotic Rhizobium bacteria and add organic carbon.
  • Biofertilizers: Application of specialized microbial inoculants including nitrogen fixers (Azotobacter chroococcum, Azospirillum brasilense), phosphate solubilizing bacteria (PSB like Bacillus megaterium), and vesicular-arbuscular mycorrhizal (VAM) fungi to enhance nutrient availability.

5. Integrated Pest, Disease, and Weed Management (IPDM) in Organic Systems

Without synthetic chemical pesticides, natural and organic farming systems employ a multilayered prophylactic and therapeutic defense strategy to keep pest populations below Economic Injury Levels (EIL).

  • Botanical Pesticides: Utilization of plant-derived secondary metabolites, most notably Azadirachtin extracted from neem (Azadirachta indica) seeds, pongamia oil, and garlic-chili extracts acting as repellents, anti-feedants, and insect growth regulators.
  • Biopesticides: Application of entomopathogenic microorganisms such as the bacterium Bacillus thuringiensis (Bt), nucleopolyhedrovirus (NPV) for lepidopteran pests like Helicoverpa armigera, and entomopathogenic fungi like Beauveria bassiana and Metarhizium anisopliae.
  • Cultural and Mechanical Controls: Crop rotation, trap cropping (e.g., marigold with tomato for nematode and fruit borer management), summer ploughing to expose pupae, yellow sticky traps, and hand-picking of egg masses.

⚡ High-Yield Exam Points (UP TGT / PGT Focus)
  • Subhash Palekar popularized Zero Budget Natural Farming (ZBNF), now renamed Subhash Palekar Natural Farming (SPNF).
  • Jeevamrutha is a potent microbial culture containing cow dung, cow urine, jaggery, pulse flour, and soil to boost soil biological activity.
  • Beejamrutha is exclusively used for seed, tuber, and rhizome treatment to prevent soil- and seed-borne fungal infections.
  • Whapasa refers to the ideal physical condition where soil contains equal proportions of moisture and air, eliminating heavy irrigation needs.
  • IFOAM (International Federation of Organic Agriculture Movements) was established in 1972 with its headquarters in Bonn, Germany.
  • Azadirachtin, the principal limonoid active compound found in neem (Azadirachta indica), serves as a broad-spectrum botanical insecticide.
  • Vermicomposting utilizes epigeic earthworm species such as Eisenia fetida for rapid bioconversion of organic waste.
  • Green manuring with Sesbania aculeata (dhaincha) typically adds 80-120 kg N/ha when incorporated at 45-60 days after sowing.
⚠️ Common Mistakes & Exam Pitfalls
  • Students often confuse Natural Farming with Organic Farming; remember that organic farming permits certified off-farm organic inputs and rock powders, whereas Natural Farming strictly forbids any purchased inputs, even organic ones.
  • Confusing Beejamrutha with Jeevamrutha: Beejamrutha is strictly for seed and planting material treatment prior to sowing, while Jeevamrutha is applied to the soil along with irrigation water.
  • Assuming zero tillage means no soil disturbance ever; in natural farming, minimal shallow surface disturbance is permitted only during initial bed preparation if required, but continuous deep ploughing is strictly avoided.

⚡ Chapter Master Summary: Concept of Natural and Organic Farming Systems in Indian Agriculture

Systematic concept review designed for quick retention and last-minute pre-exam revision.

Concept 1

Epistemological Shift from Conventional to Ecological Agriculture

The paradigm shift in modern agriculture focuses on decoupling high crop yields from synthetic chemical dependency. Ecological agriculture encompasses both organic and natural farming systems.

  • Agro-ecology: The study of ecological processes applied to agricultural production systems, emphasizing biodiversity and nutrient cycling.
  • Soil Food Web: The complex network of organisms living in the soil whose interactions determine nutrient availability and soil structure.
Concept 2

The Four Pillars of Subhash Palekar Natural Farming (SPNF)

SPNF operates on four foundational pillars designed to eliminate debt and external chemical inputs for smallholder farmers.

  • Jeevamrutha: Microbial inoculant liquid applied to soil at 200 liters per acre per month.
  • Beejamrutha: Protective coating for seeds using cow dung, urine, and lime.
  • Achhadana: Mulching using crop residues to suppress weeds and conserve soil moisture.
  • Whapasa: Maintenance of optimal soil moisture-air equilibrium for root respiration.
Concept 3

IFOAM Four Pillars of Organic Agriculture

Global organic agriculture standards are anchored in four philosophical and practical pillars.

  • Principle of Health: Sustaining and enhancing soil, plant, and human health.
  • Principle of Ecology: Modeling farming systems on natural ecosystems.
  • Principle of Fairness: Ensuring equity and a good quality of life for all.
  • Principle of Care: Protecting the environment for future generations.
Concept 4

Biological Soil Enrichment and Biofertilizers

Soil biological health is restored through organic amendments and targeted microbial inoculants.

  • Vermicomposting: Conversion of organic residues into nutrient-dense manure using Eisenia fetida.
  • Green Manuring: In-situ incorporation of leguminous crops like Crotalaria juncea to fix atmospheric nitrogen.

📚 Technical Terms Glossary

Organic Farming (Ecological Production System)
A holistic production management system that promotes and enhances agro-ecosystem health, including biodiversity, biological cycles, and soil biological activity.
Natural Farming (Prakritik Krishi / ZBNF)
A chemical-free farming system based on indigenous cow-based microbial preparations and zero external purchased inputs.
Jeevamrutha (Microbial Culture Liquid)
An anaerobic/aerobic fermented liquid concoction of cow dung, urine, jaggery, and flour used to multiply native soil microbes.
Beejamrutha (Seed Treatment Solution)
A protective slurry made from cow dung, urine, and lime applied to seeds to protect against fungal pathogens.
Whapasa (Soil Moisture-Air Equilibrium)
The ideal physical state of soil where moisture and air are balanced in pore spaces to facilitate root respiration.
Achhadana (Soil Mulching)
Covering the soil surface with crop residues or living green covers to conserve moisture and suppress weeds.
Vermicompost (Earthworm-Processed Organic Manure)
Nutrient-rich organic fertilizer produced through the breakdown of organic matter by epigeic earthworms like Eisenia fetida.
Green Manuring (In-situ Biomass Incorporation)
Growing and incorporating succulent leguminous crops into the soil to add organic matter and fixed nitrogen.
Biofertilizer (Microbial Inoculant)
Substances containing living microorganisms that colonize the rhizosphere and promote growth by increasing nutrient supply.
IFOAM (International Federation of Organic Agriculture Movements)
The worldwide umbrella organization for the organic agriculture movement, founded in 1972.
Azadirachtin (C35H44O16 Limonoid)
The primary active insecticidal compound extracted from neem seeds (Azadirachta indica).

🎯 Must-Remember High-Frequency Facts

  • Natural farming prohibits all external purchased inputs, whereas organic farming allows certified organic off-farm inputs.
  • Beejamrutha is strictly used for seed/tuber treatment; Jeevamrutha is applied to the soil.
  • Eisenia fetida is the most commonly used epigeic earthworm species in commercial vermicomposting.
  • Azadirachtin from Azadirachta indica is the standard botanical insecticide used in organic pest management.

📌 Quick Recap Bullet Points

  • Natural and organic farming systems eliminate synthetic agrochemicals to protect soil health and human well-being.
  • Subhash Palekar Natural Farming relies on four pillars: Jeevamrutha, Beejamrutha, Whapasa, and Achhadana.
  • IFOAM defines organic agriculture based on health, ecology, fairness, and care.
  • Soil fertility is sustained through vermicomposting, green manuring, biofertilizers, and crop rotations.

⚖️ Concepts Often Confused in Examinations

Natural Farming VS Organic Farming

Natural farming permits zero purchased inputs and relies exclusively on local cow-based formulations; organic farming permits certified commercial organic fertilizers and biopesticides.

Jeevamrutha VS Beejamrutha

Jeevamrutha is applied to the soil to stimulate soil microbes, whereas Beejamrutha is coated onto seeds prior to planting.

Question 1 of 18 Conceptual MCQ

Who is the prominent agriculturist credited with pioneering and popularizing Zero Budget Natural Farming (ZBNF) in India?

Explanation: Subhash Palekar developed Zero Budget Natural Farming (now termed Subhash Palekar Natural Farming) as an alternative to the high-cost chemical green revolution model in India.

Question 2 of 18 Conceptual MCQ

Which of the following is NOT one of the four pillars of Subhash Palekar Natural Farming (SPNF)?

Explanation: Chemical NPK application belongs to conventional industrial agriculture and is strictly prohibited in natural and organic farming systems. The four pillars are Jeevamrutha, Beejamrutha, Whapasa, and Achhadana.

Question 3 of 18 Conceptual MCQ

What is the primary purpose of 'Beejamrutha' in natural farming?

Explanation: Beejamrutha is a protective concoction made from cow dung, urine, lime, and soil used specifically for treating seeds, tubers, and rhizomes to protect against seed-borne pathogens.

Question 4 of 18 Conceptual MCQ

What does the term 'Whapasa' signify in natural farming systems?

Explanation: Whapasa refers to the condition where soil holds equal proportions of moisture and air in its pore spaces, which is ideal for root respiration and minimizes irrigation frequency.

Question 5 of 18 Conceptual MCQ

What is the global umbrella organization for the organic agriculture movement established in 1972?

Explanation: IFOAM (International Federation of Organic Agriculture Movements), headquartered in Bonn, Germany, is the worldwide unifying body for organic agriculture standards.

Question 6 of 18 Conceptual MCQ

Which of the following earthworm species is most widely utilized for commercial vermicomposting due to its epigeic feeding habit?

Explanation: Eisenia fetida is an epigeic earthworm that lives in upper organic layers and consumes large amounts of organic waste rapidly, making it ideal for vermicomposting.

Question 7 of 18 Conceptual MCQ

Which active chemical compound extracted from neem (Azadirachta indica) seeds acts as a powerful botanical insecticide?

Explanation: Azadirachtin is a major limonoid secondary metabolite found in neem seeds that acts as an antifeedant, repellent, and insect growth regulator.

Question 8 of 18 Conceptual MCQ

Which of the following is an example of a green manuring legume commonly incorporated in Indian agriculture?

Explanation: Sesbania aculeata (dhaincha) is a prominent green manure crop used extensively in India to fix atmospheric nitrogen and add organic carbon to paddy soils.

Question 9 of 18 Conceptual MCQ

According to IFOAM, how many core ethical principles guide organic agriculture?

Explanation: IFOAM defines organic agriculture based on four foundational principles: Health, Ecology, Fairness, and Care.

Question 10 of 18 Conceptual MCQ

What is the main function of 'Jeevamrutha' when applied to agricultural fields?

Explanation: Jeevamrutha acts as a microbial culture medium that inoculates and exponentially multiplies beneficial native soil microorganisms and earthworms.

Question 11 of 18 Conceptual MCQ

In organic farming, which of the following practices is employed to suppress weed growth and conserve soil moisture using crop residues?

Explanation: Achhadana (mulching) involves covering the soil surface with crop residues or organic materials to smother weeds, reduce evaporation, and moderate soil temperature.

Question 12 of 18 Conceptual MCQ

Which biofertilizer is specifically known for establishing a symbiotic nitrogen-fixing association with legume root nodules?

Explanation: Rhizobium species form symbiotic relationships with the roots of leguminous plants, fixing atmospheric nitrogen directly within root nodules.

Question 13 of 18 Conceptual MCQ

Which category of microorganisms includes Phosphate Solubilizing Bacteria (PSB) such as Bacillus megaterium?

Explanation: Phosphate Solubilizing Bacteria (PSB) secrete organic acids that dissolve insoluble inorganic phosphates in the soil, making phosphorus available to plants.

Question 14 of 18 Conceptual MCQ

What is the primary difference between natural farming and organic farming?

Explanation: Natural farming strictly forbids any purchased off-farm inputs (even organic ones), whereas certified organic farming permits purchased organic fertilizers, rock powders, and approved biopesticides.

Question 15 of 18 Conceptual MCQ

Which of the following is an entomopathogenic fungus commonly used in biological pest management?

Explanation: Beauveria bassiana is an entomopathogenic fungus that infects and kills a wide range of agricultural insect pests, serving as an effective biopesticide.

Question 16 of 18 Conceptual MCQ

What is the primary role of Trichoderma species in organic crop production?

Explanation: Trichoderma species (e.g., Trichoderma viride, Trichoderma harzianum) act as potent biological control agents against soil-borne phytopathogenic fungi like Pythium and Rhizoctonia.

Question 17 of 18 Conceptual MCQ

Why is deep and intensive mechanical tillage generally discouraged in natural and organic farming systems?

Explanation: Intensive deep tillage destroys mycorrhizal fungal networks, earthworm burrows, and soil aggregate stability, accelerating the oxidation of soil organic matter.

Question 18 of 18 Conceptual MCQ

Which of the following best defines 'Soil Organic Matter' (SOM) in relation to organic farming?

Explanation: Soil Organic Matter (SOM) comprises all carbon-containing organic materials in the soil, playing a vital role in water retention, cation exchange capacity, and microbial habitat.

Test Question 2 of 10 PGT Standard

In the preparation of Jeevamrutha for one acre of agricultural land, which of the following standard ingredient proportions is recommended?

Test Question 3 of 10 TGT Standard

Which of the following describes the agronomic significance of 'Whapasa' in natural farming?

Solution & Rationale: Whapasa is the physical state of soil where moisture films coat soil particles while continuous air-filled pore spaces allow optimal oxygen diffusion to plant roots.

Test Question 4 of 10 PGT Standard

What is the primary mode of action of Azadirachtin derived from neem seeds against phytophagous insect pests?

Solution & Rationale: Azadirachtin does not act as a direct knockdown neurotoxin; instead, it disrupts insect feeding behavior, acts as a repellent, and interferes with ecdysone (molting hormone) regulation.

Test Question 5 of 10 TGT Standard

Which principle among the four IFOAM organic pillars emphasizes that agricultural management should be modeled on living ecological systems?

Solution & Rationale: The Principle of Ecology states that production should be based on living ecological systems and cycles, mimicking and helping to sustain them.

Test Question 6 of 10 PGT Standard

What is the role of vesicular-arbuscular mycorrhizal (VAM) fungi in organic nutrient management?

Solution & Rationale: VAM fungi form mutualistic associations with plant roots, extending their hyphal network far into the soil to scavenge and deliver immobile nutrients, particularly phosphorus and zinc.

Test Question 7 of 10 TGT Standard

Why is green manuring with leguminous crops considered superior to uncropped fallow in organic crop rotations?

Solution & Rationale: Green manuring with legumes adds substantial organic biomass, fixes biological nitrogen via root nodules, improves soil structure, and smothers weed emergence.

Test Question 8 of 10 TGT Standard

Which epigeic earthworm species is predominantly cultured in commercial vermicomposting units across India?

Solution & Rationale: Eisenia fetida (red wiggler) is chosen for commercial vermicomposting because of its high multiplication rate, wide temperature tolerance, and voracious appetite for organic waste.

Test Question 9 of 10 TGT Standard

What distinguishes Beejamrutha from Jeevamrutha in natural farming protocols?

Solution & Rationale: Beejamrutha is specifically formulated for coating seeds and tubers to protect against fungal infections prior to sowing, while Jeevamrutha is applied to soil via irrigation.

Test Question 10 of 10 PGT Standard

What is the primary objective of incorporating Achhadana (mulching) in natural farming systems?

Solution & Rationale: Achhadana creates a protective surface barrier that shades the soil, lowers evaporation losses, moderates temperature fluctuations, and inhibits weed seed germination.
