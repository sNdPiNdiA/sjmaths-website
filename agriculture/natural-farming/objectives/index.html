Objectives and Principles of Natural and Organic Farming Systems

Introduction and Core Objectives of Organic Farming

Organic farming is a holistic production management system that promotes and enhances agro-ecosystem health, including biodiversity, biological cycles, and soil biological activity. In the context of the Indian Council of Agricultural Research (ICAR) and State Agricultural Universities (SAUs), the primary objectives are designed to achieve ecological balance and long-term agricultural sustainability.

  • Soil Fertility Enhancement: To build up and maintain long-term soil fertility through the accumulation of organic matter and stimulation of soil microbial activity.
  • Chemical Independence: To eliminate the use of synthetic agrochemicals, including mineral nitrogen fertilizers, synthetic pesticides, and synthetic growth regulators.
  • Food Safety and Quality: To produce high-quality, residue-free food that satisfies nutritional requirements while protecting public health.
  • Closed Nutrient Loops: To recycle wastes of plant and animal origin back to the farm, thereby minimizing the loss of external inputs and energy.

Philosophical and Scientific Principles of Natural Farming

Natural farming, famously pioneered by Masanobu Fukuoka in Japan and adapted in India through systems like Zero Budget Natural Farming (ZBNF) or Subhash Palekar Natural Farming (SPNF), operates on the cardinal rule of working with nature rather than attempting to conquer it. The system rests upon four foundational pillars:

  • Jiwamrita / Amritpani: Application of fermented microbial cultures made from indigenous cow dung, cow urine, jaggery, pulse flour, and live soil to inoculate barren soils with billions of beneficial microorganisms.
  • Beejamrita: Seed treatment concoction utilizing cow dung, urine, lime, and forest soil to protect seedlings from seed-borne and soil-borne fungal pathogens such as Fusarium oxysporum and Rhizoctonia solani.
  • Acchadana (Mulching): Soil cover creation using crop residues, live cover crops, or fallen leaves to conserve soil moisture, suppress weed emergence through physical shading, and moderate soil temperatures.
  • Whapasa (Moisture-Air Balance): Maintaining the optimal balance between water molecules and air molecules within the root zone to promote deep root aeration and prevent anaerobic soil conditions.

Ecological and Environmental Targets

Natural and organic farming systems aim to reverse the ecological degradation caused by intensive chemical agriculture during and after the Green Revolution. Key environmental targets include:

  • Biodiversity Conservation: Restoring natural habitats for beneficial insects, pollinators (e.g., Apis cerana indica), earthworms (e.g., Eisenia fetida), and natural predators of agricultural pests.
  • Water Resource Protection: Preventing nitrate leaching into groundwater aquifers and eliminating surface runoff pollution caused by synthetic phosphorus and heavy pesticide residues.
  • Carbon Sequestration: Enhancing soil organic carbon (SOC) pools through cover cropping, zero tillage or minimum tillage, and continuous organic residue addition, thereby mitigating global climate change.
  • Energy Efficiency: Reducing the reliance on fossil-fuel-intensive synthetic fertilizers like urea and complex phosphatic fertilizers.

Agronomic Management and Crop Protection Protocols

Managing soil fertility and plant protection in organic and natural systems requires proactive, preventive, and systemic agronomic practices rather than curative chemical interventions.

  • Crop Rotation and Diversification: Employing multi-cropping, legume-cereal rotations (e.g., Cicer arietinum followed by Triticum aestivum), and green manuring (e.g., Sesbania aculeata) to fix atmospheric nitrogen and break disease cycles.
  • Biological Pest Control: Utilizing predators, parasitoids (e.g., Trichogramma chilonis), and entomopathogenic fungi (e.g., Beauveria bassiana or Metarhizium anisopliae) to regulate insect pest populations below Economic Threshold Levels (ETL).
  • Botanical Extracts: Application of fermented botanical preparations such as neem-based formulations (azadirachtin derived from Azadirachta indica), garlic-chilli extracts, and sour buttermilk sprays for pest and disease suppression.

Economic Viability and Socio-Economic Objectives

A critical objective of organic and natural farming systems, particularly for smallholder farmers in developing nations like India, is economic emancipation from high input costs.

  • Cost Reduction: Eliminating the purchase of expensive commercial seeds, synthetic fertilizers, and proprietary pesticides, thereby drastically lowering the operational cost of cultivation.
  • Risk Mitigation: Diversifying crop production to protect farmers against monoculture crop failures, erratic monsoons, and market price fluctuations.
  • Value Addition and Premium Pricing: Enabling farmers to access certified organic markets where agricultural produce commands a higher consumer price premium.
  • Rural Livelihood Generation: Revitalizing rural economies through localized input generation, participatory certification systems, and labor-intensive sustainable farm management.

⚡ High-Yield Exam Points (UP TGT / PGT Focus)
  • Masanobu Fukuoka is globally recognized as the pioneer of natural farming and the author of 'One-Straw Revolution'.
  • Subhash Palekar developed Subhash Palekar Natural Farming (SPNF), originally known as Zero Budget Natural Farming (ZBNF), focusing on eliminating credit-based farming.
  • Jiwamrita is a fermented liquid formulation containing cow dung, cow urine, jaggery, and pulse flour that supplies approximately 10 crore active microorganisms per gram of soil.
  • Beejamrita is specifically utilized for seed and seedling treatment to protect against soil-borne fungal pathogens.
  • Acchadana (mulching) suppresses weed emergence by cutting off sunlight and drastically reduces soil moisture evaporation.
  • Whapasa refers to the ideal physiological condition where both water molecules and air molecules are present in equal proportions around soil particles.
  • Green manuring crops like Sesbania aculeata (Daincha) are incorporated into the soil to add bulk organic matter and fix biological nitrogen.
  • Trichoderma viride and Pseudomonas fluorescens are standard biological control agents used in organic systems against soil-borne plant pathogens.
  • Azadirachtin, the principal active ingredient extracted from Azadirachta indica seeds, acts primarily as an antifeedant and growth disruptor in phytophagous insects.
  • Organic farming certification in India is governed nationally through the National Programme for Organic Production (NPOP) under the Ministry of Commerce and Industry.
⚠️ Common Mistakes & Exam Pitfalls
  • Confusing natural farming with organic farming: While both reject synthetic chemicals, natural farming completely prohibits the purchase of external organic inputs (like vermicompost or purchased FYM) and tillage, whereas organic farming permits external certified organic fertilizers and mechanical tillage.
  • Assuming natural farming yields drop permanently: Yields may experience a temporary transition dip for 2-3 seasons, but stabilize and often surpass conventional systems once soil biology is fully restored.
  • Treating Jiwamrita as a plant nutrient rather than a microbial inoculant: Jiwamrita primarily acts as a catalyst to multiply native soil microorganisms rather than supplying direct NPK in large bulk quantities.

⚡ Chapter Master Summary: Objectives and Principles of Natural and Organic Farming Systems

Systematic concept review designed for quick retention and last-minute pre-exam revision.

Concept 1

Agro-Ecological Foundation

Natural and organic farming systems prioritize the restoration of soil biotic communities and natural nutrient cycles over the application of synthetic chemical inputs.

  • Agro-Ecosystem Health: Maintaining biological equilibrium between flora, fauna, and soil microbes.
  • Closed Nutrient Loop: Internal recycling of organic wastes to achieve agricultural self-sufficiency.
Concept 2

Soil Microbial Activation

Microbial inoculants serve as the cornerstone of natural farming systems to re-energize dead or chemically fatigued agricultural soils.

  • Jiwamrita: Fermented concoction providing massive microbial loads to decompose soil organic matter.
  • Beejamrita: Protective coating applied to seeds to prevent early-stage damping-off and root rot diseases.
Concept 3

Moisture and Microclimate Regulation

Physical management of the soil surface ensures optimal root zone respiration and protection against extreme thermal fluctuations.

  • Acchadana (Mulch): Physical barrier preventing weed seed germination and conserving capillary soil moisture.
  • Whapasa: Optimal soil moisture-air ratio facilitating root hair respiration and nutrient uptake.
Concept 4

Economic and Social Objectives

Achieving fiscal independence from agrarian debt traps by removing high-cost commercial inputs from the farm budget.

  • Input Cost Reduction: Lowering cost of cultivation by utilizing on-farm resources and local desi seeds.
  • Risk Resilience: Enhancing climate and market shock absorption through farm diversification.

📚 Technical Terms Glossary

Natural Farming (Fukuoka / Palekar System)
A chemical-free agricultural system that operates without tilling the soil, weeding, or applying external fertilizers or pesticides.
Organic Farming (Certified Biological Agriculture)
A production system managed in accordance with prescribed standards that rely on crop rotation, green manures, compost, and biological pest control.
Jiwamrita (Microbial Fermented Solution)
A liquid formulation of cow dung, urine, jaggery, and flour used to multiply native soil microorganisms.
Beejamrita (Seed Treatment Concoction)
A protective coating of cow dung, urine, and lime applied to seeds to prevent seed-borne pathogens.
Acchadana (Soil Mulching)
Covering topsoil with crop residues or live vegetation to conserve moisture and suppress weeds.
Whapasa (Moisture-Air Equilibrium)
The soil state where water molecules and air molecules exist in equal proportions around soil particles.
NPOP (National Programme for Organic Production)
The statutory framework governing organic certification and export standards in India.
PGS (Participatory Guarantee System)
A peer-review certification process for organic producers operating locally and nationally.
Green Manuring (In-situ Biomass Incorporation)
Growing and incorporating succulent plant tissue (e.g., Sesbania aculeata) into soil to enhance organic matter and nitrogen.
Azadirachtin (Triterpenoid Botanical Compound)
Active insecticidal constituent extracted from Azadirachta indica seeds acting as an antifeedant.
Carbon Sequestration (Atmospheric Carbon Sinks)
The capture and long-term storage of atmospheric carbon dioxide in soil organic matter.

🎯 Must-Remember High-Frequency Facts

  • Jiwamrita supplies billions of microbes, not direct bulk mineral NPK fertilizer.
  • Zero tillage is a mandatory practice in natural farming to preserve soil strata and earthworm networks.
  • Azadirachtin is derived from Azadirachta indica (neem).
  • Green manuring with Sesbania aculeata adds both organic carbon and fixed nitrogen.
  • Third-party or PGS certification is legally required to market agricultural produce as 'Organic' in India.

📌 Quick Recap Bullet Points

  • Natural and organic farming systems aim to restore soil health, biodiversity, and ecological balance without synthetic chemicals.
  • Masanobu Fukuoka and Subhash Palekar are foundational figures in developing natural farming philosophies.
  • The four pillars of natural farming are Jiwamrita, Beejamrita, Acchadana, and Whapasa.
  • Organic farming requires adherence to certified standards (e.g., NPOP, PGS) and utilizes compost, crop rotation, and biopesticides.
  • Economic objectives center on eliminating input debt, lowering production costs, and producing chemical-free nutritious food.

⚖️ Concepts Often Confused in Examinations

Natural Farming VS Organic Farming

Natural farming prohibits all tillage and purchased external organic inputs; organic farming permits tillage, composting, and approved external organic inputs.

Jiwamrita VS Beejamrita

Jiwamrita is applied to the soil to stimulate microbial activity; Beejamrita is applied specifically to seeds before sowing to prevent fungal infections.

Question 1 of 18 Conceptual MCQ

Who is internationally recognized as the pioneer of natural farming and author of 'One-Straw Revolution'?

Explanation: Masanobu Fukuoka pioneered natural farming in Japan, emphasizing minimum human interference, no tillage, no chemical fertilizers, and no weeding.

Question 2 of 18 Conceptual MCQ

What is the primary function of Jiwamrita in natural farming systems?

Explanation: Jiwamrita is a fermented microbial culture containing cow dung and urine that multiplies native soil microorganisms, enhancing nutrient availability.

Question 3 of 18 Conceptual MCQ

In the context of natural farming, what does the term 'Whapasa' signify?

Explanation: Whapasa refers to the soil condition where both water molecules and air molecules are present in equal proportions around soil particles, facilitating aerobic root respiration.

Question 4 of 18 Conceptual MCQ

Which of the following is a mandatory practice in Subhash Palekar Natural Farming (SPNF)?

Explanation: SPNF strictly prohibits deep tillage and requires zero tillage along with organic mulching (Acchadana) to protect soil biology and conserve moisture.

Question 5 of 18 Conceptual MCQ

What is the specific purpose of Beejamrita in crop production?

Explanation: Beejamrita is a seed treatment concoction made of cow dung, urine, and lime used to coat seeds, protecting them from fungal and bacterial pathogens.

Question 6 of 18 Conceptual MCQ

Which organization regulates the standards for National Programme for Organic Production (NPOP) in India?

Explanation: The National Programme for Organic Production (NPOP) is administered by the Ministry of Commerce and Industry, Government of India.

Question 7 of 18 Conceptual MCQ

What is the primary active insecticidal compound found in neem (Azadirachta indica) seeds?

Explanation: Azadirachtin is the primary active triterpenoid found in neem seeds, acting as an antifeedant, repellent, and insect growth regulator.

Question 8 of 18 Conceptual MCQ

Which green manuring crop is most commonly incorporated into Indian soils to fix nitrogen and add organic matter?

Explanation: Sesbania aculeata (Daincha) is a prominent green manure crop widely used in Indian agriculture due to its rapid biomass accumulation and nitrogen-fixing capability.

Question 9 of 18 Conceptual MCQ

What distinguishes natural farming from certified organic farming?

Explanation: Natural farming completely forbids external inputs (even purchased organic manures) and tillage, whereas organic farming allows certified organic manures and mechanical tillage.

Question 10 of 18 Conceptual MCQ

What does PGS stand for in the context of Indian organic agriculture certification?

Explanation: PGS (Participatory Guarantee System) is a peer-review quality assurance system that certifies organic producers locally based on mutual trust and participation.

Question 11 of 18 Conceptual MCQ

Which of the following biological agents is widely utilized in organic farming for controlling soil-borne fungal pathogens?

Explanation: Trichoderma viride is a well-known antagonistic fungus used extensively as a biocontrol agent against soil-borne phytopathogens.

Question 12 of 18 Conceptual MCQ

What is the primary environmental objective of carbon sequestration in organic farming?

Explanation: Carbon sequestration increases soil organic carbon (SOC), improving soil structure while actively removing carbon dioxide from the atmosphere to mitigate climate change.

Question 13 of 18 Conceptual MCQ

In natural farming, what is the role of Acchadana (Mulching)?

Explanation: Acchadana (mulching) creates a physical protective barrier that prevents weed emergence, reduces evaporation losses, and regulates soil temperatures.

Question 14 of 18 Conceptual MCQ

Which crop rotation sequence best exemplifies biological nitrogen fixation and disease break in organic systems?

Explanation: Rotating a legume crop like chickpea with a cereal like wheat fixes atmospheric nitrogen in root nodules and disrupts cereal-specific pathogen cycles.

Question 15 of 18 Conceptual MCQ

What is the principal socio-economic objective of natural and organic farming for smallholders in India?

Explanation: A major socio-economic goal is to liberate smallholder farmers from high-cost input debts, thereby improving net farm profitability and livelihood security.

Question 16 of 18 Conceptual MCQ

Which earthworm species is predominantly vermicomposted for organic nutrient recycling in Indian agriculture?

Explanation: Eisenia fetida (red earthworm) is highly efficient at converting organic wastes into nutrient-rich vermicompost for organic farming.

Question 17 of 18 Conceptual MCQ

What is the function of entomopathogenic fungi such as Beauveria bassiana in organic crop protection?

Explanation: Beauveria bassiana is an entomopathogenic fungus that infects soft-bodied insect pests, germinating on their cuticle and causing mortality.

Question 18 of 18 Conceptual MCQ

Why are synthetic mineral nitrogen fertilizers prohibited in organic farming systems?

Explanation: Synthetic N fertilizers (like urea) disrupt soil microbial balance, leach into groundwater, and require high fossil fuel energy to manufacture, violating organic principles.

Test Question 1 of 10 PGT Standard

Analyze the core tenets of Subhash Palekar Natural Farming (SPNF). Which of the following combinations represents its foundational pillars?

Solution & Rationale: SPNF relies strictly on Jiwamrita (microbes), Beejamrita (seed treatment), Acchadana (mulching), and Whapasa (moisture-air balance).

Test Question 2 of 10 PGT Standard

What is the primary scientific reason for maintaining zero tillage in natural farming systems?

Solution & Rationale: Zero tillage preserves soil macro-pore networks, mycorrhizal fungi networks, and earthworm channels, which are disrupted by mechanical ploughing.

Test Question 3 of 10 TGT Standard

How does Beejamrita protect germinating seedlings from soil-borne pathogens?

Solution & Rationale: Beejamrita provides a protective biofilm of antagonistic bacteria and lime that inhibits soil-borne fungi like Fusarium from attacking young radicles.

Test Question 4 of 10 TGT Standard

In the context of organic nutrient management, what is the significance of closed nutrient loops?

Solution & Rationale: Closed nutrient loops ensure that nutrients extracted by crops are returned via compost, manure, and residue recycling, achieving sustainable farm self-sufficiency.

Test Question 5 of 10 PGT Standard

What is the distinction between third-party organic certification and Participatory Guarantee Systems (PGS) in India?

Solution & Rationale: PGS operates through local farmer groups verifying each other, whereas third-party certification requires rigorous independent audits by accredited agencies.

Test Question 6 of 10 TGT Standard

Which botanical extract acts primarily as an antifeedant and disrupts the endocrine moulting cycle of phytophagous insects?

Solution & Rationale: Azadirachtin interferes with insect hormone systems, preventing feeding and disrupting metamorphosis in chewing and sucking insect pests.

Test Question 7 of 10 PGT Standard

Why is deep soil inversion tillage discouraged in organic and natural farming?

Solution & Rationale: Deep tillage breaks down soil aggregates, oxidizes valuable organic carbon into carbon dioxide, and brings dormant weed seeds to the surface.

Test Question 8 of 10 TGT Standard

What role do beneficial insects like Trichogramma chilonis play in organic pest management?

Solution & Rationale: Trichogramma chilonis is an egg parasitoid used extensively in biological control to parasitize and destroy pest eggs before larvae hatch.

Test Question 9 of 10 TGT Standard

What is the primary objective of incorporating green manure crops like Sesbania aculeata prior to sowing main crops?

Solution & Rationale: Green manuring adds labile organic carbon and symbiotic nitrogen fixed by root nodules, improving soil fertility and structure.

Test Question 10 of 10 PGT Standard

How do natural and organic farming systems enhance resilience against climate change impacts?

Solution & Rationale: High soil organic carbon and crop diversity buffer farms against droughts, floods, and temperature extremes, enhancing overall agro-ecosystem resilience.
