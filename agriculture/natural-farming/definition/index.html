Natural and Organic Farming Systems: Definition, Principles, and Scope

Explore the foundational definitions, core principles, and ecological parameters of natural and organic farming systems essential for the UP TGT and PGT Agriculture competitive examinations. Master the philosophical, biological, and practical dimensions of chemical-free crop production aligned with ICAR and SAU guidelines.

1. Definition and Philosophical Framework of Natural Farming

Natural farming is an ecologically sound agricultural production system that relies entirely on local natural biodiversity, internal farm inputs, and minimal human intervention, often conceptualized by pioneers like Masanobu Fukuoka (Do-Nothing Farming). It completely rejects synthetic chemical fertilizers, chemical pesticides, genetically modified organisms, and intensive tillage operations.

  • Core Tenet: Imitating natural forest ecosystems where nutrient cycling occurs organically through leaf litter, root exudates, and microbial interactions without external chemical augmentation.
  • Soil Interaction: Emphasizes living soil (rhizosphere vitality) containing diverse earthworm populations and beneficial soil microorganisms (Azotobacter, Azospirillum, phosphate-solubilizing bacteria) rather than feeding the plant directly with soluble chemical salts.
  • Tillage Principle: Zero or minimum tillage to preserve soil aggregate stability, prevent moisture evaporation, and protect the natural habitats of beneficial soil fauna.

2. Definition and Regulatory Standards of Organic Farming

Organic farming is a holistic agricultural management system that promotes and enhances agro-ecosystem health, including biodiversity, biological cycles, and soil biological activity. Unlike natural farming, organic farming operates under strict statutory certification standards such as the National Programme for Organic Production (NPOP) in India and international benchmarks set by IFOAM.

  • Definition by FAO/WHO Codex Alimentarius: A holistic food production management system which promotes and enhances agro-ecosystem health, utilizing management practices rather than synthetic off-farm inputs.
  • Prohibited Substances: Complete ban on synthetic nitrogenous fertilizers, synthetic herbicides, fungicides, insecticides, growth regulators, and synthetic livestock feed additives.
  • Conversion Period: The transition phase from conventional chemical farming to certified organic farming, typically requiring a minimum of 2 to 3 years of documented compliance with organic protocols.

3. Comparative Agronomic Principles: Natural vs. Organic Farming

While both farming systems share a common goal of environmental sustainability and chemical-free produce, their operational strategies and regulatory frameworks differ significantly.

  • Input Sourcing: Natural farming strictly utilizes on-farm or locally gathered bio-inputs such as Jeevamrita, Beejamrita, and Mulching (Acchhadana). Organic farming allows off-farm certified organic inputs like commercial vermicompost, rock phosphate, and botanical extracts (e.g., Azadirachtin).
  • Certification & Compliance: Natural farming (specifically propagated in India as Bharatiya Prakritik Krishi Paddhati or National Mission on Natural Farming) relies heavily on peer-review mechanisms (PGS - Participatory Guarantee Systems) or trust-based local markets, whereas organic farming demands rigorous third-party auditing and certification.
  • Economic Viability: Natural farming minimizes input costs to virtually zero by eliminating purchased organic inputs, whereas organic farming can involve high operational costs related to certified input procurement and documentation.

4. Soil Health, Microbiology, and Nutrient Cycling in Natural and Organic Systems

The cornerstone of both natural and organic systems is the enhancement of soil organic matter (SOM) and biological activity. Chemical fertilizers deplete soil organic carbon over time, whereas biological farming builds soil structure.

  • Carbon Sequestration: Continuous incorporation of farmyard manure (FYM), vermicompost, green manuring (e.g., Crotalaria juncea), and crop residues elevates Soil Organic Carbon (SOC) levels.
  • Microbial Bio-inoculants: Utilization of nitrogen-fixing bacteria (Rhizobium, Azotobacter chroococcum), phosphorus solubilizers (Bacillus megaterium), and potassium mobilizers (Frateuria aurantia) to unlock native soil minerals.
  • Mycorrhizal Associations: Enhancement of Arbuscular Mycorrhizal Fungi (AMF) symbiosis, which expands root absorption surface areas for immobile nutrients like phosphorus and zinc.

5. Pest, Disease, and Weed Management Strategies

Pest management in natural and organic systems shifts from eradicating pests to managing agro-ecosystem balance through ecological engineering and preventive cultural practices.

  • Cultural and Mechanical Control: Deep summer ploughing to expose diapausing pupae, crop rotation with leguminous crops, trap cropping (e.g., marigold for Helicoverpa armigera), and hand-weeding.
  • Biological Control: Conservation and augmentation of natural predators and parasitoids such as Chrysoperla carnea, Trichogramma chilonis, and entomopathogenic nematodes.
  • Botanical Concoctions: Application of Neem oil formulations, Garlic-Chili extract, and fermented cow urine-based herbal concoctions (Agniastra, Brahmastra) for repelling sucking and chewing insect pests.

⚡ High-Yield Exam Points (UP TGT / PGT Focus)
  • Masanobu Fukuoka is globally recognized as the pioneer of Natural Farming and author of 'The One-Straw Revolution'.
  • Subhash Palekar developed Zero Budget Natural Farming (ZBNF), now officially promoted in India as Bharatiya Prakritik Krishi Paddhati (BPKP) and National Mission on Natural Farming (NMNF).
  • Jeevamrita is prepared using 200 liters of water, 10 kg desi cow dung, 10 liters desi cow urine, 2 kg jaggery, 2 kg pulse flour, and a handful of live farm soil.
  • Beejamrita is specifically used for seed, tuber, and seedling treatment before sowing to protect against soil-borne and seed-borne fungal pathogens using cow dung, urine, lime, and local soil.
  • Whapasa refers to the condition where soil contains both moisture molecules and air molecules simultaneously, which is critical for root respiration in natural farming.
  • National Programme for Organic Production (NPOP) in India is legally enacted under the Foreign Trade (Development and Regulation) Act by the Ministry of Commerce and Industry.
  • Participatory Guarantee Systems (PGS) India is a peer-review-based organic certification system managed by the Ministry of Agriculture and Farmers Welfare.
  • Acchhadana (Mulching) suppresses weed growth, moderates soil temperature, and enhances soil moisture retention by covering soil with organic crop residues.
  • Vermicomposting relies primarily on epigeic earthworm species such as Eisenia fetida and Eudrilus eugeniae.
  • Azadirachtin, extracted from Azadirachta indica, acts as an effective antifeedant, repellent, and growth disruptor against phytophagous insect pests.
⚠️ Common Mistakes & Exam Pitfalls
  • Confusing Natural Farming with Organic Farming: Natural farming prohibits all external inputs including purchased organic manures, whereas organic farming permits certified organic off-farm inputs.
  • Assuming Zero Tillage means zero crop yield: Zero tillage in natural farming is practiced alongside heavy residue mulching, which over time improves soil structure and crop yields without mechanical inversion.
  • Believing NPOP certification is managed by ICAR: NPOP is governed by the Ministry of Commerce and Industry, Government of India, though ICAR provides technical backstopping.

⚡ Chapter Master Summary: Natural and Organic Farming Systems: Definition, Principles, and Scope

Systematic concept review designed for quick retention and last-minute pre-exam revision.

Concept 1

Philosophical Foundation and Definitions

Natural and organic farming represent two distinct yet complementary paradigms moving away from chemical-intensive agriculture.

  • Natural Farming: A chemical-free farming system based on agro-ecology, utilizing local biomass and eliminating external inputs.
  • Organic Farming: A production system that sustains the health of soils, ecosystems, and people, relying on ecological processes and certified organic inputs.
Concept 2

The Four Pillars of Natural Farming (SPNF)

The operational framework of natural farming rests on four core pillars designed to revive soil biology.

  • Beejamrita: Protective coating for seeds using cow dung, urine, and limestone.
  • Jeevamrita: Liquid microbial culture applied to soil to multiply native soil microbes.
  • Acchhadana: Soil mulching using crop residues to prevent moisture loss and weed germination.
  • Whapasa: Maintaining ideal soil moisture and aeration balance for root respiration.
Concept 3

Regulatory and Certification Standards

Standardization and legal verification are critical for domestic and international trade of organic produce.

  • NPOP: National Programme for Organic Production providing the 'India Organic' logo.
  • PGS-India: Decentralized organic certification system functioning on peer review and local trust for domestic markets.
Concept 4

Soil Microbiology and Nutrient Management

Biological farming substitutes synthetic chemical fertilizers with organic amendments and microbial inoculants.

  • Bio-fertilizers: Living formulations of microbes like Rhizobium and Azotobacter that fix atmospheric nitrogen.
  • Green Manuring: Incorporation of succulent green plant tissues (e.g., Sesbania rostrata) into the soil to add organic matter and nitrogen.

📚 Technical Terms Glossary

Natural Farming (Prakritik Krishi)
An agricultural system that mimics natural ecosystems without synthetic inputs or intensive tillage.
Organic Farming (Jaivik Kheti)
A holistic production management system promoting agro-ecosystem health and relying on certified organic inputs.
Jeevamrita (Microbial Inoculant Slurry)
A fermented liquid mixture of cow dung, urine, jaggery, flour, and soil used to activate soil microbes.
Beejamrita (Seed Treatment Solution)
A protective slurry applied to seeds and planting materials to prevent seed-borne and soil-borne diseases.
Acchhadana (Mulching)
Covering the soil surface with crop residues or green leaves to conserve moisture and suppress weeds.
Whapasa (Soil Moisture-Air Equilibrium)
The optimal physical condition of soil where water vapor and air co-exist within soil pore spaces.
NPOP (National Programme for Organic Production)
India's apex statutory framework governing organic production standards and export certification.
PGS (Participatory Guarantee Systems)
A peer-review certification process where local farmers verify organic integrity.
Vermicomposting (Epigeic Earthworm Composting)
The process of converting organic waste into nutrient-rich compost using epigeic earthworm species.
Biopesticide (Biological Pest Control Agent)
Pest management agents derived from natural materials such as animals, plants, bacteria, and minerals.
Green Manuring (In-situ Plant Biomass Incorporation)
Ploughing un-decomposed green plant tissue into the soil to improve fertility and organic matter.

📌 Quick Recap Bullet Points

  • Natural farming relies exclusively on internal farm inputs and mimics natural forest nutrient cycling.
  • Organic farming permits certified off-farm organic inputs and operates under strict statutory standards like NPOP.
  • The four pillars of Natural Farming are Beejamrita, Jeevamrita, Acchhadana, and Whapasa.
  • Soil organic carbon (SOC) enhancement is the primary biological objective of organic and natural farming systems.

⚖️ Concepts Often Confused in Examinations

Natural Farming VS Organic Farming

Natural farming strictly prohibits purchased off-farm organic inputs and certification, whereas organic farming allows certified organic inputs and requires formal third-party or PGS certification.

Question 1 of 19 Conceptual MCQ

Who is globally recognized as the pioneer of Natural Farming and author of 'The One-Straw Revolution'?

Explanation: Masanobu Fukuoka developed natural farming in Japan, emphasizing non-cultivation, no chemical fertilizers, no weeding, and no pruning.

Question 2 of 19 Conceptual MCQ

Which of the following is NOT one of the four pillars of Subhash Palekar Natural Farming (SPNF)?

Explanation: The four pillars of SPNF are Beejamrita, Jeevamrita, Acchhadana (Mulching), and Whapasa. Synthetic urea is a chemical fertilizer forbidden in natural farming.

Question 3 of 19 Conceptual MCQ

What is the primary purpose of 'Beejamrita' in natural farming systems?

Explanation: Beejamrita is a protective seed treatment solution formulated using cow dung, cow urine, lime, and soil to protect seeds from fungal and bacterial infections.

Question 4 of 19 Conceptual MCQ

In the context of natural farming, what does the term 'Whapasa' signify?

Explanation: Whapasa refers to the condition where soil contains both water molecules and air molecules simultaneously, which is essential for aerobic root respiration.

Question 5 of 19 Conceptual MCQ

Which apex body administers the National Programme for Organic Production (NPOP) in India?

Explanation: NPOP is legally implemented under the Foreign Trade (Development and Regulation) Act by the Ministry of Commerce and Industry, Government of India.

Question 6 of 19 Conceptual MCQ

What is the certified peer-review organic certification system operating for domestic markets in India?

Explanation: PGS-India is a peer-review-based organic certification system managed by the Ministry of Agriculture and Farmers Welfare for domestic markets.

Question 7 of 19 Conceptual MCQ

Which earthworm species is most commonly used in commercial vermicomposting due to its epigeic feeding habit?

Explanation: Eisenia fetida is an epigeic earthworm that lives in upper organic layers and rapidly converts organic waste into vermicompost.

Question 8 of 19 Conceptual MCQ

What is the primary ecological objective of 'Acchhadana' in natural farming?

Explanation: Acchhadana (mulching) involves covering soil with crop residues to moderate soil temperature, conserve moisture, and prevent weed seed germination.

Question 9 of 19 Conceptual MCQ

Which botanical extract is widely used in organic pest management as an effective antifeedant and insect repellent?

Explanation: Azadirachtin is the primary active limonoid compound in neem seed kernel extract (NSKE) utilized in organic farming for pest deterrence.

Question 10 of 19 Conceptual MCQ

What is the typical minimum conversion period required to certify a conventional farm as organic under NPOP standards?

Explanation: The conversion period under NPOP standards is typically 2 to 3 years of documented compliance with organic management practices before crops can be labeled certified organic.

Question 11 of 19 Conceptual MCQ

Which microorganism is classically used as a bio-fertilizer for symbiotic nitrogen fixation in leguminous crops?

Explanation: Rhizobium forms a symbiotic relationship with legume roots, fixing atmospheric nitrogen directly into plant-available forms.

Question 12 of 19 Conceptual MCQ

Which of the following definitions best describes 'Organic Farming' according to FAO/WHO Codex Alimentarius?

Explanation: Codex Alimentarius defines organic farming as a holistic management system that promotes agro-ecosystem health and utilizes management practices over synthetic inputs.

Question 13 of 19 Conceptual MCQ

What is the primary function of 'Jeevamrita' when applied to agricultural soil?

Explanation: Jeevamrita acts as a catalytic agent that introduces and multiplies native soil microbes, enhancing organic matter decomposition and nutrient availability.

Question 14 of 19 Conceptual MCQ

Which tillage practice is heavily emphasized in Natural Farming systems?

Explanation: Natural farming advocates zero or minimum tillage to protect soil aggregate stability, soil organic matter, and the natural habitat of soil micro-fauna.

Question 15 of 19 Conceptual MCQ

Which organization sets international guidelines and principles for organic agriculture globally?

Explanation: IFOAM - Organics International defines the global principles of organic agriculture (Health, Ecology, Fairness, Care).

Question 16 of 19 Conceptual MCQ

What is the main advantage of incorporating green manure crops like Crotalaria juncea (Sunnhemp) in organic rotations?

Explanation: Sunnhemp adds substantial biomass and fixes atmospheric nitrogen when incorporated into the soil prior to flowering.

Question 17 of 19 Conceptual MCQ

Which chemical-based weed management practice is strictly prohibited in certified organic farming?

Explanation: Synthetic herbicides like glyphosate or atrazine are strictly prohibited in organic farming, relying instead on cultural, mechanical, and biological weed control.

Question 18 of 19 Conceptual MCQ

What is the key difference between Natural Farming and Organic Farming regarding off-farm inputs?

Explanation: Organic farming permits purchased certified organic off-farm manures and bio-pesticides, while natural farming emphasizes absolute reliance on internal farm biodiversity and natural cycles.

Question 19 of 19 Conceptual MCQ

Which bio-fertilizer is classified as a phosphorus-solubilizing microorganism (PSM)?

Explanation: Bacillus megaterium is a prominent phosphate-solubilizing bacterium that secretes organic acids to unlock bound soil phosphorus for plant uptake.
