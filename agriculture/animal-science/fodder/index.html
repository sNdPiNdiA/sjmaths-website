Taxonomy, Classification, and Botanical Characteristics of Forage Crops

Forage crops form the backbone of dairy and livestock production systems in India, supplying bulk nutrients, crude fiber, and digestible dry matter. They are broadly classified into cereal forages, legume forages, and grasses.

  • Cereal Forages: Includes Maize (Zea mays), Sorghum (Sorghum bicolor), Pearl Millet (Pennisetum glaucum), and Oat (Avena sativa). They are rich in carbohydrates and total digestible nutrients (TDN).
  • Legume Forages: Includes Berseem (Trifolium alexandrinum), Lucerne or Alfalfa (Medicago sativa), Cowpea (Vigna unguiculata), and Cluster Bean (Cyamopsis tetragonoloba). Legumes are characterized by root nodules containing Rhizobium bacteria capable of symbiotic nitrogen fixation, providing high crude protein and calcium content.
  • Perennial Grasses: Includes Napier-Bajra Hybrid (Pennisetum purpureum × Pennisetum glaucum), Guinea Grass (Panicum maximum), and Para Grass (Brachiaria mutica) which provide year-round green fodder.

Agronomic Practices: Seed Rate, Sowing Geometry, and Field Preparation

Successful establishment of forage crops requires meticulous seedbed preparation, optimal seed rates, and proper sowing dates to maximize green fodder yield (GFY) and dry matter yield (DMY).

  • Berseem (Trifolium alexandrinum): Requires a seed rate of 25-30 kg/ha. Sowing time is mid-October to early November. Seeds are often inoculated with Rhizobium trifolii. Spacing is broadcasting or line sowing at 25 cm.
  • Sorghum (Sorghum bicolor) / Chari: Seed rate is 40-45 kg/ha for broadcast and 30-35 kg/ha for line sowing. Sowing is undertaken during the kharif season (February-March for multicut, June-July for single cut). Spacing is 30 cm between rows.
  • Maize (Zea mays): Seed rate is 40-50 kg/ha with a spacing of 30 cm × 10 cm. Sowing is done across kharif, rabi, and zaid seasons under irrigated conditions.
  • Oat (Avena sativa): Premier rabi forage crop requiring 80-100 kg/ha seed rate. Sowing is optimal from mid-October to mid-November. Spacing is 20-25 cm row-to-row.

Nutrient Management and Fertilizer Recommendations

Forage crops are exhaustive feeders, particularly nitrogen-loving grasses, which demand balanced fertilization to maintain high crude protein percentages and biomass accumulation.

  • Nitrogen Management: Non-legume forages (Sorghum, Maize, Oats, Napier grass) respond exceptionally well to nitrogen. For instance, Maize requires 80-100 kg N/ha, applied in splits (half basal, half top-dressed after the first irrigation or cut). Napier-Bajra hybrid requires up to 150-200 kg N/ha applied in split doses after each cutting.
  • Phosphorus and Potassium: Leguminous forages (Berseem, Lucerne) require low nitrogen (20 kg N/ha as starter dose) but high phosphorus (60-80 kg P2O5/ha) to stimulate root proliferation and nodulation. Potash application (40 kg K2O/ha) is recommended based on soil test values.
  • Urea Calculation Example: To apply 80 kg N/ha using urea (containing 46% N), the quantity of urea required is calculated as: (80 / 46) \times 100 = 173.9 kg urea per hectare.

Irrigation Scheduling and Weed Management in Forages

Water management directly influences the succulence, palatability, and regeneration capacity of multicut forage crops.

  • Irrigation Intervals: Berseem requires 5-6 irrigations during winter and 2-3 additional light irrigations in spring/summer after each cutting. Summer fodder crops like Maize and Sorghum require irrigation every 7-10 days depending on pan evaporation and soil moisture depletion.
  • Critical Stages: Crown Root Initiation (CRI) and tillering in cereals, and branching to flowering in leguminous forages are sensitive to water stress.
  • Weed Control: Fast-growing forages smother weeds naturally, but early-stage weed competition can severely reduce establishment. Intercultural operations (hoeing) 3-4 weeks after sowing in line-sown crops is effective. Herbicides like Pendimethalin at 1.0 kg a.i./ha as pre-emergence can be used in specific fodder legumes.

Pest, Disease Management, and Crop Protection

Protecting forage stands from biotic stresses ensures high-quality toxin-free feed for livestock.

  • Insect Pests: Shoot fly (Atherigona soccata) is a major pest in Sorghum and Maize. Stem borer (Chilo partellus) damages vegetative whorls. Controlled using seed treatment with Carbofuran 3G or spraying Malathion 50 EC at ETL.
  • Diseases: Powdery mildew (Erysiphe polygoni) in Berseem, and Anthracnose or Leaf blight (Colletotrichum graminicola) in Sorghum. Use resistant varieties and seed treatment with Carbendazim (2 g/kg seed).
  • Anti-Quality Factors: Hydrocyanic acid (HCN) or prussic acid poisoning occurs in sorghum when grazed or harvested at pre-flowering stages under drought stress. Nitrate toxicity can occur in heavily nitrogen-fertilized cereal forages.

Harvesting Indices, Post-Harvest Conservation, and Silage/Hay Making

Proper stage of harvesting guarantees an optimal balance between total green biomass yield and nutritional quality (digestibility and crude protein).

  • Harvesting Stage: Sorghum is harvested at 50% flowering. Maize is harvested at the milk-to-dough stage for silage making. Berseem is harvested at the pre-flowering stage (50-60 cm height) to allow quick regeneration for subsequent cuts (yielding 4-6 cuts).
  • Silage Making: Conservation of green fodder under anaerobic fermentation. Ideal moisture is 65-70%. Crops rich in soluble carbohydrates (Maize, Sorghum, Oats) are best suited for silage.
  • Hay Making: Dehydration of green forage to moisture content below 15-20%. Legume forages like Lucerne and Cowpea are commonly made into hay.

⚡ High-Yield Exam Points (UP TGT / PGT Focus)
  • Berseem is known as the 'King of Rabi Forages' due to its high protein content and multiple cuts.
  • Lucerne (Medicago sativa) is known as the 'Queen of Forages' and persists as a perennial legume.
  • Napier-Bajra hybrid exhibits vigorous growth and is propagated through rooted slips or stem cuttings rather than true seeds.
  • Hydrocyanic acid (HCN) concentration above 200 mg/kg of dry matter in Sorghum is lethal to ruminant livestock.
  • Tetraploid berseem varieties require Egyptian clover (diploid) mixture for proper seed setting (polymix).
  • Silage pH drops below 4.2 during successful anaerobic lactic acid fermentation.
  • Oat variety 'Kent' is highly popular across Uttar Pradesh for multicut rabi fodder production.
⚠️ Common Mistakes & Exam Pitfalls
  • Confusing the botanical name of Berseem (Trifolium alexandrinum) with Lucerne (Medicago sativa).
  • Believing legumes require heavy nitrogen fertilizer; legumes require only a starter dose (20 kg N/ha) and depend on Rhizobium fixation.
  • Harvesting Sorghum at the seedling stage for green feeding without realizing the extreme danger of HCN (prussic acid) toxicity.

⚡ Chapter Master Summary: Comprehensive Agronomy and Production Technology of Forder Crops

Systematic concept review designed for quick retention and last-minute pre-exam revision.

Concept 1

Classification and Importance of Forage Crops

Forage crops are cultivated specifically for livestock feed as green fodder, silage, or hay.

  • Classification: Grouped into cereals, legumes, and grasses based on botanical family and nutritional profile.
  • Nutritional Value: Legumes supply high protein and calcium, while cereals supply energy-rich carbohydrates.
Concept 2

Agronomy of Berseem and Lucerne

Rabi forage legumes provide succulent green fodder during winter when natural grasslands dry up.

  • Berseem Seed Rate: 25 to 30 kg/ha with seed inoculation.
  • Cuts: Berseem yields 4 to 6 successive cuttings from December to May.
Concept 3

Anti-Nutritional Factors in Forages

Presence of secondary metabolites or toxic compounds limits direct feeding of certain forages.

  • HCN Poisoning: Found in young Sorghum plants; inhibited by waiting until 50% flowering.
  • Nitrate Toxicity: Caused by excessive soil nitrogen accumulation in cereal fodders during drought.
Concept 4

Fodder Preservation: Silage and Hay

Conservation techniques ensure uninterrupted fodder supply during lean summer and winter scarcity periods.

  • Silage: Ensilage of green maize or sorghum in airtight silos under anaerobic conditions.
  • Hay: Sun-drying legume forages to moisture levels below 20%.

📚 Technical Terms Glossary

Berseem (Trifolium alexandrinum)
Premier annual leguminous rabi forage crop known as the king of fodders.
Lucerne (Medicago sativa)
Perennial deep-rooted leguminous forage crop referred to as the queen of forages.
Silage (Ensilage)
Succulent green fodder preserved under anaerobic fermentation in a silo.
Hay (Dehydrated Forage)
Forage conserved by drying to a moisture content below 15-20%.
HCN (Hydrocyanic Acid / Prussic Acid)
Toxic cyanogenic glycoside (dhurrin) present in young sorghum plants.
Napier Grass (Pennisetum purpureum)
Tall perennial bunch grass used in interspecific hybridisation with bajra.
Multicut Fodder (Regenerative Forage)
Fodder crops capable of producing multiple harvests from a single sowing.
TDN (Total Digestible Nutrients)
Index of the energy value of animal feed.
Dry Matter Yield (DMY)
Weight of forage crop after complete removal of moisture.
Rhizobium (Rhizobium trifolii / meliloti)
Symbiotic nitrogen-fixing bacteria used for legume seed inoculation.

  • Sorghum dhurrin hydrolysis releases toxic Hydrocyanic acid.
  • Optimum moisture for silage making is 65-70%.
  • Oat variety Kent is standard for UP conditions.

📌 Quick Recap Bullet Points

  • Berseem and Lucerne are principal rabi legume forages providing high protein.
  • Sorghum and Maize dominate kharif cereal forages.
  • HCN poisoning in sorghum can be prevented by delaying harvest until flowering.
  • Silage fermentation requires anaerobic conditions and soluble carbohydrates.

⚖️ Concepts Often Confused in Examinations

Silage VS Hay

Silage is fermented wet fodder stored anaerobically (65-70% moisture), while hay is sun-dried aerobic fodder (<20% moisture).

Test Question 2 of 10 PGT Standard

Assertion (A): Berseem seed is often mixed with diploid Egyptian clover when sowing tetraploid varieties. Reason (R): Tetraploid berseem exhibits poor seed setting unless pollinated or mixed with diploid lines.

Solution & Rationale: Tetraploid berseem strains show low fertility and poor seed setting, necessitating a polymix or admixture with diploid seed (Egyptian clover) for proper fertilization and seed production.

Test Question 3 of 10 TGT Standard

Which of the following perennial grasses is best suited for waterlogged and marshy lowland soils?

Solution & Rationale: Para Grass (Brachiaria mutica) thrives exceptionally well in waterlogged, flooded, and marshy soils where other grasses rot.

Test Question 4 of 10 TGT Standard

What is the critical moisture content threshold below which hay must be dried for safe storage without mold growth?

Solution & Rationale: Hay must be dried down to 15-20% moisture content to inhibit microbial activity, fungal growth, and spontaneous combustion.

Test Question 5 of 10 PGT Standard

Which chemical compound is responsible for prussic acid (HCN) poisoning in Sorghum?

Solution & Rationale: Dhurrin is a cyanogenic glycoside present in Sorghum tissues that breaks down into toxic hydrocyanic acid upon tissue damage or stress.

Test Question 6 of 10 PGT Standard

Match List-I (Forage Crop) with List-II (Family): (a) Berseem -> 1. Poaceae (b) Sorghum -> 2. Fabaceae (c) Lucerne -> 3. Poaceae (d) Maize -> 4. Fabaceae Correct Code:

Solution & Rationale: Berseem and Lucerne belong to Fabaceae; Sorghum and Maize belong to Poaceae.

Test Question 7 of 10 TGT Standard

What is the standard seed rate of Lucerne (Medicago sativa)?

Solution & Rationale: Lucerne is sown using 20-25 kg of viable, inoculated seed per hectare.

Test Question 8 of 10 PGT Standard

Why is molasses frequently added during silage preparation from leguminous crops?

Solution & Rationale: Legumes are low in soluble carbohydrates required by lactic acid bacteria; adding molasses supplies fermentable sugars for successful silage preservation.

Test Question 9 of 10 TGT Standard

Which of the following is an example of an annual cereal kharif forage crop?

Solution & Rationale: Sorghum (Sorghum bicolor) is a major annual cereal forage grown during the kharif season.

Test Question 10 of 10 TGT Standard

What is the primary objective of taking multiple cuts in multicut forage crops like Berseem and Napier hybrid?

Solution & Rationale: Multicut management ensures a continuous supply of tender, highly digestible, protein-rich green fodder over an extended harvest window.
