Principles of Animal Health and Disease Classification

Animal health is defined as a state of complete physical, mental, and social well-being in farm animals, not merely the absence of disease. Diseases in livestock are broadly categorized based on their etiology, transmission dynamics, and clinical manifestations into infectious and non-infectious disorders.

  • Infectious Diseases: Caused by living pathogenic agents such as bacteria, viruses, fungi, and protozoa. Examples include Foot and Mouth Disease (FMD), Bacillus anthracis (Anthrax), and Brucella abortus (Brucellosis). These diseases are generally contagious and can spread horizontally through direct contact or vectors.
  • Non-Infectious (Metabolic/Deficiency) Diseases: Caused by nutritional deficiencies, metabolic imbalances, genetic abnormalities, or toxicities. Examples include milk fever (hypocalcemia), ketosis, and bloat (tympanites). These are non-contagious and managed through nutritional adjustments.
  • Zoonotic Diseases: Pathogens transmissible between animals and humans, posing serious public health concerns in rural farming communities. Key examples include Mycobacterium bovis (Tuberculosis), rabies virus, and Brucella species.

Major Bacterial, Viral, and Protozoan Diseases in Livestock

Understanding the specific causal organisms, vectors, clinical symptoms, and control measures of major livestock diseases is vital for high-scoring performance in competitive agricultural examinations.

  • Foot and Mouth Disease (FMD): A highly contagious acute viral disease caused by an Aphthovirus affecting cloven-hoofed animals. Symptoms include high fever, excessive salivation, and vesicular lesions on the tongue, gums, interdigital spaces, and teats. Controlled through compulsory vaccination using trivalent oil-adjuvant vaccines.
  • Anthrax: A peracute to acute fatal bacterial disease caused by the spore-forming rod Bacillus anthracis. Post-mortem examination reveals uncoagulated dark blood oozing from natural orifices and an enlarged, tarry spleen. Carcasses must never be opened; deep burial with quicklime is mandatory.
  • Haemorrhagic Septicaemia (HS): An acute, fatal septicemic disease of cattle and buffaloes caused by Pasteurella multocida (serotype B:2). Characterized by sudden high fever, swelling of the throat and brisket region (hence called 'Pasteurellosis' or 'Ghotwa'), and respiratory distress. Annual pre-monsoon vaccination is recommended.
  • Black Quarter (BQ): An acute, highly fatal infectious bacterial disease caused by Clostridium chauvoei, commonly affecting young cattle aged 6 months to 2 years. Characterized by crepitant swelling in heavy muscle masses (hindquarters), lameness, and rapid death.
  • Theileriosis: A tick-borne protozoan disease caused by Theileria annulata, transmitted by the hard tick Hyalomma anatolicum. Symptoms include high fever, enlarged superficial lymph nodes, corneal opacity, and petechial hemorrhages.

Metabolic and Nutritional Disorders

Metabolic disorders occur due to physiological stress, high production demands, or nutritional imbalances, particularly during transition periods around parturition in dairy cattle.

  • Milk Fever (Parturient Paresis): Caused by acute hypocalcemia (low blood serum calcium) occurring around calving in high-yielding dairy cows. Manifests as sternal recumbency, muscular twitching, cold extremities, and progressive coma. Treated with intravenous administration of calcium borogluconate solution.
  • Ketosis (Acetonemia): Occurs during early lactation when energy expenditure exceeds energy intake, leading to hypoglycemia and mobilization of body fat into ketone bodies. Causes reduced milk yield, sweet-smelling breath (acetone odor), and nervous symptoms. Prevented by adequate energy supplementation and propylene glycol administration.
  • Bloat (Tympanites): Excessive accumulation of fermentation gases in the rumen, typically caused by grazing lush, succulent leguminous forages like berseem (Trifolium alexandrinum) or lucerne (Medicago sativa). Foamy bloat prevents eructation. Treated with anti-foaming agents like turpentine oil or edible oils administered via stomach tube.

Veterinary Pharmacology, Vaccination, and Biosecurity Protocols

Preventive veterinary medicine relies heavily on strategic vaccination schedules, strict biosecurity measures, and rational use of pharmacological agents.

  • Vaccination Calendar: FMD vaccination is performed bi-annually (May-June and November-December). HS and BQ vaccinations are administered annually just before the onset of the monsoon season. Anthrax vaccination is scheduled in endemic zones before spring or rainy seasons.
  • Deworming (Anthelmintic Therapy): Regular deworming schedule using broad-spectrum anthelmintics like Albendazole, Fenbendazole, or Ivermectin to control gastrointestinal nematodes, lungworms, and ectoparasites. Typically executed at the change of seasons (every 3 to 4 months).
  • Farm Biosecurity: Implementation of sanitary barriers, quarantine of newly purchased animals, footbaths containing quaternary ammonium compounds or potassium permanganate at animal shed entrances, vector control, and proper disposal of manure and dead animal carcasses.

⚡ High-Yield Exam Points (UP TGT / PGT Focus)
  • Foot and Mouth Disease (FMD) affects cloven-hoofed animals and is caused by an aphthovirus exhibiting multiple serotypes.
  • Anthrax carcasses must never be opened for post-mortem because exposure of vegetative bacteria to oxygen leads to spore formation, contaminating the soil for decades.
  • Milk fever in high-yielding dairy cows is treated with intravenous injection of calcium borogluconate.
  • Haemorrhagic Septicaemia (HS) is also known as 'Ghotwa' due to severe throat edema causing respiratory blockage.
  • The vector for bovine tropical theileriosis is the hard tick Hyalomma anatolicum.
  • Bloat in ruminants grazing on lush berseem is caused by entrapment of fermentation gases in stable foam within the rumen.
  • The normal rectal temperature of an adult healthy cattle ranges between 101.5°F to 102.5°F.
  • Brucellosis causes contagious abortion in cattle during the last trimester of gestation and is zoonotic (undulant fever in humans).
⚠️ Common Mistakes & Exam Pitfalls
  • Confusing the vectors of protozoan diseases: remember that Theileria is transmitted by ticks, whereas Trypanosoma evansi (Surra) is transmitted by biting flies (Tabanus spp.).
  • Believing that antibiotics are effective against viral diseases like FMD; antibiotics are only used secondary to prevent opportunistic bacterial infections.

⚡ Chapter Master Summary: Animal Health Care and Veterinary Science

Systematic concept review designed for quick retention and last-minute pre-exam revision.

Concept 1

Etiological Classification of Animal Diseases

Animal diseases are classified based on their origin into infectious (pathogenic) and non-infectious (metabolic/nutritional) disorders.

  • Infectious Pathogens: Bacteria, viruses, fungi, and protozoa that can transmit horizontally between hosts.
  • Metabolic Disorders: Physiological imbalances due to improper nutrition during high production phases.
Concept 2

Bacterial Septicemic Diseases

Acute bacterial infections leading to rapid mortality in livestock.

  • Anthrax: Caused by Bacillus anthracis; characterized by uncoagulated blood and splenic enlargement.
  • Haemorrhagic Septicaemia: Caused by Pasteurella multocida; acute respiratory distress and throat edema.
Concept 3

Viral and Protozoan Infections

Major vector-borne and contagious viral threats affecting productivity.

  • FMD Virus: Highly contagious vesicular disease managed through trivalent vaccination.
  • Theileriosis: Tick-borne protozoan disease managed via acaricide dipping and anti-protozoals.
Concept 4

Metabolic Disturbances of Dairy Cattle

Nutritional deficiency syndromes linked to lactation stress.

  • Milk Fever: Acute hypocalcemia treated with calcium borogluconate IV.
  • Bloat: Ruminal tympany caused by froth formation from leguminous forages.

📚 Technical Terms Glossary

Zoonosis (Zoonotic Infection)
Infectious diseases that can be naturally transmitted from vertebrate animals to humans.
Crepitation (Crepitant Swelling)
A crackling sound or sensation produced by the presence of gas trapped in subcutaneous tissues, typical of Black Quarter.
Eructation (Belching)
The physiological process by which ruminants expel fermentation gases from the rumen.
Acaricide (Anti-mite / Anti-tick Agent)
Chemical pesticide used to kill ticks and mites infesting livestock.
Hypocalcemia (Low Serum Calcium)
A metabolic condition characterized by abnormally low levels of calcium in the blood, leading to milk fever.
Quarantine (Biosecurity Isolation)
Strict isolation of newly introduced animals to prevent the introduction of infectious agents into a herd.
Septicemia (Blood Poisoning)
A serious medical condition where pathogenic bacteria multiply and circulate within the bloodstream.
Vesicle (Blister / Aphtha)
A small fluid-filled blister on the skin or mucous membranes, characteristic of FMD.
Anthelmintic (Deworming Agent)
A pharmacological drug used to expel parasitic worms from the gastrointestinal tract.
Tympanites (Ruminal Bloat)
Distension of the rumen and reticulum by excessive accumulation of fermentation gases.
Serotype (Antigenic Variant)
A distinctly identifiable strain of a microorganism based on its cell surface antigens.
Surra (Trypanosomiasis)
A fatal blood protozoan disease of equines and camels transmitted by biting flies.

🎯 Must-Remember High-Frequency Facts

  • Causal organism of Anthrax: Bacillus anthracis.
  • Causal organism of Haemorrhagic Septicaemia: Pasteurella multocida.
  • Treatment for Milk Fever: Intravenous Calcium Borogluconate.
  • Vector for Theileriosis: Hyalomma anatolicum tick.

📌 Quick Recap Bullet Points

  • Animal health management encompasses disease prevention, biosecurity, and prompt clinical treatment.
  • Infectious diseases require vaccination and isolation, whereas metabolic disorders require nutritional management.
  • Anthrax is a spore-forming bacterial zoonosis where carcasses must not be opened.
  • FMD is a viral disease producing vesicles in the mouth and hooves of cloven-hoofed stock.

⚖️ Concepts Often Confused in Examinations

Black Quarter VS Haemorrhagic Septicaemia

Black Quarter affects hindquarter muscles with crepitant swelling (Clostridium chauvoei), whereas HS affects the throat and brisket with severe respiratory edema (Pasteurella multocida).

Question 1 of 20 Conceptual MCQ

Which of the following is a viral disease affecting cloven-hoofed animals characterized by vesicular lesions?

Explanation: Foot and Mouth Disease (FMD) is a highly contagious viral disease caused by an Aphthovirus affecting cloven-hoofed livestock, characterized by fever and vesicles on the tongue, gums, and hooves.

Question 2 of 20 Conceptual MCQ

What is the causative agent of Anthrax in livestock?

Explanation: Anthrax is caused by the spore-forming bacterium Bacillus anthracis. Carcasses must not be opened for necropsy to prevent spore germination in contact with oxygen.

Question 3 of 20 Conceptual MCQ

Haemorrhagic Septicaemia in cattle and buffaloes is caused by which pathogen?

Explanation: Haemorrhagic Septicaemia (HS) is an acute septicemic disease caused by Pasteurella multocida (serotype B:2 in Asian buffaloes and cattle).

Question 4 of 20 Conceptual MCQ

Black Quarter is primarily caused by which bacterial species?

Explanation: Black Quarter (BQ) is caused by Clostridium chauvoei, an anaerobic, spore-forming soil bacterium affecting young cattle aged 6 months to 2 years.

Question 5 of 20 Conceptual MCQ

Milk Fever in dairy cows is caused by a severe deficiency of which mineral in the blood serum?

Explanation: Milk fever (hypocalcemia) occurs when blood serum calcium drops precipitously around parturition due to sudden onset of colostrum and milk secretion.

Question 6 of 20 Conceptual MCQ

What is the standard therapeutic treatment for acute Milk Fever in dairy cows?

Explanation: Intravenous administration of calcium borogluconate restores serum calcium levels rapidly, reviving cows down with parturient paresis.

Question 7 of 20 Conceptual MCQ

Bloat (Tympanites) in ruminants is frequently triggered by excessive ingestion of which type of forage?

Explanation: Grazing on lush, succulent leguminous forages such as berseem (Trifolium alexandrinum) or lucerne causes frothy bloat by trapping fermentation gases in stable proteinaceous foams.

Question 8 of 20 Conceptual MCQ

Which of the following is the transmitting vector for bovine tropical theileriosis?

Explanation: The tick Hyalomma anatolicum acts as the primary vector transmitting Theileria annulata, causing bovine tropical theileriosis.

Question 9 of 20 Conceptual MCQ

Brucellosis in cattle is characterized by which prominent clinical sign?

Explanation: Brucellosis causes infectious abortion (typically in the 5th to 8th month of pregnancy), retained placenta, and metritis in cattle, along with zoonotic undulant fever in humans.

Question 10 of 20 Conceptual MCQ

What is the recommended timing for Haemorrhagic Septicaemia (HS) vaccination in endemic areas?

Explanation: HS and BQ vaccinations are administered annually just before the monsoon season (typically April-May) to confer active immunity before environmental stress peaks.

Question 11 of 20 Conceptual MCQ

Which anti-foaming agent is commonly administered orally to treat frothy bloat in cattle?

Explanation: Turpentine oil or edible vegetable oils break down the surface tension of stable foam in frothy bloat, enabling eructation and gas release.

Question 12 of 20 Conceptual MCQ

What is the normal rectal temperature range of a healthy adult cow?

Explanation: The normal physiological body temperature of cattle ranges between 101.5°F and 102.5°F (38.6°C to 39.2°C). Elevations above this indicate fever.

Question 13 of 20 Conceptual MCQ

Which of the following describes Ketosis in high-yielding dairy cows?

Explanation: Ketosis (acetonemia) is a metabolic disorder occurring when energy demands exceed intake in early lactation, causing hypoglycemia and accumulation of ketone bodies in blood, milk, and urine.

Question 14 of 20 Conceptual MCQ

Why is post-mortem examination strictly prohibited in suspected Anthrax carcasses?

Explanation: Opening an anthrax carcass exposes vegetative Bacillus anthracis bacilli to atmospheric oxygen, inducing sporulation. Spores persist in soil for decades, perpetuating infection.

Question 15 of 20 Conceptual MCQ

What is the primary function of farm footbaths containing disinfectants at animal shed entrances?

Explanation: Footbaths containing disinfectants (like quaternary ammonium compounds or potassium permanganate) prevent mechanical transfer of pathogens into animal barns on footwear.

Question 16 of 20 Conceptual MCQ

Which diagnostic test is standard for screening dairy herds for Brucellosis?

Explanation: The Rose Bengal Plate Test (RBPT) is widely used as a rapid, reliable slide agglutination screening test for antibodies against Brucella in cattle serum.

Question 17 of 20 Conceptual MCQ

Mastitis is best defined as:

Explanation: Mastitis is the inflammation of the mammary gland, usually caused by bacterial pathogens entering via the teat canal, leading to altered milk composition and economic loss.

Question 18 of 20 Conceptual MCQ

Which test is utilized at the dairy farm level to detect sub-clinical mastitis?

Explanation: The California Mastitis Test (CMT) is a rapid cow-side surfactant test that detects somatic cell count increases associated with sub-clinical mastitis.

Question 19 of 20 Conceptual MCQ

Surra, a fatal protozoan disease affecting camels, horses, and cattle, is caused by:

Explanation: Surra is caused by the blood protozoan parasite Trypanosoma evansi, transmitted mechanically by biting flies, leading to intermittent fever, progressive anemia, and emaciation.

Question 20 of 20 Conceptual MCQ

What is the recommended frequency for routine deworming of adult dairy cattle under standard management?

Explanation: Routine deworming every 3 to 4 months (typically at the transition of seasons) effectively controls gastrointestinal nematodes and maintains herd production efficiency.

Match the following livestock diseases with their respective causal agents: 1. Anthrax (a) Pasteurella multocida, 2. Haemorrhagic Septicaemia (b) Clostridium chauvoei, 3. Black Quarter (c) Bacillus anthracis. Select the correct combination:

Solution & Rationale: Anthrax is caused by Bacillus anthracis (1-c), Haemorrhagic Septicaemia by Pasteurella multocida (2-a), and Black Quarter by Clostridium chauvoei (3-b).

Test Question 2 of 10 TGT Standard

In case of an outbreak of Foot and Mouth Disease (FMD) in a dairy farm, which immediate management intervention is least effective?

Solution & Rationale: Antibiotics are ineffective against viral agents like FMD virus; they are only used to manage secondary bacterial infections. Isolation and hygiene are primary controls.

Test Question 3 of 10 TGT Standard

A dairy cow in early lactation suddenly collapses with sternal recumbency, cold extremities, and dull mentation. Serum biochemical analysis reveals profound hypocalcemia. What is the diagnosis and immediate treatment?

Solution & Rationale: Sternal recumbency around parturition accompanied by hypocalcemia is classical Milk Fever (parturient paresis), treated successfully with intravenous calcium borogluconate.

Test Question 4 of 10 PGT Standard

Which of the following parasitic protozoa is correctly matched with its respective vector in Indian livestock?

Solution & Rationale: Theileria annulata is transmitted by the ixodid tick Hyalomma anatolicum. Babesia is also tick-borne, while Trypanosoma is transmitted by biting flies.

Test Question 5 of 10 PGT Standard

Why is the Rose Bengal Plate Test (RBPT) preferred over other tests for field screening of Brucellosis?

Solution & Rationale: RBPT is a rapid, inexpensive slide agglutination test requiring minimal equipment, making it ideal for field screening of herds for brucellosis antibodies.

Test Question 6 of 10 TGT Standard

Which clostridial disease affects young cattle aged 6 months to 2 years, manifesting as crepitant swellings in heavy muscle masses?

Solution & Rationale: Black Quarter (BQ) is caused by Clostridium chauvoei, characterized by acute emphysematous swelling that crackles (crepitates) upon palpation due to gas production.

Test Question 7 of 10 TGT Standard

What primary prophylactic measure must be adopted annually before the monsoon season in Northern India to protect cattle and buffaloes from septicemic bacterial killers?

Solution & Rationale: Haemorrhagic Septicaemia (HS) and Black Quarter (BQ) peak during the monsoon season due to high humidity and stress; annual pre-monsoon vaccination is mandatory.

Test Question 8 of 10 PGT Standard

Which metabolic disorder in ruminants is characterized by sweet-smelling breath (acetone odor), drop in milk yield, and nervous signs during early lactation?

Solution & Rationale: Ketosis results from severe negative energy balance in high-yielding dairy cows, leading to accumulation of ketone bodies (acetone, acetoacetate) giving breath a characteristic sweet odor.

Test Question 9 of 10 TGT Standard

In terms of biosecurity, what is the correct protocol for introducing newly purchased cattle into an existing dairy herd?

Solution & Rationale: Quarantine separates new animals to monitor health status, screen for latent infections (like tuberculosis, brucellosis, and Johne's disease), and prevent herd outbreaks.

Test Question 10 of 10 TGT Standard

What is the primary pharmacological action of anthelmintics such as Albendazole or Fenbendazole administered to livestock?

Solution & Rationale: Anthelmintics are antiparasitic drugs that kill or expel parasitic worms (nematodes, cestodes, trematodes) from the gastrointestinal tract and other organs.
