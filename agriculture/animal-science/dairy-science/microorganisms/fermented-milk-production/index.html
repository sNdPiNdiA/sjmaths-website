Fermented Milk Production

Fermented milk products represent an essential branch of dairy science involving controlled biochemical transformations driven by specific lactic acid bacteria. Mastering starter cultures, biochemical pathways, processing parameters, and quality control metrics is critical for success in the UP PGT and TGT Agriculture examinations.

Introduction and Classification of Fermented Milks

Fermented milk products are defined as products prepared from milk—whole, skimmed, partially skimmed, reconstituted, or recombined—that have undergone fermentation by the action of suitable microorganisms, resulting in a reduction of pH with or without coagulation. Fermentation extends the shelf life of highly perishable fluid milk, enhances digestibility, and improves nutritional and therapeutic value.

  • Mesophilic Fermented Milks: Produced using mesophilic starter cultures that thrive best at temperatures between 20°C and 30°C. Examples include cultured buttermilk, sour cream, and traditional kefir.
  • Thermophilic Fermented Milks: Produced using thermophilic cultures operating optimally at 40°C to 45°C. Yogurt, dahi, and acidophilus milk belong to this category.
  • Yeast-Lactic Fermented Milks: Involve both lactic acid bacteria and lactose-fermenting or non-fermenting yeasts, yielding ethanol and carbon dioxide alongside lactic acid (e.g., kefir, kumis).

Microbiology and Starter Cultures in Fermented Milks

Starter cultures are preparations containing live microorganisms used intentionally to initiate and accelerate milk fermentation. They dictate the flavor, aroma, texture, and body of the final product.

  • Lactococcus lactis subsp. lactis and Lactococcus lactis subsp. cremoris: Primary acid producers in mesophilic products.
  • Leuconostoc mesenteroides subsp. cremoris: Key aroma and carbon dioxide producer, responsible for the characteristic diacetyl flavor in cultured butter and buttermilk.
  • Lactobacillus delbrueckii subsp. bulgaricus and Streptococcus thermophilus: The symbiotic pair used in yogurt manufacturing.
  • Symbiosis: Streptococcus thermophilus grows rapidly, producing formic acid and pyruvic acid which stimulate Lactobacillus delbrueckii subsp. bulgaricus; in turn, the latter releases free amino acids (valine) via proteolysis, stimulating the streptococci.

Biochemical Pathways and Fermentation Chemistry

The core biochemical mechanism in fermented milk production is the conversion of lactose (milk sugar) into lactic acid by lactic acid bacteria (LAB).

  • Homofermentative Lactic Acid Fermentation: Organisms such as Lactobacillus acidophilus and Streptococcus thermophilus possess aldolase enzymes and metabolize glucose exclusively into lactic acid via the Embden-Meyerhof-Parnas (EMP) pathway, yielding approximately 1.5 to 1.8 moles of lactic acid per mole of glucose consumed.
  • Heterofermentative Lactic Acid Fermentation: Organisms such as Leuconostoc species lack fructose-1,6-bisphosphate aldolase and utilize the phosphoketolase pathway, producing lactic acid, ethanol (or acetic acid), and carbon dioxide.
  • Proteolysis and Lipolysis: Microbial proteinases break down casein into peptides and amino acids, enhancing digestibility. Lipases release free fatty acids that contribute to complex flavor profiles.

Manufacturing Technology of Set and Stirred Yogurt

Yogurt manufacturing is an industrial standard process requiring rigorous time-temperature combinations and hygiene control.

  • Standardization: Adjusting the fat and solids-not-fat (SNF) content, typically targeting 12–14% total solids by adding skim milk powder or through vacuum evaporation.
  • Homogenization: Performed at 60–65°C under 15–20 MPa pressure to prevent cream separation and enhance the viscosity and mouthfeel of the curd.
  • Heat Treatment (Pasteurization/Sterilization): Heating milk to 85°C for 30 minutes or 95°C for 5 minutes. This inactivates native milk enzymes, destroys competitive microflora, and denatures whey proteins (mainly eta-lactoglobulin) to interact with \kappa-casein, improving water-holding capacity.
  • Inoculation and Incubation: Cooling the milk to 42–45°C, adding 2–3% starter culture. For set yogurt, incubation occurs directly in retail containers; for stirred yogurt, incubation occurs in large vats followed by agitation, cooling, and packaging.

Defects, Quality Control, and Therapeutic Benefits

Quality assurance ensures consumer safety and product uniformity, while awareness of defects guides corrective troubleshooting.

  • Syneresis (Wheying-Off): Separation of whey from the curd, caused by excessive acidity, insufficient SNF, rough handling of warm coagulum, or high incubation temperatures.
  • Flavor Defects: Flat flavor (insufficient diacetyl), yeasty or alcoholic flavor (yeast contamination), and bitterness (excessive proteolysis by rogue psychrotrophs).
  • Therapeutic Value (Probiotics): Consumption of viable probiotic strains like Lactobacillus acidophilus and Bifidobacterium bifidum improves gut microbiota, enhances immune function, lowers serum cholesterol, and aids lactose intolerance management.

⚡ High-Yield Exam Points (UP TGT / PGT Focus)
  • Optimum incubation temperature for thermophilic yogurt cultures is 42°C to 45°C.
  • The primary aroma compound responsible for the nutty and buttery flavor in cultured buttermilk is diacetyl.
  • Syneresis in yogurt is defined as the shrinkage of the curd and expulsion of whey.
  • Total solids in standard yogurt milk are typically raised to 12-14% by adding skim milk powder.
  • Homofermentative lactic acid bacteria utilize the Embden-Meyerhof-Parnas (EMP) glycolytic pathway.
⚠️ Common Mistakes & Exam Pitfalls
  • Confusing mesophilic and thermophilic optimal growth temperatures; mesophilic operates at 20-30°C while thermophilic operates at 40-45°C.
  • Assuming lactose is completely converted to ethanol in all fermented milks; ethanol is characteristic of yeast-lactic fermentations like kefir, not standard yogurt.
  • Believing that pasteurization destroys all spores; thermoduric spore formers survive, requiring careful hygiene during starter addition.

⚡ Chapter Master Summary: Fermented Milk Production

Systematic concept review designed for quick retention and last-minute pre-exam revision.

Concept 1

Definition and Classification of Fermented Milks

Fermentation preserves milk through microbial acid production. Products are classified based on microbial temperature preference into mesophilic and thermophilic types.

  • Mesophilic Group: Fermented at 20°C–30°C (e.g., buttermilk).
  • Thermophilic Group: Fermented at 40°C–45°C (e.g., yogurt).
Concept 2

Starter Culture Symbiosis

In yogurt manufacturing, dual-strain starter cultures exhibit metabolic cooperation that accelerates acid development and flavor synthesis.

  • Protocooperation: Streptococcus thermophilus stimulates Lactobacillus bulgaricus by producing formic acid, while the latter releases valine.
Concept 3

Biochemical Pathways of Lactic Acid Fermentation

Microorganisms metabolize lactose into organic acids, lowering the pH and causing casein coagulation at its isoelectric point (pH 4.6).

  • Homofermentation: Yields >90% lactic acid via EMP pathway.
  • Heterofermentation: Yields lactic acid, acetic acid, ethanol, and carbon dioxide.
Concept 4

Thermal Processing of Milk for Yogurt

Intense heating prior to inoculation modifies physical properties to ensure stable gel formation.

  • Heat Treatment: 85°C for 30 minutes or 95°C for 5 minutes denatures whey proteins to trap water and prevent syneresis.
Concept 5

Defects and Quality Control

Understanding biochemical and physical defects allows dairy plant operators to adjust processing parameters.

  • Syneresis: Whey separation caused by over-acidification or mechanical agitation of hot curd.

📚 Technical Terms Glossary

Starter Culture (Selected microbial preparation)
A preparation of live active microorganisms inoculated into milk to initiate desired fermentation.
Syneresis (Whey expulsion / Wheying-off)
The contraction of a gel network resulting in the extrusion of liquid whey.
Homoefermentation (Glycolytic lactic acid production)
Microbial fermentation where lactic acid is the sole or primary end product (>90%).
Heterofermentation (Phosphoketolase pathway)
Fermentation producing significant amounts of ethanol, carbon dioxide, and acetic acid alongside lactic acid.
Diacetyl (2,3-butanedione)
A volatile organic compound responsible for the pleasant buttery aroma in cultured dairy products.
Acetaldehyde (Ethanal)
The principal aromatic compound imparting the characteristic green-apple flavor note to yogurt.
Probiotics (Live beneficial microorganisms)
Live microbial feed supplements that beneficially affect the host by improving intestinal microbial balance.
Set Yogurt (Container-incubated yogurt)
Yogurt that is packaged immediately after inoculation and incubated directly in the final container.
Stirred Yogurt (Vat-incubated yogurt)
Yogurt fermented in bulk vats, mechanically agitated into a smooth texture, and then packaged.
Isoelectric Point (Casein precipitation pH (~4.6))
The pH at which net electrical charge of casein micelles is zero, leading to coagulation.
Symbiosis (Mutualistic microbial interaction)
The close and long-term biological interaction between two different organisms benefiting each other.

🎯 Must-Remember High-Frequency Facts

  • Casein coagulates at its isoelectric point of pH 4.6.
  • Diacetyl is produced by Leuconostoc species in mesophilic products.
  • Total solids for yogurt are typically standardized to 12-14%.
  • Probiotics must survive gastric transit to colonize the intestinal tract.

📌 Quick Recap Bullet Points

  • Fermented milks are produced by controlled microbial acidification.
  • Mesophilic cultures operate at 20-30°C; thermophilic cultures at 40-45°C.
  • Yogurt relies on the symbiotic pair Streptococcus thermophilus and Lactobacillus bulgaricus.
  • Heat treatment of milk denatures whey proteins to improve gel structure and prevent syneresis.

⚖️ Concepts Often Confused in Examinations

Set Yogurt VS Stirred Yogurt

Set yogurt is incubated and set inside its retail container, whereas stirred yogurt is incubated in bulk vats and stirred prior to packaging.

Homofermentative VS Heterofermentative

Homofermentative bacteria produce almost exclusively lactic acid, while heterofermentative bacteria produce lactic acid, ethanol, and carbon dioxide.

Question 1 of 19 Conceptual MCQ

Which of the following microorganisms is classified as a mesophilic lactic acid bacterium?

Explanation: Lactococcus lactis subsp. lactis is a mesophilic bacterium with an optimum growth temperature of 30°C, whereas the others are thermophilic.

Question 2 of 19 Conceptual MCQ

What is the optimum incubation temperature for manufacturing traditional yogurt using thermophilic cultures?

Explanation: Thermophilic cultures like Streptococcus thermophilus and Lactobacillus bulgaricus perform optimally at 42°C to 45°C.

Question 3 of 19 Conceptual MCQ

Which volatile compound is primarily responsible for the characteristic buttery aroma in cultured buttermilk?

Explanation: Diacetyl (2,3-butanedione) is synthesized by aroma-producing bacteria such as Leuconostoc mesenteroides subsp. cremoris from citric acid.

Question 4 of 19 Conceptual MCQ

At what pH level does casein typically precipitate or coagulate to form the curd in fermented milk products?

Explanation: The isoelectric point of milk casein is approximately pH 4.6, at which point the net charge is zero and micelles aggregate into a gel.

Question 5 of 19 Conceptual MCQ

What is the primary biochemical role of Streptococcus thermophilus in its symbiotic relationship with Lactobacillus bulgaricus during yogurt production?

Explanation: Streptococcus thermophilus grows rapidly initially, producing formic acid and carbon dioxide which stimulate the growth of Lactobacillus bulgaricus.

Question 6 of 19 Conceptual MCQ

Which defect in yogurt is characterized by the spontaneous separation of liquid whey from the protein gel network?

Explanation: Syneresis refers to the contraction of the gel matrix and the expulsion of whey, often caused by over-acidification or mechanical shock.

Question 7 of 19 Conceptual MCQ

What is the typical total solids content recommended for manufacturing high-quality yogurt?

Explanation: Standard milk is usually enriched with skim milk powder to achieve 12% to 14% total solids, improving viscosity and preventing syneresis.

Question 8 of 19 Conceptual MCQ

Which pathway is utilized by homofermentative lactic acid bacteria to convert glucose into lactic acid?

Explanation: Homofermentative LAB possess aldolase and utilize the EMP glycolytic pathway, converting nearly all glucose into lactic acid.

Question 9 of 19 Conceptual MCQ

Which of the following is a yeast-lactic fermented milk product containing moderate amounts of alcohol and carbon dioxide?

Explanation: Kefir is produced using kefir grains containing lactic acid bacteria and lactose-fermenting yeasts, yielding lactic acid, ethanol, and CO₂.

Question 10 of 19 Conceptual MCQ

Why is milk subjected to intensive heat treatment (e.g., 85°C for 30 min) prior to yogurt inoculation?

Explanation: Intensive heating destroys competing microflora, denatures whey proteins (beta-lactoglobulin) to bind water, and inactivates native antimicrobial enzymes.

Question 11 of 19 Conceptual MCQ

What is the principal flavor compound responsible for the characteristic aroma of fresh yogurt?

Explanation: Acetaldehyde is the dominant volatile compound synthesized by yogurt starter cultures, imparting the characteristic refreshing aroma.

Question 12 of 19 Conceptual MCQ

Which bacterium is commonly used alongside traditional yogurt cultures for its exceptional probiotic and intestinal health benefits?

Explanation: Lactobacillus acidophilus is widely incorporated as a probiotic supplement in fermented milks due to its acid tolerance and health benefits.

Question 13 of 19 Conceptual MCQ

In the production of stirred yogurt, what is the purpose of the agitation step after incubation?

Explanation: Stirred yogurt undergoes mechanical agitation after incubation to disrupt the coagulum, yielding a smooth, homogeneous texture prior to cooling and packaging.

Question 14 of 19 Conceptual MCQ

Which of the following defects is characterized by a stringy or mucous texture in fermented milk?

Explanation: Ropiness is caused by slime-producing strains of lactic acid bacteria that synthesize exopolysaccharides, increasing viscosity.

Question 15 of 19 Conceptual MCQ

What is the primary action of proteolytic enzymes from starter cultures on milk proteins during fermentation?

Explanation: Proteolytic enzymes break down casein proteins into smaller peptides and amino acids, improving nutritional availability and digestibility.

Question 16 of 19 Conceptual MCQ

Which starter culture combination is standard for commercial yogurt production?

Explanation: The symbiotic combination of Streptococcus thermophilus and Lactobacillus delbrueckii subsp. bulgaricus is the universal standard for yogurt.

Question 17 of 19 Conceptual MCQ

What is the main function of homogenization of milk prior to yogurt fermentation?

Explanation: Homogenization reduces milk fat globule size below 2 micrometers, preventing cream rising and enhancing curd firmness and mouthfeel.

Question 18 of 19 Conceptual MCQ

Which of the following fermented products utilizes yeasts in combination with lactic acid bacteria?

Explanation: Kefir fermentation involves both lactic acid bacteria (acid production) and lactose-fermenting yeasts (alcohol and carbon dioxide production).

Question 19 of 19 Conceptual MCQ

What is the primary cause of bitterness in fermented milk products?

Explanation: Bitterness is typically caused by certain proteolytic bacterial strains that release specific bitter hydrophobic peptides during prolonged or abnormal fermentation.
