Charter Act of 1853

The Charter Act of 1853 was the last of the series of Charter Acts passed by the British Parliament between 1793 and 1853. It was a significant constitutional landmark that laid the foundation of the modern parliamentary form of government in India.

Key Provisions

  • Separation of Functions: It separated the legislative and executive functions of the Governor-General’s Council for the first time.
  • Legislative Council: It provided for the addition of six new members, called legislative councillors, to the council. This body came to be known as the Indian (Central) Legislative Council.
  • Mini-Parliament: The legislative wing functioned as a mini-parliament, adopting procedures similar to the British Parliament. Legislation was treated as a special function requiring special machinery and process.
  • Civil Services Reform: It introduced an open competition system for the selection and recruitment of civil servants. The Macaulay Committee (Committee on the Indian Civil Service) was appointed in 1854 for this purpose.
  • End of Covenanted Service: The covenanted civil service was thrown open to the Indians, effectively ending the patronage system of the Court of Directors.
  • Extension of Company Rule: Unlike previous acts, it did not specify any particular period for the continuation of the Company’s rule. It allowed the British Crown to take over the possession of Indian territories at any time.
  • Local Representation: For the first time, local representation was introduced in the Indian (Central) Legislative Council. Out of the six new legislative members, four were appointed by the local (provincial) governments of Madras, Bombay, Bengal, and Agra.

Significance for UPSC

  • Constitutional Precedent: It established the principle that legislation should be a separate function of government, distinct from executive administration.
  • Meritocracy: The introduction of open competition marked the beginning of the transition from a patronage-based bureaucracy to a merit-based one.
  • Precursor to 1857: By not renewing the Company's charter for a fixed term, the Act signaled that the British government was preparing to assume direct control, which eventually happened after the Revolt of 1857.
