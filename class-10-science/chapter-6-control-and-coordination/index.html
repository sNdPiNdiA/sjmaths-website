Concepts & Visual Theory

Complete NCERT-aligned progression • diagrams • reasoning

01

Why Control and Coordination are Needed

Living organisms constantly interact with changes in their surroundings. A change such as heat, light, touch, smell or gravity can act as a stimulus. The organism must detect it, process information and produce an appropriate response.

Master response pathway
🧠 SRCR = Stimulus → Receptor → Coordinator → Response.
02

Animals — Nervous and Muscular Tissues

In animals, control and coordination are provided mainly by nervous tissue and muscular tissue. Nervous tissue transmits information rapidly; muscles act as effectors to produce movement.

Nervous tissue

Receives information, conducts electrical impulses and coordinates responses.

Muscular tissue

Contracts in response to signals and produces movement.

03

Neuron — Structural and Functional Unit

A neuron is a nerve cell specialised to receive and transmit information. Dendrites receive information; the cell body integrates it; the axon carries the electrical impulse away from the cell body.

Neuron and direction of impulse
Impulse pathway: dendrite → cell body → axon → axon ending.
🧠 D-C-A = Dendrite → Cell body → Axon.
04

Electrical Impulse and Synapse

When information reaches a dendritic tip, chemical changes can generate an electrical impulse. It travels along the neuron. At the axon ending, chemicals are released into the small gap between neurons called the synapse. These chemicals initiate a similar impulse in the next neuron.

Neuron → synapse → next neuron
⭐ Why a chemical synapse? The electrical signal in one neuron triggers chemical release; the chemical signal starts a new electrical impulse in the next neuron.
05

Receptors — Detecting Stimuli

Receptors are specialised structures/cells that detect environmental information. Receptors occur in sense organs. Examples include receptors for taste in the tongue and smell in the nose. Detection converts environmental information into signals that the nervous system can process.

Sense Receptor location Stimulus
Smell Nose Chemicals in air
Taste Tongue Dissolved chemicals
Vision Eye Light
Hearing/balance Inner ear Sound/mechanical changes
🧠 Receptor = “receiver”. It receives information before the nervous system can respond.
06

Reflex Action — Fast Protective Response

A reflex action is a rapid, automatic response to a stimulus. For example, withdrawing the hand from a hot object. Reflex arcs are efficient because the response pathway can be coordinated through the spinal cord without waiting for the brain to make the immediate motor decision.

Reflex arc
🧠 R-S-C-M-E = Receptor → Sensory neuron → Coordinator → Motor neuron → Effector.
⭐ Exam trap: the brain is not “inactive” during a reflex. Information can also reach the brain, but the immediate response is coordinated rapidly through the spinal cord.
07

Reflex Action vs Walking

Feature Reflex action Walking
Nature Rapid, automatic Voluntary, planned/controlled
Immediate coordination Spinal cord can coordinate Brain coordinates complex movement
Purpose Often protective Intentional locomotion
Example Hand withdrawal Walking to a destination
Reflex = fast protection; walking = planned movement.
08

Human Brain — Main Coordinating Centre

The brain and spinal cord constitute the central nervous system (CNS). The brain receives information, integrates it and sends instructions. The peripheral nervous system connects the CNS with the rest of the body.

Nervous system organisation
🧠 CNS = “Control & Coordination Headquarters”.
09

Forebrain — Thinking, Interpretation & Voluntary Action

The forebrain is the main thinking part. It receives sensory information, interprets it by combining inputs and stored information, makes decisions and sends commands to motor areas controlling voluntary muscles.

Forebrain is associated with thinking + interpretation + decision-making + voluntary motor control.
🧠 FORE = “Think Before” → sensory interpretation and decisions.
10

Midbrain & Hindbrain — Involuntary Responses, Balance & Coordination

The midbrain and hindbrain participate in many involuntary responses. The medulla in the hindbrain controls involuntary actions such as blood pressure, salivation and vomiting. The cerebellum helps precision of voluntary actions and maintains posture and balance.

Midbrain

Participates in certain involuntary responses and sensory-motor coordination.

Medulla

Controls important involuntary functions such as blood pressure, salivation and vomiting.

Cerebellum

Precision, posture and equilibrium/balance.

🧠 “Cerebellum = Coordination & Balance; Medulla = Maintenance.”
11

Spinal Cord & Peripheral Nervous System

The spinal cord carries information between the brain and body and can coordinate reflex arcs. The peripheral nervous system consists of nerves arising from the brain and spinal cord, connecting the CNS with other body parts.

⭐ Spinal cord injury can disrupt communication between the brain and body below the injury, affecting sensory and motor signals.
12

Protection of Brain and Spinal Cord

The brain is protected by the skull. It is also surrounded by fluid that helps absorb shocks. The spinal cord is protected by the vertebral column/backbone.

🧠 “Skull for Brain; Spine for Spinal cord.”
13

Voluntary vs Involuntary Actions

Voluntary Involuntary
Under conscious control Not under conscious control
Example: writing, moving a chair Example: blood pressure regulation, salivation
Forebrain motor areas involved in planned actions Midbrain/hindbrain structures can control automatic functions
🧠 VOLUNTARY = “I choose”; INVOLUNTARY = “It runs automatically.”
14

Plants Coordinate Without a Nervous System

Plants do not have a nervous system or muscles. Yet they respond to stimuli such as touch, light, gravity and water. Plant movements may be growth-independent or growth-dependent.

Growth-independent

  • Fast movement possible.
  • Does not require growth.
  • Example: Mimosa leaves folding after touch.
  • Involves changes in water/turgor and cell shape.

Growth-dependent

  • Usually slow.
  • Directional growth causes movement.
  • Example: shoot bending toward light.
  • Controlled by growth regulators/hormones.
🧠 “Touch = Turgor; Tropism = Growth.”
15

Mimosa / Sensitive Plant — Movement Without Growth

When Mimosa leaves are touched, they fold and droop rapidly. This does not involve growth. Plant cells change shape because the amount of water in them changes, causing swelling or shrinking.

Touch response
16

Plant Tropisms — Directional Growth Responses

A tropism is a directional growth response of a plant part to an external stimulus. The direction of growth may be toward or away from the stimulus.

Tropism Stimulus Typical response
Phototropism Light Shoot grows toward light
Geotropism/Gravitropism Gravity Roots generally grow toward gravity; shoots away
Hydrotropism Water Roots grow toward water
Thigmotropism Touch/contact Tendrils coil around support
🧠 “Photo–Geo–Hydro–Thigmo” = Light–Gravity–Water–Touch.
17

Phototropism — Auxin and Unequal Growth

When light comes from one side, auxin redistributes toward the shaded side of a shoot. In shoots, auxin promotes cell elongation. Greater elongation on the shaded side causes the shoot to bend toward light.

Shoot bends toward light
Phototropism logic: one-sided light → auxin redistribution → unequal elongation → bending toward light.
🧠 “Auxin goes to shade; shoot goes to sun.”
18

Thigmotropism — Tendril Coiling

Pea tendrils respond to contact with a support. The side away from the support grows faster than the side touching the support, causing the tendril to curve around the object and cling to it.

🧠 “Touch → Unequal growth → Coil.”
19

Plant Hormones — Chemical Coordination

Plants use chemical messengers called plant hormones to coordinate growth, development and responses. Unlike fast nervous impulses, chemical communication can be slower but can act more broadly and persistently.

Auxins

Promote growth; important in directional growth and cell elongation.

Gibberellins

Promote growth.

Cytokinins

Promote cell division.

Abscisic acid

Growth inhibitor; associated with effects such as wilting.

Ethylene

Plant hormone involved in processes such as fruit ripening.

🧠 “A-G-C grow; ABA slows.” → Auxin, Gibberellin, Cytokinin promote growth-related processes; Abscisic acid inhibits growth.
20

Electrical vs Chemical Communication

Electrical impulses Chemical signals/hormones
Very rapid Usually slower
Travel through connected nervous tissue Can travel through body fluids such as blood in animals
Good for immediate responses Good for widespread and sustained effects
Cells need time to reset after transmitting impulses Can act steadily and persistently
Fast + precise = nervous; slower + widespread = hormonal.
21

Animal Hormones & Endocrine System

Hormones are chemical signals released by endocrine glands. They travel through the blood to target tissues/organs and coordinate functions such as growth, metabolism, blood sugar regulation, stress response and reproduction.

Hormonal coordination
🧠 G-H-B-T = Gland → Hormone → Blood → Target.
22

Adrenaline — Emergency “Fight or Flight” Response

During fear, anger or danger, the adrenal glands release adrenaline into the blood. It prepares the body for rapid action: heart rate rises, breathing rate rises, more blood is directed toward skeletal muscles and blood flow to digestive system/skin is reduced.

Heart

Beats faster → increased oxygen supply to muscles.

Breathing

Rate increases → greater oxygen availability.

Skeletal muscles

Receive increased blood supply for action.

Digestive system/skin

Blood supply is reduced during the emergency response.

🧠 Adrenaline = “Action hormone.” Heart ↑, breathing ↑, muscle blood supply ↑.
23

Endocrine Glands & Major Hormones

Hormone Gland Major function in NCERT chapter
Growth hormone Pituitary Stimulates growth in organs
Thyroxine Thyroid Regulates metabolism for body growth
Insulin Pancreas Regulates blood sugar level
Testosterone Testes Male sexual development and related changes
Ovarian hormones Ovaries Female sexual development and menstrual-cycle regulation
Adrenaline Adrenal gland Emergency response
Releasing hormones Hypothalamus Stimulate pituitary gland to release hormones
🧠 “Pituitary grows, Thyroid tunes metabolism, Pancreas controls sugar, Adrenal acts in alarm.”
24

Thyroxine, Iodine & Goitre

Iodine is required by the thyroid gland to make thyroxine. If iodine intake is insufficient, thyroid hormone production can be affected and the thyroid may enlarge, producing goitre. Iodised salt is advised as a practical source of dietary iodine.

Iodine → thyroid hormone synthesis → normal metabolism/body growth.
🧠 “Iodine feeds the thyroid.”
25

Insulin & Diabetes

Insulin regulates blood glucose. When blood sugar rises, pancreatic cells respond by producing more insulin. As blood sugar falls, insulin secretion is reduced. Insufficient insulin action can lead to diabetes; some patients are treated with insulin injections.

Negative feedback idea
🧠 “Sugar ↑ → Insulin ↑ → Sugar ↓ → Insulin ↓.”
26

Growth & Releasing Hormones

Growth hormone from the pituitary stimulates growth in organs. Releasing hormones from the hypothalamus stimulate the pituitary to release particular hormones. Hormonal systems therefore contain control relationships rather than isolated glands acting independently.

Think of the endocrine system as a hierarchy + feedback network, not merely a list of glands.
27

Feedback Mechanism — Keeping Hormones in Balance

Hormones must be released in appropriate amounts. Feedback mechanisms regulate their secretion. In the insulin example, rising blood sugar stimulates insulin release; falling blood sugar reduces insulin secretion. This prevents uncontrolled hormone action.

🧠 “Change → correction → return toward normal.”
28

Nervous vs Hormonal Coordination — Master Comparison

Feature Nervous Hormonal
Signal Electrical impulse + synaptic chemicals Chemical hormone
Route Nervous tissue Body fluid/blood in animals
Speed Very fast Usually slower
Range Specific connected pathways Potentially widespread
Duration Often short-lived Can be sustained
Best suited for Immediate response Growth, metabolism, longer coordination
🧠 “Nerve = Now; Hormone = Longer.”
29

NCERT Exercises — Questions + Answers

Complete Chapter 6 end-of-chapter exercise set

1. Which of the following is a plant hormone? (a) Insulin (b) Thyroxin (c) Oestrogen (d) Cytokinin.
ANSWER(d) Cytokinin. Cytokinin is a plant hormone involved in cell division and growth-related processes.
2. The gap between two neurons is called a (a) dendrite (b) synapse (c) axon (d) impulse.
ANSWER(b) Synapse.
3. The brain is responsible for (a) thinking (b) regulating the heartbeat (c) balancing the body (d) all of the above.
ANSWER(d) All of the above. Different brain regions perform these functions: thinking involves the forebrain, involuntary functions include medulla control, and balance involves the cerebellum.
4. What is the function of receptors in our body? Think of situations where receptors do not work properly. What problems are likely to arise?
ANSWERReceptors detect changes/stimuli in the environment and help initiate signals for appropriate responses. If receptors fail, a person may not properly detect stimuli such as heat, pain, smell, taste, light or sound, increasing the risk of injury and impairing normal responses.
5. Draw the structure of a neuron and explain its function.
ANSWERA neuron has dendrites, cell body and axon. Dendrites receive information, the cell body integrates it, and the axon carries the electrical impulse toward its ending, where chemical signals can cross a synapse to the next neuron.
6. How does phototropism occur in plants?
ANSWERWhen light falls from one side, auxin redistributes toward the shaded side of the shoot. In shoots, auxin promotes cell elongation. Greater growth on the shaded side causes the shoot to bend toward the light.
7. Which signals will get disrupted in case of a spinal cord injury?
ANSWERSignals travelling between the brain and body through the spinal cord may be disrupted. Depending on the location and severity, sensory information reaching the brain and motor commands travelling to muscles below the injury can be affected.
8. How does chemical coordination occur in plants?
ANSWERPlants use chemical messengers called hormones. Different plant hormones coordinate growth, development and responses. Auxins, gibberellins and cytokinins promote growth-related processes, while abscisic acid inhibits growth.
9. What is the need for a system of control and coordination in an organism?
ANSWEROrganisms encounter changing internal and external conditions. A control and coordination system allows them to detect stimuli, integrate information and produce suitable responses so that body activities remain organised and the organism can survive and function effectively.
10. How are involuntary actions and reflex actions different from each other?
ANSWERBoth are not consciously controlled, but a reflex action is a rapid, specific response to a stimulus, often coordinated through a reflex arc involving the spinal cord. Involuntary actions include automatic functions such as blood pressure regulation, salivation and vomiting, controlled by brain regions such as the medulla.
11. Compare and contrast nervous and hormonal mechanisms for control and coordination in animals.
ANSWERNervous coordination uses rapid electrical impulses through nervous tissue and is suited to quick, specific responses. Hormonal coordination uses chemical hormones released by endocrine glands and carried through the blood; it is generally slower, broader and more sustained. Both coordinate activities and maintain organised responses.
12. What is the difference between the manner in which movement takes place in a sensitive plant and the movement in our legs?
ANSWERSensitive-plant leaf movement after touch is rapid and does not involve growth; changes in water content cause cells to swell or shrink and change shape. Leg movement is produced by contraction of muscles under nervous control and is coordinated through the nervous system.

PYQ Practice

Board-style questions • reveal answers after thinking

1 MARK

Name the gap between two neurons.

Synapse.
1 MARK

Which part of the brain maintains posture and equilibrium?

Cerebellum.
1 MARK

Name a plant hormone that inhibits growth.

Abscisic acid (ABA).
2 MARKS

Why is reflex action faster than a voluntary response?

A reflex arc can be coordinated rapidly through the spinal cord, producing an immediate protective response without waiting for the brain to make the initial motor decision.
2 MARKS

Differentiate between phototropism and geotropism.

Phototropism is directional growth in response to light; geotropism/gravitropism is directional growth in response to gravity. Shoots generally show positive phototropism and negative geotropism, while roots generally show positive geotropism.
3 MARKS

Explain the role of auxin in phototropism.

One-sided light causes auxin redistribution toward the shaded side. Auxin promotes cell elongation in the shoot, so the shaded side grows more. This unequal growth bends the shoot toward light.
3 MARKS

Explain how adrenaline prepares the body for an emergency.

Adrenaline increases heart rate and breathing rate, increases blood supply to skeletal muscles and reduces blood flow to the digestive system and skin. Together these changes prepare the body for rapid action.
3 MARKS

How does a synapse help transmission of information between neurons?

An impulse reaching the axon ending causes chemicals to be released. These chemicals cross the synaptic gap and initiate a similar electrical impulse in the next neuron.
5 MARKS

Compare nervous and hormonal coordination in animals.

Nervous coordination uses electrical impulses through nervous tissue, is very rapid and is suited to precise immediate responses. Hormonal coordination uses chemical hormones released by endocrine glands and transported through body fluids, is generally slower and can produce widespread, sustained effects. Nervous control is especially important for rapid movement; hormones regulate growth, metabolism, blood sugar, stress and reproduction.
5 MARKS

Describe the organisation of the human nervous system and functions of major brain regions.

The CNS consists of brain and spinal cord; the PNS consists of nerves connecting the CNS to the rest of the body. The forebrain is the main thinking and interpretation centre and controls voluntary actions. Midbrain participates in certain involuntary responses. The hindbrain includes the medulla, which controls important involuntary functions, and the cerebellum, which maintains posture, balance and precision of voluntary actions.

Complete Revision Notes

All concepts • mnemonics • tips • traps • answer frameworks

① Chapter Core — The Master Model

  • Control and coordination allow an organism to detect changes, process information and produce appropriate responses.
  • Animals mainly use nervous and hormonal coordination.
  • Plants have no nervous system or muscles but respond using electrical/chemical signalling, changes in cell water balance and growth regulators.
  • Basic pathway: stimulus → receptor → coordinator → effector → response.
🧠 SRCR = Stimulus → Receptor → Coordinator → Response.

② Neuron — Complete Recall

  • Dendrites receive information.
  • Cell body contains nucleus and integrates signals.
  • Axon carries electrical impulse away from cell body.
  • Axon ending releases chemicals at the synapse.
  • Impulse direction: dendrite → cell body → axon → axon ending.
🧠 D-C-A = Dendrite → Cell body → Axon.
🎯 Draw a neuron with clear labels and an arrow showing impulse direction.

③ Synapse — What Happens?

  • Electrical impulse reaches axon ending.
  • Chemicals are released.
  • Chemicals cross the synaptic gap.
  • They trigger a similar electrical impulse in the next neuron.
🧠 “Electric → Chemical → Electric.”

④ Receptors

  • Receptors detect stimuli.
  • Sense organs contain specialised receptors.
  • Taste → gustatory receptors.
  • Smell → olfactory receptors.
  • Vision → light receptors in eye.
  • Hearing/balance → receptors in inner ear.
🧠 Receptor = Receiver.

⑤ Reflex Action

  • Rapid and automatic response to a stimulus.
  • Often protective.
  • Reflex arc can be coordinated through spinal cord.
  • Information also reaches the brain, allowing awareness of the event.
  • Sequence: receptor → sensory neuron → spinal cord → motor neuron → effector.
🧠 R-S-C-M-E = Receptor → Sensory → Coordinator → Motor → Effector.
🎯 Trap: do not write “reflexes are controlled only by the brain.” The immediate reflex arc can be coordinated through the spinal cord.

⑥ Reflex vs Voluntary vs Involuntary

  • Reflex: rapid, automatic response to a stimulus.
  • Voluntary: conscious, planned action such as writing or walking.
  • Involuntary: automatic body functions such as blood pressure regulation and salivation.
🧠 Reflex = React; Voluntary = Decide; Involuntary = Automatic.

⑦ CNS + PNS

  • CNS = brain + spinal cord.
  • PNS = nerves arising from brain and spinal cord.
  • CNS receives/integrates information and coordinates responses.
  • PNS connects the CNS with sensory receptors and effectors.
🧠 CNS = Control centre; PNS = Pathways.

⑧ Brain — High-Yield Functions

  • Forebrain: thinking, interpretation, decision-making and voluntary motor control.
  • Midbrain: participates in certain involuntary responses.
  • Medulla: involuntary functions such as blood pressure, salivation and vomiting.
  • Cerebellum: precision of voluntary actions, posture and balance/equilibrium.
🧠 “Fore thinks, Cerebellum balances, Medulla maintains.”

⑨ Brain & Spinal Cord Protection

  • Brain → skull/bony box + fluid cushioning.
  • Spinal cord → vertebral column/backbone.
🧠 Skull → Brain; Spine → Cord.

⑩ Plant Coordination — Two Movement Types

  • Growth-independent: fast; no growth required; Mimosa leaf folding after touch; changes in water/turgor cause cell-shape changes.
  • Growth-dependent: slow; caused by directional growth; shoot bending toward light and roots growing according to gravity/water.
🧠 Touch = Turgor; Tropism = Growth.

⑪ Tropisms — Complete List

  • Phototropism: response to light.
  • Geotropism/Gravitropism: response to gravity.
  • Hydrotropism: response to water.
  • Thigmotropism: response to touch/contact.
  • Positive = toward stimulus; negative = away from stimulus.
🧠 PGHT = Photo–Geo–Hydro–Thigmo.

⑫ Phototropism — Exam Answer in 4 Steps

  • Light comes from one side.
  • Auxin redistributes toward shaded side.
  • Auxin promotes elongation in shoot cells.
  • Shaded side grows more → shoot bends toward light.
🧠 “Auxin → Shade → Stretch → Sun.”
🎯 If asked “why does the shoot bend?”, always mention unequal growth, not merely “auxin causes bending”.

⑬ Thigmotropism — Tendril

  • Tendril touches support.
  • Growth becomes unequal on the two sides.
  • Side away from support grows faster.
  • Tendril curves and coils around support.
🧠 Touch → Unequal growth → Coil.

⑭ Plant Hormones — Memory Table

  • Auxin: growth/cell elongation; directional growth.
  • Gibberellin: promotes growth.
  • Cytokinin: promotes cell division.
  • Abscisic acid (ABA): inhibits growth; associated with wilting.
  • Ethylene: involved in fruit ripening.
🧠 “A-G-C grow; ABA brakes.”

⑮ Electrical vs Chemical Communication

  • Nervous signals are very fast and suited to immediate responses.
  • Electrical impulses travel through connected nervous tissue.
  • Chemical hormones are generally slower.
  • Hormones can reach widespread target tissues and produce longer-lasting effects.
🧠 Nerve = Now; Hormone = Longer.

⑯ Animal Hormones — Gland → Hormone → Function

  • Pituitary → growth hormone → stimulates growth.
  • Thyroid → thyroxine → metabolism/body growth.
  • Pancreas → insulin → blood sugar regulation.
  • Adrenal gland → adrenaline → emergency response.
  • Testes → testosterone → male sexual development.
  • Ovaries → ovarian hormones → female sexual development/menstrual cycle regulation.
  • Hypothalamus → releasing hormones → stimulate pituitary.
🧠 G-H-B-T = Gland → Hormone → Blood → Target.

⑰ Adrenaline — Emergency Response

  • Released by adrenal glands during fear/danger.
  • Heart rate increases.
  • Breathing rate increases.
  • More blood is directed to skeletal muscles.
  • Blood flow to digestive system and skin is reduced.
  • Overall effect: body is prepared for rapid action.
🧠 “Alarm → Heart & Air ↑ → Muscle Supply ↑.”

⑱ Thyroxine, Iodine & Goitre

  • Iodine is needed by thyroid gland to make thyroxine.
  • Thyroxine regulates metabolism important for body growth.
  • Iodine deficiency can impair thyroid hormone production and may cause thyroid enlargement/goitre.
  • Iodised salt is an important preventive dietary measure.
🧠 Iodine → Thyroid → Thyroxine → Metabolism.

⑲ Insulin & Blood Sugar Feedback

  • High blood sugar → pancreas produces more insulin.
  • Insulin helps regulate blood sugar.
  • As blood sugar falls → insulin secretion decreases.
  • This is a feedback mechanism.
  • Diabetes may require insulin treatment in some patients.
🧠 ↑ Sugar → ↑ Insulin → ↓ Sugar → ↓ Insulin.

⑳ Feedback Mechanism

  • Hormone secretion must be controlled in amount and timing.
  • A change in the internal condition can trigger a corrective response.
  • As the condition moves toward normal, the signal for further correction is reduced.
🧠 Change → Correct → Check.

  • Do not say hormones always act immediately; hormonal responses are generally slower.
  • Do not confuse insulin with thyroxine or adrenaline.
