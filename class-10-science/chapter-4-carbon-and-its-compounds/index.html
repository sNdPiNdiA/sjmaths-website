01

Why Carbon is So Important

Carbon is present in a huge variety of substances we use—food, clothes, medicines and fuels—and living structures are carbon based. The chapter therefore begins by asking why one element can make such a large variety of compounds.

Carbon at the centre of everyday compounds
The enormous variety is mainly explained by two NCERT keywords: tetravalency and catenation.
🧠 “4 + C” → 4 valency + C–C catenation = countless carbon compounds.
02

Carbon Electronic Configuration & Why it Shares Electrons

Carbon has atomic number 6, so its electronic configuration is 2,4. It has four electrons in its outermost shell and would need to gain or lose four electrons to reach a noble-gas configuration.

Carbon: 2 electrons in K shell, 4 in L shell

Why not C⁴⁻?

  • Nucleus has only 6 protons.
  • Holding 4 extra electrons is difficult.
  • So gaining four electrons is not favourable.

Why not C⁴⁺?

  • Removing four electrons requires a large amount of energy.
  • A carbon cation with only two electrons would result.
Carbon overcomes the problem by sharing valence electrons with carbon or other atoms. This produces covalent bonds.
✍ Board answer: “Carbon forms covalent compounds because it completes its outer shell by sharing electrons rather than by gaining or losing four electrons.”
03

Covalent Bond — What “Sharing” Actually Means

A covalent bond is formed when two atoms share an electron pair. The shared electrons count for the outer shells of both atoms, helping them attain a noble-gas configuration.

H₂: one shared pair = one single covalent bond

Single

1 shared pair

H—H

Double

2 shared pairs

O=O

Triple

3 shared pairs

N≡N
⭐ If asked for an electron-dot structure, show the shared electron pairs/lone pairs as required; a simple line formula alone is not the full dot structure.
04

Single, Double & Triple Bonds: Build Them Step by Step

Oxygen needs two more electrons, so two shared pairs form O₂. Nitrogen needs three more electrons, so three shared pairs form N₂. Hydrogen needs one more electron, so one shared pair forms H₂.

Double bond; two shared pairs.

N₂

N ≡ N

Triple bond; three shared pairs.

H₂

H — H

Single bond; one shared pair.

05

Methane — The Best Model for Carbon's Tetravalency

Methane is CH₄. Carbon has four valence electrons and hydrogen has valency 1. Carbon shares one electron with each of four hydrogen atoms, making four C—H covalent bonds.

CH₄: four C—H bonds around one carbon
Remember: “tetra” = four. Carbon's tetravalency means it can form four covalent bonds.
06

Why Covalent Compounds Usually Have Low Melting/Boiling Points & Poor Conductivity

The chapter compares carbon compounds with ionic compounds. Covalently bonded molecules have strong bonds within a molecule, but the forces between molecules are weak. Also, covalent bonding does not produce ions.

Inside vs between molecules
✍ In a 2–3 mark answer, connect bonding → ions/intermolecular forces → property. Do not merely state “low boiling point”.
07

Allotropes: Diamond, Graphite & Fullerene

Diamond and graphite are both forms of carbon, but their carbon atoms are bonded differently. Therefore their physical properties differ greatly.

Diamond

  • Each carbon bonded to four carbon atoms.
  • Rigid 3-D structure.
  • Hardest substance known in the source.

Graphite

  • Each carbon bonded to three in the same plane.
  • Hexagonal arrays form layers.
  • Smooth/slippery and conducts electricity.
Different structures → different physical properties
Also: C-60 fullerene is another class of carbon allotrope; the source describes its carbon atoms as arranged like a football.
08

Tetravalency + Catenation = Versatile Carbon

Catenation is carbon's ability to form bonds with other carbon atoms, producing long chains, branched chains and rings. Carbon-carbon bonds are strong and stable.

Three ways carbon skeletons can grow
Carbon can also make single, double or triple bonds between carbon atoms. That creates saturated and unsaturated compounds.
🧠 C-C-C = catenation; C₄ = tetravalency.
09

Saturated & Unsaturated Compounds — Build the Difference

Start by joining carbon atoms. In ethane, a single C—C bond leaves each carbon with three remaining valencies, which are satisfied by hydrogen. In ethene, a double C=C bond is needed to satisfy the valencies.

Ethane vs ethene vs ethyne
🧠 ANE = single, ENE = double, YNE = triple.
10

Hydrocarbons, Alkanes, Alkenes & Alkynes

Carbon compounds containing only carbon and hydrogen are hydrocarbons. Saturated hydrocarbons are alkanes. Unsaturated hydrocarbons with double bonds are alkenes; those with triple bonds are alkynes.

Family Bond Example General formula
Alkane C—C Ethane C₂H₆ CₙH₂ₙ₊₂
Alkene C=C Ethene C₂H₄ CₙH₂ₙ
Alkyne C≡C Ethyne C₂H₂ CₙH₂ₙ₋₂
✍ When identifying a hydrocarbon from its formula, first check the C:H pattern and then the presence/type of multiple bond.
11

Straight Chains, Branched Chains, Rings & Structural Isomers

Carbon skeletons can be straight, branched or cyclic. Two compounds can have the same molecular formula but different arrangements of atoms. Such compounds are called structural isomers.

C₄H₁₀ has two structural arrangements

Rings also occur. Cyclohexane has formula C₆H₁₂. Benzene is C₆H₆ and is an unsaturated cyclic compound shown in the chapter.

🧠 Isomer = “same formula, different arrangement.”
12

Functional Groups — The “Personality” of a Carbon Compound

Hydrogen in a carbon chain can be replaced by other elements/groups. The group attached to the carbon chain gives characteristic properties to the compound and is called a functional group.

Class Group Name ending/prefix Example
Haloalkane —Cl / —Br chloro-/bromo- Chloropropane
Alcohol —OH -ol Propanol
Aldehyde —CHO -al Propanal
Ketone >C=O -one Propanone
Carboxylic acid —COOH -oic acid Propanoic acid
Alkene C=C -ene Propene
Alkyne C≡C -yne Propyne
⭐ Functional group can determine the characteristic chemical behaviour even when the carbon chain length changes.
13

Homologous Series — Same Family, One CH₂ Step at a Time

A homologous series is a family of compounds in which the same functional group is attached to carbon chains of different lengths. Members have similar chemical properties.

Alcohol homologous series

Chemical properties

  • Similar because functional group is the same.
  • Functional group controls characteristic behaviour.

Physical properties

  • Gradual change as molecular mass increases.
  • Melting/boiling points generally increase.
🧠 HOMO = same family; LOGOUS = one step apart. Remember the exam keyword: CH₂ difference.
14

Nomenclature — A Reliable 4-Step Method

Names are built from the basic carbon chain plus a prefix/suffix showing the functional group or unsaturation.

Naming flow
propane − e + one = propanone
propane → propene     propane → propyne
✍ Source rule: if the suffix begins with a vowel, the final “e” of the carbon-chain name is deleted before adding the suffix, e.g. propanone.
15

Combustion — Carbon Compounds as Fuels

Carbon and most carbon compounds release a large amount of heat and light on burning. Complete combustion in sufficient oxygen produces carbon dioxide and water for compounds containing carbon and hydrogen.

C + O₂ → CO₂ + heat + light
CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O + heat + light
Why flame colour changes
A blackened cooking-vessel bottom indicates incomplete combustion; air holes may be blocked and fuel is being wasted.
16

Oxidation — Ethanol → Ethanoic Acid

Carbon compounds can be oxidised on combustion. Alcohols can also be converted into carboxylic acids. Alkaline potassium permanganate and acidified potassium dichromate are oxidising agents.

Oxidation pathway
CH₃CH₂OH → CH₃COOH
✍ “Oxidising agent” is the key term. The source specifically names alkaline KMnO₄ and acidified K₂Cr₂O₇.
17

Addition Reaction — Unsaturated → Saturated

Unsaturated hydrocarbons can add hydrogen across their multiple bond in the presence of catalysts such as nickel or palladium. The multiple bond is converted into a more saturated structure.

Hydrogenation
⭐ Recognition: if a multiple bond “takes in” another molecule, think addition.
18

Substitution Reaction — One Atom Takes Another's Place

Saturated hydrocarbons are comparatively unreactive. In sunlight, chlorine reacts rapidly with hydrocarbons and can replace hydrogen atoms one by one. Because one atom/group replaces another, it is called a substitution reaction.

CH₄ + Cl₂ → CH₃Cl + HCl    (sunlight)
Substitution visual
🧠 SUB = Swap → substitution means one atom/group swaps into the place of another.
19

Ethanol — Properties & Reaction with Sodium

Ethanol is a liquid at room temperature, is soluble in water in all proportions and is used as a solvent in medicines such as tincture iodine, cough syrups and tonics. The source also notes that pure ethanol can be lethal and long-term consumption causes health problems.

Ethanol + sodium
2Na + 2CH₃CH₂OH → 2CH₃CH₂O⁻Na⁺ + H₂
Observation: hydrogen gas evolves. The other product is sodium ethoxide.
20

Dehydration of Ethanol → Ethene

When ethanol is heated at 443 K with excess concentrated sulphuric acid, water is removed from ethanol and ethene is formed. Therefore concentrated sulphuric acid acts as a dehydrating agent.

CH₃CH₂OH → CH₂=CH₂ + H₂O    (hot conc. H₂SO₄, 443 K)
Remove H₂O from ethanol
⭐ 443 K + excess conc. H₂SO₄ + ethanol is a high-frequency board combination. Learn it as one unit.
21

Ethanoic Acid — Acetic Acid, Vinegar & Weak Acidity

Ethanoic acid is commonly called acetic acid and belongs to carboxylic acids. A 5–8% aqueous solution is vinegar and is used as a preservative in pickles. Carboxylic acids are weak acids compared with completely ionised mineral acids such as HCl.

Ethanoic acid identity map
Pure ethanoic acid freezes around 290 K, giving the name glacial acetic acid.
22

Ethanoic Acid + Base, Carbonate & Hydrogencarbonate

Ethanoic acid reacts with sodium hydroxide to form sodium ethanoate and water. With carbonates/hydrogencarbonates it forms a salt, carbon dioxide and water.

CH₃COOH + NaOH → CH₃COONa + H₂O
2CH₃COOH + Na₂CO₃ → 2CH₃COONa + H₂O + CO₂
CH₃COOH + NaHCO₃ → CH₃COONa + H₂O + CO₂
CO₂ identification
✍ For carbonate questions: write the equation + identify CO₂ + state the lime-water test if asked.
23

Esterification — Acid + Alcohol → Ester + Water

Esters are commonly formed when an acid reacts with an alcohol in the presence of an acid catalyst. Ethanoic acid reacts with ethanol to form an ester. Esters are generally sweet-smelling and are used in perfumes and flavouring agents.

Esterification reaction map
CH₃COOH + C₂H₅OH ⇌ ester + H₂O
Memory chain: acid + alcohol → ester. Sweet smell → perfumes/flavouring.
24

Saponification — Why Ester Hydrolysis is Connected to Soap

When an ester is treated with sodium hydroxide, it is converted back into an alcohol and the sodium salt of a carboxylic acid. The reaction is called saponification because it is used in soap preparation.

Ester → soap-related products
25

Soap Molecule — Two Ends, Two Jobs

Soap molecules are sodium or potassium salts of long-chain carboxylic acids. One end is hydrophilic and interacts with water; the hydrocarbon end is hydrophobic and interacts with hydrocarbons/oil.

Soap molecule
🧠 HEAD = H₂O → hydrophilic head likes water. TAIL = TRAIL of oil → hydrophobic tail associates with oil.
26

Micelle Formation — The Cleaning Mechanism

Most dirt is oily and oil does not dissolve in water. Soap molecules arrange around an oil droplet so that hydrophobic tails point inward toward the oil while ionic ends face the surrounding water. This cluster is a micelle.

Micelle: tails in, ionic heads out
The micelle keeps oily dirt suspended in water as a colloid. Ion-ion repulsion helps prevent micelles from precipitating, so the dirt can be rinsed away.
✍ 5-mark answer structure: oil insoluble in water → two-ended soap molecule → micelle orientation → emulsification/suspension → rinsing.
27

Soap vs Detergent — Why Hard Water Matters

Calcium and magnesium salts cause hardness of water. Soap reacts with these ions and forms an insoluble curdy precipitate called scum, reducing cleaning efficiency. Detergents remain effective because their charged ends do not form insoluble precipitates with Ca²⁺ and Mg²⁺ in hard water.

Soap + hard water

  • Ca²⁺/Mg²⁺ present.
  • Insoluble scum forms.
  • Foam formation is reduced.
  • More soap may be needed.

Detergent + hard water

  • Does not form the same insoluble precipitate.
  • Remains effective in hard water.
  • Used in shampoos and clothes-cleaning products.
Hard-water comparison

NCERT Exercises — Questions + Answers

All 15 end-of-chapter questions

1. Ethane, with molecular formula C₂H₆ has: (a) 6 covalent bonds (b) 7 (c) 8 (d) 9.
ANSWER(b) 7 covalent bonds. There are six C—H bonds and one C—C bond.
2. Butanone is a four-carbon compound with which functional group?
ANSWERKetone. The suffix “-one” identifies a ketone.
3. While cooking, if the bottom of the vessel is getting blackened on the outside, what does it mean?
ANSWERThe fuel is not burning completely. Incomplete combustion produces soot; insufficient air can cause this.
4. Explain the nature of the covalent bond using bond formation in CH₃Cl.
ANSWERCarbon shares electrons with three hydrogen atoms and one chlorine atom. These shared electron pairs form covalent bonds. Sharing allows the outer shells of the bonded atoms to attain stable configurations. No ions are formed by simple electron transfer.
5. Draw electron-dot structures for (a) ethanoic acid (b) H₂S (c) propanone (d) F₂.
ANSWER
Ethanoic acid: CH₃—C(=O)—OH
H₂S: H—S—H
Propanone: CH₃—C(=O)—CH₃
F₂: F—F

In a full electron-dot drawing, show the relevant shared and lone electron pairs.

6. What is a homologous series? Explain with an example.
ANSWERA family of compounds having the same functional group and similar chemical properties, with successive members differing by a —CH₂— unit. Example: methanol, ethanol, propanol and butanol.
7. How can ethanol and ethanoic acid be differentiated on the basis of physical and chemical properties?
ANSWEREthanol is an alcohol; ethanoic acid is a carboxylic acid and is acidic. Ethanoic acid reacts with sodium hydrogencarbonate/carbonate to release CO₂, while ethanol does not give this characteristic reaction. Ethanoic acid also has a characteristic acidic behaviour; ethanol is not a carboxylic acid.
8. Why does micelle formation take place when soap is added to water? Will a micelle be formed in ethanol also?
ANSWERSoap has a hydrophilic ionic end and a hydrophobic hydrocarbon end. In water, the tails cluster inward around oily material while the ionic ends face water, producing a micelle. The behaviour depends on the solvent, so the usual water-based micelle arrangement should not simply be assumed for ethanol.
9. Why are carbon and its compounds used as fuels for most applications?
ANSWERMost carbon compounds release a large amount of heat and light on burning, making them useful sources of energy. Many commonly used fuels are carbon or carbon compounds.
10. Explain formation of scum when hard water is treated with soap.
ANSWERHard water contains calcium and magnesium salts. Ca²⁺/Mg²⁺ react with soap and form insoluble salts, producing a white/curdy precipitate called scum. This reduces the effectiveness of soap.
11. What change will you observe if you test soap with litmus paper (red and blue)?
ANSWERSoap solution is alkaline. It turns red litmus blue; blue litmus does not show a corresponding change.
12. What is hydrogenation? What is its industrial application?
ANSWERHydrogenation is addition of hydrogen to an unsaturated compound in the presence of a catalyst such as nickel or palladium. It is used industrially in the hydrogenation of vegetable oils.
13. Which hydrocarbons undergo addition reactions: C₂H₆, C₃H₈, C₃H₆, C₂H₂ and CH₄?
ANSWERC₃H₆ and C₂H₂. They are unsaturated hydrocarbons and contain multiple bonds.
14. Give a test to differentiate saturated and unsaturated hydrocarbons.
ANSWERUse bromine water as a test for unsaturation: an unsaturated hydrocarbon decolourises bromine water under the standard test conditions, whereas a saturated hydrocarbon does not readily do so.
15. Explain the mechanism of the cleaning action of soaps.
ANSWERSoap has hydrophobic hydrocarbon tails and hydrophilic ionic ends. The tail interacts with oily dirt, while the ionic end interacts with water. Soap molecules arrange into micelles with dirt in the centre, suspend/emulsify it in water, and allow the dirt to be rinsed away.
NCERT writing tip: For structure/reaction questions, do not stop at the final formula. Show the structure/equation and add the key condition or reason where the question asks “explain”.

PYQ Practice

Board-style chapter questions • answers reveal on demand

1 MARK

Why does carbon form covalent bonds?

Give the reason in one precise sentence.

Answer: Carbon has four valence electrons and cannot easily gain or lose four electrons, so it completes its outer shell mainly by sharing electrons.
2 MARKS

State and explain the two properties responsible for carbon's versatility.

Use the exact keywords.

Answer: Tetravalency and catenation. Carbon forms four covalent bonds and can form strong C—C chains, branches and rings.
2 MARKS

Differentiate saturated and unsaturated carbon compounds.

Answer: Saturated compounds have only single C—C bonds; unsaturated compounds have C=C or C≡C bonds.
3 MARKS

Why do diamond and graphite have different physical properties though both are carbon?

Answer: Their carbon atoms are bonded differently. Diamond has a rigid three-dimensional structure with each carbon bonded to four others; graphite has layered hexagonal arrays with each carbon bonded to three others. Hence their physical properties differ.
3 MARKS

Explain homologous series with an example and state the CH₂ relation.

Answer: A homologous series contains compounds with the same functional group and similar chemical properties. Successive members differ by CH₂. Example: CH₃OH, C₂H₅OH, C₃H₇OH.
3 MARKS

Write the reaction of ethanol with sodium and identify the gas evolved.

2Na + 2CH₃CH₂OH → 2CH₃CH₂O⁻Na⁺ + H₂
Hydrogen gas is evolved; sodium ethoxide is formed.
3 MARKS

What happens when ethanol is heated with concentrated H₂SO₄ at 443 K?

CH₃CH₂OH → CH₂=CH₂ + H₂O
Concentrated H₂SO₄ acts as a dehydrating agent and removes water.
3 MARKS

Explain addition and substitution reactions with one example each.

Addition: unsaturated compound adds hydrogen, e.g. C₂H₄ + H₂ → C₂H₆ in presence of Ni/Pd. Substitution: one atom/group replaces another, e.g. CH₄ + Cl₂ → CH₃Cl + HCl in sunlight.
5 MARKS

Explain the cleansing action of soap with a labelled micelle diagram.

Answer structure: (1) oil does not dissolve in water; (2) soap has hydrophobic tail + hydrophilic ionic head; (3) tails point toward oil; (4) heads face water; (5) micelles suspend/emulsify dirt so it can be rinsed away.

Rapid Revision

5–10 minute pre-exam recall

① Carbon Basics

  • Atomic number = 6; configuration = 2,4.
  • Four valence electrons → tetravalency.
  • Shares electrons → covalent bonds.
  • Covalent compounds generally have weak intermolecular forces and no ions.

② Why Carbon is Versatile

  • Tetravalency.
  • Catenation.
  • Strong C—C bonds.
  • Chains, branches, rings.
  • Single, double and triple C—C bonds.

③ Hydrocarbons

  • Hydrocarbon = carbon + hydrogen only.
  • Alkane = saturated.
  • Alkene = C=C.
  • Alkyne = C≡C.
  • Structural isomers = same formula, different structure.

④ Functional Groups & Naming

  • —OH → alcohol → -ol.
  • —CHO → aldehyde → -al.
  • >C=O → ketone → -one.
  • —COOH → carboxylic acid → -oic acid.
  • C=C → -ene; C≡C → -yne.

⑤ Homologous Series

  • Same functional group.
  • Similar chemical properties.
  • Successive members differ by CH₂.
  • Physical properties show gradual change with molecular mass.

⑥ Reaction Box

  • Combustion → CO₂ + H₂O + heat/light for complete combustion of hydrocarbons.
  • Oxidation → alcohol → carboxylic acid; KMnO₄/K₂Cr₂O₇ as oxidising agents.
  • Addition → unsaturated + H₂ → saturated.
  • Substitution → one atom/group replaces another.

⑦ Ethanol

  • 2Na + 2C₂H₅OH → 2C₂H₅ONa + H₂.
  • 443 K + excess conc. H₂SO₄ → ethene + water.
  • H₂SO₄ acts as dehydrating agent in the latter reaction.

⑧ Ethanoic Acid

  • Acetic acid; 5–8% aqueous solution = vinegar.
  • Weak carboxylic acid.
  • With NaOH → sodium ethanoate + water.
  • With Na₂CO₃/NaHCO₃ → salt + CO₂ + water.
  • CO₂ turns lime-water milky.

⑨ Soap & Detergent

  • Soap = sodium/potassium salts of long-chain carboxylic acids.
  • Hydrophilic head + hydrophobic tail.
  • Micelle traps oily dirt.
  • Hard water + soap → Ca/Mg scum.
  • Detergents remain effective in hard water.
🧠 Final memory chain: C = 4 → Catenation → Hydrocarbons → Functional groups → Homologous series → Naming → COAS reactions → Ethanol/Ethanoic acid → Soap/Micelles.
