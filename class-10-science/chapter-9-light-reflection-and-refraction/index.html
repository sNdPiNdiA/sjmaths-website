Worked Examples

Examples 9.1–9.4

The numerical examples printed in the chapter are expanded here into a consistent sequence: given values, sign convention, formula, substitution, calculation and interpretation.

Example 9.1

Convex mirror used as a rear-view mirror

Question: A convex mirror used for rear-view on an automobile has a radius of curvature of 3.00 m. If a bus is located at 5.00 m from this mirror, find the position, nature and size of the image.

1. Given: R = +3.00 m, u = −5.00 m, v = ?, h′ = ?.
2. Focal length: For a spherical mirror of small aperture, R = 2f.
f = R/2 = 3.00/2 = +1.50 m
3. Mirror formula:
1/v + 1/u = 1/f
1/v − 1/5.00 = 1/1.50
1/v = 1/1.50 + 1/5.00
v = +1.15 m (approximately)
4. Magnification:
m = −v/u = −(+1.15)/(−5.00) ≈ +0.23
5. Interpretation: The positive v places the image behind the mirror. The positive magnification indicates a virtual, erect image. Its size is 0.23 times the object size, so it is diminished.
Example 9.2

Concave mirror: screen position, nature and size

Question: An object 4.0 cm in size is placed at 25.0 cm in front of a concave mirror of focal length 15.0 cm. At what distance from the mirror should a screen be placed to obtain a sharp image? Find the nature and size of the image.

1. Given: h = +4.0 cm, u = −25.0 cm, f = −15.0 cm.
2. Mirror formula:
1/v + 1/u = 1/f
1/v = 1/f − 1/u
1/v = −1/15 − (−1/25)
1/v = −1/15 + 1/25
1/v = −2/75
v = −37.5 cm

Therefore the screen should be placed 37.5 cm in front of the mirror.

3. Magnification:
m = −v/u = −(−37.5)/(−25.0) = −1.5
4. Image height:
h′ = mh = (−1.5)(4.0) = −6.0 cm
5. Interpretation: The negative image distance gives a real image in front of the mirror. Negative magnification means inverted; magnitude 1.5 means enlarged. Image size = 6.0 cm.
Example 9.3

Concave lens: object distance and magnification

Question: A concave lens has focal length of 15 cm. At what distance should the object be placed so that it forms an image at 10 cm from the lens? Also find the magnification.

1. Assign signs: A concave lens forms a virtual image on the same side as the object, so v = −10 cm. Also f = −15 cm.
2. Lens formula:
1/v − 1/u = 1/f
−1/10 − 1/u = −1/15
−1/u = 1/30
u = −30 cm
3. Magnification:
m = v/u = (−10)/(−30) = +1/3 ≈ +0.33
4. Interpretation: The object is 30 cm in front of the lens. Positive magnification means a virtual, erect image. The image is about one-third the object size.
Example 9.4

Convex lens: complete image calculation

Question: A 2.0 cm tall object is placed perpendicular to the principal axis of a convex lens of focal length 10 cm. The object is 15 cm from the lens. Find the nature, position and size of the image. Also find the magnification.

1. Given: h = +2.0 cm, f = +10 cm, u = −15 cm.
2. Lens formula:
1/v − 1/u = 1/f
1/v + 1/15 = 1/10
1/v = 1/30
v = +30 cm
3. Magnification:
m = v/u = (+30)/(−15) = −2
4. Image height:
h′ = mh = (−2)(2.0) = −4.0 cm
5. Interpretation: The image is 30 cm from the lens on the other side. Negative magnification and image height show that it is real and inverted. Its size is 4 cm, twice the object size.
01

Light and straight-line propagation

Light makes objects visible when reflected light reaches our eyes. In the chapter's ray treatment, light is taken to travel in straight lines in a transparent medium.

How we see an object
  • This is why an object cannot be seen in a completely dark room.
  • The straight-ray model is useful here, although the chapter notes that it does not describe diffraction.
02

Laws of reflection

For reflection, angle of incidence equals angle of reflection. The incident ray, reflected ray and normal at the point of incidence lie in the same plane.

Reflection at a plane surface
  • Remember that both angles are measured from the normal..
  • The normal is the reference line for both angles.
03

Plane mirror image

A plane mirror forms an image that is virtual, erect, equal in size to the object, equally distant behind the mirror, and laterally inverted.

Plane mirror image properties
  • Thus a plane-mirror image is virtual and erect, equal in size, equally distant behind the mirror and laterally inverted.
04

Spherical mirrors: concave and convex

A spherical mirror is part of a sphere. Concave mirrors curve inward; convex mirrors curve outward. The chapter uses the spoon as a simple everyday analogy.

Concave vs convex mirror
  • Concave surfaces curve inward; convex surfaces bulge outward.
05

Pole, centre of curvature, radius, principal axis

Pole P is the centre of the reflecting surface. Centre of curvature C is the centre of the sphere. Radius of curvature R is PC. The line through P and C is the principal axis.

Spherical mirror vocabulary
  • For a spherical mirror of small aperture, the focal length is half the radius of curvature.
06

Principal focus and focal length

Parallel rays incident on a concave mirror meet at F. For a convex mirror, reflected rays appear to come from F. The focal length is the distance PF.

Parallel rays and the principal focus — concave mirror
  • For a concave mirror the parallel rays actually meet at F; for a convex mirror they appear to come from F.
  • The relation R = 2f is used throughout the numerical questions on spherical mirrors.
07

Four standard mirror rays

Ray diagrams become manageable when you use any two standard rays: parallel-to-axis ray, focus ray, centre-of-curvature ray, or pole ray.

Parallel ray
  • A ray parallel to the principal axis is directed towards the principal focus after reflection, with the corresponding apparent behaviour for a convex mirror.
  • A ray through the principal focus emerges parallel to the principal axis after reflection.
  • A ray through the centre of curvature is normally incident on the spherical surface and returns along the same path.
  • A ray striking the pole follows the ordinary law of reflection.
08

Concave mirror image formation

The nature and size of the image depend on the object's position relative to P, F and C. A real image forms on a screen; a virtual image does not.

Concave mirror: image formation map
  • When the object is at F, the reflected rays are parallel and the image is effectively at infinity.
  • Placing the object between P and F gives a virtual, erect and enlarged image behind the mirror.
09

Uses of spherical mirrors

Concave mirrors can make powerful parallel beams, enlarge nearby objects, and concentrate sunlight. Convex mirrors give erect, diminished images with a wide field of view.

Mirror use → reason
  • The use follows directly from the way the mirror forms the image or directs the light.
10

New Cartesian sign convention for mirrors

Take P as origin, principal axis as x-axis. Left of P is negative, right of P positive; above axis is positive, below axis negative.

New Cartesian sign convention
  • With the object placed to the left of P, the object distance is negative.
  • With this convention, f is negative for a concave mirror and positive for a convex mirror.
11

Mirror formula and magnification

Use the mirror formula for spherical mirrors: 1/v + 1/u = 1/f. Magnification is m = h′/h = −v/u.

Mirror formula and magnification
  • In a numerical problem, assign the signs first and then substitute them in the formula.
  • m < 0 indicates a real, inverted image; m > 0 indicates a virtual, erect image.
Worked example

Object 20 cm in front of a concave mirror

An object is placed 20 cm in front of a concave mirror of focal length 15 cm. Using the new Cartesian convention, take u = −20 cm and f = −15 cm.

1/v + 1/u = 1/f
1/v − 1/20 = −1/15
1/v = −1/60
v = −60 cm

The negative value of v places the image in front of the mirror. The image is therefore real and inverted; its magnification is m = −v/u = −3, so it is three times the object's size.

12

Refraction of light

When light travels obliquely from one transparent medium to another, its direction changes. This bending is called refraction.

Refraction: rarer to denser medium
  • A ray bends towards the normal on entering an optically denser medium and away from the normal on entering an optically rarer medium.
  • The change in direction is associated with the change in speed of light from one medium to another.
13

Refraction through a rectangular glass slab

At the first surface the ray bends towards the normal; at the second surface it bends away. Because the faces are parallel, the emergent ray is parallel to the incident ray, but laterally shifted.

Rectangular glass slab — parallel emergence and lateral shift
  • The opposite bends at the two parallel faces leave the emergent ray parallel to the incident ray, but displaced sideways.
  • At normal incidence there is no change in direction at the interface.
14

Laws of refraction and Snell's law

Incident ray, refracted ray and normal lie in the same plane. For a given colour and a given pair of media, sin i / sin r is constant.

Snell's law
  • The angles are measured with respect to the normal before applying Snell's law.
  • This constant is the refractive index of the second medium with respect to the first.
15

Refractive index and optical density

Relative refractive index compares speeds. Absolute refractive index is n = c/v when the first medium is air or vacuum. Higher refractive index means optically denser medium, not necessarily greater mass density.

Refractive index: speed and optical density
  • Optical density should not be confused with mass density.
  • The numerical table in the chapter provides refractive indices for common materials.
Worked example

Refractive index: water

The chapter gives the refractive index of water as about 1.33. Using n = c/v:

v = c/n = (3 × 10⁸)/1.33 ≈ 2.26 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹

The result is lower than the speed of light in vacuum, which is why the ray changes direction when it enters water obliquely.

16

Convex and concave lenses

A lens is a transparent material bounded by two surfaces, at least one of which is spherical. Convex lenses are thicker at the centre and converge rays; concave lenses are thicker at the edges and diverge rays.

Convex and concave lenses
  • A convex lens converges light rays, whereas a concave lens diverges them.
  • These two names are often used directly in short-answer questions.
17

Lens terms and principal foci

A lens has two centres of curvature C₁ and C₂, principal axis, optical centre O and two principal foci F₁ and F₂. The focal length is the distance from O to a principal focus.

Thin lens vocabulary
  • A ray through the optical centre is taken to pass without deviation in the thin-lens approximation.
18

Image formation by a convex lens

The convex-lens image depends on object position relative to F₁ and 2F₁. The image can be real/inverted or virtual/erect, and can be diminished, same-size or enlarged.

Convex lens: image formation map
  • When the object is at 2F₁, the image is formed at 2F₂ with the same size.
  • Between F₁ and O, the image is virtual, erect and enlarged on the same side of the lens as the object.
Worked example

Convex lens: locating a real image

An object is placed 15 cm in front of a convex lens of focal length 10 cm. Take u = −15 cm and f = +10 cm.

1/v − 1/u = 1/f
1/v + 1/15 = 1/10
1/v = 1/30
v = +30 cm

The positive image distance places the image on the opposite side of the lens. The image is real and inverted, and its magnification is m = v/u = −2, so it is twice the object's size.

19

Concave lens image formation

A concave lens always forms a virtual, erect and diminished image, irrespective of object position.

Concave lens: always virtual, erect, diminished
  • A concave lens forms a virtual, erect and diminished image for all object positions considered here.
20

Lens ray rules

For lens ray diagrams, use two convenient standard rays: parallel ray, focus-directed ray, and optical-centre ray.

Three standard rays used for lens ray diagrams
  • Any two suitable standard rays are sufficient to locate the image.
  • A neat diagram with the relevant rays and labels is sufficient.
21

Lens formula and magnification

For a spherical lens: 1/v − 1/u = 1/f. Magnification m = h′/h = v/u using the chapter's sign convention.

Lens formula and magnification
  • Under the lens sign convention, the focal length of a convex lens is positive and that of a concave lens is negative.
  • After obtaining the numerical value, state what the sign and magnitude tell you about the image.
22

Power of a lens

Power measures the degree of convergence or divergence. P = 1/f when f is in metres. The SI unit is dioptre (D). Convex lens → positive power; concave lens → negative power.

Power of a lens
  • One dioptre is the power of a lens whose focal length is one metre.
  • The magnitude of power indicates how strongly the lens converges or diverges light.
Worked example

Power from focal length

A lens has focal length f = −0.50 m.

P = 1/f = 1/(−0.50) = −2 D

The negative power tells us that the lens is concave and therefore diverging.

9.2 — Spherical mirrors

Define the principal focus of a concave mirror.

Answer: It is the point on the principal axis where rays parallel to the principal axis meet after reflection from a concave mirror.

The radius of curvature of a spherical mirror is 20 cm. What is its focal length?

Solution: R = 20 cm.
Solution: R = 2f.
Solution: f = 20/2 = 10 cm.
Answer: 10 cm.

Name a mirror that can give an erect and enlarged image of an object.

Answer: A concave mirror, when the object is between P and F.

Why do we prefer a convex mirror as a rear-view mirror in vehicles?

First: It gives an erect image.
Second: The image is diminished.
Third: Its outward curvature gives a wider field of view.
Therefore: the driver can see a larger area behind the vehicle.

9.2.4 — Mirror formula and magnification

Find the focal length of a convex mirror whose radius of curvature is 32 cm.

Solution: R = +32 cm.
Solution: f = R/2 = 16 cm.
Answer: +16 cm.

A concave mirror produces three times magnified real image of an object placed at 10 cm in front of it. Where is the image located?

Solution: Real enlarged image ⇒ m = −3.
Solution: Object in front ⇒ u = −10 cm.
Solution: m = −v/u.
Solution: −3 = −v/(−10) = v/10.
Solution: v = −30 cm.
Answer: Image is 30 cm in front of the mirror.

9.3 — Refraction and refractive index

A ray of light travelling in air enters obliquely into water. Does it bend towards the normal or away from the normal? Why?

Answer: Towards the normal. Water is optically denser than air; the speed of light decreases on entering it.

Light enters from air to glass having refractive index 1.50. What is the speed of light in the glass? The speed of light in vacuum is 3 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹.

Solution: n = c/v.
Solution: 1.50 = (3 × 10⁸)/v.
Solution: v = 2 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹.
Answer: 2 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹.

Find from Table 9.3 the medium having highest and lowest optical density.

Solution: Larger refractive index means optically denser.
Solution: Highest listed n = 2.42 for diamond.
Solution: Lowest listed n = 1.0003 for air.
Answer: Diamond highest; air lowest.

Given kerosene, turpentine and water, in which does light travel fastest?

Solution: Water n = 1.33, kerosene n = 1.44, turpentine n = 1.47.
Solution: Smaller n means greater speed.
Answer: Water.

The refractive index of diamond is 2.42. What does this statement mean?

Meaning: The ratio of the speed of light in air/vacuum to the speed in diamond is 2.42, i.e. c/v = 2.42.

9.3.8 — Power of a lens

Define 1 dioptre of power of a lens.

Answer: A lens with focal length 1 metre has power 1 dioptre. 1 D = 1 m⁻¹.

A convex lens forms a real and inverted image of a needle at a distance of 50 cm from it. Where is the needle placed if the image is equal to the size of the object? Also find the power.

Solution: Equal real image with convex lens occurs at 2F when object is at 2F.
Solution: 2f = 50 cm, so f = 25 cm = 0.25 m.
Solution: Object is 50 cm in front of the lens.
Solution: P = 1/f = 1/0.25 = +4 D.
Answer: Object at 50 cm; power +4 D.

Find the power of a concave lens of focal length 2 m.

Solution: Concave lens ⇒ f = −2 m.
Solution: P = 1/f = −0.5 D.
Answer: −0.5 D.

What you have learnt

Chapter summary

  • Light seems to travel in straight lines.
  • Mirrors and lenses form real or virtual images depending on object position.
  • Reflecting surfaces obey the laws of reflection and refracting surfaces obey the laws of refraction.
  • New Cartesian sign conventions are used for spherical mirrors and lenses.
  • Mirror formula: 1/v + 1/u = 1/f.
  • For a small-aperture spherical mirror, f = R/2.
  • Mirror magnification is m = h′/h = −v/u.
  • From rarer to denser medium, light bends towards the normal; from denser to rarer, it bends away.
  • Light travels fastest in vacuum at about 3 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹; its speed changes in different media.
  • Absolute refractive index is the ratio of the speed in vacuum/air to the speed in the medium.
  • In a rectangular glass slab, the emergent ray is parallel to the incident ray but shifted sideways.
  • Lens formula: 1/v − 1/u = 1/f.
  • Power of a lens is the reciprocal of focal length and is measured in dioptres.
Revision

Revision

01

Mirror map

Concave: ∞→F, beyond C→F–C, C→C, C–F→beyond C, F→∞, P–F→behind mirror.

Virtual + erect + enlarged means the object is between P and F.
02

Convex mirror

Always virtual, erect and diminished; image lies behind mirror between P and F.

Rear-view mirror = convex because of erect, diminished image + wide field of view.
03

Glass slab

Emergent ray ∥ incident ray; lateral displacement occurs.

At first surface: towards normal. At second: away from normal.
04

Refraction

Rarer→denser: slower + towards normal. Denser→rarer: faster + away from normal.

Optical density follows refractive index, not mass density.
05

Convex lens

At 2F → same-size; between F and 2F → enlarged; between O and F → virtual, erect, enlarged.

Concave lens always gives virtual, erect, diminished.
06

Formula discipline

Mirror: 1/v + 1/u = 1/f. Lens: 1/v − 1/u = 1/f. Power: P = 1/f.

Keep units consistent; use metres for power in dioptres.
07

Magnification

Mirror: m = −v/u. Lens: m = v/u. Also m = h′/h.

Negative image height/magnification indicates inversion in the chapter’s sign convention.
08

Ray-diagram rule

Use two standard rays only: parallel, focus-directed, centre/optical-centre.

A clean diagram with labelled P, F, C or F₁, O, F₂ is safer than an overcrowded sketch.

Basic

10 questions

BASIC • Q1

What is the SI unit of power of a lens?

Answer

Dioptre (D).

BASIC • Q2

What is the relation between R and f for a spherical mirror of small aperture?

Answer

R = 2f.

BASIC • Q3

Which mirror always gives an erect and diminished image?

Answer

Convex mirror.

BASIC • Q4

Which lens is converging?

Answer

Convex lens.

BASIC • Q5

State the two laws of reflection.

Answer

i = r; incident ray, reflected ray and normal lie in the same plane.

BASIC • Q6

What happens to a ray entering a denser medium from a rarer medium?

Answer

It bends towards the normal and slows down.

BASIC • Q7

What is the refractive index formula in terms of c and v?

Answer

n = c/v.

BASIC • Q8

What is the image nature of a concave lens?

Answer

Virtual, erect and diminished.

BASIC • Q9

Write the lens formula.

Answer

1/v − 1/u = 1/f.

BASIC • Q10

A plane mirror has m = +1. What does it mean?

Answer

Virtual, erect image of same size.

Standard

10 questions

STANDARD • Q1

An object is at C of a concave mirror. State image position, size and nature.

Answer

At C; same size; real and inverted.

STANDARD • Q2

Why is a convex mirror preferred as a rear-view mirror?

Answer

It gives an erect, diminished image and a wider field of view.

STANDARD • Q3

A convex mirror has R = 20 cm. Find f using the chapter relation.

Answer

f = R/2 = +10 cm.

STANDARD • Q4

Why does a glass slab give a lateral shift but a parallel emergent ray?

Answer

Bending at two parallel faces is equal and opposite, so the emergent ray is parallel but displaced.

STANDARD • Q5

A lens has f = +0.5 m. Find its power and type.

Answer

P = +2 D; convex/converging.

STANDARD • Q6

If m = −2 for a mirror, what does the sign tell you?

Answer

Image is real and inverted; magnitude 2 means twice the object height.

STANDARD • Q7

A ray through the optical centre of a thin lens behaves how?

Answer

It emerges without deviation.

STANDARD • Q8

Which position of a convex lens object gives same-size real image?

Answer

At 2F₁.

STANDARD • Q9

Why does a concave lens not form a real image on a screen for an ordinary object?

Answer

It always forms a virtual, erect and diminished image.

STANDARD • Q10

What is the physical cause of refraction highlighted in the chapter?

Answer

Change in speed of light as it enters another transparent medium.

Advanced

10 questions

ADVANCED • Q1

A concave mirror forms a real image at 30 cm for an object at 20 cm. What formula should be applied first?

Answer

Mirror formula: 1/v + 1/u = 1/f with the correct signs.

ADVANCED • Q2

A convex lens has f = 10 cm and u = −15 cm. State the expected nature of image from the table/section.

Answer

Real, inverted and enlarged.

ADVANCED • Q3

If n = 1.5 and c = 3×10⁸ m/s, find v from n = c/v.

Answer

v = 2×10⁸ m/s.

ADVANCED • Q4

A mirror gives m = +0.4. What can you conclude about image nature?

Answer

Virtual and erect; size is 0.4 times the object.

ADVANCED • Q5

Why can a covered half of a convex lens still form a complete image?

Answer

Each portion of the lens forms the complete image, but covering part reduces intensity.

ADVANCED • Q6

For a concave mirror, why does a ray through C retrace its path?

Answer

It falls normally on the spherical surface at that point.

ADVANCED • Q7

A lens has power −2 D. Find focal length and lens type.

Answer

f = −0.5 m; concave/diverging.

ADVANCED • Q8

Which is optically denser: water (1.33) or crown glass (1.52)?

Answer

Crown glass, because it has the higher refractive index.

ADVANCED • Q9

If light passes normally into a second medium, what happens to its direction?

Answer

It does not deviate; its speed can still change.

ADVANCED • Q10

State the two most important formula pairs to memorise for this chapter.

Answer

Mirror: 1/v + 1/u = 1/f, m = −v/u. Lens: 1/v − 1/u = 1/f, m = v/u.
