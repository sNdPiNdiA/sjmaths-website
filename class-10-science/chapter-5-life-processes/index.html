Concepts & Visual Theory

Complete chapter progression • detailed explanations • diagrams

01

What are Life Processes?

Living organisms constantly maintain an organised internal structure. Even while sleeping, cells need energy, materials and repair. The maintenance functions that keep an organism alive are called life processes.

The four core life processes
Life-process chain: obtain materials → release/use energy → distribute materials → remove wastes.
🧠 N-R-T-E = Nutrition → Respiration → Transportation → Excretion.
02

Why Diffusion Alone is Not Enough in Humans

A unicellular organism is small enough for its whole surface to contact the environment. In a large multicellular organism, many cells are far from the external environment, so diffusion would be too slow to supply oxygen and remove wastes.

Small body vs large body
✍ High-frequency reasoning: large body → cells not directly exposed → diffusion path becomes long → specialised transport system needed.
03

Nutrition — Autotrophic vs Heterotrophic

Nutrition provides energy and raw materials. Autotrophs make complex organic food from simple inorganic materials; heterotrophs obtain complex food directly or indirectly from other organisms.

Autotrophic

  • CO₂ + water are raw materials.
  • Sunlight provides energy in photosynthesis.
  • Chlorophyll captures light.
  • Green plants are major examples.

Heterotrophic

  • Food is obtained from other organisms.
  • Complex food may need enzymatic breakdown.
  • Animals and fungi are examples.
  • Parasitic strategies also occur.
🧠 AUTO = “self-food”; HETERO = “other-food”.
04

Photosynthesis — The Complete Input → Process → Output Chain

Photosynthesis converts carbon dioxide and water into carbohydrates using light energy captured by chlorophyll. Oxygen is released as a by-product. Unused carbohydrate may be stored as starch.

CO₂ + H₂O → C₆H₁₂O₆ + O₂ sunlight + chlorophyll
Photosynthesis map
Three essential events: light absorption by chlorophyll → light energy converted to chemical energy and water split → CO₂ reduced to carbohydrates.
🧠 “Light → Split → Build”: absorb light → split water → build carbohydrate from CO₂.
05

Stomata & Guard Cells — Gas Exchange with Water Control

Stomata are tiny pores on leaves through which gases move. Guard cells control opening and closing. When guard cells take in water and swell, the pore opens; when they lose water and shrink, the pore closes.

Open vs closed stomatal pore
⭐ Stomata solve a trade-off: CO₂ entry for photosynthesis vs water loss by transpiration.
06

Raw Materials for Photosynthesis & Their Sources

Requirement Source Role
CO₂ Air through stomata Carbon source for carbohydrate
Water Soil → roots → xylem Hydrogen source and participates in photosynthesis
Sunlight Sun Energy source
Chlorophyll Chloroplasts Absorbs light energy
Nitrogen etc. Soil Proteins and other body materials
🧠 “Air + Soil + Sun + Green” → CO₂ + water/minerals + light + chlorophyll.
07

Heterotrophic Nutrition — Different Strategies

Different organisms obtain food in different ways. Fungi such as bread mould, yeast and mushrooms can break food down outside the body and absorb simpler substances. Parasites such as Cuscuta, ticks, lice, leeches and tapeworms obtain nutrition from living hosts.

Holozoic

Food is taken in and digested inside the body; common in animals.

Saprophytic

Complex food is broken down outside and soluble material is absorbed.

Parasitic

Nutrition is obtained from a living host without immediately killing it.

08

Amoeba — Nutrition in a Single-Celled Organism

Amoeba uses temporary finger-like extensions called pseudopodia to surround a food particle and form a food vacuole. Digestion occurs inside the vacuole; useful nutrients enter the cytoplasm and undigested material is expelled.

Amoeba nutrition sequence
🧠 “P-F-D-A-E” → Pseudopodia → Food vacuole → Digestion → Absorption → Egestion.
09

Human Digestive System — Follow the Food

The alimentary canal is a continuous tube from mouth to anus. Different regions specialise in ingestion, digestion, absorption and egestion.

Alimentary canal flow
Food pathway: Mouth → Oesophagus → Stomach → Small intestine → Large intestine → Rectum/Anus.
🧠 “My Old Sister Sings Like Rap” → Mouth, Oesophagus, Stomach, Small intestine, Large intestine, Rectum.
10

Mouth & Saliva — First Stage of Digestion

Teeth mechanically crush food and the tongue mixes it with saliva. Salivary glands secrete salivary amylase, which breaks down starch into simpler sugars. Moistening also makes swallowing easier.

Starch digestion starts in the mouth
⭐ Iodine + starch gives the characteristic blue-black colour. If saliva digests starch, less starch remains to react.
11

Stomach — Acid, Pepsin & Mucus

Gastric glands release hydrochloric acid, pepsin and mucus. HCl creates an acidic medium suitable for pepsin. Pepsin digests proteins. Mucus protects the stomach lining from the acid under normal conditions.

HCl

Creates acidic medium and supports pepsin action.

Pepsin

Protein-digesting enzyme active in acidic medium.

Mucus

Protective layer for the stomach lining.

🧠 “H-P-M” = HCl prepares, Pepsin processes protein, Mucus protects.
12

Small Intestine — Complete Digestion + Absorption

The small intestine receives bile from the liver and pancreatic juice from the pancreas. The acidic food from the stomach is made alkaline so pancreatic enzymes can work. Bile salts emulsify large fat globules into smaller droplets, increasing the surface available for enzyme action.

Secretion Main role
Bile Makes acidic food alkaline and emulsifies fats.
Pancreatic juice Contains enzymes such as trypsin for proteins and lipase for fats.
Intestinal juice Completes digestion to absorbable molecules.
End products: carbohydrates → glucose; proteins → amino acids; fats → fatty acids + glycerol.
13

Villi — How the Small Intestine is Designed for Absorption

The inner lining of the small intestine has numerous finger-like projections called villi. They enormously increase surface area and contain a rich blood-vessel network, allowing absorbed nutrients to be transported to body cells.

Villus: large area + short transport distance
🧠 VILLI = “Very Increased Large Lining Interface” — think more area + blood supply = better absorption.
14

Peristalsis, Large Intestine & Egestion

Rhythmic muscular contractions called peristalsis move food along the alimentary canal. The large intestine absorbs more water from unabsorbed material. Remaining waste is removed through the anus; the anal sphincter regulates its exit.

Movement and final removal
15

Respiration — Why Cells Need It

Respiration breaks down organic compounds such as glucose and releases energy. The energy is captured in the form of ATP, which powers cellular activities.

Glucose → Pyruvate → energy pathways
Important distinction: breathing is the physical exchange of gases; cellular respiration is the chemical breakdown of food to release usable energy.
🧠 Breathing brings O₂; respiration releases energy.
16

Glucose Breakdown — The Three Pathways

The first step is common: glucose, a six-carbon molecule, is broken down into the three-carbon molecule pyruvate in the cytoplasm. Pyruvate then follows different pathways depending on oxygen availability and organism/tissue.

Glucose → pyruvate → three routes
🧠 “Y-M-A” → Yeast = Alcohol, Muscle = Lactic acid, Aerobic = CO₂ + Water.
17

Aerobic vs Anaerobic Respiration

Feature Aerobic Anaerobic
Oxygen Required Not required
Site after pyruvate Mitochondria Cytoplasmic pathway
Products CO₂ + H₂O Ethanol + CO₂ in yeast OR lactic acid in muscles
Energy Much more Less
⭐ If the question asks why cramps occur after sudden activity: low oxygen in muscle cells → pyruvate converted to lactic acid → accumulation associated with cramps.
18

ATP — The Energy Currency of the Cell

Respiration provides energy that is used to synthesise ATP from ADP and inorganic phosphate. ATP then supplies a convenient energy source for processes such as muscle contraction, protein synthesis and nerve impulses.

Cellular energy transfer
🧠 ATP = “A Tiny Power-pack” — immediate usable cellular energy.
19

Human Respiratory System & Gas Exchange

Air enters through the nostrils, passes through the respiratory passages and reaches the lungs. The passages contain hairs and mucus that help filter inhaled particles. Cartilage rings help prevent collapse of the air passage.

Air pathway
20

Alveoli — Designed for Maximum Gas Exchange

Alveoli are balloon-like structures at the ends of fine air passages. They provide a huge surface area, have thin walls and are surrounded by an extensive blood-vessel network. These features make diffusion of oxygen and carbon dioxide efficient.

Why alveoli are efficient
🧠 “3T” for alveoli: Tons of area, Thin wall, Tight capillary network.
21

Transport of Oxygen & Carbon Dioxide

Haemoglobin in red blood cells has a high affinity for oxygen and transports it from lungs to tissues. Carbon dioxide is more soluble in water than oxygen and is transported mostly in dissolved form in blood.

Oxygen

  • Enters blood at alveoli.
  • Haemoglobin binds oxygen.
  • Carried to tissues.
  • Released where needed.

Carbon dioxide

  • Produced by cells.
  • Enters blood from tissues.
  • More soluble than O₂.
  • Carried to lungs for release.
Haemoglobin deficiency reduces oxygen-carrying capacity, so tissues may receive less oxygen for respiration.
22

Human Circulatory System — Blood + Heart + Vessels

The circulatory system transports oxygen, carbon dioxide, food, salts and nitrogenous wastes. It needs a pump (heart), a transport fluid (blood) and a network (blood vessels).

Plasma

Fluid medium transporting dissolved food, CO₂, nitrogenous wastes and salts.

RBCs

Contain haemoglobin and transport oxygen.

Platelets

Help clot blood at sites of vessel injury.

🧠 “P-R-P” → Plasma transports, RBCs carry oxygen, Platelets plug leaks.
23

Four Chambers of the Human Heart — Blood Route

Double circulation route
Double circulation: blood passes through the heart twice in one complete cycle — heart → lungs → heart and heart → body → heart.
🧠 “Right = Receive/Respire; Left = Launch” → right side receives deoxygenated blood and sends it to lungs; left side sends oxygenated blood to body.
24

Arteries, Veins & Capillaries — Don't Memorise by Oxygen

Vessel Direction Structure Special point
Artery Away from heart Thick, elastic walls High pressure
Vein Towards heart Thinner walls + valves Prevents backflow
Capillary Connects arterial/venous networks One-cell-thick wall Exchange with tissues
⭐ Trap: arteries are not defined as “oxygenated vessels”. Pulmonary artery carries deoxygenated blood. Define them by direction from the heart.
🧠 ARTERY = AWAY. This one word solves most direction questions.
25

Blood Pressure, Valves, Platelets & Lymph

Blood pressure is the force exerted by blood on vessel walls. Systolic pressure occurs during ventricular contraction; diastolic pressure occurs during ventricular relaxation. Valves prevent backward blood flow. Platelets help clot damaged vessels.

Lymph returns tissue fluid to blood
26

Transportation in Plants — Xylem vs Phloem

Xylem Phloem
Transports Water + minerals Food/photosynthetic products + amino acids
Major direction Root → aerial parts Both upward and downward depending on need
Driving idea Root pressure + transpiration pull ATP-dependent translocation and pressure flow
🧠 Xylem = Xtra water upward. Phloem = Food flows to places that need it.
27

Root Pressure & Transpiration Pull

Root cells actively take up ions, causing water to move into roots and contributing to root pressure. But this alone is usually insufficient for very tall plants. During the day, evaporation of water through stomata creates a suction called transpiration pull, which is a major force moving water upward in xylem.

Water movement in a tall plant
⭐ Day: transpiration pull dominates. Night: root pressure becomes relatively more important.
28

Phloem Translocation — Food Moves Where Needed

Soluble products of photosynthesis, especially sucrose, are moved through phloem. This process is called translocation. It can occur upward or downward according to the plant's needs and uses energy from ATP.

Source → sink concept
Key sequence: sucrose loading → water enters phloem → pressure develops → food moves toward lower-pressure sink.
29

Excretion — Why Waste Removal is Essential

Metabolic reactions produce waste materials that may be harmful. Excretion is the biological process of removing such wastes. Unicellular organisms may remove wastes by diffusion, while complex multicellular organisms use specialised organs.

Excretion as the waste-removal pathway
30

Human Excretory System — Kidney to Urethra

The human excretory system contains a pair of kidneys, a pair of ureters, a urinary bladder and a urethra. Kidneys filter blood and produce urine; ureters carry urine to the bladder; the bladder stores it; the urethra carries it out.

Urine pathway
🧠 K-U-B-U → Kidney → Ureter → Bladder → Urethra.
31

Nephron — The Functional Unit of the Kidney

Each kidney contains many nephrons. A cluster of thin-walled blood capillaries is associated with the cup-shaped Bowman's capsule. Filtration produces an initial filtrate; useful substances such as glucose, amino acids, salts and much water are selectively reabsorbed along the tubule.

Simplified nephron map
Nephron logic: filter first → keep useful substances → retain/adjust water → remove remaining waste as urine.
🧠 F-R-E = Filter → Reabsorb useful material → Excrete the remainder.
32

How the Amount of Urine is Regulated

The amount of water reabsorbed depends on how much excess water is present in the body and how much dissolved waste needs to be excreted. Thus the kidney does not simply remove a fixed amount of water.

More excess water

  • Less need to conserve water.
  • More water remains in urine.
  • Urine volume can increase.

Less water / more need to conserve

  • More water is reabsorbed.
  • Urine becomes smaller in volume.
  • Waste still needs to be removed.
⭐ Do not write “kidneys remove all water”. Their key role includes selective reabsorption.
33

Dialysis — Artificial Kidney

When kidneys fail, nitrogenous wastes accumulate. An artificial kidney can remove these wastes by dialysis. Blood passes through tubes with a semipermeable lining while waste diffuses into dialysing fluid; purified blood is returned to the patient.

Basic dialysis principle
Unlike the normal nephron, dialysis removes wastes by diffusion but does not perform normal tubular reabsorption.
34

Excretion in Plants — Several Routes

Plants do not have a single specialised excretory organ like kidneys. Different wastes are handled in different ways. Oxygen can be released during photosynthesis; excess water can be lost by transpiration; other wastes can be stored or removed through leaves and surrounding tissues.

Vacuoles

Waste products may be stored inside cellular vacuoles.

Leaves

Some wastes are stored in leaves and removed when leaves fall.

Gums & resins

Stored especially in old xylem tissues.

Other routes include releasing some wastes into surrounding soil and losing excess water by transpiration.
🧠 “V-L-G-S” → Vacuoles, Leaves, Gums/resins, Soil.
35

Chapter-Wide Process Map

How all four life processes connect
🧠 N-R-T-E loop: Nutrition supplies fuel/material → Respiration releases usable energy → Transport distributes materials and collects wastes → Excretion removes wastes.

NCERT Exercises — Questions + Answers

Complete end-of-chapter exercise set

1. The kidneys in human beings are a part of the system for (a) nutrition (b) respiration (c) excretion (d) transportation.
ANSWER(c) Excretion. Kidneys filter blood and remove nitrogenous wastes by forming urine.
2. The xylem in plants are responsible for (a) transport of water (b) transport of food (c) transport of amino acids (d) transport of oxygen.
ANSWER(a) Transport of water. Xylem conducts water and minerals from roots to other parts.
3. The autotrophic mode of nutrition requires (a) CO₂ and water (b) chlorophyll (c) sunlight (d) all of the above.
ANSWER(d) All of the above.
4. Breakdown of pyruvate to give carbon dioxide, water and energy takes place in (a) cytoplasm (b) mitochondria (c) chloroplast (d) nucleus.
ANSWER(b) Mitochondria. Aerobic breakdown of pyruvate occurs in mitochondria.
5. How are fats digested in our bodies? Where does this process take place?
ANSWERFat digestion occurs mainly in the small intestine. Bile salts emulsify large fat globules into smaller droplets, increasing surface area for enzymes. Pancreatic lipase then breaks fats down; intestinal digestion completes the process to fatty acids and glycerol.
6. What is the role of saliva in the digestion of food?
ANSWERSaliva moistens food and contains salivary amylase, which begins digestion of starch into simpler sugars.
7. What are the necessary conditions for autotrophic nutrition and what are its by-products?
ANSWERAutotrophic nutrition by photosynthesis requires CO₂, water, sunlight and chlorophyll. The main carbohydrate product is formed for the plant's food/energy needs and oxygen is released as a by-product.
8. What are the differences between aerobic and anaerobic respiration? Name some organisms that use the anaerobic mode.
ANSWERAerobic respiration uses oxygen and gives CO₂, water and much more energy. Anaerobic pathways do not require oxygen and give less energy; yeast can produce ethanol + CO₂, while human muscles can produce lactic acid when oxygen is insufficient.
9. How are the alveoli designed to maximise the exchange of gases?
ANSWERAlveoli provide a very large surface area, have thin and delicate walls and are surrounded by a rich network of blood capillaries. Their moist surface and close blood supply make diffusion efficient.
10. What would be the consequences of a deficiency of haemoglobin in our bodies?
ANSWERHaemoglobin carries oxygen in blood. Its deficiency reduces oxygen-carrying capacity, so tissues may receive less oxygen for cellular respiration and energy production, leading to weakness/fatigue and reduced performance.
11. Describe double circulation of blood in human beings. Why is it necessary?
ANSWERDeoxygenated blood travels from body → right side of heart → lungs. Oxygenated blood returns lungs → left side of heart → body. Thus blood passes through the heart twice in one cycle. Separation prevents mixing of oxygenated and deoxygenated blood and supports efficient oxygen delivery in birds and mammals.
12. What are the differences between the transport of materials in xylem and phloem?
ANSWERXylem transports water and minerals, mainly upward from roots, using root pressure and transpiration pull. Phloem transports soluble food such as sucrose and other substances; translocation can occur in both directions according to plant needs and requires energy.
13. Compare the functioning of alveoli in lungs and nephrons in kidneys with respect to their structure and functioning.
ANSWERBoth have specialised structures closely associated with thin blood vessels and exchange/processing substances from blood. Alveoli are thin-walled air sacs for gas exchange; nephrons are filtration units in kidneys where blood is filtered and useful substances are selectively reabsorbed while wastes remain for urine formation.

PYQ Practice

Board-style questions • reveal answers after thinking

1 MARK

Name the process by which plants lose water vapour through stomata.

Transpiration.
1 MARK

Where does glycolysis/initial glucose breakdown occur?

Cytoplasm. Glucose is first broken into pyruvate there.
2 MARKS

Why are multicellular organisms dependent on specialised transport systems?

Many cells are not in direct contact with the external environment, so diffusion alone cannot rapidly supply oxygen/nutrients or remove wastes across long distances.
2 MARKS

Differentiate between arteries and veins.

Arteries carry blood away from the heart and have thick elastic walls. Veins carry blood toward the heart and have valves to prevent backflow.
3 MARKS

Explain why the small intestine is the main site of absorption.

It is long and extensively coiled; digestion is completed there; its inner surface has numerous villi that greatly increase surface area and have rich blood supply for transporting absorbed nutrients.
3 MARKS

Explain the three pathways of pyruvate breakdown.

With oxygen: pyruvate is broken down in mitochondria to CO₂ + H₂O with more energy. In yeast without oxygen: ethanol + CO₂ + less energy. In oxygen-deficient muscles: lactic acid + less energy.
3 MARKS

Why is separation of oxygenated and deoxygenated blood necessary in mammals?

Mammals have high energy demands for maintaining body temperature. Complete separation prevents mixing and ensures tissues receive efficiently oxygenated blood.
5 MARKS

Explain the formation of urine in a nephron.

Blood enters the capillary cluster; filtration occurs into Bowman's capsule. The filtrate passes through the tubule. Useful substances such as glucose, amino acids, salts and much water are selectively reabsorbed. Remaining dissolved wastes and excess water form urine, which passes to the ureter and bladder.
5 MARKS

Explain transportation of water in plants.

Roots absorb ions and water enters root xylem, creating root pressure. Water moves through continuous xylem vessels. During the day, evaporation through stomata creates transpiration pull, which provides the major upward driving force. Water and dissolved minerals reach leaves and other parts.

Complete Revision Notes

All concepts • mnemonics • tips • traps • formulas

① Life Processes — Core Idea

  • Life processes are maintenance functions required to keep an organism alive.
  • Main processes: nutrition, respiration, transportation and excretion.
  • Multicellular organisms need specialised tissues/organs because diffusion alone is too slow over long distances.
  • Think of the body as a system: input → energy → distribution → waste removal.
🧠 N-R-T-E = Nutrition → Respiration → Transportation → Excretion.

② Nutrition — Autotrophic & Heterotrophic

  • Nutrition supplies energy and raw materials.
  • Autotrophs synthesise complex organic food from simple inorganic materials.
  • Heterotrophs depend directly/indirectly on organisms that make food.
  • Heterotrophic strategies include holozoic, saprophytic and parasitic modes.
  • Fungi can digest food outside the body; parasites obtain nutrition from living hosts.
🎯 Tip: If asked “why different nutrition modes?”, connect food availability + organism's body design + method of obtaining food.
🧠 AUTO = self; HETERO = other.

③ Photosynthesis — Complete Recall

  • Raw materials: CO₂ + water.
  • Conditions: sunlight + chlorophyll.
  • CO₂ enters mainly through stomata; water comes through roots.
  • Chlorophyll in chloroplasts absorbs light.
  • Light energy is converted to chemical energy; water is split; CO₂ is reduced to carbohydrates.
  • Unused carbohydrate may be stored as starch.
  • O₂ is released as a by-product.
  • Plants also need mineral nutrients such as nitrogen, phosphorus, iron and magnesium from soil.
CO₂ + H₂O → C₆H₁₂O₆ + O₂ sunlight + chlorophyll
🧠 “CO₂ + H₂O + Sun + Green” → food + O₂.
🎯 Experiment trick: destarch → expose → iodine test. Green area turns blue-black because starch is formed where chlorophyll is present.

④ Stomata & Guard Cells

  • Stomata are tiny pores for gaseous exchange.
  • Guard cells regulate opening and closing.
  • Swollen guard cells → pore opens.
  • Shrunken guard cells → pore closes.
  • Stomata allow CO₂ entry but also cause water loss.
🧠 “Swollen = Space; Shrunken = Shut.”

⑤ Human Digestion — Organ-by-Organ

  • Mouth: teeth crush food; saliva moistens; salivary amylase starts starch digestion.
  • Oesophagus: food moves by peristalsis.
  • Stomach: HCl creates acidic medium; pepsin digests proteins; mucus protects lining.
  • Small intestine: receives bile + pancreatic juice; complete digestion of carbohydrates, proteins and fats; major absorption site.
  • Bile: makes acidic food alkaline and emulsifies fats.
  • Pancreas: trypsin acts on proteins; lipase acts on fats.
  • End products: carbohydrates → glucose; proteins → amino acids; fats → fatty acids + glycerol.
  • Villi: increase surface area and have rich blood supply.
  • Large intestine: absorbs more water.
  • Anus: egestion controlled by anal sphincter.
🧠 “M-O-S-S-L-A” = Mouth → Oesophagus → Stomach → Small intestine → Large intestine → Anus.
🎯 Digestion answer trick: write organ + secretion/enzyme + substrate + product.

⑥ High-Yield Digestion Facts

  • Salivary amylase → starch → simpler sugars.
  • HCl → acidic medium → helps pepsin.
  • Pepsin → protein digestion.
  • Mucus → stomach protection.
  • Bile salts → emulsification of fats.
  • Trypsin → protein digestion.
  • Lipase → fat digestion.
  • Villi → absorption.
  • Peristalsis → forward movement of food.
🧠 “H-P-M” = HCl prepares, Pepsin processes, Mucus protects.

⑦ Respiration — Must-Know Pathway

  • Glucose is first broken into pyruvate in the cytoplasm.
  • Pyruvate has three possible pathways depending on oxygen and organism/tissue.
  • Aerobic: mitochondria → CO₂ + H₂O + much more energy.
  • Yeast anaerobic: ethanol + CO₂ + less energy.
  • Muscle oxygen shortage: lactic acid + less energy.
  • Lactic acid accumulation during sudden activity is associated with cramps.
🧠 “Y-M-A” = Yeast–Alcohol, Muscle–Lactic acid, Aerobic–CO₂ + H₂O.

⑧ Aerobic vs Anaerobic — Quick Table

  • O₂: aerobic yes; anaerobic no.
  • Energy: aerobic much more; anaerobic less.
  • Products: aerobic CO₂ + H₂O; anaerobic depends on pathway.
  • Site after pyruvate: aerobic in mitochondria; anaerobic pathway does not use mitochondrial aerobic breakdown.
🎯 Trap: Do not write “anaerobic respiration always produces alcohol.” In human muscles, lactic acid is produced under oxygen deficiency.

⑨ ATP — Energy Currency

  • Respiration energy is used to synthesise ATP from ADP + inorganic phosphate.
  • ATP provides usable energy for cellular work.
  • Examples: muscle contraction, protein synthesis, nervous impulses.
🧠 ATP = A Tiny Power-pack.

⑩ Human Respiration — Alveoli & Gas Exchange

  • Air enters through nostrils; hairs/mucus filter particles.
  • Cartilage rings prevent collapse of air passages.
  • Air reaches alveoli through progressively smaller tubes.
  • Alveoli have large surface area, thin walls and rich blood supply.
  • O₂ diffuses into blood; CO₂ diffuses into alveoli.
  • Haemoglobin carries oxygen; CO₂ is transported largely dissolved in blood.
  • Inhalation: ribs lift + diaphragm flattens → chest volume increases → air enters.
  • Exhalation reverses these volume changes.
🧠 Alveoli = “3T”: Tons of area + Thin wall + Tight capillary network.

⑪ Transportation in Humans

  • Blood = plasma + cells.
  • Plasma transports dissolved food, CO₂, nitrogenous wastes and salts.
  • RBCs with haemoglobin transport O₂.
  • Platelets help clot damaged vessels.
  • Heart is the pump; vessels form the network.
  • Right side handles deoxygenated blood; left side handles oxygenated blood.
  • Valves prevent backward flow.
🧠 “Right → Respiratory organs; Left → Life tissues.”

⑫ Double Circulation — Exact Route

  • Body → vena cava → right atrium → right ventricle → pulmonary artery → lungs.
  • Lungs → pulmonary vein → left atrium → left ventricle → aorta → body.
  • Blood passes through heart twice in one complete cycle.
  • Separation prevents oxygenated/deoxygenated mixing and supports high energy demands.
🧠 “Right sends to lungs; Left launches to body.”
🎯 Trap: artery/vein is defined by direction relative to heart, not oxygen content.

⑬ Blood Vessels — Artery / Vein / Capillary

  • Artery: away from heart; thick elastic walls; high pressure.
  • Vein: toward heart; thinner walls; valves.
  • Capillary: one-cell-thick; exchange with tissues.
🧠 ARTERY = AWAY. VEIN = towards the heart.

⑭ Blood Pressure & Lymph

  • Blood pressure = force of blood against vessel wall.
  • Systolic = ventricular contraction.
  • Diastolic = ventricular relaxation.
  • Valves ensure one-way flow.
  • Platelets plug leaks and help clot.
  • Lymph/tissue fluid forms from material escaping through capillary walls.
  • Lymph returns excess tissue fluid and transports absorbed fats from intestine.

⑮ Transportation in Plants

  • Xylem transports water + minerals from roots.
  • Root pressure contributes to upward movement.
  • Transpiration is loss of water vapour from aerial parts through stomata.
  • Transpiration creates suction/transpiration pull.
  • During day, transpiration pull is the major driving force for upward xylem movement.
  • Phloem transports soluble photosynthetic products, amino acids and other substances.
  • Phloem translocation can occur upward or downward according to plant needs.
  • Phloem loading uses ATP and produces pressure differences that drive movement.
🧠 Xylem = Water; Phloem = Food.
🎯 Plant transport answer: always mention tissue + material + direction + driving force.

⑯ Excretion in Humans

  • Excretory system: kidneys, ureters, urinary bladder, urethra.
  • Kidneys remove nitrogenous wastes such as urea/uric acid from blood.
  • Nephron = functional filtration unit.
  • Bowman's capsule collects filtrate from capillary cluster.
  • Selective reabsorption returns useful substances such as glucose, amino acids, salts and much water.
  • Remaining waste + excess water becomes urine.
  • Urine → ureter → bladder → urethra.
🧠 K-U-B-U = Kidney → Ureter → Bladder → Urethra.
🎯 Nephron trick: FRE = Filter → Reabsorb → Excrete.

⑰ Urine Regulation & Dialysis

  • Water reabsorption depends on body water status and dissolved waste requirements.
  • More water available → less need to conserve it → more may remain in urine.
  • Kidney failure causes waste accumulation.
  • Dialysis uses a semipermeable lining and waste-free dialysing fluid.
  • Waste diffuses from blood into dialysing fluid; purified blood returns to patient.
  • Normal kidney function includes reabsorption; dialysis does not replicate normal tubular reabsorption.
🧠 Dialysis = “Diffuse waste, Return blood.”

⑱ Excretion in Plants

  • Plants have no single kidney-like excretory organ.
  • O₂ can be released during photosynthesis.
  • Excess water is lost by transpiration.
  • Wastes can be stored in vacuoles.
  • Wastes can accumulate in leaves and leave when leaves fall.
  • Gums and resins can store wastes, especially in old xylem.
  • Some wastes can be released into surrounding soil.
🧠 V-L-G-S = Vacuoles → Leaves → Gums/resins → Soil.
