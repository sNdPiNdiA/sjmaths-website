Introduction to Hydrocarbons

The term hydrocarbon is self-explanatory: it denotes organic compounds composed exclusively of carbon and hydrogen. Hydrocarbons play an indispensable role in daily life and industrial technology as vital fuels, lubricants, and chemical feedstocks.

Key Fuels from Petroleum & Gas

  • LPG (Liquefied Petroleum Gas): Domestic fuel consisting predominantly of propane and butane. Clean burning with minimal pollution.
  • CNG (Compressed Natural Gas): Methane compressed at high pressures for automobiles; significantly lowers greenhouse emissions.
  • LNG (Liquefied Natural Gas): Liquefied methane for efficient long-distance maritime transport.
  • Petrol, Diesel & Kerosene: Fractions obtained from the fractional distillation of crude petroleum.

Industrial Applications

  • Polymers: Manufacture of polythene, polypropylene, polystyrene, PVC, and synthetic rubbers.
  • Solvents & Feedstocks: Higher alkanes act as solvents in paints and varnishes. Arenes are starting materials for pharmaceuticals, dyes, explosives, and fragrances.

9.1 Classification of Hydrocarbons

Depending upon the types of carbon-carbon bonds and structure, hydrocarbons are broadly categorized into three classes:

2n+2$) Unsaturated Double ($C_n H2n$) or Triple ($C_n H2n-2$) Aromatic (Arenes) Cyclic conjugated $(4n+2)π$ e⁻ Open chain / Cycloalkanes Methane, Cyclohexane Alkenes (C=C) Ethene, Propene Alkynes (C≡C) Ethyne, Propyne Benzenoid & Non-benzenoid Benzene, Naphthalene
  • Saturated Hydrocarbons: Contain only carbon-carbon and carbon-hydrogen single $σ$ bonds. If open-chain, they are called alkanes (general formula: $\mathbf{C_n H2n+2}$). If closed rings, they are cycloalkanes ($\mathbf{C_n H2n}$).
  • Unsaturated Hydrocarbons: Contain carbon-carbon multiple bonds. Alkenes contain at least one $C=C$ double bond ($\mathbf{C_n H2n}$); alkynes contain at least one $C\equiv C$ triple bond ($\mathbf{C_n H2n-2}$).
  • Aromatic Hydrocarbons (Arenes): Special cyclic planar unsaturated compounds that exhibit resonance stabilization conforming to Hückel's Rule of $(4n+2)π$ electrons.

9.2 Alkanes (Paraffins)

Alkanes are open-chain saturated hydrocarbons where carbon atoms are $sp^3$ hybridised. Because of their relative inertness towards acids, bases, oxidising agents, and reducing agents under normal room conditions, they were historically designated as paraffins (Latin: parum = little, affinis = affinity).

Structure of Methane & Alkane Geometry

In methane ($CH_4$), the central carbon undergoes $sp^3$ hybridisation. Four $sp^3$ hybrid orbitals overlap head-on with $1s$ orbitals of four hydrogen atoms to form four equivalent $CH$ $σ$ bonds directed towards the four corners of a regular tetrahedron. The $H-C-H$ bond angle is $109.5^°$ ($109^° 28'$). In higher alkanes, $CC$ bond length is $154 pm$ and $CH$ bond length is $112 pm$.

9.2.1 Nomenclature & Isomerism in Alkanes

Methane ($CH_4$), ethane ($C_2H_6$), and propane ($C_3H_8$) have only one possible structural arrangement. Butane ($C_4H10$) can exist in two chain isomeric forms:

  1. Continuous unbranched chain: $CH_3CH_2CH_2CH_3$ ($n$-Butane, b.p. $273 K$)
  2. Branched chain: $CH_3CH(CH_3)CH_3$ ($2$-Methylpropane / isobutane, b.p. $261 K$)

Pentane ($C_5H12$) exhibits three chain isomers: $n$-pentane (b.p. $309 K$), $2$-methylbutane / isopentane (b.p. $301 K$), and $2,2$-dimethylpropane / neopentane (b.p. $282.5 K$). As molecular size increases, the number of structural isomers escalates rapidly: $C_6H14$ has $5$, $C_7H16$ has $9$, and $C10H22$ has $75$ isomers.

Types of Carbon Atoms ($1^°, 2^°, 3^°, 4^°$)

  • Primary ($1^°$) Carbon: Attached to no other carbon (as in methane) or to only one other carbon atom (terminal carbons are always primary).
  • Secondary ($2^°$) Carbon: Attached directly to two other carbon atoms.
  • Tertiary ($3^°$) Carbon: Attached directly to three other carbon atoms.
  • Quaternary ($4^°$) Carbon: Attached directly to four other carbon atoms.

9.2.2 Methods of Preparation of Alkanes

1. From Unsaturated Hydrocarbons (Catalytic Hydrogenation / Sabatier-Senderens)

Dihydrogen gas adds to alkenes and alkynes in the presence of finely divided metal catalysts like $Pt$, $Pd$, or $Ni$:

$$CH_2=CH_2 + H_2 \xrightarrow{Pt/Pd/Ni} CH_3CH_3 \quad (Ethene \to Ethane)$$ $$CH_3C\equivCH + 2H_2 \xrightarrow{Pt/Pd/Ni} CH_3CH_2CH_3 \quad (Propyne \to Propane)$$

Note: $Pt$ and $Pd$ catalyse at room temperature; $Ni$ requires higher temperature ($≈ 523\text{}573 K$) and pressure.

2. From Alkyl Halides

(a) Reduction with Zinc and Dilute Acid:

$$RX + Zn + H^+ \to RH + Zn2+ + X^-$$ $$CH_3Cl + H_2 \xrightarrow{Zn, H^+} CH_4 + HCl$$ $$C_2H_5Cl + H_2 \xrightarrow{Zn, H^+} C_2H_6 + HCl$$

(b) Wurtz Reaction: Alkyl halides on treatment with sodium metal in dry ethereal solution give higher symmetrical alkanes containing an even number of carbon atoms:

$$2CH_3Br + 2Na \xrightarrow{dry ether} CH_3CH_3 + 2NaBr \quad (Bromomethane \to Ethane)$$ $$2C_2H_5Br + 2Na \xrightarrow{dry ether} CH_3CH_2CH_2CH_3 + 2NaBr \quad (Bromoethane \to n-Butane)$$
Limitation of Wurtz Reaction: If two different alkyl halides ($RX$ and $R'X$) are taken, a mixture of three alkanes ($RR, RR', R'R'$) is obtained, which have very close boiling points and are virtually impossible to separate cleanly. Methane cannot be synthesized by Wurtz reaction.

3. From Carboxylic Acids

(a) Decarboxylation with Soda Lime: Heating sodium salts of carboxylic acids with soda lime ($NaOH + CaO$ in $3:1$ ratio) eliminates carbon dioxide, forming an alkane with one carbon atom less than the carboxylic acid:

$$CH_3COO^-Na^+ + NaOH \xrightarrow[Δ]{CaO} CH_4 + Na_2CO_3$$

Role of CaO: Quicklime ($CaO$) keeps $NaOH$ dry (prevents deliquescence) and raises the fusion temperature.

(b) Kolbe's Electrolytic Method: Electrolysis of a concentrated aqueous solution of sodium or potassium carboxylate yields a symmetrical alkane containing an even number of carbon atoms at the anode:

$$2CH_3COO^-Na^+ + 2H_2O \xrightarrow{Electrolysis} CH_3CH_3 + 2CO_2 \uparrow + H_2 \uparrow + 2NaOH$$
Mechanism of Kolbe Electrolysis:
  • At Anode: Oxidation: $2CH_3COO^- \xrightarrow{-2e^-} 2CH_3COO^\bullet \xrightarrow{-2CO_2} 2CH_3^\bullet \to CH_3CH_3$
  • At Cathode: Reduction: $2H_2O + 2e^- \to 2OH^- + H_2 \uparrow$
Note: Methane cannot be prepared by Kolbe's electrolytic method because the coupling requires at least two free radicals ($1+1=2$ carbons).

9.2.3 Physical and Chemical Properties of Alkanes

Physical Properties: Alkanes are non-polar because electronegativity difference between $C$ and $H$ is negligible ($2.5$ vs $2.1$). They are held by weak dispersion (van der Waals) forces. $C_1$ to $C_4$ are colorless gases; $C_5$ to $C17$ are liquids; $C18+$ are waxy solids. Alkanes are hydrophobic (insoluble in water) but dissolve in non-polar organic solvents like benzene and ether ("like dissolves like").

Boiling Point Trends: Boiling points increase regularly with increasing molecular mass ($≈ 20\text{}30 K$ per $-CH_2-$ unit) due to increase in molecular surface area and dispersion forces. However, among isomeric alkanes, boiling point decreases with increased branching. Branching makes the molecule more spherical, diminishing surface area of contact and weakening intermolecular attractions: $n-pentane (309.1 K) > isopentane (301 K) > neopentane (282.5 K)$.

Chemical Reactions of Alkanes

1. Free Radical Halogenation

Reaction of alkanes with halogens in presence of ultraviolet light ($hν$) or heat ($573\text{}773 K$):

$$CH_4 + Cl_2 \xrightarrow{hν} CH_3Cl \xrightarrow{hν, Cl_2} CH_2Cl_2 \xrightarrow{hν, Cl_2} CHCl_3 \xrightarrow{hν, Cl_2} CCl_4$$

Reactivity order of halogens: $F_2 > Cl_2 > Br_2 > I_2$. Ease of replacement of $H$: $3^° > 2^° > 1^°$. Fluorination is explosively violent; iodination is extremely slow and reversible, requiring oxidising agents like $HIO_3$ or $HNO_3$ to consume $HI$ ($5HI + HIO_3 \to 3I_2 + 3H_2O$).

Three-Step Free Radical Chain Mechanism:
  1. Chain Initiation: Homolytic fission of weak $ClCl$ bond by light: $ClCl \xrightarrow{hν} 2Cl^\bullet$
  2. Chain Propagation:
    • (a) $CH_4 + Cl^\bullet \to CH_3^\bullet + HCl$
    • (b) $CH_3^\bullet + Cl_2 \to CH_3Cl + Cl^\bullet$ (propagating radical regenerated)
  3. Chain Termination: Radicals combine to stop the chain:
    • $Cl^\bullet + Cl^\bullet \to Cl_2$
    • $CH_3^\bullet + Cl^\bullet \to CH_3Cl$
    • $CH_3^\bullet + CH_3^\bullet \to CH_3CH_3$ (explains formation of ethane byproduct!)

2. Combustion & Incomplete Combustion

$$C_nH2n+2 + (3n+12)O_2 \to nCO_2 + (n+1)H_2O \quad (Δ_c H^° < 0)$$ $$CH_4(g) + 2O_2(g) \to CO_2(g) + 2H_2O(l); \quad Δ_c H^° = -890 kJ mol-1$$ $$C_4H10(g) + 132O_2(g) \to 4CO_2(g) + 5H_2O(l); \quad Δ_c H^° = -2875.84 kJ mol-1$$

Incomplete Combustion: In limited oxygen, methane forms carbon black used in printer inks and pigments: $CH_4(g) + O_2(g) \xrightarrow{limited O_2} C(s) + 2H_2O(l)$.

3. Controlled Catalytic Oxidation

  • Methanol: $2CH_4 + O_2 \xrightarrow[100 atm]{Cu / 523 K} 2CH_3OH$
  • Methanal (Formaldehyde): $CH_4 + O_2 \xrightarrow[Δ]{Mo_2O_3} HCHO + H_2O$
  • Ethanoic Acid: $2CH_3CH_3 + 3O_2 \xrightarrow[Δ]{(CH_3COO)_2Mn} 2CH_3COOH + 2H_2O$
  • Tertiary CH Oxidation: Alkanes with a $3^°$ hydrogen are oxidised by alkaline $KMnO_4$: $(CH_3)_3CH \xrightarrow[Oxidation]{KMnO_4} (CH_3)_3COH$ ($2$-Methylpropan-$2$-ol).

4. Isomerisation, Aromatisation, Steam Reforming & Pyrolysis

  • Isomerisation: $n$-Alkanes heated with anhydrous $AlCl_3 + HCl(g)$ convert to branched alkanes: $$CH_3(CH_2)_4CH_3 \xrightarrow{Anhyd. AlCl_3 / HCl} 2-Methylpentane + 3-Methylpentane$$
  • Aromatisation (Reforming): $n$-Hexane heated to $773 K$ at $10\text{}20 atm$ over $Cr_2O_3, V_2O_5 or Mo_2O_3$ supported on alumina ($Al_2O_3$) cyclises and dehydrogenates into benzene. $n$-Heptane yields toluene.
  • Reaction with Steam: $CH_4 + H_2O(g) \xrightarrow[Δ, 1273 K]{Ni} CO + 3H_2$ (Synthesis of syngas/dihydrogen).
  • Pyrolysis (Cracking): Higher alkanes decompose at high temperatures ($773\text{}973 K$) into lower alkanes, alkenes, and hydrogen via free radical pathways. $C12H26 \xrightarrow[Pt/Pd/Ni]{973 K} C_7H16 + C_5H10 + \dots$

9.2.4 Conformations of Ethane

Because the $σ$-molecular orbital in a $CC$ single bond has cylindrical electron density along the internuclear axis, rotation about the single bond is permissible. Spatial arrangements of atoms in space that can interconvert simply by rotation around a carbon-carbon single bond are called conformations, conformers, or rotamers.

However, rotation is not 100% free; it is resisted by a small energy barrier of $1\text{}20 kJ mol-1$ ($12.5 kJ mol-1$ in ethane) called torsional strain caused by weak electrostatic repulsive interactions between adjacent bonding electron pairs.

  • Eclipsed Conformation: Hydrogen atoms on adjacent carbons are positioned as close to each other as possible. Dihedral angle $θ = 0^°$. Maximum electron-cloud repulsions and torsional strain; highest energy, least stable.
  • Staggered Conformation: Hydrogen atoms are as far apart as possible. Dihedral angle $θ = 60^°$. Minimum repulsive interactions; lowest potential energy, maximum stability.
  • Skew Conformation: Any intermediate spatial arrangement between eclipsed and staggered ($0^° < θ < 60^°$).
    • Relative Stability: $Staggered > Skew > Eclipsed$. The $12.5 kJ mol-1$ barrier is readily supplied by thermal kinetic energy at room temperature via molecular collisions, making rotation virtually unrestricted.

9.3 Alkenes (Olefins)

Alkenes are unsaturated hydrocarbons containing at least one carbon-carbon double bond ($>C=C<$). General formula: $\mathbf{C_n H2n}$. Historically termed olefins (oil-forming) because ethene reacts with chlorine to form an oily liquid ($1,2$-dichloroethane).

9.3.1 Structure of Carbon-Carbon Double Bond

Each carbon of the double bond is $sp^2$ hybridised. The $C=C$ double bond consists of:

  • One strong $σ$ bond: Formed by axial/head-on overlap of $sp^2$ hybrid orbitals (bond enthalpy $≈ 397 kJ mol-1$).
  • One weaker $π$ bond: Formed by lateral/sideways overlap of unhybridised $2p_z$ orbitals (bond enthalpy $≈ 284 kJ mol-1$).

Total $C=C$ bond enthalpy is $681 kJ mol-1$, compared to $348 kJ mol-1$ for $CC$ in ethane. The $C=C$ bond length is $134 pm$ (substantially shorter than $CC$ at $154 pm$). The loosely held mobile $π$-electrons lie in clouds above and below the molecular plane, making alkenes electron-rich centers readily attacked by electrophiles ($E^+$).

9.3.2 Nomenclature & Isomerism in Alkenes

Alkenes exhibit both Structural Isomerism (Chain & Position) and Stereoisomerism (Geometrical Cis-Trans Isomerism).

Geometrical Isomerism

Because rotation about a $C=C$ double bond is restricted (requires breaking the $π$ bond, which demands $≈ 284 kJ mol-1$), groups attached to the double-bonded carbons have fixed spatial arrangements:

  • Cis-Isomer: Two identical or similar atoms/groups lie on the same side of the double bond.
  • Trans-Isomer: Two identical or similar atoms/groups lie on opposite sides of the double bond.

Condition for Geometrical Isomerism: Each doubly bonded carbon must have two different groups attached (e.g. $abC=Cab$ or $abC=Cde$). If either carbon has two identical groups ($aaC=Cxy$), cis-trans isomerism is impossible.

9.3.4 Preparation of Alkenes

1. From Alkynes (Stereoselective Reduction)

  • Lindlar's Catalyst ($Pd/CaCO_3$ poisoned with quinoline or sulfur): Partial reduction yields cis-alkenes via syn-addition. $$RC\equivCR + H_2 \xrightarrow{Pd/C, quinoline} cis-Alkene$$
  • Birch Reduction ($Na$ in liquid $NH_3$): Yields trans-alkenes via anti-addition. $$RC\equivCR + 2Na + 2NH_3 \to trans-Alkene + 2NaNH_2$$

2. From Alkyl Halides (Dehydrohalogenation / $β$-Elimination)

Alkyl halides heated with alcoholic $KOH$ eliminate one molecule of halogen acid to form alkenes ($β$-elimination):

$$CH_3CH_2Cl + alc. KOH \xrightarrow{Δ} CH_2=CH_2 + KCl + H_2O$$

Reactivity of halogens: $I > Br > Cl$. Reactivity of alkyl groups: $3^° > 2^° > 1^°$.

3. From Vicinal Dihalides (Dehalogenation)

Vicinal dihalides (two halogens on adjacent carbons) treated with zinc dust lose $ZnX_2$:

$$CH_2BrCH_2Br + Zn \xrightarrow{Δ} CH_2=CH_2 + ZnBr_2$$

4. From Alcohols (Acid-Catalysed Dehydration)

Alcohols heated with concentrated $H_2SO_4$ at $443 K$ undergo $β$-elimination of water:

$$CH_3CH_2OH \xrightarrow[443 K]{conc. H_2SO_4} CH_2=CH_2 + H_2O$$

9.3.5 Chemical Reactions of Alkenes

1. Addition of Halogens (Bromine Water Test for Unsaturation)

$$CH_2=CH_2 + Br_2 \xrightarrow{CCl_4} CH_2BrCH_2Br \quad (1,2-Dibromoethane)$$

Diagnostic Test: The reddish-orange color of bromine in $CCl_4$ is discharged. This confirms the presence of carbon-carbon unsaturation.

2. Addition of Hydrogen Halides: Markovnikov's Rule vs Peroxide Effect

Markovnikov's Rule: When an unsymmetrical reagent adds to an unsymmetrical alkene, the negative part of the addendum attaches to that doubly bonded carbon atom which carries the lesser number of hydrogen atoms.

$$CH_3CH=CH_2 + HBr \to CH_3CHBrCH_3 \quad (2-Bromopropane, Major)$$
Markovnikov Mechanism (Electrophilic Addition):
  1. Electrophile $H^+$ attacks $CH_3CH=CH_2$ to generate carbocations:
    • Path A: Generates $2^°$ carbocation $CH_3\overset{+}{C}HCH_3$ (more stable due to hyperconjugation & $+I$).
    • Path B: Generates $1^°$ carbocation $CH_3CH_2\overset{+}{C}H_2$ (less stable).
  2. $Br^-$ nucleophile rapidly attacks the more stable $2^°$ carbocation forming 2-bromopropane.

Anti-Markovnikov Addition (Peroxide Effect / Kharasch Effect): In the presence of organic peroxides [e.g. benzoyl peroxide $(C_6H_5CO)_2O_2$], addition of $\mathbf{HBr}$ takes place contrary to Markovnikov's rule:

$$CH_3CH=CH_2 + HBr \xrightarrow{(C_6H_5CO)_2O_2} CH_3CH_2CH_2Br \quad (1-Bromopropane, Major)$$
Why only HBr exhibits Peroxide Effect?
  • $HCl$ bond is too strong ($430.5 kJ mol-1$) to be cleaved homolytically by peroxide radicals.
  • $HI$ bond is weak ($296.8 kJ mol-1$), but iodine free radicals ($I^\bullet$) rapidly combine with each other to form $I_2$ molecules instead of adding to the double bond.

3. Oxidation Reactions

  • Baeyer's Reagent (Cold, dilute, alkaline $KMnO_4$): Oxidises alkenes to vicinal glycols with discharge of purple colour (another unsaturation test!): $$CH_2=CH_2 + H_2O + [O] \xrightarrow[273 K]{dil. KMnO_4} CH_2(OH)CH_2(OH) \quad (Ethane-1,2-diol)$$
  • Acidic $KMnO_4$ / Acidic $K_2Cr_2O_7$: Cleaves double bond into ketones and/or carboxylic acids: $$CH_3CH=CHCH_3 \xrightarrow[Δ]{KMnO_4 / H^+} 2CH_3COOH \quad (Ethanoic acid)$$ $$(CH_3)_2C=CH_2 \xrightarrow[Δ]{KMnO_4 / H^+} (CH_3)_2C=O + CO_2 + H_2O$$

4. Ozonolysis

Ozone ($O_3$) adds to the double bond forming a cyclic ozonide intermediate, which is cleaved by zinc dust and water ($Zn + H_2O$) to yield aldehydes and/or ketones. Highly valuable for locating the position of double bonds in unknown alkenes!

$$CH_3CH=CH_2 + O_3 \to Ozonide \xrightarrow{Zn / H_2O} CH_3CHO + HCHO + ZnO$$

Role of Zinc Dust: Zinc consumes hydrogen peroxide ($H_2O_2$) formed during the reaction ($Zn + H_2O_2 \to ZnO + H_2O$), preventing further oxidation of aldehydes into carboxylic acids.

5. Polymerisation

$$n(CH_2=CH_2) \xrightarrow[Catalyst]{High T, P} -(CH_2CH_2)_n- \quad (Polythene)$$ $$n(CH_3CH=CH_2) \xrightarrow[Catalyst]{High T, P} -[CH(CH_3)CH_2]_n- \quad (Polypropene)$$

9.4 Alkynes

Alkynes are unsaturated acyclic hydrocarbons containing at least one carbon-carbon triple bond ($-C\equiv C-$). General formula: $\mathbf{C_n H2n-2}$. The simplest stable member is ethyne ($C_2H_2$), commonly known as acetylene (used with oxygen in oxyacetylene flame for high-temperature welding).

9.4.2 Structure of Triple Bond in Ethyne

In ethyne ($HC\equivCH$), each carbon is $sp$ hybridised with linear geometry ($H-C-C$ angle is $180^°$). The triple bond comprises:

  • One strong $σ$ bond: Formed by axial head-on overlap of two $sp$ hybrid orbitals.
  • Two $π$ bonds: Formed by lateral overlap of mutually perpendicular unhybridised $2p_y$ and $2p_z$ orbitals.

The $π$-electron clouds merge to form a cylindrical sleeve of electron density surrounding the internuclear axis. The $C\equiv C$ bond length is very short ($120 pm$), and bond enthalpy is high ($823 kJ mol-1$).

9.4.3 Preparation of Alkynes

9.4.4 Chemical Properties of Alkynes

A. Acidic Character of Terminal Alkynes

Because the carbon in ethyne is $sp$ hybridised with $50%$ $s$-character (compared to $33.3%$ in $sp^2$ ethene and $25%$ in $sp^3$ ethane), its electronegativity is the highest. It strongly attracts the shared electron pair of the $CH$ bond, enabling the hydrogen to be released as a proton ($H^+$) upon reaction with strong bases like sodium metal ($Na$) or sodamide ($NaNH_2$):

$$HC\equivCH + Na \to HC\equivC^-Na^+ + 12H_2 \uparrow \quad (Monosodium ethynide)$$ $$HC\equivC^-Na^+ + Na \to Na^+C^-\equivC^-Na^+ + 12H_2 \uparrow \quad (Disodium ethynide)$$ $$CH_3C\equivCH + NaNH_2 \to CH_3C\equivC^-Na^+ + NH_3 \uparrow$$

Order of Acidic Strength: $HC\equivCH > CH_2=CH_2 > CH_3CH_3$ and $HC\equivCH > CH_3C\equivCH \gg CH_3C\equivCCH_3$ (non-terminal alkynes have no acetylenic hydrogen and are non-acidic).

B. Addition Reactions of Alkynes

  • Hydrogenation: $HC\equivCH \xrightarrow[Pt/Pd/Ni]{H_2} CH_2=CH_2 \xrightarrow{H_2} CH_3CH_3$
  • Halogenation: $HC\equivCH + Br_2 \xrightarrow{CCl_4} CHBr=CHBr \xrightarrow{Br_2} CHBr_2CHBr_2$ ($1,1,2,2$-Tetrabromoethane).
  • Hydrogen Halides: Adds in two steps conforming to Markovnikov's rule to yield geminal dihalides: $$CH_3C\equivCH + HBr \to CH_3CBr=CH_2 \xrightarrow{HBr} CH_3CBr_2CH_3 \quad (2,2-Dibromopropane)$$
  • Hydration (Addition of Water): Alkynes warm with $1%\ HgSO_4$ and $40%\ H_2SO_4$ at $333 K$ to yield enols that tautomerize into carbonyl compounds: $$HC\equivCH + H_2O \xrightarrow[333 K]{Hg2+ / H^+} [CH_2=CHOH] \xrightarrow{tautomerises} CH_3CHO \quad (Ethanal / Acetaldehyde)$$ $$CH_3C\equivCH + H_2O \xrightarrow{Hg2+ / H^+} [CH_3C(OH)=CH_2] \xrightarrow{tautomerises} CH_3COCH_3 \quad (Propanone / Acetone)$$

C. Polymerisation of Ethyne

  • Linear Polymerisation: Produces polyacetylene $-(CH=CH-CH=CH)_n-$. Conducts electricity under specialized doping conditions; used for lightweight polymer battery electrodes.
  • Cyclic Polymerisation (Aromatisation): Ethyne passed through a red hot iron tube at 873 K polymerises to form benzene ($3C_2H_2 \to C_6H_6$). Vital pathway from aliphatic to aromatic chemistry!

9.5 Aromatic Hydrocarbons (Arenes)

Aromatic hydrocarbons are known as arenes (Greek: aroma = sweet smelling). Compounds containing a benzene ring are termed benzenoids (e.g. benzene, toluene, naphthalene, biphenyl); those with aromatic stability lacking a benzene ring are termed non-benzenoids (e.g. azulene, tropylium cation, cyclopentadienyl anion).

9.5.2 Structure & Resonance Stability of Benzene

Michael Faraday isolated benzene in 1825 ($C_6H_6$). In 1865, Friedrich August Kekulé proposed a hexagonal ring with alternating single and double bonds. Because benzene only yields one monosubstituted derivative and one ortho-disubstituted derivative, Kekulé proposed an oscillating equilibrium between the two canonical structures.

Resonance & Orbital Description

  • All 6 carbon atoms are $sp^2$ hybridised, forming a planar hexagonal ring with $CCC$ bond angles of $120^°$.
  • Each carbon has one unhybridised $2p_z$ orbital perpendicular to the ring plane. These 6 $p$-orbitals overlap laterally to form a completely delocalised $π$-electron cloud in the shape of two continuous doughnut-shaped rings—one above and one below the plane of the carbon atoms.
  • X-ray diffraction confirms that all six $CC$ bond lengths are identical at $\mathbf{139 pm}$, exactly intermediate between a single bond ($154 pm$) and a double bond ($133 pm$).
  • Resonance energy of benzene is $\mathbf{150.6 kJ mol-1}$ ($36 kcal mol-1$), accounting for its extraordinary stability and strong resistance to addition reactions.

9.5.3 Hückel's Rule of Aromaticity

For any planar cyclic system to be aromatic, it must satisfy three criteria:

  1. Planarity: Ring atoms must lie in the same geometric plane.
  2. Complete Delocalisation: Conjugated cyclic ring of unhybridised $p$-orbitals.
  3. Hückel's Rule: Contains $\mathbf{(4n+2)π}$ electrons, where $n = 0, 1, 2, 3 \dots$
    • $n=0 \implies 2π$ e⁻ (e.g. cyclopropenyl cation)
    • $n=1 \implies 6π$ e⁻ (e.g. benzene, cyclopentadienyl anion, pyrrole, furan, pyridine)
    • $n=2 \implies 10π$ e⁻ (e.g. naphthalene)
    • $n=3 \implies 14π$ e⁻ (e.g. anthracene, phenanthrene)

  • Decarboxylation of Sodium Benzoate: $C_6H_5COONa + NaOH \xrightarrow[Δ]{CaO} C_6H_6 + Na_2CO_3$
  • Reduction of Phenol: $C_6H_5OH + Zn dust \xrightarrow{Δ} C_6H_6 + ZnO$

9.5.5 Electrophilic Aromatic Substitution ($S_E$) Mechanism

Because benzene is an electron-rich $π$-system, it readily attracts electrophiles ($E^+$). Substitution preserves the highly stable aromatic resonance sextet, whereas addition destroys aromaticity.

General 3-Step Mechanism of $S_E$:

  1. Step 1: Generation of Electrophile ($E^+$):
    • Nitration: $HNO_3 + 2H_2SO_4 ⇌ \mathbf{NO_2^+} + H_3O^+ + 2HSO_4^-$ ($H_2SO_4$ acts as acid, $HNO_3$ acts as base!)
    • Halogenation: $Cl_2 + AlCl_3 \to \mathbf{Cl^+} + [AlCl_4]^-$
    • Friedel-Crafts Alkylation: $RCl + AlCl_3 \to \mathbf{R^+} + [AlCl_4]^-$
    • Friedel-Crafts Acylation: $RCOCl + AlCl_3 \to \mathbf{RC\overset{+}{=}O} + [AlCl_4]^-$
  2. Step 2: Attack of $E^+$ & Formation of Arenium Ion ($σ$-Complex):

    The electrophile attacks the ring, using two $π$-electrons to form a $CE$ bond at an $sp^3$ hybridised carbon. The resulting carbocation is delocalised over the remaining 5 carbon atoms and stabilised by three resonance structures.

  3. Step 3: Loss of Proton to Restore Aromaticity:

    A weak base (e.g. $HSO_4^-$ or $[AlCl_4]^-$) removes the proton from the $sp^3$ carbon, returning the electron pair to the ring to regenerate the stable $(4n+2)π$ aromatic system.

Major Electrophilic Reactions of Benzene

  • Nitration: $C_6H_6 + conc. HNO_3 \xrightarrow[323333 K]{conc. H_2SO_4} C_6H_5NO_2 + H_2O$ (Nitrobenzene)
  • Halogenation: $C_6H_6 + Cl_2 \xrightarrow{Anhyd. FeCl_3 / AlCl_3} C_6H_5Cl + HCl$ (Chlorobenzene). Excess $Cl_2$ yields hexachlorobenzene ($C_6Cl_6$).
  • Sulphonation: $C_6H_6 + H_2SO_4 (SO_3 / Oleum) \xrightarrow{Δ} C_6H_5SO_3H + H_2O$ (Benzenesulphonic acid)
  • Friedel-Crafts Alkylation: $C_6H_6 + CH_3Cl \xrightarrow{Anhyd. AlCl_3} C_6H_5CH_3 + HCl$ (Toluene).
    Rearrangement Note: Treating benzene with $1$-chloropropane gives isopropylbenzene (cumene) as major product because $1^°$ carbocation rearranges via $1,2$-hydride shift to more stable $2^°$ carbocation!
  • Friedel-Crafts Acylation: $C_6H_6 + CH_3COCl \xrightarrow{Anhyd. AlCl_3} C_6H_5COCH_3 + HCl$ (Acetophenone).

Addition & Combustion Reactions of Benzene

  • Hydrogenation: $C_6H_6 + 3H_2 \xrightarrow[Δ, high P]{Ni} C_6H12$ (Cyclohexane)
  • Chlorination under UV Light: $C_6H_6 + 3Cl_2 \xrightarrow{hν} C_6H_6Cl_6$ (Benzene Hexachloride / BHC / Gammaxane / Lindane)
  • Combustion: Burns with a smoky/sooty flame due to high carbon content ($92.3%$): $$C_6H_6 + 152O_2 \to 6CO_2 + 3H_2O$$

9.5.6 Directive Influence of Substituents

When a monosubstituted benzene undergoes further substitution, the incoming electrophile is directed to specific positions depending upon the substituent already attached:

Ortho & Para Directing Groups

Ring Activating: Donate electrons by resonance ($+R$ or $+M$), creating high electron density specifically at ortho and para positions:

Examples: $-OH, -NH_2, -NHR, -OCH_3, -CH_3, -C_2H_5$ (alkyls activate via hyperconjugation and $+I$).

Deactivating Ortho-Para Directors (Halogens): Halogens ($-F, -Cl, -Br, -I$) exhibit strong electron-withdrawing inductive effect ($-I$) which deactivates the ring overall, but their resonance ($+R$) effect directs the incoming electrophile to ortho/para positions.

Meta Directing Groups

Ring Deactivating: Withdraw electrons via $-R$ / $-M$ and $-I$ effects, placing partial positive charges at ortho and para positions. Consequently, the meta position remains comparatively electron-rich, directing electrophilic attack to meta:

Examples: $-NO_2, -CN, -CHO, -COR, -COOH, -COOR, -SO_3H$.

9.6 Carcinogenicity and Toxicity

Benzene and polynuclear aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs) containing more than two benzene rings fused together are toxic and possess cancer-producing (carcinogenic) properties.

Such polynuclear hydrocarbons are formed during the incomplete combustion of organic matter like tobacco, coal, petroleum, and charbroiled foods. Once inhaled or ingested, they enter cells and undergo biochemical oxidation by cytochrome P450 enzymes into reactive epoxide metabolites that covalently bind to DNA bases, producing mutations and initiating cancer.

Prominent Carcinogenic Hydrocarbons:

  • $1,2$-Benzanthracene
  • $3$-Methylcholanthrene
  • $1,2$-Benzpyrene and $9,10$-Dimethyl-1,2-benzanthracene

9.1
How do you account for the formation of ethane during chlorination of methane?

Solution:

Chlorination of methane proceeds via a free radical chain mechanism consisting of initiation, propagation, and termination steps.

  • Initiation: $Cl_2 \xrightarrow{hν} 2Cl^\bullet$
  • Propagation: $$CH_4 + Cl^\bullet \to CH_3^\bullet + HCl$$ $$CH_3^\bullet + Cl_2 \to CH_3Cl + Cl^\bullet$$
  • Termination: As the reaction progresses and reactant concentration decreases, the probability of free radicals colliding with each other increases. This coupling reaction in the chain termination step accounts for the presence of trace amounts of ethane as an impurity/byproduct during the chlorination of methane.

9.10
Why is benzene extraordinarily stable though it contains three double bonds?

Solution:

Benzene is exceptionally stable because of cyclic $π$-electron resonance delocalisation:

  1. The six $π$-electrons are not fixed between pairs of carbon atoms; instead, they are completely delocalised into a continuous cyclic ring forming two doughnut-shaped electron clouds above and below the planar hexagonal ring.
  2. This delocalisation gives benzene an enormous resonance energy of $150.6 kJ mol-1$ ($36 kcal mol-1$).
  3. Because all six $CC$ bonds have equal bond length ($139 pm$) and partial double-bond character, addition reactions would disrupt the closed aromatic sextet and forfeit this massive resonance stabilisation. Consequently, benzene resists addition and preferentially undergoes electrophilic substitution.
9.11
What are the necessary conditions for any system to be aromatic?

Solution:

For a compound to be aromatic, it must fulfill the following criteria:

  1. Cyclic Structure: The molecule must be cyclic.
  2. Planarity: Ring atoms must be coplanar (all ring atoms are $sp^2$ or $sp$ hybridised) to permit unhindered lateral overlap of $p$-orbitals.
  3. Complete Conjugation: A continuous, uninterrupted loop of overlapping $p$-orbitals around the entire perimeter of the ring.
  4. Hückel's Rule: The cyclic conjugated $π$-system must contain $(4n+2)π$ electrons, where $n = 0, 1, 2, 3 \dots$ ($2, 6, 10, 14 \dots$ $π$-electrons).

Solution:

  1. Cyclooctatetraene ($C_8H_8$): Contains $8\ π$-electrons, which equals $4n$ ($n=2$), violating Hückel's $(4n+2)$ rule. Moreover, to minimize anti-aromatic destabilization, it adopts a non-planar tub-shaped conformation, preventing complete planar delocalisation. Hence, it is non-aromatic.
  2. Cyclopentadiene: Contains an $sp^3$ hybridised $-CH_2-$ carbon atom in the ring. This breaks the continuous cyclic conjugation of $p$-orbitals, rendering the molecule non-aromatic.
  3. Cycloheptatrienyl radical: Has 7 $π$-electrons. This is an odd number of electrons, which does not satisfy Hückel's $(4n+2)$ rule ($4n+2$ gives $2, 6, 10$). Hence, it is non-aromatic. (In contrast, the cycloheptatrienyl cation has $6\ π$-electrons and is aromatic).

9.15
What effect does branching of an alkane chain have on its boiling point?

Solution:

Branching of an alkane chain lowers its boiling point.

Scientific Reason: As branching increases, the molecular geometry becomes more spherical and compact. A sphere has the minimum surface area for a given volume. This diminished surface area of contact reduces the magnitude of intermolecular van der Waals (London dispersion) forces between adjacent molecules. Consequently, less thermal energy is required to overcome intermolecular attractions, resulting in a lower boiling point.

Example: Among isomeric pentanes ($C_5H12$):
$n$-Pentane (unbranched, b.p. $309.1 K$) > Isopentane (1 branch, b.p. $301.0 K$) > Neopentane (2 branches, b.p. $282.5 K$).

  2. $CH_3CH_2Br + 2Na + BrCH_2CH_3 \to CH_3CH_2CH_2CH_3 + 2NaBr \quad (n-Butane, b.p. 273 K)$
  3. $CH_3Br + 2Na + BrCH_2CH_3 \to CH_3CH_2CH_3 + 2NaBr \quad (Propane, b.p. 231 K)$

Conclusion: A mixture of three alkanes (ethane, propane, and butane) is obtained. Because these homologues have close boiling points, fractional distillation is difficult and the yield of the desired odd-carbon alkane is extremely poor. Hence, the Wurtz reaction is unsuitable for odd-carbon alkanes.
