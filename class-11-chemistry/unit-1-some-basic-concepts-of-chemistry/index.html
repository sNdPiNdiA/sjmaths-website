FOUNDATION

What is Chemistry?

Chemistry studies the preparation, properties, structure and reactions of material substances. It connects with physics, biology, geology and many other fields.

Development in one line: Ancient Indian chemical practices included metallurgy, medicine, glass, dyes, cosmetics and fermentation; chemistry developed through alchemy and iatrochemistry, and modern chemistry took shape in 18th-century Europe.

1.1 Importance of Chemistry

Chemistry contributes to food, healthcare, agriculture, medicines, materials, industries, national economy and environmental problem-solving. It also enables new materials with magnetic, electrical and optical properties.

LifeFood, medicines, healthcare, detergents and everyday materials.
IndustryFertilisers, acids, alkalis, dyes, polymers, metals and alloys.
TechnologySuperconducting ceramics, conducting polymers and optical fibres.
EnvironmentSafer refrigerants and study of greenhouse gases and degradation.
Mnemonic — “FARM-E”
Food • Agriculture • Remedies • Materials • Environment

1.2 NATURE OF MATTER

Matter, states and classification

What is matter?

Anything that has mass and occupies space is matter.

States of matter

SolidParticles close and ordered; definite shape and definite volume.
LiquidParticles close but mobile; definite volume, no definite shape.
GasParticles far apart and fast-moving; neither definite shape nor volume.

Temperature and pressure can interconvert the states. Heating generally gives solid → liquid → gas; cooling reverses the process.

Classification of matter

Matter→Pure substanceorMixture
Pure substanceOne kind of constituent particle; fixed composition. It may be an element or compound.
MixtureTwo or more pure substances; variable composition. It may be homogeneous or heterogeneous.
ElementParticles contain one type of atom; may occur as atoms or molecules.
CompoundDifferent elements combine in a fixed ratio; constituents can be separated chemically, not physically.
Mnemonic — “PME-C”
Pure/Mixture → Element/Compound. Homogeneous = uniform; heterogeneous = non-uniform.

1.3 PROPERTIES & MEASUREMENT

Measure chemistry quantitatively

Physical vs chemical properties

Physical: observed/measured without changing identity (colour, odour, melting point, boiling point, density). Chemical: involve chemical change (composition, combustibility, reaction with acids/bases).

Measurement

A quantitative observation is written as number + unit, e.g. 6 m. The SI system provides a common international standard.

Seven SI base quantities

Quantity SI unit Symbol
Length metre m
Mass kilogram kg
Time second s
Electric current ampere A
Thermodynamic temperature kelvin K
Amount of substance mole mol
Luminous intensity candela cd

Useful quantities

Densityρ = mass / volume
Volume1 L = 1000 mL = 1000 cm³ = 1 dm³
TemperatureK = °C + 273.15
Mass vs weightMass is amount of matter; weight is gravitational force and can vary with location.

SI prefixes

Very small/large quantities use prefixes such as yocto (10⁻²⁴), zepto (10⁻²¹), atto (10⁻¹⁸), femto (10⁻¹⁵), pico (10⁻¹²), nano (10⁻⁹), micro (10⁻⁶), milli (10⁻³), centi (10⁻²), deci (10⁻¹), deca (10¹), hecto (10²), kilo (10³), mega (10⁶), giga (10⁹), tera (10¹²), peta (10¹⁵), exa (10¹⁸), zetta (10²¹), yotta (10²⁴).

Memory line — “kg is SI mass; mol is SI amount.”
Do not confuse litre with an SI base unit: volume is derived and L is a commonly used non-SI unit.

1.4 UNCERTAINTY

Scientific notation, significant figures and dimensions

Scientific notation

Write a number as N × 10ⁿ, where N lies from 1 to less than 10. Moving the decimal left gives a positive exponent; moving it right gives a negative exponent.

Example: 232.508 = 2.32508 × 10²; 0.00016 = 1.6 × 10⁻⁴.

Significant figures

  1. All non-zero digits are significant.
  2. Zeros before the first non-zero digit are not significant.
  3. Zeros between non-zero digits are significant.
  4. Terminal zeros are significant when they are to the right of a decimal point; ambiguous terminal zeros are best written in scientific notation.
  5. Exact counted numbers have unlimited significant figures.
  6. All digits shown in scientific notation are significant.

Precision vs accuracy

Precision = closeness of repeated measurements to one another. Accuracy = closeness to the true value.

Mnemonic — “P = Pair together; A = Actual value.”

Rules for calculations

Addition/SubtractionKeep the result to the smallest number of decimal places.
Multiplication/DivisionKeep the result to the smallest number of significant figures.
RoundingRemoved digit >5: increase previous digit; <5: unchanged; 5: retain even previous digit / increase odd previous digit.

Dimensional analysis

Use conversion factors equal to 1 so unwanted units cancel. Choose the factor orientation that leaves the desired unit.

Example: 3 in × (2.54 cm / 1 in) = 7.62 cm.
Example: 2 days × 24 h/day × 60 min/h × 60 s/min = 172800 s.

1.5 LAWS

Laws of Chemical Combinations

1. Law of Conservation of Mass — Lavoisier

In physical and chemical changes there is no net change in mass; matter is neither created nor destroyed.

2. Law of Definite Proportions — Proust

A given compound always contains the same elements in the same proportion by mass, irrespective of source.

3. Law of Multiple Proportions — Dalton

If two elements form more than one compound, the masses of one element combining with a fixed mass of the other are in a ratio of small whole numbers.

Example: for fixed 2 g H, O masses 16 g and 32 g in H₂O and H₂O₂ give 1:2.

4. Gay Lussac’s Law of Gaseous Volumes

When gases react or are produced, their volumes are in simple whole-number ratios when measured at the same temperature and pressure.

Example: 100 mL H₂ + 50 mL O₂ → 100 mL water vapour, ratio 2:1.

5. Avogadro’s Law

Equal volumes of all gases at the same temperature and pressure contain equal numbers of molecules.

Mnemonic — “Con-D-M-G-A”
Conservation • Definite • Multiple • Gaseous volumes • Avogadro

1.6 DALTON

Dalton’s Atomic Theory

  1. Matter consists of indivisible atoms.
  2. Atoms of a given element were proposed to have identical properties and mass; atoms of different elements differ in mass.
  3. Compounds form when atoms of different elements combine in fixed ratios.
  4. Chemical reactions reorganise atoms; atoms are neither created nor destroyed.
Limitation: Dalton’s theory could not explain gaseous volume laws or why atoms combine; later discoveries required refinement of the atomic model.
Mnemonic — “Same, Different, Fixed, Rearrange”

1.7 ATOMIC & MOLECULAR MASSES

Mass at the atomic scale

Atomic mass unit

Carbon-12 is the standard. One unified mass unit, u, equals exactly one-twelfth of the mass of one carbon-12 atom. \(1u \approx 1.66056\times10^{-24}\,g\).

Average atomic mass

For naturally occurring isotopes, average atomic mass is the abundance-weighted average:

Average atomic mass = Σ (fractional abundance × isotopic mass)
NCERT example: Carbon isotopes give an average atomic mass of about 12.011 u.

Molecular mass

Sum the atomic masses of all atoms in a molecule. Example: CH₄ = 12.011 + 4(1.008) = 16.043 u; H₂O ≈ 18.02 u.

Formula mass

Ionic solids such as NaCl do not consist of discrete molecules. Use formula mass: NaCl = 23.0 + 35.5 = 58.5 u.

1.8 MOLE CONCEPT

Counting microscopic entities

A mole is a counting unit for atoms, molecules, ions, electrons or other specified entities.

\(1\ mol = 6.02214076\times10^{23}\) entities
Moles from particlesn = N / Nₐ
Particles from molesN = nNₐ
Moles from massn = m / M
Mass from molesm = nM

Molar mass is the mass of one mole in g mol⁻¹. Its numerical value equals the atomic/molecular/formula mass expressed in u.

NCERT Problem 1.1: C₆H₁₂O₆ = 6(12.011)+12(1.008)+6(16.00)=180.162 u.
Mnemonic — “Mole = bridge”
Particles ↔ moles ↔ mass. Convert to moles before using stoichiometric ratios.

1.9 PERCENTAGE COMPOSITION

Mass percentage and formula determination

Mass % of element = (mass of element in one mole of compound / molar mass of compound) × 100
Water: H = 11.18%; O = 88.79% (using the chapter values).

Empirical vs molecular formula

Empirical formula = simplest whole-number ratio of atoms. Molecular formula = actual number of each type of atom in a molecule.

Step 1Assume 100 g when percentages are given.
Step 2Convert each element’s mass to moles.
Step 3Divide all mole values by the smallest value.
Step 4Convert to the simplest whole-number ratio and write empirical formula.
Step 5Empirical formula mass; \(n =\) molar mass / empirical formula mass; multiply subscripts by n.
NCERT Problem 1.2: 4.07% H, 24.27% C, 71.65% Cl gives empirical formula CH₂Cl; empirical mass ≈ 49.48 g mol⁻¹; with molar mass 98.96, n=2, so molecular formula C₂H₄Cl₂.
Mnemonic — “% → g → mol → ÷smallest → whole → formula → mass ratio”

1.10 STOICHIOMETRY

Balanced equations → quantitative chemistry

Stoichiometry deals with calculating amounts of reactants and products in a chemical reaction. A balanced equation gives mole/particle relationships through its coefficients.

Balancing equations

Balance atoms using coefficients; never change subscripts. Example:

C₃H₈ + 5O₂ → 3CO₂ + 4H₂O
1Write correct formulas.
2Balance carbon.
3Balance hydrogen.
4Balance oxygen.
5Verify every element.

Stoichiometric pathway

Given quantity→moles→mole ratio→required moles→asked unit
Problem 1.3: CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O. 16 g CH₄ = 1 mol; 1 mol CH₄ gives 2 mol H₂O = 36 g H₂O.
Problem 1.4: 22 g CO₂ = 0.5 mol CO₂, so 0.5 mol CH₄ is required.

Limiting reagent

The reactant consumed first limits product formation. Compare the amount available with the amount required by the balanced equation; calculate product using the limiting reactant.

Problem 1.5: For N₂ + 3H₂ → 2NH₃, 50.0 kg N₂ gives 1.786×10³ mol N₂ and 10.0 kg H₂ gives 4.96×10³ mol H₂. H₂ is limiting; NH₃ formed ≈ 3.30×10³ mol = 56.1 kg.

Concentration of solutions

Mass percent (w/w)Mass of solute / mass of solution × 100.
Mole fraction\(\chi_A=n_A/(n_A+n_B+\cdots)\); fractions of all components sum to 1.
Molarity (M)Moles of solute / volume of solution in litres. Temperature-dependent because volume changes.
Molality (m)Moles of solute / mass of solvent in kg. Temperature-independent because mass is unchanged.
Dilution: \(M_1V_1=M_2V_2\). The moles of solute stay constant during dilution; solvent amount changes.
Mnemonic — “Mole First!”
For reaction calculations: balance → convert to moles → ratio → convert back.

Exam traps

  • Do not change subscripts while balancing equations.
  • Do not confuse molarity (per litre of solution) with molality (per kg of solvent).
  • Do not treat leading zeros as significant figures.
  • For addition/subtraction use decimal-place rule; for multiplication/division use significant-figure rule.
  • For limiting reagent, do not choose the reactant with the smaller mass automatically; compare stoichiometric amounts.
  • Use empirical formula mass before finding the multiplier for a molecular formula.
