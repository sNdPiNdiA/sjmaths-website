Exercise 3.2: Syllogism

Master categorical propositions, Venn diagram models, mediate deductions, immediate conversions, and valid deductive conclusions.

Core Theoretical Principles

Systematic principles and analytical strategies prescribed by the CBSE curriculum for Syllogism.

Standard Proposition Classifications:
Four fundamental forms:
• A (Universal Affirmative): 'All S are P'
• E (Universal Negative): 'No S is P'
• I (Particular Affirmative): 'Some S are P'
• O (Particular Negative): 'Some S are not P'
Immediate Conversion Laws:
• A-type converts by limitation to: 'Some P are S'
• E-type converts exactly to: 'No P is S'
• I-type converts exactly to: 'Some P are S'
• O-type has no valid standard conversion.
Deductive Rules & Possibility Analysis:
• Two negative or two particular premises yield no valid deduction.
• If one premise is negative, the conclusion must be negative.
• Definite conclusion vs. Possibility ('can be'): If an intersection is not forbidden by any premise, the possibility holds.

Solved Illustrative Examples

Example 1 (Universal & Particular Deduction)
Statements: All pencils are sticks. Some sticks are boxes.
Conclusions: I. Some boxes are pencils. II. All pencils are boxes.
Analysis:
• The set of pencils is entirely inside sticks.
• The set of boxes overlaps with sticks, but is not required to touch pencils.
• Conclusion I ('Some boxes are pencils') is possible but not definitely certain.
• Conclusion II ('All pencils are boxes') is definitely false.
Answer: Neither (I) nor (II) follows.
Example 2 (Possibility 'Can Be')
Statements: All doctors are humans. Some humans are teachers.
Conclusions: I. Some doctors can be teachers. II. Some teachers are doctors.
Analysis:
• Doctors are a subset of humans. Humans overlap with teachers.
• Since there is no contradiction or negative constraint between doctors and teachers, the possibility holds: Some doctors can be teachers.
• However, as a definite certainty, conclusion II does not necessarily follow.
Answer: Only conclusion (I) follows.
Example 3 (Negative Proposition)
Statements: No bird is a mammal. All mammals are animals.
Conclusions: I. No bird is an animal. II. No mammal is a bird.
Analysis:
• Statement: 'No bird is a mammal' is an E-type proposition.
• E-type converts directly to: 'No mammal is a bird'. Hence Conclusion II follows.
• Conclusion I does not follow because birds could overlap with animals outside mammals.
Answer: Only conclusion (II) follows.

CYP 3.2.1 CBSE Textbook Question
Statements: Some fruits are sweets. All sweets are tasty.
Conclusions: I. Some fruits are tasty. II. All fruits are tasty.
Fruits overlap sweets (I). Sweets are contained in tasty (A). $\implies$ Intersection of fruits and sweets is inside tasty. Hence, 'Some fruits are tasty' follows definitely. 'All fruits are tasty' does not follow.
Answer: Only conclusion (I) follows.
CYP 3.2.2 CBSE Textbook Question
Statements: All books are papers. Some papers are files.
Conclusions: I. Some files are books. II. Some papers are books.
A-type proposition 'All books are papers' converts by limitation to 'Some papers are books'. Hence II follows. The overlap between papers and files does not necessarily reach books. Hence I does not follow.
Answer: Only conclusion (II) follows.
CYP 3.2.3 CBSE Textbook Question
Statements: No pen is a marker. Some markers are erasers.
Conclusions: I. Some erasers are pens. II. No pen is an eraser.
Erasers overlap markers, but their relation to pens is unconstrained. Either can be true, but neither follows with certainty. Since 'Some erasers are pens' and 'No pen is an eraser' form a complementary pair, either (I) or (II) or neither follows depending on context.
Answer: Neither follows definitely.
CYP 3.2.4 CBSE Board 3-Premise Drill

Statements:
1. All cars are buses.
2. Some buses are trucks.
3. No truck is a train.
Conclusions:
I. Some buses are not trains.
II. Some cars are trucks.
III. No train is a car.

View Step-by-Step Solution & Venn Analysis

Venn Diagram Construction:

  • Statement 1 ($Cars \subseteq Buses$): The circle of 'Cars' is entirely enclosed inside 'Buses'.
  • Statement 2 ($Buses \cap Trucks \neq \emptyset$): 'Buses' and 'Trucks' share an overlapping region. The circle of 'Trucks' may or may not overlap with 'Cars'.
  • Statement 3 ($Trucks \cap Trains = \emptyset$): 'Trucks' and 'Trains' are completely disjoint.

Evaluating Conclusions:

  • Conclusion I (Some buses are not trains): Those buses which are inside the 'Trucks' circle cannot overlap with 'Trains' (since no truck is a train). Because Statement 2 guarantees there are buses that are trucks, those specific buses can never be trains. Therefore, Conclusion I definitely follows.
  • Conclusion II (Some cars are trucks): While 'Trucks' overlaps 'Buses', it is not required to touch 'Cars'. A Venn diagram can be drawn where 'Trucks' is completely outside 'Cars'. Hence, Conclusion II is only a possibility, not a certainty. (Does NOT follow).
  • Conclusion III (No train is a car): 'Trains' cannot touch 'Trucks', but 'Trains' could potentially intersect 'Cars' without touching 'Trucks'. Hence, Conclusion III does NOT definitely follow.

Final Answer: Only Conclusion I logically follows.

CYP 3.2.5 Immediate Inference Drill

Given the statement: "No circle is a triangle."
Evaluate which of the following immediate inferences are valid:
(i) No triangle is a circle.
(ii) Some circles are not triangles.
(iii) All triangles are circles.

View Step-by-Step Solution

The given statement is an E-type proposition (Universal Negative): $Circle \cap Triangle = \emptyset$.

  • (i) Simple Conversion: An E-proposition converts simply to another E-proposition ($E \implies E$). If $A \cap B = \emptyset$, then $B \cap A = \emptyset$. Hence, "No triangle is a circle" is strictly valid.
  • (ii) Subalternation (Conversion by Limitation): If no circles are triangles, it logically follows that any non-empty sub-part of circles is also not triangles ($E \implies O$). Hence, "Some circles are not triangles" is valid.
  • (iii) Contrary Assertion: "All triangles are circles" contradicts the universal negative. (Invalid).

Final Answer: Inferences (i) and (ii) are logically valid.

Section A: Conceptual & Objective Drill 3 Questions • 1 Mark Each

Q1. Term Distribution in Propositions 1 Mark

Identify which term (Subject or Predicate) is distributed in the universal negative proposition: "No politician is honest."

Show Model Solution

In an E-type proposition (Universal Negative, No S is P), both the Subject and the Predicate are distributed because the statement makes a complete assertion of total exclusion regarding all members of both classes.

Answer: Both terms (Politicians and Honest persons) are distributed.

Q2. Fallacy of Undistributed Middle 1 Mark

Given the premises: "Some managers are accountants" and "Some accountants are writers." Explain why no valid conclusion can be drawn between managers and writers.

Show Model Solution

Both premises are I-type propositions (Particular Affirmative). In an I-proposition, neither the subject nor the predicate is distributed. The middle term (accountants) is undistributed in both premises.

By the fundamental rule of syllogism, the middle term must be distributed at least once to establish a logical bridge. Failure to do so commits the Fallacy of the Undistributed Middle.

Answer: No conclusion follows because the middle term 'accountants' is not distributed in either premise.

Q3. Valid Conversion of Universal Affirmative 1 Mark

State whether the universal affirmative statement "All roses are flowers" can be converted simply to "All flowers are roses". If not, state its correct conversion.

Show Model Solution

An A-type proposition (All S is P) cannot undergo simple conversion because the predicate term $P$ is undistributed in the original statement. It can only be converted by limitation (per accidens) into an I-type proposition.

Correct Conversion: "Some flowers are roses."

Section B: Applied Analytical Problem Sets 2 Questions • 2 Marks Each

Q4. Two-Statement Syllogism Verification 2 Marks

Statements: All poets are day-dreamers. All painters are day-dreamers.
Conclusions:
I. All poets are painters.
II. Some day-dreamers are not poets.
Determine which conclusion(s) logically follow using Venn diagram analysis.

Show Model Solution

Venn Diagram Analysis:

  • $Poets \subseteq Day\text{-}dreamers$ (Circle of Poets is inside Day-dreamers).
  • $Painters \subseteq Day\text{-}dreamers$ (Circle of Painters is inside Day-dreamers).

Evaluation:

  • Conclusion I: Both sets (Poets and Painters) are subsets of Day-dreamers, but there is no requirement that they intersect each other. They could be completely disjoint inside Day-dreamers. Hence, Conclusion I does not follow.
  • Conclusion II: In classical formal logic, if all Day-dreamers happen to be Poets ($Day\text{-}dreamers = Poets$), then "Some day-dreamers are not poets" would be false. Unless specified that $Poets$ is a proper subset, Conclusion II cannot be definitively asserted.

Answer: Neither Conclusion I nor II follows.

Q5. Complementary Pairs ("Either-Or") 2 Marks

Statements: Some pencils are pens. Some pens are erasers.
Conclusions:
I. Some pencils are erasers.
II. No pencil is an eraser.
State the relationship between Conclusions I and II and determine if an "Either-Or" condition holds.

Show Model Solution

Test for Complementary Pair:

  1. Both conclusions have the identical Subject (pencils) and Predicate (erasers).
  2. Conclusion I is a Particular Affirmative (I-type), and Conclusion II is a Universal Negative (E-type). (I-E forms a standard contradictory pair).
  3. In the basic Venn diagram, since the middle term 'pens' is undistributed, neither Conclusion I nor Conclusion II is definitely true on its own.
  4. However, in any conceivable reality, pencils and erasers must either share at least one element ($I$ is true) or be completely disjoint ($E$ is true). Both cannot be simultaneously false, nor simultaneously true.

Answer: Either Conclusion I or Conclusion II follows.

Section C: Comprehensive Board-Level Problem 1 Question • 4 Marks

Q6. Three-Premise Corporate Syllogism 4 Marks

Statements:
1. All directors are shareholders.
2. Some shareholders are investors.
3. No investor is an employee.
Conclusions:
I. Some shareholders are not employees.
II. No director is an employee.
III. Some directors are investors.
IV. Some investors are shareholders.
(i) Construct the primary Venn diagram and alternate configurations.
(ii) Determine the truth value of each conclusion.
(iii) State the definitive final set of valid conclusions.

Show Model Solution

(i) Diagrammatic Setup:

  • $Directors \subseteq Shareholders$ (D is inside S).
  • $Shareholders \cap Investors \neq \emptyset$ (S and I overlap). The overlap region $S \cap I$ may or may not intersect D.
  • $Investors \cap Employees = \emptyset$ (I and E circles never touch). E may or may not intersect S or D.

(ii) Evaluation of Conclusions:

  • Conclusion I (Some shareholders are not employees): Statement 2 guarantees there is a non-empty group of individuals who are both Shareholders and Investors ($S \cap I$). Since Statement 3 strictly prohibits any Investor from being an Employee, this common group ($S \cap I$) can NEVER be Employees. Thus, there definitely exist Shareholders who are not employees. $\implies$ TRUE (Valid).
  • Conclusion II (No director is an employee): Circle E cannot touch I, but E can freely overlap with D and S. Thus, directors could be employees. $\implies$ FALSE (Not certain).
  • Conclusion III (Some directors are investors): The overlap between S and I is not required to touch D. $\implies$ FALSE (Not certain).
  • Conclusion IV (Some investors are shareholders): Statement 2 ("Some shareholders are investors") is an I-type proposition. Simple conversion yields "Some investors are shareholders". $\implies$ TRUE (Valid).

(iii) Final Conclusion: Only Conclusions I and IV follow.

The Four Categorical Propositions (A, E, I, O)

Type Form Quantity & Quality Set Representation Distributed Terms
A All $S$ are $P$ Universal Affirmative $S \subseteq P$ Subject only
E No $S$ is $P$ Universal Negative $S \cap P = \emptyset$ Both Subject & Predicate
I Some $S$ are $P$ Particular Affirmative $S \cap P \neq \emptyset$ Neither term
O Some $S$ are not $P$ Particular Negative $S - P \neq \emptyset$ Predicate only

The 6 Golden Rules of Valid Deductions

1. Middle Term Distribution:

The middle term must be distributed in at least one premise; otherwise, no valid connection exists (Undistributed Middle Fallacy).

2. Negative Premise Rule:

If one premise is negative (E or O), the conclusion MUST be negative. Two negative premises yield NO valid conclusion.

3. Particular Premise Rule:

If one premise is particular (I or O), the conclusion MUST be particular. Two particular premises yield NO valid conclusion.

4. Term Distribution in Conclusion:

No term can be distributed in the conclusion unless it was already distributed in the premises.

Checklist for the "Either-Or" Complementary Case

All three conditions must hold simultaneously:

  • The Subject and Predicate in both conclusions must be identical.
  • One conclusion must be affirmative and the other negative (Valid pairs: I & E, I & O, or A & O). Note: A & E is NOT complementary!
  • Both individual conclusions must be uncertain (doubtful) based on the primary Venn diagram.

Explanation: Premise combination $I + A = I$. Those kings who are queens are necessarily inside 'beautiful'. Hence, "Some kings are beautiful" definitely follows.

Correct Answer: (B) No herbivore is a tiger
Explanation: An E-proposition converts simply: "No S is P" converts to "No P is S".

Section B: Short Answer Type I (Q5 – Q6) 2 Questions • 2 Marks Each

Question 5 2 Marks

Statements: No apple is a mango. All mangoes are bananas.
Conclusions:
I. Some bananas are not apples.
II. No apple is a banana.
Determine the validity of both conclusions with justifications.

Show Step-by-Step Solution & Marking Scheme

Step 1: Analyze Premise Sets [1 Mark]:
$Apple \cap Mango = \emptyset$ and $Mango \subseteq Banana$.
Since all mangoes are inside bananas, that portion of bananas which contains mangoes can NEVER intersect apples (as no apple is a mango).

Step 2: Evaluate Conclusions [1 Mark]:
- Conclusion I: Those bananas which are mangoes cannot be apples. Hence, "Some bananas are not apples" logically follows.
- Conclusion II: Apples cannot touch mangoes, but apples could intersect the rest of bananas outside mangoes. Hence, "No apple is a banana" does not follow.
Answer: Only Conclusion I follows.

Question 6 2 Marks

Explain the difference between a Contradictory Pair and a Contrary Pair in categorical propositions, giving one example of each.

Show Step-by-Step Solution & Marking Scheme

Contradictory Propositions [1 Mark]: Two propositions are contradictory if one is the exact denial of the other; they cannot both be true and cannot both be false at the same time. Valid pairs: A and O, or E and I.
Example: "All metals are solid" (A) and "Some metals are not solid" (O).

Contrary Propositions [1 Mark]: Two universal propositions with the same terms (A and E) that cannot both be true, but CAN both be false.
Example: "All swans are white" (A) and "No swans are white" (E).

Question 7 3 Marks

Statements:
1. Some doctors are researchers.
2. All researchers are scientists.
3. Some scientists are professors.
Conclusions:
I. Some doctors are scientists.
II. Some scientists are doctors.
III. Some researchers are professors.
Evaluate all conclusions with full Venn deduction proofs.

Show Step-by-Step Solution & Marking Scheme

Venn Diagram Verification [2 Marks]:
- $Doctors \cap Researchers \neq \emptyset$.
- $Researchers \subseteq Scientists$. Because Researchers is completely inside Scientists, the overlapping region between Doctors and Researchers is also automatically enclosed inside Scientists.
- Therefore, $Doctors \cap Scientists \neq \emptyset$.
- Scientists and Professors overlap, but there is no requirement that this overlap intersects Researchers.

Evaluating Conclusions [1 Mark]:
- Conclusion I: Valid (proven above).
- Conclusion II: Valid (Simple conversion of Conclusion I: $I \implies I$).
- Conclusion III: Invalid (Researchers and Professors may be disjoint).
Answer: Both Conclusions I and II follow.

Question 8 3 Marks

State the formal definition of Subalternation and prove why "Some dogs are pets" logically follows from "All dogs are pets" in classical categorical logic.

Show Step-by-Step Solution & Marking Scheme

Definition of Subalternation [1.5 Marks]:
Subalternation is the logical relationship between a universal proposition (superaltern: A or E) and its corresponding particular proposition of the same quality (subaltern: I or O), having the identical subject and predicate.

Proof [1.5 Marks]:
If "All $S$ are $P$" is true, then the entire set $S$ is a subset of $P$ ($S \subseteq P$). Assuming existential import (the class $S$ is non-empty), any non-empty sample $x \in S$ must also belong to $P$ ($x \in P$). Hence, there exists at least one $S$ that is $P$, making "Some $S$ are $P$" undeniably true.

Section D: Case-Based Integrated Problem (Q9 – Q10) 2 Sub-parts • 4 Marks Total

Question 9 & 10 Case Study Case Study • 4 Marks

Case Background: Corporate Compliance Audit
A corporate internal audit team establishes regulatory rules governing senior executive access. The compliance bylaws state:
1. All authorized officers are certified chartered accountants.
2. No certified chartered accountant is an external contractor.
3. Some external contractors are software vendors.

Q9 (2 Marks): An audit officer concludes: "No authorized officer is an external contractor." Prove whether this compliance finding is logically valid or invalid using set-theoretic relations.
Q10 (2 Marks): The board claims: "Some software vendors are not authorized officers." Determine if the board's statement is definitively guaranteed by the compliance bylaws.

Show Step-by-Step Solution & Marking Scheme

Solution to Q9 [2 Marks]:
Let $A = \text{Authorized Officers}$, $C = \text{Chartered Accountants}$, and $E = \text{External Contractors}$.
Bylaw 1: $A \subseteq C$.
Bylaw 2: $C \cap E = \emptyset$.
Since $A$ is entirely contained within $C$, and $C$ has no common elements with $E$, $A$ can have zero common elements with $E$ ($A \cap E = \emptyset$).
Conclusion: The finding "No authorized officer is an external contractor" is strictly valid.

Solution to Q10 [2 Marks]:
Let $V = \text{Software Vendors}$.
Bylaw 3 guarantees that $E \cap V \neq \emptyset$ (there exist software vendors who are external contractors).
From Q9, no external contractor can be an authorized officer ($E \cap A = \emptyset$).
Therefore, those software vendors who are external contractors can NEVER be authorized officers.
Conclusion: The board's statement "Some software vendors are not authorized officers" is definitively guaranteed (strictly valid).
