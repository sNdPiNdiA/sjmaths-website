8.4 Combinations: Selection Theory, Committee Formation, and Geometric Counting

In Applied Mathematics, corporate governance, portfolio asset selection, and statistical sampling, a combination represents a collection of items chosen from a broader pool where order does not matter. Choosing a project team of 4 engineers from a pool of 10 is fundamentally a combination problem, as the order of selection has no bearing on team composition.

Fundamental Formulae & Core Theorems

  • Definition: The number of combinations (selections) of \(n\) distinct objects taken \(r\) at a time is denoted by \(^n C_r\) or \(\binom{n}{r}\): \[ ^n C_r = \frac{n!}{r! (n - r)!} = \frac{^n P_r}{r!} \quad (0 \le r \le n) \]
  • Boundary Values: \[ ^n C_0 = 1, \quad ^n C_n = 1, \quad ^n C_1 = n \]
  • Complementary Selection Theorem: Selecting \(r\) objects to include is mathematically identical to selecting \((n - r)\) objects to reject: \[ ^n C_r = ^n C_{n - r} \]
  • Equivalence Property: \[ ^n C_x = ^n C_y \implies x = y \quad \text{or} \quad x + y = n \]
  • Pascal's Identity (Addition Formula): \[ ^n C_r + ^n C_{r - 1} = {}^{n + 1} C_r \]
  • Sum of All Combinations: The total number of ways to choose any number of items from \(n\) distinct objects (from 0 to \(n\)): \[ \sum_{r=0}^n \binom{n}{r} = \binom{n}{0} + \binom{n}{1} + \dots + \binom{n}{n} = 2^n \] Ways to select at least one object: \(2^n - 1\).

Comprehensive Corporate Worked Example: Audit Committee Selection

Scenario: A corporate board of directors consists of 7 Senior Partners and 5 Independent Directors. An internal audit committee of 5 members is to be formed.

In how many ways can the committee be constituted if:

  1. There are no restrictions on composition?
  2. The committee must consist of exactly 3 Senior Partners and 2 Independent Directors?
  3. The committee must contain at least 3 Independent Directors?

Solution:

  1. No restrictions: Choose 5 members from the total 12 directors: \[ \binom{12}{5} = \frac{12 \times 11 \times 10 \times 9 \times 8}{5 \times 4 \times 3 \times 2 \times 1} = 792 \text{ ways.} \]
  2. Exactly 3 Senior Partners and 2 Independent Directors: \[ \binom{7}{3} \times \binom{5}{2} = \frac{7 \times 6 \times 5}{6} \times \frac{5 \times 4}{2} = 35 \times 10 = 350 \text{ ways.} \]
  3. At least 3 Independent Directors (Mutually exclusive cases):
    • Case A (3 Independent, 2 Senior): \(\binom{5}{3} \times \binom{7}{2} = 10 \times 21 = 210\) ways.
    • Case B (4 Independent, 1 Senior): \(\binom{5}{4} \times \binom{7}{1} = 5 \times 7 = 35\) ways.
    • Case C (5 Independent, 0 Senior): \(\binom{5}{5} \times \binom{7}{0} = 1 \times 1 = 1\) way.
    By the Addition Principle: \[ \text{Total Ways} = 210 + 35 + 1 = 246 \text{ ways.} \]

Problem 1 (Equivalence Property in Combinations)

If \(^{18}C_r = {}^{18}C_{r + 2}\), find the value of \(^r C_5\).

View Step-by-Step Solution

Solution:

We know that if \(^n C_x = ^n C_y\), then either \(x = y\) or \(x + y = n\).

Since \(r \neq r + 2\), we must have: \[ r + (r + 2) = 18 \implies 2r + 2 = 18 \implies 2r = 16 \implies r = 8. \] Now find \(^r C_5\) for \(r = 8\): \[ ^8 C_5 = ^8 C_{8 - 5} = ^8 C_3 = \frac{8 \times 7 \times 6}{3 \times 2 \times 1} = 56. \] Answer: \(^r C_5 = 56\).

Problem 2 (Collinear Points and Triangle Counting)

A plane contains 12 points, out of which 5 points are strictly collinear. How many distinct:

  1. Straight lines can be formed joining pairs of these points?
  2. Triangles can be formed having these points as vertices?
View Step-by-Step Solution

Solution:

Here \(n = 12\) and \(m = 5\) collinear points.

Part (a): Straight lines:

\[ \text{Lines} = \binom{12}{2} - \binom{5}{2} + 1 \] \[ = \frac{12 \times 11}{2} - \frac{5 \times 4}{2} + 1 = 66 - 10 + 1 = 57 \text{ lines.} \]

Part (b): Triangles:

\[ \text{Triangles} = \binom{12}{3} - \binom{5}{3} \] \[ = \frac{12 \times 11 \times 10}{6} - \frac{5 \times 4 \times 3}{6} = 220 - 10 = 210 \text{ triangles.} \]

Problem 3 (Polygon Diagonals)

A regular polygon has 44 diagonals. How many sides does it have?

View Step-by-Step Solution

Solution:

The number of diagonals of an \(n\)-sided polygon is given by:

\[ \frac{n(n - 3)}{2} = 44 \implies n(n - 3) = 88 \] Factoring 88 into two factors differing by 3: \[ 88 = 11 \times 8 = 11 \times (11 - 3) \implies n = 11. \] Answer: The polygon has 11 sides (an hendecagon).

Quick Revision & Formula Cheat Sheet

Combination Identities

  • \(^n C_r = \frac{n!}{r!(n - r)!}\)
  • \(^n C_r = ^n C_{n - r}\)
  • \(^n C_x = ^n C_y \implies x = y\) or \(x + y = n\)
  • \(^n C_r + ^n C_{r - 1} = {}^{n + 1} C_r\)

Geometric Formulas

  • Lines: \(\binom{n}{2} - \binom{m}{2} + 1\)
  • Triangles: \(\binom{n}{3} - \binom{m}{3}\)
  • Diagonals: \(\frac{n(n - 3)}{2}\)
  • Parallelograms: \(\binom{m}{2} \binom{p}{2}\)

Subset Selection

  • Total subsets of \(n\) objects: \(2^n\)
  • At least one object: \(2^n - 1\)
  • Relationship with Permutations: \(^n P_r = r! \times {}^n C_r\)
