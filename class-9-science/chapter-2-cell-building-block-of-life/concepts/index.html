Origin of Life

Where Did Life Begin?

It is widely accepted amongst the scientific community that life originated in water. Many researchers believe that life may have originated in small water pools with changing environmental conditions rather than in the oceans. Hot springs are prime examples of such environments.

Real World Example: Puga Valley

In India, the hot springs of Puga Valley in Ladakh maintain very high temperatures (nearly at the boiling point of water) even in an extremely cold climate. These conditions are very similar to those on the early Earth, about 3.5 billion years ago.

The organisms living in these hot springs today are mostly unicellular, heat-loving bacteria called thermophiles. Scientists from the Birbal Sahni Institute of Palaeosciences, Lucknow, studied these hot springs and found that calcium carbonate formed rapidly around them. These deposits may have protected early organic molecules from harmful radiation and extreme conditions, and they may have also helped in the formation of the first protective membrane — the barrier that defines a cell.

Levels of Life & Aided Vision

How Do We Study Cells?

All living organisms are made up of cells. The cell represents the basic level at which life exists. Some organisms are unicellular (e.g., bacteria, yeast), while others are multicellular (e.g., plants, birds, humans):

Cells → Tissues → Organs → Organ Systems → Organism

For example, nasal pores, the nasal cavity, trachea, and lungs together organize to form the respiratory system. Yet, the cell remains the fundamental unit of structure and function.

The Limit of Resolution

The human eye has a limit of resolution of about 0.1 mm (when viewed from 25 cm). This means if two points are closer than 0.1 mm, they appear as a single point. Because cells are typically much smaller (1 to 100 micrometres), we need magnification tools.

Tool Type Resolution Limit Magnification Source
Human Eye ~0.1 mm Unaided lens
Light Microscope ~200 nm (0.0002 mm) Visible light passing through convex lenses
Electron Microscope ~0.1 nm (nanometre scale) Electromagnetic lenses directing an electron beam
Historical Milestone

Robert Hooke (1665) was the first to observe box-like compartments in a slice of cork under a self-designed microscope (200-300X magnification) and named them 'cells'.

Estimating the Size of a Cell (Activity 2.1)

If you place a transparent ruler with millimetre (mm) markings under a microscope and observe the field of view diameter, you can estimate cell sizes. For example:
1. If field of view diameter = 5 mm ⇒ 5 × 1000 = 5000 µm.
2. If you count 25 onion cells in a straight line along this diameter, then:

Estimated Size = Field Diameter (µm) / Number of Cells
Size = 5000 µm / 25 = 200 µm

The Cell Boundary

The Cell Membrane & Wall

The cell membrane (plasma membrane) is a selectively permeable lipid bilayer (7 to 10 nm thick) present in all cells. It is described by the Fluid-Mosaic Model:

  • Fluid: Lipids and proteins can slide sideways, rotate, and move within the membrane.
  • Mosaic: Various proteins are embedded within the lipid bilayer, resembling mosaic tiles.
  • Lipid Bilayer: Composed of double layers of lipids with water-attracting (hydrophilic) heads outwards and water-repelling (hydrophobic) tails pointing inwards.

Cell Wall: Plants' Extra Support

Unlike animals, plants, fungi, and bacteria have a rigid cell wall outside the cell membrane. In plants, it is made of cellulose (a strong glucose polymer). The cell wall keeps plants upright and protects them against extreme wind and rain.

Transport Processes

Osmosis & Tonicity

Diffusion is the net movement of particles from a higher concentration to a lower concentration. Osmosis is specifically the diffusion of water molecules across a selectively permeable membrane.

Activity 2.2: Potato Osmometer

If you place a potato piece in plain water (Beaker A), water moves in, and the potato swells. In a 20% salt/sugar solution (Beaker B), water moves out, and the potato shrinks. The cell membrane acts as a semi-permeable boundary.

Tonicity: How Solutions Affect Cells

Tonicity describes the relative concentration of solutes dissolved in solution which determines the direction and extent of diffusion of water (osmosis). Based on concentration differences, solutions are categorized into three types:

1. Hypotonic Solution (Dilute Medium): Solute concentration of extracellular medium is less than solute concentration of intracellular medium. Water moves into the cell by endosmosis, causing the cell to swell.
Formula: [Solute]Outside < [Solute]Inside
2. Isotonic Solution (Balanced Medium): Solute concentration of extracellular medium is equal to solute concentration of intracellular medium. There is no net movement of water. The cell size remains constant.
Formula: [Solute]Outside = [Solute]Inside
3. Hypertonic Solution (Concentrated Medium): Solute concentration of extracellular medium is greater than solute concentration of intracellular medium. Water moves out of the cell by exosmosis, causing the cell to shrink.
Formula: [Solute]Outside > [Solute]Inside

Plasmolysis

When Rhoeo leaf cells are put in 20% sugar solution, their inner cytoplasm shrinks and pulls away from the cell wall because of water loss. This is called plasmolysis. Animal cheek cells shrink completely because they lack a rigid cell wall.

Interior structure

Cell Interior, Types & Organelles

The interior of a living cell is a highly coordinated working system consisting of the jelly-like cytoplasm, a prominent nucleus, and various sub-cellular components called organelles. Cells are broadly categorized based on their structural complexity into Prokaryotic and Eukaryotic cells.

Ready to Go Beyond: Acellular Agents

Certain infectious agents lack cell structures entirely:
• Viruses: Composed of genetic material enclosed in a protein coat.
• Viroids: Consist of naked genetic material, lacking a protein coat.
• Prions: Misfolded infectious proteins that contain no genetic material.

Detailed Organelle Index

Nucleus — House of Coded Instructions

The nucleus coordinates all cellular activities. It has a double-layered nuclear membrane containing nuclear pores which control the transfer of materials to the cytoplasm.
• Nucleolus: A dense round body where synthesis of ribosomal subunits takes place.
• Chromatin & Chromosomes: DNA molecules associate with specific proteins to form chromatin threads. When the cell is about to divide, this chromatin condenses into distinct rod-shaped chromosomes containing genes (functional segments of DNA).

Threads of Curiosity: Enucleated RBCs

Mature human Red Blood Cells (RBCs) lack a nucleus to provide more space for haemoglobin, optimizing oxygen transport. Because they lack a nucleus, they cannot divide or self-repair, resulting in a short lifespan of about 120 days.

Ribosomes & Endoplasmic Reticulum (ER)

Ribosomes are tiny protein factories found free in the cytoplasm or bound to the Endoplasmic Reticulum.

Endoplasmic Reticulum (ER) is a continuous membrane network involved in manufacturing and transport:

  • Rough Endoplasmic Reticulum (RER): Surface is studded with ribosomes. Main site for protein synthesis and secretion (highly developed in gland cells like pancreatic cells).
  • Smooth Endoplasmic Reticulum (SER): Lacks ribosomes. Site of lipid (fat) and hormone synthesis and storage.

Golgi Apparatus & Lysosomes

Golgi Apparatus: Stacks of flattened, membrane-bound sac-like structures that modify, sort, and package proteins and lipids into vesicles for shipping.

Meet a Scientist: Camillo Golgi (1898)

First observed a thread-like network in the nerve cells of a barn owl using special silver-staining techniques he developed. Decades later, electron microscopes confirmed its structure, naming it in his honor.

Lysosomes: The clean-up system of the cell. Single membrane-bound sacs filled with powerful digestive enzymes capable of breaking down waste materials and worn-out cell parts.

Threads of Curiosity: Sperm Fertilisation

Human sperm cells carry lysosomal enzymes at their tips. When a sperm meets an egg, these enzymes dissolve the outer protective membrane of the egg to allow successful fertilization.

Mitochondria — Powerhouse of the Cell

Mitochondria generate chemical energy currency called Adenosine Triphosphate (ATP) via cellular respiration.
• Double Membrane: Smooth, porous outer membrane and an inner membrane folded into finger-like projections called cristae to maximize surface area for ATP production.
• Evolutionary Origin: Mitochondria have their own circular DNA and ribosomes, allowing them to make some of their own proteins, suggesting they originated from ancient symbiotic bacteria.

Plastids — Food Centres in Plant Cells

Plastids are double-membrane bound organelles found in plant cells. Like mitochondria, they possess their own DNA and ribosomes:

  • Chloroplasts: Green plastids containing chlorophyll. Internally divided into disc-shaped stacks suspended in a semi-fluid matrix called the stroma. Site of photosynthesis.
  • Chromoplasts: Color plastids containing yellow, orange, or red pigments. Brightly colors petals and fruits to attract animal pollinators and fruit-eating seed dispersers.
  • Leucoplasts: Colorless food storage plastids. They store starch, oils, or proteins (e.g. starch storage in potato and Colocasia cells).

Vacuoles — Storage & Turgidity

Vacuoles store water, nutrients, minerals, and wastes:
• Plant Cells: Have a single, massive central vacuole filled with cell sap. It exerts turgor pressure against the cell wall, keeping cells firm and leaves upright. Dehydration causes the vacuole to shrink, leading to plant wilting.
• Animal Cells: Have small, temporary vacuoles for transient storage.

Synthetic Biology: Craig Venter (2010)

Craig Venter and his team synthesized the complete DNA sequence of a simple bacterium and successfully transplanted it into another bacterium whose DNA had been removed. The host cell immediately began dividing and functioning according to the synthetic DNA instructions, demonstrating DNA's master coordination role.

Cellular Reproduction

How Cells Grow & Divide

Growth in organisms occurs not because cells grow infinitely larger, but because they divide to form new cells. There are two primary types of cell division:

Feature Mitosis Meiosis
Purpose Growth, tissue repair, and asexual reproduction Gamete formation (sperms/eggs) for sexual reproduction
Daughter Cells 2 identical cells 4 unique cells
Chromosome Number Same as parent (diploid) Halved (haploid)
Regulation and Cancer

Healthy cells undergo contact inhibition (stop dividing when touching neighbors) and Programmed Cell Death (PCD / Apoptosis). Cancer cells lose this control, dividing indefinitely to form tumors.
