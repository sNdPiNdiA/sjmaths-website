Exploring Mixtures and their Separation: Concepts

Classification

Homogeneous vs. Heterogeneous Mixtures

A mixture is formed when two or more substances (elements or compounds) are physically combined in any ratio, without undergoing chemical bonding. Mixtures can be broadly classified into two categories:

1. Homogeneous Mixtures

Have a uniform composition throughout. The components are indistinguishable even under a microscope. Also called Solutions.
• Examples: Salt solution, sugar dissolved in water, air, brass (alloy).

2. Heterogeneous Mixtures

Have a non-uniform composition. The boundary surfaces separating components are visible, and particles often settle over time.
• Examples: Sand in water, oil-water mixture, smoke, muddy water.

Quantitative

Concentration of a Solution

The concentration of a solution is defined as the amount of solute dissolved in a given quantity of the solvent or the total solution. Dr. Dilip Mahalanabis used these precise proportions to formulate Oral Rehydration Solution (ORS), saving millions of lives from dehydration.

Three Main Mathematical Methods:

A. Mass by Mass Percentage (% m/m or % w/w) $$\text{Mass by Mass Percentage} = \frac{\text{Mass of Solute}}{\text{Mass of Solution}} \times 100$$ $$\text{Mass of Solution} = \text{Mass of Solute} + \text{Mass of Solvent}$$
B. Mass by Volume Percentage (% m/v or % w/v) $$\text{Mass by Volume Percentage} = \frac{\text{Mass of Solute}}{\text{Volume of Solution (mL)}} \times 100$$
C. Volume by Volume Percentage (% v/v) $$\text{Volume by Volume Percentage} = \frac{\text{Volume of Solute}}{\text{Volume of Solution}} \times 100$$
Thermodynamics

Solubility & Crystallization

The maximum amount of solute that can be dissolved in $100\text{ g}$ of solvent at a specific temperature is defined as its solubility. A solution that cannot dissolve any more solute at that temperature is called a saturated solution.

  • Temperature Effect: The solubility of solid solutes in liquid solvents generally increases with temperature (meaning cooling a hot saturated solution will precipitate excess solute), whereas the solubility of gases in liquids decreases as temperature rises.
  • Crystallization: The process of forming pure, geometric solid crystals from a saturated solution. Crystals are solids made of particles arranged in a regular geometric pattern.

Everyday & Natural Crystals

We observe crystals daily in rock salt, candy sugar (mishri), snowflakes, and window frost. Naturally, spectacular crystal deposits form in caves like the **Mawsmai Cave in Sohra (Cherrapunji)** and as quartz mineral clusters inside the Earth's crust.

India's Salt Heritage

Salt crystallization is an ancient Indian coastal process. Local communities produced two main types: **Panga salt** (obtained by boiling concentrated sea brines) and **Karkatch salt** (produced by solar evaporation of seawater in pans).

Crystallization Experiment (Activity 5.3)

Dissolving copper sulfate in hot water with a drop of dilute sulfuric acid (which prevents hydrolysis and unwanted reactions). Filtering the hot solution and allowing it to cool slowly yields large, blue, shiny crystals. Rapid cooling in ice water yields smaller, less formed crystals.

Thermal Separation

Simple & Fractional Distillation

Distillation is a technique used to separate two miscible liquids based on the differences in their boiling points.
• Simple Distillation: Preferred when the component boiling points differ by at least $25^\circ\text{C}$ (e.g., Acetone at $56^\circ\text{C}$ and Water at $100^\circ\text{C}$).
• Fractional Distillation: Used when boiling point differences are less than $25^\circ\text{C}$ (e.g., separating crude oil fractions in petroleum refineries).

Kannauj's Mitti ka Ittar

In Kannauj (perfume capital of India), the earthy fragrance of wet soil is captured as Mitti ka Ittar. This natural perfume is manufactured using a traditional hydro-distillation method called the **Deg-Bhapka method** passed down through generations.

Petroleum Refining (Fractional)

In crude oil refineries, fractional distillation separates complex hydrocarbons with small boiling point differences (less than 25°C) into fractions: petroleum gas (LPG), petrol, kerosene (aviation fuel), diesel, lubricating oils, and bitumen.

Adsorption

Paper Chromatography

Paper chromatography separates components of a mixture based on their differential solubility in a moving solvent and their rate of adsorption onto a stationary paper phase. The retention factor ($R_f$) is defined as:

$$R_f = \frac{\text{Distance travelled by the component from origin}}{\text{Distance travelled by the solvent front from origin}}$$

Etymology & Practice

The term chromatography originates from the Greek words chroma (meaning 'colour') and graphein (meaning 'to write'). It is widely used to separate pigments from plant leaf extracts (like spinach) or flower petals. Depending on the polarity of the pigments, solvents like alcohol, water, or organic mixtures are selected.

Density Difference

Separation of Immiscible Liquids

Immiscible liquids do not dissolve in one another and separate into distinct layers based on their densities. A separating funnel isolates them by draining the denser bottom layer through a stopcock valve first.

Advanced Tech

Sublimation, Centrifugation & Colloids

Additional vital separation processes studied in Chapter 5 include:

  • Sublimation & Deposition: Direct transition from solid to vapour (below melting point) without melting (e.g., Camphor, Naphthalene, Dry Ice). The reverse (cooling vapour directly to solid) is called deposition.
  • Centrifugation: Rapid spinning separates solids from liquids by pushing denser particles to the bottom.
    • Analogy: Relates to the outward pull felt in the spinning game Phugadi (Marathi) or Kikli (Punjabi).
    • Paperfuge: A hand-powered cardboard/string device separating blood components (RBCs, plasma) without electricity.
  • Coagulation: Fine suspended mud particles clump together by adding Alum (Fitkari). Similarly, forming paneer (cheese) from milk uses lemon juice or vinegar as an acid coagulant.
  • Colloids & Emulsions: Colloids consist of a dispersed phase inside a dispersion medium (1–1000 nm).
    • Emulsions: Liquid-in-liquid colloids. Divided into **oil-in-water** (e.g., milk, vanishing creams) and **water-in-oil** (e.g., butter, cold cream, lotions), stabilized by emulsifying agents (like proteins).
  • Tyndall Effect: Scattering of light by colloid/suspension particles making the path visible. First explained by John Tyndall, seen in dark dusty rooms or sports stadium floodlights.
Real World

Alloys & Environmental Science

Separation concepts extend to material design and global sustainability:

Understanding Alloys

Alloys are homogeneous solid mixtures of two or more metals (or a metal and a non-metal) that cannot be separated by physical methods.
• Brass: ~80% Copper, 20% Zinc.
• Bronze: ~80% Copper, 20% Tin.
• Stainless Steel: Iron mixed with Carbon (0.03–0.8%), Chromium (16–18%), Nickel (10–14%), and Molybdenum (2%).

Environmental Separation

Separation is vital to life and ecosystems:
• Kidneys: Biological filters removing wastes from blood.
• Sewage Treatment: Relies on sequential sedimentation, coagulation, and filtration.
• Waste Segregation: Sorting recyclable dry waste (plastic, glass, metals) from compostable wet food scrap waste.
• Battery Recovery: Chemical processes extracting valuable lithium from recycled electronics.
