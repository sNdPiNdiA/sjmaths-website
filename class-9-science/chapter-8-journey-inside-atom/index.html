Chapter Overview

What You'll Learn in This Chapter

Science is not only about what we know, but also about how we know it. In this introductory chapter, we step into secondary science using the magnifying glass of careful observation and the compass of structured reasoning.

8.1 Rediscovering the Roots of Atomic Theory

Let us embark on a journey that takes us back more than 2,000 years, to the intellectual landscapes of ancient India and ancient Greece. In these distant yet remarkably parallel civilisations, profound thinkers, such as Acharya Kanada in India, and Leucippus and Democritus in Greece, pondered over the same fundamental question that has continued to inspire human inquiry across centuries — What is everything made up of?

Acharya Kanada suggested that if matter (dravya) is divided repeatedly, you will reach a stage where you would encounter the smallest particles that can no longer be divided. He called these particles parmanus. His ideas are recorded in the Sanskrit text Vaisesika Sutras. A parmanu is infinitely small and cannot be perceived by the senses.

The Greek philosophers Leucippus and Democritus also proposed a similar idea. They called these indivisible particles atomos (in Greek, atomos means indivisible).

8.2 A Short Historical Journey Through Atomic Models

More than a hundred years ago, scientists tried to imagine what atoms might look like by proposing simple models. As new experiments were performed and new evidence came to light, these models were changed and improved.

8.2.1 Thomson’s model of an atom

When J. J. Thomson discovered tiny negatively charged particles called electrons, he faced a puzzle — atoms are neutral, so where is the positive charge present? To solve this, Thomson proposed the atom to be a sphere of positive charge with electrons distributed throughout it. This is known as the plum‑pudding model.

8.2.2 Testing Thomson’s model: The gold foil experiment

In 1911, Geiger and Marsden, working under Ernest Rutherford, tested Thomson’s model of the atom through what became famous as the gold foil experiment. They aimed a narrow beam of alpha particles at an extremely thin sheet of gold foil.

A. Rutherford’s model of an atom

From the gold foil experiment, Rutherford concluded that the positive charge of an atom is concentrated in a tiny region called the nucleus. The atom is mostly empty space.

B. Limitations of Rutherford’s model

Rutherford’s model could not explain why atoms are stable; an orbiting electron would radiate energy and spiral into the nucleus.

C. Discovery of the proton

Rutherford showed that the nucleus carries positive charge which comes from particles called protons.

8.2.3 Bohr’s model of the atom

To explain atomic stability, Niels Bohr proposed a model where electrons revolve in fixed circular paths called stationary states or shells (K, L, M, N, …). Electrons do not lose energy while in these fixed orbits.

8.3 What Components Contribute to the Mass of an Atom?

Rutherford’s model showed that most of the mass of an atom is in its nucleus. Electrons are very light.

8.3.1 Discovery of the Neutron

In 1932, James Chadwick discovered the neutron, a neutral particle with a mass similar to a proton.

8.4 Symbols of Elements

John Dalton introduced pictorial symbols for elements. Later, Berzelius suggested using letters derived from Latin names, which became the modern chemical symbols.

8.5 Atomic Number

The atomic number (Z) is the number of protons in the nucleus and uniquely identifies an element.

8.6 Mass Number

The mass number (A) is the total number of protons and neutrons in the nucleus.

8.7 How Are Electrons Distributed in Different Energy Levels?

Bohr and Bury gave the rule: maximum electrons in a shell = 2n², where n is the shell number.

8.8 Combining Capacity of an Atom: Valency

The valency is the combining capacity of an atom, determined by the number of electrons needed to complete an octet.

8.9 A Deeper Look into Atomic Structure

8.9.1 Isotopes

Isotopes are atoms of the same element with the same atomic number but different mass numbers due to varying neutrons.

8.9.2 IsobarsIsobars are atoms of different elements that have the same mass number but different atomic numbers.

At a Glance

  • Atoms are the building blocks of matter.
  • J.J. Thomson proposed electrons embedded in a positively charged sphere.
  • Rutherford’s model described a dense nucleus.
  • Bohr’s model introduced energy levels.
  • James Chadwick discovered neutrons.
