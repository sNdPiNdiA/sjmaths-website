Q1. Choose the correct options related to Rutherford’s gold foil experiment.

4 Marks
Statements / Options:
  1. The experiment clearly showed the existence of neutrons in the nucleus.
  2. The results disproved the plum pudding model and led to the idea of a nucleus.
  3. Large deflections indicated that positive charge and most of the mass are concentrated in a tiny region.
  4. The experiment directly established the fixed orbits of electrons.
View Ideal Answer & Marking Scheme
Model Answer:

The correct statements are (ii) and (iii).

Rutherford’s experiment showed that the atom has a tiny, dense, positively charged nucleus and that most of the atom is empty space. It did not discover the neutron and did not establish Bohr’s fixed electron orbits.

Q2. Which statement is correct according to Bohr’s atomic model?

4 Marks
Statements / Options:
  1. Electrons continuously lose energy and fall into the nucleus.
  2. Electrons can exist at any arbitrary energy.
  3. Electrons occupy allowed stationary energy levels without continuously losing energy.
  4. Electrons must remain between two energy levels.
View Ideal Answer & Marking Scheme
Model Answer:

Only (iii) is correct.

In Bohr’s model, electrons occupy specific allowed energy levels. While remaining in a stationary state they do not continuously lose energy.

Q3. The nuclei of X, Y and Z contain X = 18 protons, 19 neutrons; Y = 17 protons, 18 neutrons; Z = 17 protons, 20 neutrons. Find the relation between Y and Z and between Z and X.

3 Marks
View Ideal Answer & Marking Scheme
Model Answer:

For Y: A = 17 + 18 = 35.

For Z: A = 17 + 20 = 37.

Y and Z have the same atomic number but different mass numbers. Therefore, they are isotopes.

X has A = 18 + 19 = 37. Z also has A = 37, but their atomic numbers differ. Therefore, Z and X are isobars.

Q5. Arrange the atomic models chronologically: Dalton, Thomson, Rutherford and Bohr.

2 Marks

Model Answer:

The correct chronological order is:

Dalton → Thomson → Rutherford → Bohr

Dalton proposed the indivisible atom, Thomson proposed the plum pudding model, Rutherford proposed the nuclear model, and Bohr introduced fixed energy levels.

Q6. Electrons move around the nucleus. Why do they not fly away from the atom?

2 Marks
View Ideal Answer & Marking Scheme
Model Answer:

The negatively charged electron is attracted towards the positively charged nucleus.

In Bohr’s model, electrons remain in permitted stationary energy levels rather than continuously losing energy and collapsing into the nucleus.

Q10. Why did Rutherford’s model fail to explain atomic stability while Bohr’s model succeeded?

3 Marks
View Ideal Answer & Marking Scheme
Model Answer:

Rutherford’s model did not explain why an accelerating electron would not continuously lose energy and spiral into the nucleus.

Bohr introduced stationary energy levels. Electrons could remain in allowed energy levels without continuously losing energy.

Q14. An element X has mass number 35 and contains 18 neutrons. Find its electrons, protons, atomic number, identity, electronic configuration, valence electrons and the relation of the new atom formed by adding two neutrons.

5 Marks
View Ideal Answer & Marking Scheme
Model Answer:

Protons: 35 − 18 = 17.

For a neutral atom, electrons = 17.

Atomic number = 17, so X is chlorine (Cl).

Electronic configuration: 2, 8, 7.

Valence electrons = 7.

Adding two neutrons gives mass number: 35 + 2 = 37.

The new atom is an isotope of X.

Q15. An atom has 12 protons and 12 neutrons. If all electrons are replaced by hypothetical particles having the same charge as electrons but 500 times greater mass, what changes?

4 Marks
View Ideal Answer & Marking Scheme
Model Answer:

Atomic number remains 12 because atomic number depends only on the number of protons.

Mass number remains 24 because mass number counts protons and neutrons.

The actual mass of the atom would increase because the replacement particles are much heavier than electrons.

Overall charge remains neutral because the replacement particles have the same charge as electrons.
