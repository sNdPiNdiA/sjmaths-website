Solved Worked Examples (6.1 – 6.8)

EX 6.1 Two forces of 10 N and 6 N act on a block. Find the magnitude and direction of the net force when they act (a) in the same direction, (b) opposite with 10 N to the right, (c) opposite with 10 N to the left.
Show Ideal Answer
  • (a) Net force $= 10 + 6 = 16\text{ N}$, towards the right.
  • (b) Net force $= 10 - 6 = 4\text{ N}$, towards the right.
  • (c) Net force $= 10 - 6 = 4\text{ N}$, towards the left.

Rule: forces in the same direction add; forces in opposite directions subtract, with the net force along the larger force.

EX 6.2 A person pushes a moving box forward with a force equal to the force of friction. Will the box continue moving or come to rest?
Show Ideal Answer

The applied force and friction are equal and opposite, so they balance. The net force is zero. By Newton's first law, the box continues moving with constant velocity.

EX 6.3 Draw (i) position-time and (ii) velocity-time graphs for an object on which no net force is acting.
Show Ideal Answer
  • Object at rest: position-time graph is a horizontal line (position constant); velocity-time graph stays at zero.
  • Object moving with constant velocity: position-time graph is a straight inclined line; velocity-time graph is a horizontal line (constant velocity).
EX 6.4 A weightlifter holds a barbell with 10 kg on each side and a 10 kg bar. What force does she apply to keep it steady?
Show Ideal Answer

Total mass $= 30\text{ kg}$. Weight $F = mg = 30 \times 9.8 = 294\text{ N}$ downward. To keep it steady, she applies an equal and opposite force of 294 N upward.

EX 6.5 A student pushes a stationary 25 kg block on a horizontal floor. The maximum friction opposing motion is 50 N. Find the displacement in 2 s if pushed with (i) 50 N and (ii) 55 N forward.
Show Ideal Answer
  • (i) 50 N: Applied force equals friction, so the net force is zero. The block remains stationary (displacement = 0 m).
  • (ii) 55 N: Net force $= 55 - 50 = 5\text{ N}$. Acceleration $a = F/m = 5/25 = 0.2\text{ m s}^{-2}$. Using $s = ut + \frac{1}{2}at^2$ with $u = 0$ and $t = 2\text{ s}$: $s = \frac{1}{2} \times 0.2 \times (2)^2 = 0.4\text{ m}$ forward.
EX 6.6 A 1500 kg sports car moving east has a velocity-time graph. Calculate the force during (i) 0–5 s, (ii) 5–10 s, and (iii) 10–15 s.
Show Ideal Answer
  • (i) 0–5 s: Straight inclined line ⇒ constant acceleration. Using $v = u + at$: $10 = 0 + 5a \Rightarrow a = 2\text{ m s}^{-2}$. $F = ma = 1500 \times 2 = 3000\text{ N}$ towards the east.
  • (ii) 5–10 s: Line parallel to time axis ⇒ constant velocity ⇒ no force acting.
  • (iii) 10–15 s: $0 = 10 + 5a \Rightarrow a = -2\text{ m s}^{-2}$. $F = 1500 \times (-2) = -3000\text{ N}$, acting towards the west (opposite to motion).
EX 6.7 The Earth and a fruit apply equal and opposite gravitational forces. Why does the fruit move towards the Earth but the Earth does not seem to move?
Show Ideal Answer

The forces are equal in magnitude, but the mass of the Earth is so large that its acceleration ($a = F/m$) is extremely small. Thus, its effect on the Earth is too small to be noticed.

EX 6.8 A 0.1 kg bullet is fired from a 5 kg gun with a force of 2 N. Find the initial accelerations of the bullet and the gun.
Show Ideal Answer
  • By Newton's third law, the recoil force on the gun is also 2 N.
  • Gun: $a = 2/5 = 0.4\text{ m s}^{-2}$.
  • Bullet: $a = 2/0.1 = 20\text{ m s}^{-2}$.

Although the forces are equal, the accelerations differ because the masses are different.

Revise, Reflect, Refine

Textbook Exercise Solutions

Complete model answers for all "Revise, Reflect, Refine" questions from the textbook.

Q1 Using a horizontal force F, a table is moved across the floor at a constant velocity. How much is the frictional force exerted by the floor on the table?
Show Ideal Answer

Since the table moves with constant velocity, its acceleration is zero, so the net force is zero. Thus, the frictional force is equal to F but acts in the opposite direction (backward).

Q2 For a ball on a smooth frictionless surface, choose the correct word for each statement: (i) no net force ⇒ velocity will remain the same/increase/decrease. (ii) net force in direction of motion ⇒ velocity remains the same/increase/decrease. (iii) net force opposite to motion ⇒ velocity remains the same/increase/decrease.
Show Ideal Answer
  • (i) remain the same
  • (ii) increase
  • (iii) decrease
Q3 Block P has forces of 4 N and 5 N in opposite directions; block Q moves with constant velocity. Which statement is correct: (i) P experiences a net force and Q does not. (ii) P does not and Q does. (iii) Both experience a net force. (iv) Neither experiences a net force.
Show Ideal Answer

Option (i). P has unbalanced forces (5 N − 4 N = 1 N net force), while Q moving with constant velocity has zero net force.

Q4 In a snake boat race, 95 of 100 oarsmen row backwards (forward force) while 5 row in the opposite direction by mistake. If each applies 200 N, what is the net force on the boat? (Ignore drag.)
Show Ideal Answer

Forward force $= 95 \times 200 = 19000\text{ N}$; backward force $= 5 \times 200 = 1000\text{ N}$. Net force $= 19000 - 1000 = 18000 N in the forward direction.

Q5 When a net force acts on an object, the object accelerates: (i) opposite to the force, acceleration proportional to force. (ii) opposite to the force, proportional to mass. (iii) in the direction of force, inversely proportional to force. (iv) in the direction of force, proportional to force.
Show Ideal Answer

Option (iv). The acceleration is in the direction of the net force and is directly proportional to the force (Newton's second law).

Q6 The position-time graph for four objects A, B, C and D moving along a straight line is given (Fig 6.37). A net force acts on which object?
Show Ideal Answer

A net force acts on the object whose position-time graph is curved (parabolic), indicating a changing velocity (acceleration). Objects whose position-time graphs are straight lines move with constant velocity, so no net force acts on them.

Q7 A sailor jumps out from a small boat to the shore. As the sailor jumps forward, will the boat move? If yes, in which direction and why?
Show Ideal Answer

Yes. By Newton's third law, when the sailor pushes the boat backwards to jump forward, the boat pushes the sailor forward with an equal and opposite force. Therefore the boat moves backward (towards the water, opposite to the sailor's jump).

Q8 During a high jump event, a landing mat or sand bed is placed for the athlete to fall upon. Explain the reason behind it.
Show Ideal Answer

The landing mat increases the time over which the athlete's velocity reduces to zero, decreasing the acceleration and hence the force (and risk of injury). This follows from Newton's second law — smaller acceleration means a smaller stopping force.

Q9 A loaded hand cart collides with an identical but empty hand cart. Which statement is correct? (i) loaded exerts larger force, (ii) empty exerts larger force, (iii) neither exerts a force, (iv) both exert equal magnitude of force on each other.
Show Ideal Answer

Option (iv). By Newton's third law, both carts exert equal and opposite forces on each other during the collision.

Q10 The acceleration-mass graph for the acceleration produced by a force on objects of different masses is plotted (Fig 6.40). Plot the force-mass graph for this case.
Show Ideal Answer

Since $a = F/m$ and $F = ma$ is constant for the given force, the force-mass graph is a straight line parallel to the mass axis at the value of the constant force (force does not depend on mass).

Q11 The velocity-time graph of an object of mass 10 kg is shown (Fig 6.41). Calculate the force acting on the object using the graph.
Show Ideal Answer

From the graph, velocity rises from 10 m/s to 30 m/s over 4 s (a straight inclined line), so the acceleration $a = (30 - 10)/4 = 5\text{ m s}^{-2}$. Using $F = ma$, $F = 10 \times 5 = 50 N$. Where the graph is horizontal (constant velocity), the force is zero.

Q12 A bullet of mass 50 g moving at 100 m/s enters a heavy stationary wooden block and stops after penetrating 50 cm. Estimate the stopping force on the bullet (assume constant acceleration).
Show Ideal Answer

Mass $= 0.05\text{ kg}$, $u = 100\text{ m/s}$, $v = 0$, $s = 0.5\text{ m}$. Using $v^2 = u^2 + 2as$: $0 = 100^2 + 2a(0.5) \Rightarrow a = -10000\text{ m s}^{-2}$. Stopping force $F = ma = 0.05 \times (-10000) = -500\text{ N}$, i.e. about 500 N opposing the motion.

Q13 A footballer kicks a football (0.4 kg) to 108 km/h with a force of 800 N. Calculate the time of contact between foot and ball.
Show Ideal Answer

Speed $108\text{ km/h} = 108 \times 5/18 = 30\text{ m/s}$. Acceleration $a = F/m = 800/0.4 = 2000\text{ m s}^{-2}$. Using $v = u + at$ with $u = 0$: $30 = 2000 \times t \Rightarrow t = 0.015 s$.

Q14 A 2 kg object moving at 10 m/s encounters a rough patch with friction 7 N, plus an additional 3 N opposing force. How far does it travel before coming to rest?
Show Ideal Answer

Total opposing force $= 7 + 3 = 10\text{ N}$. Acceleration $a = F/m = -10/2 = -5\text{ m s}^{-2}$ (retardation). Using $v^2 = u^2 + 2as$: $0 = 10^2 + 2(-5)s \Rightarrow s = 10 m$.

Q15 A tractor pulls a harrow of mass $m_1$ with force F giving acceleration $a_1$, and a trolley of mass $m_2$ with force F giving acceleration $a_2$. If it now pulls the trolley with the harrow placed on it (same force F), obtain an expression for the resulting acceleration in terms of $a_1$ and $a_2$. (Ignore friction.)
Show Ideal Answer

$F = m_1 a_1$ and $F = m_2 a_2$, so $m_1 = F/a_1$ and $m_2 = F/a_2$. With both together, total mass $= m_1 + m_2$, and $a = F/(m_1 + m_2)$.
Using $\frac{1}{a} = \frac{m_1 + m_2}{F} = \frac{1}{a_1} + \frac{1}{a_2}$,
$$a = \frac{a_1 a_2}{a_1 + a_2}$$

Q16 A bar magnet and a compass needle exert equal and opposite magnetic forces on each other (Newton's third law). Yet the needle moves while the bar magnet does not. Explain why.
Show Ideal Answer

The forces are equal, but the bar magnet has a much larger mass than the light compass needle. Since $a = F/m$, the needle's acceleration is large enough to move it, while the bar magnet's acceleration is too small to notice.

Extension Projects

The Journey Beyond Solutions

JB Investigate friction: Does it depend on how hard the surfaces press each other? Is the friction before motion begins larger than after? Is rolling friction less than sliding friction? Create an infographic and explain why the wheel was a major milestone.
Show Ideal Answer
  • Friction generally increases as the surfaces are pressed harder together.
  • Static friction (before motion) is larger than kinetic friction (during motion).
  • Rolling friction is much smaller than sliding friction — this is why wheels made transport far easier in human history.
JB Two toy cars of equal mass with bar magnets repel each other. Measure time and distance travelled before each stops, repeat after adding equal masses, plot a distance-versus-mass graph, and discuss findings.
Show Ideal Answer

By Newton's third law, both cars experience equal and opposite forces and should travel equal distances in opposite directions for equal masses. Adding equal masses keeps them balanced but reduces acceleration (larger mass ⇒ smaller acceleration), so distances decrease as mass increases. Friction eventually brings both to rest.

JB Wrap a rope around a rough post. Each extra turn greatly increases the grip (friction) needed to hold a heavy bucket. Explain why friction does not increase in a simple linear way.
Show Ideal Answer

Each additional turn multiplies the holding grip because friction grows exponentially with the number of turns (the rope presses harder onto the post over more contact length). Small changes in contact can therefore lead to large changes in force — the same principle that holds large ships safely at a pier.

JB Explore how Newton formulated the laws of motion by reading excerpts from his original work, the Principia (1687).
Show Ideal Answer

Newton's Principia introduced the concept of inertia and the three laws in his masterpiece. Studying the original text and commentaries online reveals how these laws describe motion from everyday objects to planets and stars, needing modification only near massive objects, near the speed of light, and at atomic scales.
