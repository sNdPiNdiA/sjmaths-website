How Forces Affect Motion: Concepts

Introduction

The Concept of Force

A force can make an object move from rest, change the speed and direction of motion of a moving object, and even change the shape of an object. For example, a ball at rest starts moving when you kick it, a cricket bat changes the direction of a cricket ball, and your fingers squeeze a lemon.

Force is a Vector Quantity

Force needs direction along with its magnitude and unit, just like position, displacement, velocity and acceleration.

SI unit: the newton (symbol N), written with a small 'n' (newton), symbol capitalised as N.

The magnitude of a force expresses its strength. If either the magnitude or direction — or both — changes, the effect of the force also changes.

Measurement

Measuring the Magnitude of a Force

A spring balance can measure not just the weight of an object but the magnitude of a force in general. If you pull on the free end of the spring balance, it measures the force with which you pull on the spring inside it.

  • Weight of an object is the gravitational force with which the Earth pulls the object.
  • In everyday life, the smallest forces we can directly feel are of the order of millinewtons ($10^{-3}\text{ N}$), such as a light touch.
  • Scientists can measure forces far smaller — down to yoctonewtons ($10^{-24}\text{ N}$) in specialised experiments (as of 2026).
Net Force

Balanced and Unbalanced Forces

In real life, more than one force usually acts on an object. For example, a box being pushed on a surface experiences both your push and the force of friction in the opposite direction. A ball floating on water experiences the downward gravitational force and the upward buoyant force.

  • Balanced forces: Two forces equal in magnitude but opposite in direction. When they act, the net force is zero and the object does not move (like two equal teams in a tug of war).
  • Unbalanced forces: When one force is larger, a non-zero net force acts and the object moves in the direction of the larger force.
  • When two forces act in opposite directions but unequal magnitudes, the net force equals the difference between them, in the direction of the larger force.
  • When forces act in the same direction, the net force equals the sum of their magnitudes, in the same direction (like two people pushing a stalled car).
  • Rotation: Equal and opposite forces applied to the two ends of an extended object make it rotate (e.g., turning a handlebar or a tap). Forces acting at an angle are studied in higher grades.

Example 6.1: Net Force on a Block

Two forces of 10 N and 6 N act on a block:
(a) Same direction: Net force $= 10 + 6 = 16\text{ N}$, towards the right.
(b) Opposite, 10 N right: Net force $= 10 - 6 = 4\text{ N}$, towards the right.
(c) Opposite, 10 N left: Net force $= 10 - 6 = 4\text{ N}$, towards the left.
Multiple forces may act on an object, but its motion depends only on the net force.

Always combine all forces (accounting for direction) to find the net force before applying Newton's laws.

Friction

The Force of Friction: Often Overlooked but Always Present

When you apply a force on an object resting on the floor, the object may not move at first. This is because the force of friction arises between the bottom surface of the object and the floor, acting in the direction opposite to your applied force. The object starts moving when your applied force is larger than the force of friction, producing a net force in the direction of motion.

  • Besides the applied force and friction, two other forces act on a pushed object: the gravitational force (weight) acting downwards, and the normal force exerted by the surface acting upwards, perpendicular to the surface. These two are balanced.
  • Air also exerts friction on a moving object, but in many cases its magnitude is so small that it can be neglected.
  • Once you stop pushing, friction brings a moving object to rest — you must continuously apply a force to counter friction and keep an object moving at constant velocity.

All Forces on a Pushed Object


• Applied force — forward direction
• Force of friction — backward direction
• Gravitational force (weight) — downward
• Normal force — upward, perpendicular to the surface
• Air friction — usually negligible

Key Conclusions (Activities 6.1 & 6.2)


• Friction depends on the nature of the surfaces in contact.
• A smaller force of friction allows the object to travel a larger distance before coming to rest and its velocity decreases more slowly.
• A spring balance gives an approximate measure of the force of friction.

Galileo's Thought Experiment

If a body moves along a horizontal plane and all impediments (friction) are removed, it will continue to move indefinitely. If friction were zero, an object set in motion would never come to rest on its own.

Law of Inertia

Newton's First Law of Motion

Statement

An object at rest remains at rest, and an object in motion continues to move with a constant velocity, unless a net force acts upon the object.

  • If the net force acting on an object is zero, the object cannot begin to move or change its velocity — its acceleration is zero.
  • Constant velocity means no change in the magnitude or direction of velocity. If non-zero, the motion is in a straight line with the magnitude unchanged.
  • Newton used the word inertia for the tendency of objects to resist change in their state of rest or uniform motion — the idea behind his first law (1687).

Example 6.2: If a person pushes a moving box forward with a force equal to the friction, the two forces balance (net force = zero). By Newton's first law, the box continues moving with a constant velocity.

Example 6.3: When no net force acts, the object is either at rest (position-time graph horizontal, velocity-time graph at zero) or moving with constant velocity (position-time graph a straight line, velocity-time graph horizontal).

F = ma

Newton's Second Law of Motion

Statement

When a net force acts on an object, the object accelerates in the direction of the net force. The magnitude of the acceleration is proportional to the magnitude of the net force and inversely proportional to the mass of the object.

Key Equations

A. Acceleration $$a = \frac{F}{m}$$
B. Force $$F = ma$$
If $m = 1\text{ kg}$ and $a = 1\text{ m s}^{-2}$, then $F = 1\text{ kg m s}^{-2} = 1\text{ N}$. One newton is the force that produces an acceleration of $1\text{ m s}^{-2}$ on an object of mass $1\text{ kg}$.
C. Weight (Gravitational Force) $$F = mg \quad (g = 9.8\text{ m s}^{-2}\ \approx 10\text{ m s}^{-2})$$
The acceleration due to gravitational force by the Earth, $g$, does not depend on the mass of the object.

Momentum Form (Ready to Go Beyond)

  • Momentum of an object is the product of its mass and velocity; its direction is the same as the velocity.
  • Newton's second law states that the rate of change of momentum is proportional to the net force and takes place in the direction of the net force. This form applies even when the mass is not constant.

Everyday Applications

Catching a Cricket Ball

A fielder pulls their hands backwards while catching a fast ball, increasing the time over which the velocity reduces to zero. This reduces acceleration, requiring a smaller force and minimising injury.

Airbags & Cracking a Coconut

Airbags increase the time over which a passenger's head and chest stop in a collision, reducing force. A coconut stops in a very short time on hitting a hard surface, so the ground exerts a huge force that breaks the shell.

Example 6.4: Weightlifter

A barbell with 10 kg on each side plus a 10 kg bar has a total mass of 30 kg. Its weight is $F = mg = 30 \times 9.8 = 294\text{ N}$ downward, so the weightlifter must apply 294 N upward to hold it steady.

Action–Reaction

Newton's Third Law of Motion

Statement

Whenever one object is exerting a force on a second object, the second object is simultaneously exerting an equal and opposite force on the first object. Forces always occur in pairs, but these two forces act on two different objects.

  • Because the action–reaction pair acts on two different objects, they do not balance each other. Equal and opposite forces acting on the same object balance each other.
  • Newton's third law applies to all types of forces — contact (pushing a table) and non-contact (magnetic, electrostatic, gravitational).
  • Walking/Running: You push the ground backwards with your feet; friction acts forward on you and pushes you ahead. Without friction, your foot would slip.
  • Row a canoe: Pushing water backwards makes the water push the paddle forward — the canoe moves forward.
  • Rocket launch: The engine expels gas downwards; the gas exerts an equal upward force on the rocket. When this upward force exceeds the rocket's weight, it lifts off.
  • Balloon activity: Air rushing out of a balloon pushes the balloon in the opposite direction — a model for how a rocket moves.

Example 6.7: Earth & Fruit

The Earth and the fruit exert equal and opposite gravitational forces. But the Earth's mass is so huge that its acceleration is extremely small ($a = F/m$), so its motion is not noticed.

Example 6.8: Bullet & Gun

A 2 N force recoils a 5 kg gun at $2/5 = 0.4\text{ m s}^{-2}$, while a 0.1 kg bullet accelerates at $2/0.1 = 20\text{ m s}^{-2}$. Forces are equal, but accelerations differ because masses differ.

Systems

Forces Acting on a System of Objects

Newton's laws can be applied to two or more objects connected together. Consider two boxes of masses $m_1$ and $m_2$ on a frictionless surface connected by a string, pulled by a force $F$. The string transmits a tension $T$ — by Newton's third law, each box pulls the other with equal and opposite tension.

  • Internal forces (like tension) act within the system and need not be considered when treating the system as a whole.
  • External forces (like the pulling force $F$) act from outside and determine the motion.
Acceleration of a connected system $$a = \frac{F}{m_1 + m_2}$$

The system of two boxes accelerates just like a single object of mass $m_1 + m_2$. Apart from $F$, the external forces are the gravitational force $(m_1 g + m_2 g)$ acting downwards, balanced by the total normal force $(N_1 + N_2)$ acting upwards from the ground. Treating connected objects as a system simplifies analysis — for example, your whole body can be studied as a single object while walking.

Real World

At a Glance: Summary of the Chapter

  • Friction acts on an object in the direction opposite to its direction of motion.
  • Newton's First Law: An object at rest remains at rest, and an object in motion continues to move with constant velocity, unless a net force acts upon it.
  • Newton's Second Law: When a net force acts, the object accelerates in the direction of the net force; acceleration is proportional to net force and inversely proportional to mass ($F = ma$).
  • Newton's Third Law: When one object exerts a force on a second, the second simultaneously exerts an equal and opposite force on the first.
  • Chandrayaan-3: The Vikram lander fired its engine in the direction of motion so the exhaust pushed it opposite, slowing it for a soft landing near the Moon's south pole.
  • Limits of Newton's laws: They need modification only very close to massive objects, near the speed of light, and at atomic scales.
