Chapter Overview

What You'll Learn in This Chapter

In Chapter 4, you learned to describe the motion of an object in terms of its position, velocity and acceleration. This chapter uncovers what causes motion — the force. We investigate forces, balanced and unbalanced forces, the ever-present force of friction, and Newton's three laws of motion, learning how to apply them to everyday phenomena from catching a cricket ball to launching a rocket.

Key Topics Covered

- The Concept of Force: force as a vector needing direction, magnitude and unit (SI unit: the newton, N); how a change in magnitude or direction changes the effect of a force
- Measuring the magnitude of a force using a spring balance; range of forces from millinewtons (10⁻³ N) to yoctonewtons (10⁻²⁴ N)
- Balanced and unbalanced forces: net force as the sum (same direction) or difference (opposite directions) of individual forces
- Equal and opposite forces on an extended object causing rotation; forces acting at an angle to each other
- The Force of Friction: opposing motion, dependence on the nature of surfaces, and the gravitational (weight) and normal forces that balance an object
- Galileo's thought experiments and the idea that in the absence of friction an object keeps moving at constant velocity
- Newton's First Law of Motion (Law of Inertia): an object at rest stays at rest, and an object in motion continues with constant velocity unless a net force acts on it
- Position-time and velocity-time graphs for objects at rest and moving with constant velocity
- Newton's Second Law of Motion: a = F/m and F = ma; the definition of 1 newton = 1 kg m s⁻²
- Weight as a gravitational force: F = mg with g = 9.8 m s⁻² (≈ 10 m s⁻² for quick estimates)
- The more complete momentum form of Newton's second law: rate of change of momentum is proportional to the net force
- Applications of Newton's second law: catching a fast cricket ball, airbags in vehicles, and cracking a coconut
- Newton's Third Law of Motion: equal and opposite action–reaction pairs acting on two different objects
- Applications of Newton's third law: walking and running (friction), climbing a coconut tree, rowing a canoe, and rocket launch (including Chandrayaan-3's Vikram lander)
- Newton's third law applies to both contact and non-contact forces (magnetic, electrostatic, and gravitational)
- Forces acting on a system of objects: internal forces (tension) vs external forces; acceleration of connected bodies as a single system a = F/(mm₁ + m₂)
- "At a Glance" summary statements of all three laws of motion and friction
