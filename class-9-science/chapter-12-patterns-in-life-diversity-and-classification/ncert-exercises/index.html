Patterns in Life: Diversity and Classification

Revise, Reflect, Refine

1.
1 Marks
Meena and Hari observed an animal in their garden. Hari called it an insect while Meena said it was an earthworm. Choose the correct option which confirms that it is an insect.
  1. (i) Bilateral symmetrical body
  2. (ii) Body with jointed legs
  3. (iii) Cylindrical body
  4. (iv) Body with little segmentation
View Solution & Marking Scheme

Answer: (ii) Body with jointed legs. Jointed legs are a characteristic feature of arthropods, the animal group to which insects belong.

2.
1 Marks
Sponges represent one of the simplest animal body plans. Their bodies lack true tissues and organs. Which feature of sponge cells supports its classification under the animal kingdom?
  1. (i) Absence of mitochondria
  2. (ii) Ability to photosynthesise
  3. (iii) Presence of a cell membrane
  4. (iv) Presence of a cell wall
View Solution & Marking Scheme

Answer: (iii) Presence of a cell membrane. Sponge cells have the basic cellular organisation of animal cells; the other options do not support their classification under Animalia.

3.
2 Marks
Observe two different animals in your immediate environment. What features help you distinguish between them? How do these features help place them into different groups?
View Solution & Marking Scheme

Observe features such as body symmetry, segmentation, appendages, body covering, skeleton, level of organisation, habitat, mode of nutrition and reproduction. Shared and contrasting characteristics help scientists place organisms into meaningful groups.

4.
3 Marks
How would a scientist justify choosing cellular organisation as a more fundamental characteristic for the basis of classification rather than the presence of xylem and phloem?
View Solution & Marking Scheme

Cellular organisation is a broad characteristic applicable to all living organisms. Xylem and phloem are specialised tissues found only in particular plant groups. Therefore, cellular organisation provides a more fundamental basis for major classification.

5.
3 Marks
You find an unlabelled slide of a single-celled organism that has a well-defined nucleus and multiple cilia. Which group would it most likely belong to? Give reasons.
View Solution & Marking Scheme

Protista. The organism is single-celled and has a well-defined nucleus, so it is a unicellular eukaryote. Multiple cilia are consistent with protists such as Paramecium.

6.
3 Marks
How does the diversity of organisms contribute to the balance and stability of an ecosystem?
View Solution & Marking Scheme

Different organisms perform different ecological roles. Plants produce food and oxygen, animals pollinate flowers and disperse seeds, while microorganisms recycle nutrients. The interdependence of these roles supports food webs, nutrient cycling and ecosystem stability.

7.
3 Marks
If all unicellular organisms were grouped into a single kingdom, what problems would arise?
View Solution & Marking Scheme

Unicellular organisms are not all alike. Some are prokaryotic while others are eukaryotic; their nutrition, cell structures and ecological roles also differ. Placing them all in one kingdom would hide these fundamental differences.

8.
3 Marks
Viruses were studied in earlier classes. Why are they not placed in any of the five kingdoms? Give reasons.
View Solution & Marking Scheme

Viruses are acellular and therefore do not possess the cellular organisation used as a fundamental basis of the five-kingdom classification. They contain genetic material but remain inactive outside a host cell.

9.
3 Marks
If you were asked to revise the five kingdom classification, would you create a separate category for viruses or keep them outside the system? Justify your answer and explain what this indicates about the evolving nature of scientific classification.
View Solution & Marking Scheme

A justified answer is to keep viruses outside the five-kingdom system because they lack cellular organisation. A revised system could define a suitable category if future evidence supports one. This demonstrates that scientific classification is an evolving process and can change as new evidence becomes available.

10.
3 Marks
Viruses contain genetic material like living organisms but lack cellular organisation. Which features prevent them from fitting into the five kingdom system? What does this tell us about the limitations of classification systems?
View Solution & Marking Scheme

Viruses lack cellular organisation and remain inactive outside a host cell, so they do not fit the cellular criteria used for the five kingdoms. This shows that classification systems are models based on selected characteristics and available scientific evidence, and therefore have limitations.

11.
4 Marks
Both pteridophytes and bryophytes lack flowers and seeds, yet they are placed in different groups. Explain this classification using their key features.
View Solution & Marking Scheme

Bryophytes lack true roots, stems and leaves and do not have vascular tissues; they require water for reproduction. Pteridophytes have true roots, stems and leaves and possess xylem and phloem, although they still reproduce through spores and require water for reproduction. These structural differences justify their separate groups.

12.
2 Marks
In the classification hierarchy, which group — class or genus — has fewer members but more features in common? Explain your answer.
View Solution & Marking Scheme

Genus. As we move downward through the classification hierarchy, the number of organisms in a group generally decreases while the number of shared characteristics increases. A genus is therefore more specific than a class.

13.
3 Marks
A scientist discovers a new organism with the characteristic features of locomotion and autotrophic nutrition. Which character(s) would help the scientist identify the organism belonging to Protista according to the five kingdom classification?
View Solution & Marking Scheme

The organism should be unicellular and eukaryotic. Locomotion and autotrophic nutrition alone are not sufficient because different organisms can show these features. The chapter uses unicellularity and the presence of a true nucleus as important criteria for Protista.

14.
3 Marks
A researcher identified a unicellular eukaryotic organism as fungi. What identification key would you suggest according to the five kingdom classification to keep a unicellular organism in the Kingdom Fungi?
View Solution & Marking Scheme

Look for fungal characteristics such as a chitin cell wall and heterotrophic nutrition by absorption. The chapter uses yeast as an example of a unicellular organism placed in Fungi because it has fungal characteristics.

15.
5 Marks

During a long-term ecological study, students examined organisms collected from three different environments — a freshwater pond, damp soil near decaying logs and the digestive tract of animals. Instead of naming organisms directly, scientists recorded only structural, cellular and nutritional features as given in the table below.

OrganismsKey Observations
PMicroscopic; no true nucleus; rigid cell covering; survives high salinity and temperature
QMulticellular; filamentous body; cell wall present; no chlorophyll; grows on dead organic matter
RUnicellular; true nucleus; contractile vacuole present; moves using flagella; shows photosynthesis in light but heterotrophic in the absence of light
SMulticellular; well-differentiated tissues; backbone present; aquatic respiration during early life stage
TAcellular; contains genetic material; remains inactive outside a host cell

The students realised that some organisms fit neatly into Whittaker’s five kingdom classification, while others challenged the very basis of this classification.

Based on the case study, answer the following questions —

  1. Identify one organism that clearly belongs to the Kingdom Fungi. State one observation that supports your answer.
  2. Which organism would be placed in the Kingdom Monera? Mention one characteristic that justifies this placement.
  3. Organisms R and Q are both eukaryotic, yet they are placed in different kingdoms. Analyse the criteria that separate them.
  4. Explain why organism S cannot be classified using the mode of nutrition alone.
  5. Organism T does not fit into any of the five kingdoms. Which fundamental characteristic used in classification does it lack and what does this reveal about the limitations of classification systems?
View Solution & Marking Scheme

(i) Q — Fungi. It is multicellular, filamentous, has a cell wall, lacks chlorophyll and grows on dead organic matter.

(ii) P — Monera. It has no true nucleus, so it is prokaryotic.

(iii) R is unicellular and eukaryotic with mixed nutrition and locomotion, fitting Protista. Q is multicellular, filamentous and heterotrophic by absorption, fitting Fungi.

(iv) Mode of nutrition alone cannot classify S because many organisms are heterotrophic. Its multicellularity, differentiated tissues and backbone provide important structural criteria.

(v) T lacks cellular organisation because it is acellular. This shows that a classification system based on cellular organisation has limits when organisms fall outside those criteria.

The Journey Beyond

  • Visit a nearby park or a water body. Observe the water body and its surroundings. Identify ten organisms and classify them into kingdoms.
  • Interview a gardener or a forest worker. Ask them how they identify species. Compare the traditional scientific methods of identification.
  • If you were studying the Pakke Tiger Reserve or any other bird sanctuary of your choice, how would you organise the information about the bird species?
  • Conduct a survey in a forest area near a village, identify edible and poisonous mushrooms with the help of indigenous knowledge of community resource person(s).
  • Collect and study the postal stamps of India and the world released over time on diversified flora and fauna. Based on your learning, make your own criteria and classify them. Organise a philately (collection of postage stamps) exhibition in your school and showcase your work in this exhibition.
  • Investigate an extinct species and analyse how its disappearance impacted the organisms that depend on it, and how this affected the ecological balance of the ecosystem.
  • Study the diversity of farm animals used in different types of farming practices in various parts of the country.

What knowledge do you think scientists would come up with further and how would it affect the classification system?

The Quest Continues…

(vi) If classification were based only on habitat, which organisms might be incorrectly grouped together? Explain the scientific consequences of such a classification.

View Solution

Organisms sharing a habitat could be unrelated and could differ in cellular organisation, nutrition, body structure and ancestry. Such a system would group organisms that do not share meaningful biological relationships and could obscure evolutionary relationships.

(vii) Imagine scientists discover a new organism that is multicellular, eukaryotic, lacks chlorophyll and absorbs nutrients from a host externally. Should it be placed under fungi or animalia? Justify your reasoning using classification criteria.

View Solution

Fungi would be the closer placement using the chapter's criteria. The organism is multicellular and eukaryotic, lacks chlorophyll and obtains nutrition by absorption, which aligns with fungal characteristics.
