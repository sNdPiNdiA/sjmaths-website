Exercise Solutions

Revise, Reflect, Refine

Q1. In the previous chapter, a weightlifter is shown holding a barbell steady in her hands. Is she doing any work on the barbell while holding it steady?
Answer: No, she is not doing any work on the barbell. According to the definition of work (W = F × s), work is done only when a force causes a displacement. Since the barbell is held steady, there is no displacement (s = 0), so W = 0. Even though the weightlifter feels tired, no work is done on the barbell in the scientific sense.
Q2. Is the work done by friction on the stack of coins that travels on a rough surface — positive, negative or zero?
Answer: The work done by friction is negative. Friction acts opposite to the direction of motion (displacement). Since the force of friction and the displacement are in opposite directions, the work done is negative.
Q3. When you pedal a bicycle on a flat road, your muscles supply energy. In what forms does this muscular energy appear as you ride?
Answer: The muscular energy appears as:
  • Kinetic energy of the bicycle and rider (motion)
  • Thermal energy (heat generated in muscles and due to friction in the chain and tyres)
  • Sound energy (from the chain and moving parts)
Some energy is also lost as heat to the surroundings.
Q4. Two objects A and B of mass m and 4m have the same kinetic energy. What is the ratio of the magnitude of velocities of A and B?
Answer: Given KE_A = KE_B

½mv_A² = ½(4m)v_B²

v_A² = 4v_B²

v_A = 2v_B

Therefore, v_A : v_B = 2 : 1.
Q5. Does the kinetic energy of an object which moves with constant velocity change with its position?
Answer: No. Kinetic energy depends on mass and velocity (KE = ½mv²). If velocity is constant, kinetic energy remains constant regardless of position.
Q6. Does the potential energy of an object near the surface of the Earth change if it moves with constant velocity in the horizontal direction? What if the object is gradually raised in the vertical direction?
Answer:
  • Horizontal motion at constant velocity: No change in PE, because height (h) remains constant.
  • Gradually raised vertically: PE increases because PE = mgh and h increases.
Q7. For the situation depicted in Fig. 7.19, calculate the mechanical energy of the ball just before it hits the ground and show that even at this position, it is mgh.
Answer: Just before hitting the ground, the height h' ≈ 0.

PE = mgh' ≈ 0

KE = ½mv² = ½m(2gh) = mgh (using v² = 2gh from conservation)

Total mechanical energy = PE + KE = 0 + mgh = mgh.

This shows mechanical energy is conserved throughout the fall.
Q8. You may have seen an exhibit like that in Fig. 7.22 in a science park, where a ball is released from the highest point. Describe how the kinetic energy and potential energy change at points A, B and C. Why do subsequent points, such as C, D and E usually have lower heights compared to the previous ones? Could it have anything to do with the energy lost due to friction?
Answer:
  • At A (highest point): Maximum PE, KE = 0
  • At B (lowest point): Maximum KE, minimum PE
  • At C (rising again): KE decreases, PE increases, but total energy is slightly less than at A due to friction
Points C, D, E have lower heights because friction and air resistance dissipate mechanical energy as thermal energy. The total mechanical energy decreases slightly at each bounce, so the ball cannot return to its original height.
Q9. Explain why roads on hills are built to wind around in gentle slopes rather than going straight up.
Answer: A gentle slope increases the length (L) of the inclined plane while keeping the height (h) the same. Since mechanical advantage MA = L/h, a longer slope means less effort force is needed to raise the vehicle. Going straight up would require a very large force equal to the full weight of the vehicle.
Q10. To reach a higher floor, we find climbing an inclined ladder easier in comparison to climbing a vertical ladder. Explain why.
Answer: An inclined ladder acts as an inclined plane. The mechanical advantage is greater than 1 because the length of the ladder is larger than the vertical height. This reduces the effort force needed, even though the total work done (mgh) remains the same.
Q11. Why is it easier to open the lid of a can by using a spoon as shown in Fig. 7.35?
Answer: The spoon acts as a Class 2 lever. The fulcrum is at the edge of the can, the load is the lid's resistance, and the effort is applied at the handle end. By increasing the effort arm, the spoon multiplies the applied force, making it easier to pry open the lid.
Q12. Why do you push an object closer to the scissors' fulcrum when you want to cut an object which is hard?
Answer: Moving the object closer to the fulcrum decreases the load arm. Since MA = effort arm / load arm, decreasing the load arm increases the mechanical advantage, giving a larger cutting force at the object.
Q13. Throughout history, many designs of perpetual motion machines have been proposed but none actually work. Why do all real machines eventually slow down and stop? Explain in terms of work and energy.
Answer: No machine can run forever because of friction and air resistance. These non-conservative forces do negative work on the machine, converting mechanical energy into thermal energy (heat). Since energy is dissipated, the machine cannot sustain motion indefinitely. This also obeys the law of conservation of energy — energy is not destroyed, but transformed into unusable forms.

Additional Exercises

Q5 (Long). A student is slowly lifted straight up in an elevator from ground level to the top floor of a building (h = 72.5 m). Later, the same student climbs the staircase to the same top. Given m = 50 kg, g = 10 m/s², find the gain in PE in both cases and state your conclusion about path dependence.
Answer:

(i) Elevator: ΔPE = mgh = 50 × 10 × 72.5 = 36,250 J

(ii) Staircase: ΔPE = mgh = 50 × 10 × 72.5 = 36,250 J

Conclusion: Potential energy depends only on the vertical displacement (height), not on the path taken. Both methods give the same gain in PE.
Q6 (Long). A crane lifts a mass m to the 10th floor in time t. It then raises the same mass to the 20th floor in double the time. How much more energy and power are required?
Answer: Let height of each floor = h.

To 10th floor: height = 10h, Energy = mg(10h), Power = mg(10h) / t

To 20th floor: height = 20h, Energy = mg(20h) = 2 × previous energy

Power = mg(20h) / (2t) = mg(10h) / t = same power

Energy doubles, but power remains the same because the time also doubles.
Q7 (Long). Which factors determine the energy required to raise a flag from the ground to the top of a tall flagpole using a pulley? Does raising the flag slowly or quickly change the amount of work done? If the speed at which the flag is raised is doubled, how does the power requirement change?
Answer:

Factors: Mass of flag (m), height of pole (h), and g.

Work done = mgh. It does not depend on speed or time.

Raising slowly or quickly requires the same amount of work.

Power = Work / time. If speed doubles, time halves, so power doubles.
Q8 (Long). A man of mass 60 kg rides a scooter of mass 100 kg. He accelerates the scooter to velocity v. The next day, his son (40 kg) joins as a passenger. If the scooter reaches the same speed in the same time interval, what is the ratio of fuel used on the two days?
Answer:

Day 1: Total mass = 160 kg. KE = ½(160)v² = 80v²

Day 2: Total mass = 200 kg. KE = ½(200)v² = 100v²

Ratio of fuel (Day 2 / Day 1) = 100v² / 80v² = 5 : 4

More mass means more kinetic energy for the same speed, so more fuel is needed.
Q10 (Long). A ball of mass 2 kg is thrown up with velocity 20 m/s. (i) Identify the sign of work done by gravity during upward and downward motion. (ii) If the ball reaches 19.4 m, how much work was done by air resistance? (g = 10 m/s²)
Answer:

(i) Upward: Gravity acts downward, displacement upward → negative work.

Downward: Gravity acts downward, displacement downward → positive work.

(ii) Without air resistance, max height = v²/2g = 400/20 = 20 m.

Actual height = 19.4 m.

Work against gravity = mgh = 2 × 10 × 20 = 400 J

Work against air resistance = mgh_actual = 2 × 10 × 19.4 = 388 J

Work done by air resistance = 400 - 388 = 12 J (negative, as it opposes motion).
