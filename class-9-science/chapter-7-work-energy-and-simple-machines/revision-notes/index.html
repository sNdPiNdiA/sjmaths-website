02 · Work

Work — Complete Revision

\[W=F\times s\]

Work is done when a force acting on an object produces displacement in the direction of that force. \(F\) is force and \(s\) is displacement in the direction of the force.

  • SI unit: joule (J).
  • \(1\,\mathrm{J}=1\,\mathrm{N\,m}\).
  • Work is a scalar quantity; its sign describes the energy effect.
The F–D–D Work Test

Mnemonic: “F–D–D” → Force → Displacement → Direction.

  1. Is a force acting?
  2. Is there displacement?
  3. Is there displacement in the direction of that particular force?
When Is Work Zero?
  • No force: \(F=0\).
  • No displacement: \(s=0\).
  • Force is perpendicular to displacement.

Classic trap: A person can feel tired while holding a heavy object stationary, but the mechanical work done on that stationary object is zero because its displacement is zero.

Positive, Negative and Zero Work
TypeDirectionTypical effect
PositiveForce and displacement same directionEnergy transferred to object
NegativeForce opposite displacementEnergy removed from motion
ZeroNo displacement along forceNo work by that force

Mnemonic: “Same = +, Against = −, Across = 0.”

Variable Force / Graph Trick

When force varies with displacement, work is represented by the area under the force–displacement graph: \[ W=\text{area under the }F\text{–}s\text{ graph}. \]

Exam Traps
  • Force alone does not guarantee work.
  • Displacement alone does not guarantee work by a particular force.
  • Always identify which force is being considered.
  • Do not confuse physical effort/fatigue with mechanical work on an object.
03 · Work–Energy Theorem

Work–Energy Theorem

\[W=\Delta E\]

Work done on an object or system equals the change in its energy. For translational motion: \[ W_{\rm net}=\Delta K=K_f-K_i. \]

  • Positive net work → kinetic energy increases.
  • Negative net work → kinetic energy decreases.
  • Zero net work → kinetic energy remains unchanged.

Numerical trick: If force/displacement and initial/final speeds are involved, the work–energy theorem can often solve the problem directly without a long sequence of kinematic steps.

Stopping trick: If an object finally stops, \(K_f=0\), so \(W_{\rm net}=-K_i\).

05 · Mechanical Energy

Mechanical Energy

\[E_m=K+U\]

Mechanical energy is the combination of kinetic and potential energy.

Mnemonic: “Move = K; Position/Shape = U.”

06 · Kinetic Energy

Kinetic Energy — Formula, Dependence and Tricks

\[K=\frac12mv^2\]

Kinetic energy is the energy possessed by an object because of its motion.

Dependence
  • \(K\propto m\): double mass → double KE.
  • \(K\propto v^2\): double speed → four times KE.
  • Triple speed → nine times KE.
  • Half speed → one-fourth KE.

Mnemonic: “Mass once, Speed square.”

Ratio Shortcut
\[ \frac{K_1}{K_2} = \frac{m_1}{m_2} \left(\frac{v_1}{v_2}\right)^2 \]
Equal Kinetic Energy
\[m_1v_1^2=m_2v_2^2\]

For equal KE, the lighter body has the greater speed.

Unit Conversion Trap
\[v(\mathrm{m/s})=\frac5{18}v(\mathrm{km/h})\]

Mnemonic: km/h → m/s = ×5/18.

Stopping
\[W_{\rm stopping}=-K_i\]
07 · Potential Energy

Potential Energy — Complete Classification

Potential energy is stored by deformation or by the relative positions of objects in a system.

TypeStored because ofExample
ElasticDeformationStretched rubber band, compressed spring, bent bow
MagneticRelative positions of magnetsSeparated magnetic poles
ElectrostaticRelative positions of chargesSeparated electric charges
GravitationalRelative position in gravitational fieldRaised object near Earth
Gravitational Potential Energy
\[U=mgh\]

Near Earth's surface, \(g\) is treated as approximately constant. \(h\) is measured relative to the chosen reference level.

Mnemonic: “m-g-h = mass × gravity × height.”

Path Independence

For gravitational potential energy near Earth's surface: \[ \Delta U=mg\Delta h. \] The change depends on vertical height change, not whether the object travels vertically, along stairs or along a ramp, when initial and final heights are the same.

Exam trick: Same \(m\), same \(g\), same height change → same gravitational PE change.

08 · Conservation

Conservation of Mechanical Energy

\[K_i+U_i=K_f+U_f\]

When the stated ideal conditions hold—no frictional/mechanical-energy loss and no other relevant non-conservative work changing the mechanical energy— the sum \(K+U\) remains constant.

Free-Fall Pattern
PositionPotential EnergyKinetic Energy
TopMaximumMinimum / zero if released from rest
MiddleDecreasingIncreasing
BottomMinimum relative to zero levelMaximum

Mnemonic: “Down U, Up K.”

Free-Fall Speed Shortcut
\[mgh=\frac12mv^2\Rightarrow v=\sqrt{2gh}\]

Mass cancels, so ideal free-fall speed from a given height is independent of mass.

Friction

With friction, mechanical energy is transferred partly into thermal energy and possibly sound. Total energy remains accounted for, but mechanical energy \(K+U\) is not constant.

Exam distinction: Total energy conservation is broader than mechanical-energy conservation.

09 · Power

Power — Rate of Doing Work

\[P=\frac{W}{t}\]
  • SI unit: watt (W).
  • \(1\,\mathrm{W}=1\,\mathrm{J\,s^{-1}}\).
  • \(1\,\mathrm{hp}=746\,\mathrm{W}\).

Mnemonic: “Power = Work per Time.”

Same work in less time means greater power. For fixed work, \(P\propto1/t\). For fixed time, \(P\propto W\).

Lifting
\[W=mgh,\qquad P=\frac{mgh}{t}\]
Acceleration
\[W=\Delta K=\frac12m(v^2-u^2),\qquad P=\frac{\Delta K}{t}\]
10 · Simple Machines

Simple Machines — Master Framework

Simple machines make tasks easier by changing the magnitude or direction of the applied force. They do not reduce total work in the ideal case.

\[\mathrm{MA}=\frac{\text{Load}}{\text{Effort}}\]

Mnemonic: “L over E” → Load on top, Effort below.

MachineMain advantageKey relation
Fixed pulleyChanges directionIdeal MA = 1
Movable/system pulleyCan reduce effortMA can exceed 1
Inclined planeReduces effort by increasing distance\(MA=L/h\)
LeverUses arm lengths to multiply force\(F_ed_e=F_ld_l\)

Golden machine rule: “Gain force, lose distance; save force, spend distance.”

11 · Pulley

Pulley — Fixed and Movable

Fixed Pulley
  • Ideal effort equals load.
  • Ideal mechanical advantage = 1.
  • Main benefit is changing the direction of effort.

Mnemonic: “Fixed = Direction.”

Movable / Pulley System
  • Can have mechanical advantage greater than 1.
  • Can lift heavier loads with smaller effort.
  • Used in practical systems such as cranes and elevators.

Trap: Do not say every pulley automatically gives MA greater than 1; a fixed pulley has ideal MA 1.

12 · Inclined Plane

Inclined Plane — Formula + Geometry

An inclined plane trades force for distance. A gentler and longer ramp requires less effort over a greater distance.

\[F'L=mgh\] \[\mathrm{MA}=\frac{mg}{F'}=\frac{L}{h}\]
Geometry Shortcut

If vertical height \(h\) and horizontal base \(b\) are given:

\[L=\sqrt{h^2+b^2}\]

Then use \(MA=L/h\).

Mnemonic: “Ramp MA = Long / Height.”

Making the Ramp Gentler
  • Ramp length increases.
  • Height remains the same.
  • Mechanical advantage increases.
  • Required effort decreases.
  • Distance through which effort acts increases.
13 · Lever

Lever — Complete Revision

A lever is a rigid bar rotating about a fixed point called the fulcrum. The three parts are fulcrum, load and effort. Their distances from the fulcrum are the load arm and effort arm.

\[F_ed_e=F_ld_l\] \[\mathrm{MA}=\frac{F_l}{F_e}=\frac{d_e}{d_l}\]

Mnemonic: “Longer effort arm = easier lifting.”

Seesaw / Balance Trick
\[m_1d_1=m_2d_2\]

Do not compare masses alone. Compare mass × distance from the fulcrum. A heavier person must sit closer to the fulcrum to balance a lighter person.

Three Classes of Lever
ClassMiddle partExamples
IFulcrumScissors, crowbar, pliers, balance scale, seesaw
IILoadLemon squeezer, wheelbarrow, bottle opener
IIIEffortTongs, tweezers, broom, hammer, oar

Mnemonic: “1-F, 2-L, 3-E.”
Class I → Fulcrum in middle.
Class II → Load in middle.
Class III → Effort in middle.

Second cue: F-L-E = 1-2-3.

16 · Numerical Tricks

Fast Problem-Solving Toolkit

A. Work Questions

Write \(W=Fs\), then determine direction. Opposite direction means negative work; perpendicular means zero work by that force.

B. Speed / Force / Displacement
\[W_{\rm net}=\frac12m(v^2-u^2)\]

Use this directly when the question links work and speed change.

C. Height / Speed
\[ mgh+\frac12mu^2=mgh'+\frac12mv^2 \]

For ideal motion between heights, cancel \(m\) early.

D. Power

First find work/energy change, then divide by time: \(P=W/t\).

E. Ramp
  1. Draw the right triangle.
  2. Find ramp length \(L\) using Pythagoras if needed.
  3. Use \(MA=L/h\).
  4. If effort is required, use \(MA=Load/Effort\).
F. Seesaw / Lever
\[ \text{force}\times\text{distance} = \text{force}\times\text{distance} \]

For masses in the same gravitational field: \(m_1d_1=m_2d_2\).

G. Graphs
  • Force–displacement graph → area gives work.
  • Potential-energy graph on a frictionless track → use mechanical-energy conservation.

19 · Error Check

Top Exam Mistakes

  1. Using \(W=Fs\) without checking direction.
  2. Thinking force alone guarantees work.
  3. Thinking tiredness automatically means mechanical work on the object.
  4. Forgetting the square on velocity in KE.
  5. Using km/h directly in \(K=\frac12mv^2\).
  6. Forgetting the chosen reference level for potential energy.
  7. Assuming mechanical energy is always conserved even with friction.
  8. Confusing power with work.
  9. Writing \(MA=Effort/Load\) instead of \(Load/Effort\).
  10. Using horizontal base instead of ramp length in \(MA=L/h\).
  11. Confusing effort arm with load arm.
  12. Balancing a seesaw by mass alone instead of mass × distance.
  13. Assuming every pulley has MA greater than 1.
  14. Thinking a machine creates energy.
  15. Ignoring friction in real-machine or roller-coaster questions.
