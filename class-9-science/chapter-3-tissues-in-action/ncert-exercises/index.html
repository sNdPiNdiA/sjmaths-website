Q1 1. Meristematic tissues divide repeatedly. What property of their cells allows them to do this?
(i) They have thick walls for protection.
(ii) They contain large vacuoles that store nutrients.
(iii) They have thin walls, dense cytoplasm and large prominent nucleus.
(iv) They are functionally differentiated cells.
Show Ideal Answer

Correct Option: (iii) They have thin walls, dense cytoplasm and large prominent nucleus.

Explanation:

  • Thin Cellulose Walls: Facilitate easy cell division, expansion, and stretchability during growth.
  • Dense Cytoplasm: Highly active metabolically, providing the necessary cellular organelles and proteins for rapid cell replication.
  • Large Prominent Nucleus: Controls transcription and replicates DNA rapidly to keep pace with the cell cycle.
  • Lack of Vacuoles: Dividing cells do not need storage spaces or turgidity-regulating vacuoles, which would otherwise physically obstruct cell division.
Q2 2. If a plant is unable to transport food from leaves to roots which tissue is malfunctioning?
(i) Xylem
(ii) Phloem
(iii) Epidermis
(iv) Sclerenchyma
Show Ideal Answer

Correct Option: (ii) Phloem

Explanation:

  • Phloem: It is the complex vascular tissue responsible for translocation—the bidirectional transport of food (organic solutes/sucrose) synthesized in the leaves to roots, storage organs, and developing tissues.
  • Xylem: Functions in the transport of water and dissolved minerals from the roots up to the leaves.
  • Epidermis: Functions in protection and gaseous exchange.
  • Sclerenchyma: Provides mechanical strength and rigidity to plant parts.
Q3 3. Why are the epithelial tissues that line an animal’s internal organs usually only one or a few cells thick?
(i) To store food efficiently.
(ii) To provide maximum strength.
(iii) To allow quick exchange of materials across them.
(iv) To reduce friction.
Show Ideal Answer

Correct Option: (iii) To allow quick exchange of materials across them.

Explanation:

  • Internal organs (like the alveoli of the lungs, blood vessels, and nephrons of kidneys) require efficient diffusion, filtration, or absorption.
  • A thin layer (such as simple squamous epithelium) minimizes the barrier thickness, allowing molecules like oxygen, carbon dioxide, nutrients, and waste products to cross rapidly via passive transport mechanisms.
Q4 4. You can perform these two jumps:
Straight-leg jump — keep knees and ankles stiff.
Normal jump — bend knees and ankles naturally.
How did your ankle, knee and hip positions differ between the two jumps?
Show Ideal Answer

Observations and Differences:

  • Normal Jump (Natural Flexion):
    • Preparation: The ankles, knees, and hips undergo significant flexion (bending). This stretches the attached muscles and tendons (like the Achilles tendon), storing elastic potential energy.
    • Takeoff & Landing: Rapid extension of these joints produces vertical thrust. On landing, they flex again to absorb the impact force over time, acting as biological shock absorbers.
  • Straight-Leg Jump (Stiff Joints):
    • Preparation & Takeoff: The ankles and knees are kept locked and extended. Movement is restricted to minor ankle/foot extension or hip adjustment. The thrust generated is minimal.
    • Landing: Landing with stiff joints forces the impact shock directly up the skeletal column (tibia, femur, pelvis, and spine), resulting in a harsh, uncomfortable landing.
Q5 5. Which type of joint is involved when you bend your knees and ankles?
(i) Ball and socket
(ii) Hinge
(iii) Pivot
Show Ideal Answer

Correct Option: (ii) Hinge

Explanation:

  • Hinge Joints: Allow movement primarily in a single plane (flexion and extension), resembling the movement of a door hinge. Bending the knee and ankle joint involves movement in this single plane.
  • Ball and Socket: (e.g., shoulder and hip joints) allows rotational movement in multiple directions.
  • Pivot Joint: (e.g., neck joint between atlas and axis) allows rotation around a central axis.
Q6 6. In each of the following cases (A, B, C and D), choose the correct option as given below:
(i) Both (A) and (R) are true, and (R) is the correct explanation of (A).
(ii) Both (A) and (R) are true, but (R) is not the correct explanation of (A).
(iii) (A) is true, but (R) is false.
(iv) (A) is false, but (R) is true.

A. Assertion: Epithelium is well-suited for gas exchange in the lungs.
Reason: It consists of multiple layers of tall cells that slow down diffusion.

B. Assertion: Cardiac muscle can contract continuously without fatigue.
Reason: Cardiac muscle cells have a high number of mitochondria and an abundant blood supply.

C. Assertion: Tendons connect bone to bone and allow joint movement.
Reason: Tendons are made of tough connective tissue that transmits force from muscle to bone.

D. Assertion: In a hinge joint, movement occurs primarily in one plane.
Reason: The bone ends are shaped to allow sliding in all directions.
Show Ideal Answer

Solutions:

  • A: (iii) (A) is true, but (R) is false.
    Justification: The alveolar epithelium is extremely thin (simple squamous, one-cell thick) to maximize and speed up gas exchange, not slow it down. Multiple layers of tall cells would hinder diffusion.
  • B: (i) Both (A) and (R) are true, and (R) is the correct explanation of (A).
    Justification: Cardiac muscles contract rhythmically and continuously. To support this relentless metabolic demand without fatigue, they contain a vast network of blood vessels for oxygen delivery and an exceptionally high concentration of mitochondria to generate ATP.
  • C: (iv) (A) is false, but (R) is true.
    Justification: The assertion is false because ligaments connect bone to bone; tendons connect muscle to bone. The reason is true because tendons are tough, fibrous connective tissues that transmit muscular force to bones.
  • D: (iii) (A) is true, but (R) is false.
    Justification: In a hinge joint, movement is limited to a single plane. The reason is false because the articulating bone ends are shaped like a cylinder in a trough to restrict movement, preventing sliding in all directions.

Q8 8. In a forest, it was observed that one of the trees was severely debarked by an elephant to meet its food requirements (Fig. 3.22). Answer the following:
(i) Which function(s) of the tree is/are hampered by debarking?
(ii) Which plant tissue would be affected by further damage to the tree trunk even after debarking?
(iii) Which function of the tree would be hampered if the tissues beneath the bark were severely damaged?
(iv) What assumptions are you making to answer the questions above? How would the answer change if your assumptions are also changed?
Show Ideal Answer

Analysis:

  • (i) Hampered Functions due to Debarking:
    • Protection: Removing the bark strips away the outer protective cork layer, exposing the delicate inner tissues to pathogens, fungi, and insect infestations.
    • Translocation of Food: The inner bark contains the active **phloem** tissue. Stripping the bark halts the downward transport of synthesized sugars from leaves to the root system, starving the roots of energy.
  • (ii) Tissues Affected by Further Deep Damage:
    Deeper damage would penetrate the lateral meristems (vascular cambium) and the primary/secondary xylem tissue (wood).
  • (iii) Functions Hampered if Beneath-Bark Tissues are Damaged:
    Severe damage to the vascular cambium prevents secondary growth (healing and widening of the stem). If the **xylem** is damaged, the ascending transport of water and dissolved minerals from the soil to the leaves is cut off, causing rapid wilting and tree death.
  • (iv) Assumptions Made & Alternative Scenarios:
    • Assumption 1: Girdling: We assume the elephant stripped the bark completely around the circumference (ring-girdling). If the debarking is only partial, the remaining strip of intact phloem can still transport food, allowing the tree to survive and eventually heal.
    • Assumption 2: Tree Type: We assume the tree is a dicotyledonous woody tree with distinct, layered bark (phloem) and wood (xylem). If it were a monocotyledon (like a palm tree) with scattered vascular bundles throughout the stem, stripping the outer surface would not stop conduction because many vascular bundles reside deep within the core.
Q9 9. Aamrapali observed that a young mango sapling’s stem bends flexibly during monsoon winds and does not break. Which tissue is responsible for this flexibility? Predict and provide your explanation of the impact if the existing tissue was replaced by sclerenchyma.
Show Ideal Answer

Solution:

  • Responsible Tissue: Collenchyma tissue is responsible for providing mechanical strength combined with elasticity and flexibility in young dicot stems, petioles, and leaf margins.
  • Impact of Replacement with Sclerenchyma:
    • Sclerenchyma is composed of dead, highly lignified cells with extremely thick, rigid cell walls.
    • If collenchyma were replaced by sclerenchyma, the young stem would become brittle, stiff, and completely lose its flexibility.
    • Under heavy monsoon winds, instead of bending elastically to disperse kinetic energy, the stem would undergo high shear stress and snap or break easily.
Q10 10. Sohan designed an experiment for the regeneration of sugarcane, where he used cuttings to grow sugarcane. He used two types of cuttings, type ‘A’ and type ‘B’ (Fig. 3.23). After a few weeks, type ‘B’ cuttings sprouted and developed into sugarcane plants, whereas the type ‘A’ cuttings did not sprout.
(i) Why were the type ‘B’ cuttings able to grow as sugarcane but type ‘A’ could not?
(ii) What difference was present in type ‘B’ compared to type ‘A’?
(iii) What observation or measurement was made to determine whether this change had an effect?
(iv) What parameters should be kept the same for both types of cuttings to ensure a fair comparison?
Show Ideal Answer

Solution:

  • (i) Growth Difference: Type ‘B’ cuttings contained nodes bearing lateral buds with active **intercalary or lateral meristematic tissue**. Meristematic cells are capable of active division, allowing shoots and adventitious roots to differentiate and grow. Type ‘A’ cuttings lacked nodes (only internodes) and therefore had no meristematic cells to initiate growth.
  • (ii) Structural Difference: Type ‘B’ cuttings possessed active nodes/buds, whereas Type ‘A’ cuttings consisted solely of internodes.
  • (iii) Metric of Assessment: The presence/absence of sprouting shoots and developing root systems (growth success) observed after a few weeks.
  • (iv) Control Parameters (Fair Comparison): Soil quality, moisture levels (watering frequency), sunlight exposure, temperature, cutting length, cutting thickness, depth of planting, and the parent sugarcane variety from which cuttings were obtained.
Q11 11. During the discussion in class, Rohan gives a statement that, “A tissue is a group of similar cells performing similar functions”. But Rajiv counter argues that, “this is true in case of simple tissues but little different in case of complex tissues”. Provide your explanation in view of the discussion in class.
Show Ideal Answer

Explanation:

Both Rohan and Rajiv are correct in their respective contexts, and their statements represent the classification of plant tissues into simple and complex forms:

  • Rohan’s Statement (Simple Tissues):
    Perfectly describes simple permanent tissues (like parenchyma, collenchyma, and sclerenchyma). These tissues are structurally homogeneous—consisting of a single type of cell (all similar in structure, origin, and shape) that coordinates to perform one specific function (e.g., storage, structural support).
  • Rajiv’s Statement (Complex Tissues):
    Correctly highlights the exception seen in complex permanent tissues (such as xylem and phloem). Complex tissues are structurally heterogeneous—consisting of multiple types of cells (e.g., xylem is made of tracheids, vessels, xylem fibres, and xylem parenchyma) that look different but coordinate tightly to perform a common, unified function (conducting water/minerals).
Q12 12. Coconut husk fibres are used for mats which are tough and fibrous. Which tissue has structural features suitable for providing this strength? Explain why living parenchyma couldn’t serve the same purpose.
Show Ideal Answer

Solution:

  • Responsible Tissue: Sclerenchyma tissue (specifically sclerenchymatous fibres).
    • Structural Suitability: Sclerenchyma cells are dead, highly elongated, and have cell walls uniformly thickened with **lignin** (a chemical cement). Lignin provides exceptional tensile strength, rigidity, and water impermeability, preventing the fibers from collapsing under physical load.
  • Why Living Parenchyma Cannot Serve the Purpose:
    • Parenchyma cells are living, thin-walled, and composed of flexible **cellulose**.
    • They contain large vacuoles and are structurally soft, optimized for metabolic activities, storage, and photosynthesis.
    • Lacking lignified thick walls, parenchyma cells lack tensile strength and would easily collapse, tear, or decay when subjected to the mechanical wear and tear that floor mats experience.
Q13 13. Vibha claims to her friend Neha that, “Meristematic cells are located only at the root and shoot apices”. What do you think about this statement? What question can Neha ask Vibha to help her understand further if the statement is incorrect?
Show Ideal Answer

Critique: Vibha’s statement is incorrect and incomplete. Meristems at the root and shoot apices are only one category (Apical Meristems). Meristematic tissues are situated in other regions of the plant body as well.

Clarifying Questions Neha can ask Vibha:

  1. "If meristems only exist at the tips for length, how does a tree trunk grow wider and increase in diameter (girth) over the years?"
    (Helps Vibha discover the role of **Lateral Meristems** / cambium).
  2. "How do grass leaves rapidly elongate and grow back after they are eaten by herbivores or cut by a lawnmower, if the apical tips have been completely removed?"
    (Helps Vibha discover the role of **Intercalary Meristems** located at nodes and internodes).
Q14 14. A plant cell and an animal cell are of the same size.
(i) Which cell will have a larger vacuole? Give reasons.
(ii) What assumptions are you making to answer the question above?
Show Ideal Answer

Solution:

  • (i) Larger Vacuole: The plant cell will have a significantly larger, central vacuole (often occupying 50% to 90% of the cell volume).
    Reasons:
    • Turgidity & Rigidity: Plants lack skeletal systems; the central vacuole exerts turgor pressure against the cell wall, maintaining the structural shape and erect posture of the plant cell.
    • Metabolic Storage: It stores cell sap, amino acids, sugars, and metabolic waste products.
    • Animal cells, if they contain vacuoles at all, have multiple small, temporary vacuoles used for digestion or excretion.
  • (ii) Assumptions Made:
    • We assume the plant cell is a mature, differentiated vegetative cell (e.g., parenchyma). Younger, actively dividing meristematic plant cells lack large central vacuoles.
    • We assume a typical somatic animal cell, rather than a highly specialized storage cell.
Q15 15. A textbook states, “Each plant tissue performs only one specific function”. What questions would you ask to critically examine the correctness of this statement? What examples of tissues would you take to find out the answers to these questions?
Show Ideal Answer

Critical Inquiry: The textbook statement is too simplistic and technically incorrect, as many tissues are multi-functional.

Questions to Ask:

  1. "Do complex tissues containing different cell types have distinct cells performing different sub-functions that coordinate together?"
  2. "Can a single simple tissue type modify its structure to perform entirely different functions under different environments?"
  3. "Does a protective tissue like the epidermis also engage in physiological exchanges like absorption and transpiration?"

Examples to Test and Disprove the Statement:

  • Xylem (Complex Tissue): Conducts water and dissolved minerals upwards (via vessels/tracheids), stores nutrients (via xylem parenchyma), and provides mechanical skeleton support (via xylem fibres).
  • Parenchyma (Simple Tissue): Stores food in potato tubers, conducts photosynthesis in leaves (as **chlorenchyma**), and provides buoyancy/gas exchange channels in aquatic plants (as **aerenchyma**).
  • Epidermis: Protects internal tissues, secretes a waxy cuticle to prevent transpiration, regulates gaseous exchange via stomata, and absorbs water via specialized root hairs.

Projects, Activities & Real-World Connections

Project 1 Visit a doctor and find out what happens in ligament rupture, cartilage rupture and fracture of bones. How can we reduce the risk by changing our lifestyle and nutritional balance?
Show Guided Solution

Clinical Insights:

  • Ligament Rupture (Sprain/Tear): Ligaments are fibrous connective tissues linking bone to bone. Rupture (common in ACL tears of the knee) causes joint instability, severe swelling, and loss of motion control. Healing takes time because ligaments have a limited direct blood supply.
  • Cartilage Rupture (Meniscus Tear): Cartilage cushions joint articulations. A tear causes clicking sounds, locking joints, pain, and early onset of osteoarthritis. Since mature cartilage is avascular (lacks blood vessels), it does not heal on its own and often requires arthroscopic cleanup.
  • Bone Fracture: A partial or complete break in bone tissue. Activates osteoblasts to rebuild bone minerals, requiring immobilization (plasters or rods).

Risk Reduction Strategies:

  • Nutrition: Abundant intake of **Calcium** and **Vitamin D** (for bone density), and **Vitamin C** and **Proteins** (for collagen synthesis in ligaments and cartilage).
  • Lifestyle: Low-impact strength training to build supporting muscles, mobility exercises to maintain joint elasticity, and proper hydration to support cartilage lubrication.

Biomechanical Analysis:

  • Achilles Tendon: Connecting the calf muscles (gastrocnemius and soleus) to the heel bone (calcaneus), it is the thickest and strongest tendon in the human body.
  • Toe Action:
    • Plantarflexion (pointing toes down): Calf muscles contract, pulling the Achilles tendon upward, raising the heel.
    • Dorsiflexion (pointing toes up): Calf muscles stretch, and the tendon tightens under tension.
  • Other Prominent Tendons to Locate:
    • Patellar Tendon: Just below the kneecap (patella). Handles force when straightening the leg.
    • Biceps Tendon: At the front crease of the elbow. Flexes the forearm.
Reflection Reflect on physical practices (e.g., yoga, kabaddi) supporting musculoskeletal health, gardening practices (e.g., pruning, grafting) supporting plant tissues, and adaptations observed during a nature walk.
Show Guided Solution

1. Physical Practices (Yoga & Kabaddi):

  • Yoga: Static stretching stimulates cartilage nutrition via synovial fluid circulation, increases ligament flexibility, and strengthens bone cells (osteoblasts) through weight-bearing poses.
  • Kabaddi: High-impact agile movements improve bone mineralization and build quick-twitch muscle fibers, enhancing skeletal strength and cardiovascular health.

2. Gardening Practices & Plant Tissues:

  • Pruning: Cuts off apical buds to remove apical dominance, stimulating lateral buds (intercalary and lateral meristems) to branch out, resulting in a bushier plant.
  • Grafting: Unites the vascular cambium (lateral meristem) of the scion and stock, allowing xylem and phloem paths to fuse for seamless water and food transport.

3. Nature Walk & Leaf Adaptations:

  • Desert leaves (Xerophytes): Reduced to spines to curb water loss, thick cuticle, stomata sunken deep in cavities.
  • Aquatic leaves (Hydrophytes): Contain large air cavities (**aerenchyma**) in petiole and leaves for buoyancy and gas storage.
  • Repelling water: The **Lotus effect**, where superhydrophobic micro-structured epidermal cells covered in wax papillae prevent water droplets from wetting the surface.
Activity 2 Study tribal dance joint movements and explore the possibility of obtaining a complete animal from a single cell.
Show Guided Solution

Dance Joint Movements:

  • Many traditional tribal dances involve rhythmic movements using hinge joints (knees, elbows), ball-and-socket joints (hips, shoulders), and gliding joints (wrists, ankles) coordinating in unison with skeletal muscles to express complex postures.

Deep Frontier: Animal Regeneration from a Single Cell (Cloning vs. Plant Totipotency)

  • Unlike plant cells which are widely **totipotent** (a single parenchyma cell can regenerate a whole plant under tissue culture), adult animal cells are highly differentiated and have locked down their genomic pluripotency.
  • Is it possible? Yes, through **Somatic Cell Nuclear Transfer (SCNT)**—the process used to clone Dolly the sheep. The nucleus of a somatic animal cell is placed into an enucleated egg cell, which is then stimulated to develop into an embryo.
  • Advantages: Preservation of endangered species, rapid production of transgenic livestock with ideal qualities, and therapeutic cloning for personalized organ transplants.
  • Challenges: High rate of developmental defects, premature cellular aging (telomere shortening), and complex ethical dilemmas surrounding reproductive cloning.
