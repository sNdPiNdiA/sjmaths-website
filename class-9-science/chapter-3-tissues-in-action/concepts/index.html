Levels of Organization

What is a Tissue?

Life on Earth begins with a single cell that divides repeatedly to give rise to a complex organism. During this development, cells organize themselves into specialized teams to construct the skin, muscles, bones, nerves, and organs. This coordination is one of nature’s greatest engineering marvels.

Cells → Tissues → Organs → Organ Systems → Organism

In unicellular organisms (e.g., Amoeba), a single cell performs all physiological processes like respiration, intake of food, and excretion. In multicellular organisms, cells differentiate to perform specialized functions. A tissue is defined as a group of cells (similar in structure and origin) that work together to perform a specific function.

Division of Labour

Grouping cells into tissues permits the division of labour, which dramatically increases the body's functional efficiency. For example, muscle tissues handle locomotion, nerve tissues transmit coordinate messages, and plant vascular tissues conduct water and nutrients.

Evolutionary Divergence

Plant vs. Animal Tissues

Because plants and animals lead completely different lifestyles, their cellular and tissue structures have evolved divergent pathways:

Feature Plant Tissues Animal Tissues
Mobility Fixed/Stationary. Require structural rigidity. Mobile. Require cellular flexibility for locomotion.
Cell Wall Present (composed of cellulose/lignin). Absent (allows cell membrane flexibility).
Nutrition Mode Autotrophic (tissues capture solar energy via photosynthesis). Heterotrophic (tissues geared for food ingestion and digestion).
Growth Pattern Indeterminate. Continues throughout life in localized meristems. Determinate. Uniform and stops after reaching maturity.
Plant Growth Zones

Meristematic Tissues

Growth in plants is localized to specific regions containing actively dividing cells. This active tissue is called meristematic tissue. Based on their location, they are classified into three types:

Apical Meristem

Located at the growing tips of shoots and roots. Responsible for growth in length (primary growth).

Lateral Meristem

Located in lateral rings parallel to stems and roots. Responsible for growth in girth/thickness (secondary growth).

Intercalary Meristem

Located at nodes or bases of internodes. Helps grass regenerate after mowing and hedges sprout branch clusters.

Active Meristem Cell Properties:

  • Small, tightly packed cells with no intercellular space.
  • Thin cellulose walls facilitating division.
  • Dense cytoplasm with a prominent, large nucleus.
  • Vacuoles are absent: Dividing cells do not need storage space, nor do they require large vacuoles which could physically obstruct mitotic division.
Onion Root Tip Experiment (Activity 3.1)

Growing two onion bulbs in jars (Jar A and Jar B) reveals that cutting the tip off the roots in Jar B halts root growth completely. This proves that mitotic cell division is restricted strictly to the apical root tip zones.

Structural Differentiation

Plant Permanent Tissues

When meristematic cells divide, the daughter cells eventually lose their ability to divide. They undergo changes in structure, shape, and function through a process called differentiation to become permanent tissues.

1. Simple Permanent Tissues (Supporting Tissues)

Composed of only one type of cell working together:

Tissue Cell State Cell Wall & Composition Modifications & Functions
Parenchyma Living Thin cellulose walls, loosely packed. Storage of nutrients. Modified into Chlorenchyma (for photosynthesis) or Aerenchyma (provides buoyancy in aquatic plants).
Collenchyma Living Unevenly thickened at corners due to pectin deposition. Provides support and elasticity, permitting petioles and stems to bend flexibly without breaking.
Sclerenchyma Dead Uniformly thickened with lignin, leaving no internal cell space. Extreme rigidity and mechanical strength. Found in leaf veins, seed coats, and coconut husk fibres.

2. Complex Permanent Tissues (Vascular System)

Composed of multiple types of cells coordinating together to conduct materials up and down the plant:

  • Xylem: Transports water and minerals upwards from roots. It is made of four elements: Tracheids (dead, tubular), Vessels (dead, hollow tubes), Xylem Parenchyma (living, stores food), and Xylem Fibres (dead, mechanical support).
  • Phloem: Transports synthesized food bidirectionally from leaves to roots/storage zones. It is made of: Sieve tubes (living cells with perforated sieve plates, lacking a nucleus at maturity), Companion cells (regulate sieve tube transport), Phloem Parenchyma (stores food, latex, tannins), and Phloem Fibres (sclerenchymatous, support).

3. Dermal Tissue System (Protective Outer Coverings)

  • Epidermis: Outermost layer consisting of flat, tightly packed cells covered with a waxy cuticle (composed of cutin) to limit transpiration water loss and resist parasites.
  • Stomata: Epidermal pores in leaves flanked by guard cells for gaseous exchange and transpiration.
  • Root Hairs: Hair-like projections from root epidermal cells that maximize surface absorption area.
  • Bark Formation: In older trees, a lateral cambium layer (cork cambium) divides to produce dead, compact cork cells. Suberin deposition makes them impermeable to water and gases, forming tree bark.
Animal Physiology

Animal Tissues: Covering & Binding

Animal tissues are classified into four main types: Epithelial, Connective, Muscular, and Nervous. Here we study the first two types:

1. Epithelial Tissues

Closely packed sheets of cells lining body surfaces and internal cavities, providing protection, absorption, and secretion:

Epithelial Class Cell Structure Key Function Location in Body
Simple Squamous Single layer of thin, flat cells. Rapid diffusion and exchange of materials. Blood vessels lining, lung alveoli.
Stratified Squamous Multiple layers of packed flat cells. Protects against wear, tear, and entry of microbes. Skin outer surface, mouth cavity, esophagus.
Glandular (Secretory) Cuboidal or columnar cells folded inwards. Synthesizes and secretes hormones, enzymes, sweat, saliva. Sweat glands, salivary glands, stomach lining.
Ciliated / Sensory Receptor cells bearing hair-like cilia. Sensing stimuli (smell, taste, sound, balance). Nostrils, taste buds, inner ear.
Columnar (Absorption) Tall, pillar-like cells, often with microvilli. Efficient uptake of water and nutrients. Lining of the small intestine.

2. Connective Tissues

Binds, connects, supports, and cushions organs. Consists of cells embedded in an intercellular matrix:

  • Blood: A fluid connective tissue. Consists of a liquid matrix (plasma) containing:
    • RBCs: Contain red, iron-rich hemoglobin protein to transport oxygen; replaced every 4 months.
    • WBCs: Defend against pathogens; accumulate at infections (forming pus and inflammation).
    • Platelets: Essential for blood clotting at injury sites.
  • Bone: Hard, rigid matrix containing calcium and phosphorus compounds. Forms the protective skeletal framework.
  • Cartilage: Soft, flexible matrix that cushions joint ends (nose, outer ear, trachea).
  • Tendons: Tough, fibrous tissue connecting muscle to bone to transmit tensile force.
  • Ligaments: Strong, elastic tissue connecting bone to bone to stabilize joints.
Locomotion & Control

Movement & Coordination

1. Muscular Tissues

Contractile tissues responsible for voluntary and involuntary motions:

Skeletal Muscle

Voluntary (conscious control). Cells are long, cylindrical, unbranched, multinucleate, and show dark/light bands (striated).

Smooth Muscle

Involuntary. Cells are spindle-shaped, uninucleate, and lack bands (unstriated). Found in stomach and intestine lining.

Cardiac Muscle

Involuntary. Cylindrical, branched, uninucleate cells with faint striations and intercalated discs. Work tirelessly without fatigue.

2. Nervous Tissue

Forms the body's control network. Composed of neurons (nerve cells) specialized to transmit impulses. Structurally divided into:

  • Cell Body: Contains the nucleus and coordinates cellular metabolism.
  • Dendrites: Branch-like terminals that receive incoming chemical/electrical signals.
  • Axon: A long single fiber that propagates the impulse forward.
  • Axon Terminals: Transmit messages to downstream muscle or neural cells at synapses.

3. The Musculoskeletal System & Joint Action

Coordinates bones, muscles, joints, cartilage, tendons, and ligaments to bring about motion. The adult skeleton accounts for about 12-15% of total body weight. Movement is dictated by the type of joints:

  • Ball and Socket Joint: Rounded head fits into a hollow socket, permitting free rotational movement in multiple planes (e.g., shoulder, hip).
  • Hinge Joint: Allows uniaxial movement in a single plane (flexion and extension), similar to a door hinge (e.g., elbow, knee with protecting patella/kneecap).
  • Pivot Joint: Allows rotational turning (e.g., neck joint linking the skull to the spine's vertebral column).
  • Fixed Joints: Articulations locked immovably to protect vital organs (e.g., sutures between skull bones).

Frontiers of Biology

Bridging Science, Society & Frontiers

1. Cellular Totipotency (F. C. Steward, 1958)

Steward demonstrated that mature plant cells (specifically phloem cells of carrot) retain the ability to regenerate into a complete new plant. When grown in nutrient mediums containing growth hormones, these cells dedifferentiate into an undifferentiated mass, divide, and redifferentiate into roots and shoots. This capacity is known as totipotency.

2. Agrobacterium: Nature's Genetic Engineer

In nature, Agrobacterium tumefaciens causes crown gall disease—inducing tumor-like growths on plant stems by transferring its own genetic segment into host cells. Biologists harnessed this biological trick, converting the bacterium into a delivery tool to transfer useful, disease-resistant genes into crops and synthesize pharmaceutical compounds.

3. Stem Cells & Bone Marrow Transplants

Bone marrow contains specialized stem cells capable of dividing and differentiating into red blood cells, white blood cells, and platelets. Bone marrow transplants are used clinically to treat blood cancers (Leukemia) and genetic hemoglobin diseases (Thalassemia).

4. Meet the Scientists

  • B. G. L. Swamy: Renowned Indian botanist and morphologist. His famous Kannada book Hasuru Honnu won the Kendra Sahitya Akademi Award in 1978, beautifully blending Western Ghats botanical exploration, culture, and science.
  • Sipra Guha Mukherjee & S. C. Maheshwari: Made a breakthrough discovery in plant tissue culture by developing complete haploid plants through anther culture, contributing significantly to modern agriculture.

5. Yoga and Musculoskeletal Fitness

Maintaining correct posture, regular stretching, and yoga asanas (like the 12 poses of Surya Namaskar) stimulates synovial fluid circulation, keeps bones strong, builds muscle tone, and prevents joint stiffness.
