Earth as a System: Energy, Matter, and Life

Concept 1

Earth as an Interconnected System

Earth is a system in which energy and matter move continuously between interacting parts. The Sun is the main external source of energy, while Earth’s hot interior and chemical reactions also drive processes.

SphereWhat it includesExamples
GeosphereSolid Earth, rocks, soil, landforms and interiorDeccan Plateau, Thar Desert
HydrosphereLiquid waterOceans, rivers, lakes, groundwater
CryosphereFrozen waterHimalayan glaciers, snow, polar ice caps
AtmosphereGases surrounding EarthAir and weather systems
BiosphereLiving organisms and habitatsForests, farms, plankton, coral reefs

Systems rule: a disturbance in one sphere can propagate into several others.

Deep understanding: what makes it a system?

A system is understood through components, interactions and changes. In this chapter the five spheres are the components, while the movement of energy and matter creates the interactions. A change in one component can therefore alter conditions in another component.

For example, snow stored in the cryosphere can later become liquid water in the hydrosphere. That water can support plants in the biosphere, while the plants interact with the atmosphere through transpiration and with the geosphere through their roots and soil.

Concept-check

If a question gives a change in only one sphere, do not stop there. Identify the first direct effect and then at least one secondary effect in another sphere.

Concept 2

Sphere-to-Sphere Interactions

The spheres are not isolated. Reduced snowfall in the cryosphere can reduce summer lake water in the hydrosphere, which can reduce grass available to animals in the biosphere. Warmer Arabian Sea water increases evaporation and can alter the southwest monsoon, while warming can accelerate glacier melt and threaten low-lying coastal regions.

Exam chain

Change → immediate sphere → linked sphere → ecological/social consequence. Trace at least two links in system-interaction questions.

Deep cause-and-effect analysis

The chapter's examples show that Earth-system changes often form a chain rather than a single event. The scientifically useful question is not simply “what happens?” but “through which process does it happen?”

Initial disturbanceProcessLater consequence
Less snowfallLess meltwater entering lakesLess water and grass in summer
Warmer Arabian SeaMore evaporation and altered atmospheric moistureMonsoon/rainfall variability
Atmospheric warmingFaster glacier/ice meltingSea-level rise and habitat loss

Exam skill

Use linking words such as therefore, because, consequently, which in turn. They make the causal chain explicit.

Concept 3

Solar Radiation and Electromagnetic Spectrum

Solar radiation is the main energy source for Earth. It reaches Earth as electromagnetic waves, which can travel through vacuum at about 3 × 108 m s−1. The electromagnetic spectrum ranges from high-frequency, short-wavelength gamma rays and X-rays to lower-frequency, longer-wavelength radio waves.

Solar radiation reaching Earth is concentrated mainly in the UV, visible and infrared regions. Visible light powers photosynthesis; infrared contributes strongly to surface warming; much harmful UV is absorbed by the ozone layer.

Important distinction

Sound waves require a material medium, whereas electromagnetic waves can travel through vacuum.

Deep understanding: wavelength, frequency and energy

The electromagnetic spectrum is ordered by wavelength and frequency. The chapter describes gamma rays and X-rays as high-frequency/short-wavelength radiation and infrared and radio waves as lower-frequency/longer-wavelength radiation. The important Earth-system idea is that different parts of this spectrum interact differently with the atmosphere and surface.

UV is important because ozone absorbs much of the harmful short-wavelength UV. Visible radiation reaches the surface and supports photosynthesis. The surface then emits infrared radiation, which is central to the greenhouse process.

Three-region memory map

UV → ozone protection; Visible → photosynthesis; IR → heat/radiation balance.

Concept 4

Insolation and Solar Constant

Insolation is the amount of the Sun’s radiation received by Earth’s surface. The solar constant is approximately 1.4 kW m−2 at the top of the atmosphere for a surface perpendicular to the Sun’s rays. Atmospheric gases, clouds and dust absorb and scatter some energy, so surface insolation is lower; under clear conditions the chapter gives about 1 kW m−2.

Numerical pattern

E = intensity × area × time. For 1 kW m−2 on 1 m2 for one hour: E = 1000 × 1 × 3600 = 3.6 × 106 J.

Deep understanding: top of atmosphere versus surface

A common conceptual mistake is treating the solar constant as the energy actually received at the ground. It is defined at the top of the atmosphere for a surface perpendicular to the Sun's rays. The atmosphere then absorbs, scatters and reflects part of the incoming radiation.

Numerical reasoning

Always convert kilowatts into joules per second when the time is given in seconds: 1 kW = 1000 J s−1. Then multiply by area and time. Keep the units visible at every step.

Check your answer

Energy must have units of joule (J). If your final unit is W m−2, you have found an intensity, not an amount of energy.

Concept 5

Albedo and Surface Heating

Albedo is the fraction of incoming solar radiation reflected by a surface. High-albedo surfaces reflect more and generally remain cooler; low-albedo surfaces absorb more and warm more.

SurfaceChapter value / tendency
Snow0.80–0.90 — very high reflection
Ice0.50–0.70 — high reflection
Crushed rock0.25–0.30
Black soil / ocean waterLower albedo; greater absorption

Urban heat island: concrete, asphalt, steel and other built surfaces absorb and retain heat, while vegetation cools surroundings through shade and transpiration.

Deep understanding: absorption controls heating

When radiation reaches a surface, some is reflected and the remainder can be absorbed. Therefore colour and surface properties influence how quickly a surface warms. Dark roads absorb more solar radiation than light-coloured surfaces, which is why they can become hotter.

Albedo is especially important at the planetary scale because a large reflecting surface sends more incoming solar energy back rather than converting it into surface heating. Snow and ice therefore have a strong cooling influence in the chapter's explanation.

Reasoning pattern

Albedo ↑ → reflection ↑ → absorption ↓ → heating tendency ↓.
Albedo ↓ → reflection ↓ → absorption ↑ → heating tendency ↑.

Concept 6

Latitude, Earth’s Shape and Uneven Heating

Because Earth is spherical, sunlight strikes different latitudes at different angles. Near the equator, radiation is concentrated over a smaller area; toward the poles it is spread over a larger area. Therefore equatorial regions receive more concentrated heating and polar regions receive less.

Earth’s shape and axial tilt also contribute to seasonal variation and changing day length. Uneven heating produces temperature differences that help drive global winds and ocean currents.

Deep understanding: why geometry changes heating

The same incoming solar energy is not distributed over the same area at every latitude. Because Earth is spherical, rays are more concentrated near the equatorial region and spread over a larger area toward the poles.

This is why the chapter connects geometry directly to climate: different energy received per unit area → different temperatures → pressure differences → atmospheric circulation. Earth’s tilt adds seasonal changes by changing solar conditions and day length during its revolution.

Olympiad-style thinking

When comparing two places, first compare the angle and concentration of incoming radiation, then infer the likely heating difference. Do not simply say “one place is closer to the Sun.”

Concept 7

Atmosphere: Composition and Layers

The atmosphere is held by gravity and consists mainly of about 78% nitrogen and 21% oxygen, with smaller quantities of argon, carbon dioxide, water vapour and other gases.

LayerApprox. altitudeKey feature
Troposphere0–12 kmMost weather; temperature decreases with height
Stratosphere12–50 kmOzone layer; temperature increases with height
MesosphereAbove stratosphereUpper atmospheric layer
ThermosphereHigherUpper atmosphere
ExosphereOutermostTransitions toward outer space

Weather → troposphere. This is a frequent exam trap.

Deep understanding: atmosphere as an energy regulator

The atmosphere is not just “air we breathe.” In this chapter it is an active part of Earth's energy system. It modifies incoming radiation, contains greenhouse gases that interact with outgoing infrared radiation, and provides the medium in which most weather processes occur.

The vertical temperature structure also matters. In the troposphere, the surface heats the air and temperature generally decreases with height. In the stratosphere, ozone absorption causes temperature to increase with height.

Layer-selection rule

If the question mentions rain, storms, clouds or ordinary weather → troposphere. If it mentions ozone and UV absorption → stratosphere.

Concept 8

Greenhouse Effect and Ozone Layer

The Earth’s surface absorbs sunlight and re-radiates energy as infrared radiation. Greenhouse gases such as CO2, CH4 and water vapour absorb some outgoing heat, helping keep Earth warm enough for life. Excess greenhouse gases intensify this effect and contribute to global warming.

The ozone layer in the stratosphere absorbs harmful UV radiation. CFCs contributed to severe ozone loss over Antarctica. The Montreal Protocol reduced CFC use and the chapter notes that the ozone layer is slowly recovering.

Do not confuse

Stratospheric ozone protects from UV. Ground-level ozone is a harmful air pollutant involved in smog.

Deep understanding: two different radiation problems

ProcessRadiation involvedMain atmospheric componentRole
UV protectionUltravioletOzoneAbsorbs harmful UV.
Greenhouse effectOutgoing infraredCO2, CH4, water vapourAbsorbs part of outgoing heat.

The natural greenhouse effect is necessary because it helps keep Earth warm enough for life. The concern is excess greenhouse gases, which enhance heat retention and contribute to warming.

High-frequency trap

Do not write that CO2 directly absorbs all incoming sunlight. The chapter's mechanism is that the surface absorbs solar radiation, re-radiates infrared energy, and greenhouse gases absorb part of that outgoing heat.

Concept 9

Why Uneven Heating Produces Wind

Wind is air moving from a region of high pressure toward low pressure. Uneven solar heating creates temperature differences, which create pressure differences.

Valley breeze: during the day, sun-facing slopes heat faster, air rises and cooler valley air moves upslope. Mountain breeze: after sunset, slopes cool faster; dense cool air flows downslope.

At planetary scale, intense equatorial heating creates an equatorial low-pressure belt; sinking air near 30° forms subtropical highs, rising air near 60° forms subpolar lows, and cold sinking air near the poles forms polar highs.

Deep understanding: pressure creates the wind direction

Heating alone does not describe the complete mechanism. Heating changes air temperature and density; this produces pressure differences, and air moves from higher pressure toward lower pressure.

In a valley during the day, warmed slope air rises and creates relatively lower pressure near the slopes. Cooler valley air therefore moves upslope. At night, cooled dense air over the slopes moves downward.

One-line mechanism

Heating difference → density/pressure difference → air movement. The time of day tells you which surface is warmer.

Concept 10

Planetary Winds and Earth’s Rotation

Planetary pressure belts establish large-scale wind circulation. Earth’s rotation deflects moving air from a straight path: winds are deflected toward the right in the Northern Hemisphere and toward the left in the Southern Hemisphere.

Reasoning chain

Uneven heating → temperature difference → pressure difference → air movement → rotation deflects the path.

Deep understanding: from local circulation to global circulation

The same physical principle used for valley and mountain breezes operates on a much larger scale. Unequal heating between equator and poles creates broad pressure belts. Rising and sinking air establish circulation cells, while Earth's rotation curves the moving air.

RegionDominant vertical motion describedPressure character
EquatorWarm air risesLow pressure
~30° N/SCooled air sinksSubtropical high pressure
~60° N/SAir risesSub-polar low pressure
PolesCold dense air sinksPolar high pressure

Direction trap

Earth's rotation produces rightward deflection in the Northern Hemisphere and leftward deflection in the Southern Hemisphere.

Concept 11

Ocean Currents and Gyres

Ocean currents are continuous movements of large masses of seawater. Surface currents are strongly influenced by planetary winds, while temperature, salinity, Earth’s rotation and land distribution also affect circulation.

Warm equatorial water tends to move toward the poles near the surface; colder, denser water can move back toward the equator at depth. Higher-salinity water is denser and tends to sink. Earth’s rotation helps form large circular patterns called gyres: clockwise in the Northern Hemisphere and counter-clockwise in the Southern Hemisphere.

Ocean currents redistribute heat and nutrients, moderating climate and supporting ecosystems.

Deep understanding: why ocean water moves

Surface and deep ocean movements are connected. Winds can drag surface water, while differences in temperature and salinity alter density. Dense water tends to sink, allowing deeper return flows.

Continents also matter because water cannot continue through solid land; land masses redirect current paths. Earth’s rotation then contributes to large circular gyres.

Integrated chain

Uneven heating → winds + temperature differences → ocean movement; salinity → density differences → sinking; rotation + continents → curved/redirected paths.

The climate effect is equally important: currents move heat away from warm regions and toward colder regions, reducing temperature contrasts and supporting marine ecosystems through nutrient transport.

Concept 12

Biogeochemical Cycles

A biogeochemical cycle is the cyclic movement of matter between biotic and abiotic components. Water, carbon, nitrogen and oxygen continuously move among the atmosphere, hydrosphere, geosphere and biosphere.

Why cycles matter

They keep essential nutrients available, sustain life, regulate climate and help ecosystems maintain balance.

Core idea

Matter is recycled; energy flows through Earth systems rather than being recycled in the same way.

Deep understanding: cycling of matter

Biogeochemical cycles connect the living and non-living parts of Earth. A nutrient may be present in air, soil, water, rocks or organisms at different stages, but the element itself is transferred rather than permanently consumed.

This is why disruption of a cycle can affect organisms even when the element still exists somewhere in the Earth system. It may be in a form or reservoir that organisms cannot readily use.

Key distinction

Energy enters and moves through the Earth system; matter is repeatedly transferred and recycled.

Concept 13

Water Cycle and Climate Change

The water cycle includes evaporation, transpiration, condensation, precipitation, runoff, infiltration and groundwater movement. Water moves between surface reservoirs, atmosphere and subsurface systems.

A warmer atmosphere can hold more moisture, contributing to heavier rainfall in some regions and drought in others. Glacier melt can alter river flow and contribute to long-term sea-level rise. Intense rainfall can increase runoff and soil erosion while reducing infiltration and groundwater recharge.

Sphere linkage

Cryosphere ↔ hydrosphere ↔ atmosphere ↔ geosphere ↔ biosphere.

Deep understanding: reservoirs and transfers

The water cycle should be understood as movement between reservoirs, not merely as “rain.” Oceans, lakes, rivers, groundwater, glaciers, the atmosphere and living organisms can all store or transfer water.

Evaporation and transpiration add water vapour; condensation forms clouds; precipitation returns water to the surface; runoff moves it over land; infiltration transfers it into soil and rocks.

Climate reasoning

If intense rainfall increases runoff but decreases infiltration, the result can be more soil erosion + less groundwater recharge. This explains why heavy rain does not automatically mean better water availability.

Concept 14

Carbon Cycle

Carbon is present in proteins, carbohydrates, fats and DNA. It cycles among the atmosphere as CO2, biosphere, geosphere and hydrosphere.

Fast cycle: plants use atmospheric CO2 in photosynthesis; respiration and decomposition return carbon to the atmosphere. Slow cycle: buried organic matter can become fossil fuels over millions of years; combustion releases carbon rapidly as CO2.

Oceans absorb atmospheric CO2, forming dissolved carbonate and bicarbonate ions; phytoplankton use these in photosynthesis and some marine organisms use them to form shells.

Deep understanding: carbon moves at very different speeds

The carbon cycle contains both rapid biological transfers and very slow geological storage. This time-scale difference is essential. Plants can move atmospheric carbon into organic matter within a short period, while fossil-fuel formation represents storage over millions of years.

Human combustion reverses this time scale: carbon stored over geological periods can be transferred to the atmosphere rapidly as CO2.

High-value comparison

Photosynthesis stores atmospheric CO2 in organic matter; respiration, decomposition and combustion return carbon toward the atmosphere.

Concept 15

Nitrogen Cycle

Nitrogen is essential for proteins and nucleic acids. Although atmospheric N2 is abundant, it is relatively unreactive and cannot be directly used by plants and animals.

  1. Nitrogen fixation: bacteria such as Rhizobium and Azotobacter convert atmospheric nitrogen into usable compounds.
  2. Assimilation: plants absorb nitrogen compounds; animals obtain nitrogen through food.
  3. Ammonification: decomposers return nitrogen compounds such as ammonia to soil.
  4. Nitrification: bacteria convert ammonia → nitrite → nitrate.
  5. Denitrification: bacteria such as Pseudomonas convert some nitrates back to atmospheric nitrogen.

The chapter also describes the Haber–Bosch process, which industrially fixes nitrogen for fertiliser production.

Deep understanding: why nitrogen fixation is necessary

The atmosphere contains abundant N2, but abundance does not mean direct biological availability. Because N2 is relatively non-reactive, it must first be converted into compounds that organisms can use.

The cycle is therefore a sequence of chemical and biological transformations rather than a simple movement of nitrogen gas.

ProcessWhat to remember
FixationAtmospheric N2 → usable nitrogen compounds.
AssimilationPlants take up nitrogen; animals obtain it through food.
AmmonificationDecomposition returns nitrogen as ammonia/related compounds.
NitrificationAmmonia → nitrite → nitrate.
DenitrificationNitrate → atmospheric nitrogen.
Concept 16

Oxygen Cycle

Oxygen forms about 21% of Earth’s atmosphere. Respiration and combustion consume oxygen and release CO2; photosynthesis uses sunlight, water and CO2 to form glucose and release O2.

Balance

Photosynthesis restores O2; respiration + combustion consume O2.

Deep understanding: oxygen is linked to carbon

The oxygen cycle is not an isolated cycle. Photosynthesis simultaneously uses CO2 and releases O2, while respiration does the reverse overall by consuming O2 and releasing CO2. Combustion also consumes oxygen and releases CO2.

Therefore questions about deforestation can legitimately ask about both carbon and oxygen: fewer plants means less photosynthetic restoration of O2 and less removal of atmospheric CO2.

Memory pair

Photosynthesis: O2 out, CO2 in.
Respiration/combustion: O2 in, CO2 out.

Concept 17

Human Disruption of Earth’s Processes

Burning fossil fuels and deforestation increase atmospheric CO2. Excess CO2 also increases ocean absorption and can make seawater more acidic, threatening plankton and coral reefs.

Excess fertiliser adds nitrogen to rivers and lakes, causing eutrophication: algal blooms grow rapidly, oxygen is depleted and fish can die. Deforestation reduces photosynthesis and transpiration, can alter albedo and rainfall, increases soil erosion and destroys habitats.

Deep understanding: one action can disturb multiple cycles

Human impacts become easier to understand when classified by the Earth-system process they interrupt.

Human actionImmediate system changeWider consequence
Fossil-fuel burningMore atmospheric CO2Enhanced greenhouse warming and climate impacts.
DeforestationLess photosynthesis/transpirationCarbon, water, soil, climate and habitat changes.
Fertiliser overuseExcess nitrogen enters waterEutrophication and oxygen depletion.
Vehicle emissionsPollutants react with sunlightSmog and harmful ground-level ozone.

Exam strategy

Whenever a question asks for “other consequences,” move across the spheres instead of repeating the same type of effect.

Concept 18

Air Pollution, Smog and Ozone

Vehicular emissions can react with sunlight to form ground-level smog and ground-level ozone, which is harmful. This must be distinguished from stratospheric ozone, which absorbs UV and protects life.

The chapter connects local actions with international cooperation: the Montreal Protocol is presented as a successful example of ozone protection, while the Kyoto Protocol and Paris Agreement are noted as less successful in reducing CO2 emissions.

Deep understanding: location changes the meaning of ozone

The word “ozone” alone is not enough to decide whether an effect is protective or harmful. The chapter makes a location-based distinction.

OzoneWhere?Role
Protective ozoneStratosphereAbsorbs harmful UV radiation.
Ground-level ozoneNear Earth’s surfaceForms through sunlight-driven reactions involving pollutants and is harmful to health.

Vehicular emissions therefore connect atmospheric chemistry with human health. This is another example of a local process becoming part of the broader Earth-system discussion.

Concept 19

Solutions and Sustainable Choices

Restoring Earth-system balance requires conserving energy and resources, using renewable energy such as solar and wind, planting trees, saving water, sustainable farming, and reducing, reusing and recycling materials.

Systems thinking: a good solution should reduce pressure on more than one sphere or cycle where possible—for example, renewable energy can reduce fossil-fuel emissions and help slow disruption of the carbon cycle.

Deep understanding: sustainability as systems management

The solutions listed in the chapter are connected to the same processes that human activity disrupts. Renewable energy can reduce dependence on fossil fuels; tree planting supports carbon uptake and ecosystems; water conservation reduces pressure on the hydrosphere; sustainable farming can reduce nutrient pollution.

Individual actions such as reducing waste, reusing and recycling are presented as part of a larger collective response. The chapter also highlights Mission LiFE as an India-led global initiative encouraging environmentally mindful lifestyles.

Best conceptual answer

A sustainable solution should be explained by stating which pressure it reduces and which Earth-system process benefits.

Concept 20

High-Level Exam Reasoning and Connections

For long-answer questions, connect processes rather than listing facts.

Climate chain

Solar radiation → uneven heating → pressure differences → winds → ocean currents → heat redistribution → climate.

Human-impact chain

Fossil fuels/deforestation → CO2 rise → greenhouse warming → ice/water/ecosystem changes.

Nutrient chain

Biogeochemical cycles → nutrient availability → organisms → decomposition → return to environment.

Water chain

Warming → more atmospheric moisture → changed rainfall/runoff → erosion/recharge changes → agriculture and ecosystems affected.

Answer-writing rule: state the cause, explain the mechanism, identify the affected sphere or cycle, then state the consequence.

Deep reasoning toolkit

Chapter 13 questions often look different on the surface but can be solved using a small number of process chains.

Four-step long-answer method

1. Define the key term. 2. Explain the mechanism in sequence. 3. Connect it to another sphere/cycle. 4. Conclude with the consequence or example from the chapter.
