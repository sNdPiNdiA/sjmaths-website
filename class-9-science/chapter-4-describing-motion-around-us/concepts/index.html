Concepts: Describing Motion Around Us - Interactive 3D Model

Frame of Reference

Describing Position, Rest & Motion

To analyze the motion of an object, we must first describe its position at different instants of time. This requires specifying a fixed point called the reference point or origin (O).

The distance and direction of the object with respect to this origin describe its position. If the position of an object changes with time, it is said to be in motion; if the position does not change, it is at rest.

Directional Sign Conventions

For straight-line (1D) motion, positions to the right of origin $O$ are generally taken as positive ($+$), and to the left of $O$ as negative ($-$). Direction is specify-marked by plus/minus signs.

Instant vs. Interval of Time

An instant of time is a single clock reading (e.g. $t = 4\text{ s}$), whereas a time interval is the duration between two instants (e.g. $\Delta t = t_2 - t_1 = 16\text{ s} - 4\text{ s} = 12\text{ s}$).

Kinematic Quantities

Distance vs. Displacement

When an object moves, it traces a path between starting and stopping positions. Two different quantities define this change:

  • Distance Travelled: The total length of the path actually covered by the moving object. It is a scalar quantity because it requires only numerical value (magnitude), not direction.
  • Displacement: The net change in the position of the object (shortest straight-line path from initial to final position). It is a vector quantity because it requires specifying both magnitude and direction.

The SI unit for both distance and displacement is the metre (m).

Crucial Relations

The magnitude of displacement is always less than or equal to the total distance travelled: $$\text{Magnitude of Displacement} \le \text{Total Distance Travelled}$$ They are equal only if the object moves in a straight line without reversing direction.

Rates of Change

Average Speed & Average Velocity

To specify how fast or slow an object moves, we define rates of change with respect to time:

1. Average Speed

The total distance travelled divided by the time interval taken to cover that distance:

$$\text{Average Speed} = \frac{\text{Total Distance Travelled}}{\text{Time Interval}}$$

Speed is a scalar quantity. If an object covers equal distances in equal intervals of time, it is in uniform motion (constant speed). If it covers unequal distances, it is in non-uniform motion.

India's Historical Contributions

Calculations involving speed and distance were established in ancient India. The treatise Aryabhatiya (5th century CE) laid down early foundations, and the comprehensive mathematical text Ganitakaumudi (14th century CE) solved problems such as determining when two postmen walking towards each other at different speeds ($9$ and $5$ yojanas per day) would meet.

2. Average Velocity

The rate of change of position, calculated as displacement divided by the time interval:

$$\text{Average Velocity } (v_{av}) = \frac{\text{Change in Position (Displacement)}}{\text{Time Interval}} = \frac{s}{t}$$

Velocity is a vector quantity, having the same direction as the displacement vector (indicated by $+$ or $-$ signs). The SI unit of speed and velocity is metre per second ($m\cdot s^{-1}$ or $m/s$).

Sarang's Swimming Pool (Example 4.2)

Sarang takes $50\text{ seconds}$ to swim a $25\text{ m}$ pool back and forth ($50\text{ m}$ total distance, returning to start).
• $\text{Average Speed} = \frac{50\text{ m}}{50\text{ s}} = 1\text{ m/s}$.
• $\text{Average Velocity} = \frac{0\text{ m (displacement)}}{50\text{ s}} = 0\text{ m/s}$.

Velocity Changes

Average Acceleration

When you feel a jolt as a car suddenly starts moving from rest or brakes to a stop, you are experiencing a change in velocity. The average acceleration ($a$) is the change in velocity divided by the time interval:

$$\text{Average Acceleration } (a) = \frac{\text{Change in Velocity}}{\text{Time Interval}} = \frac{v - u}{t_2 - t_1}$$

Where $u$ is initial velocity at time $t_1$, and $v$ is final velocity at time $t_2$. The SI unit of acceleration is metre per second squared ($m\cdot s^{-2}$ or $m/s^2$).

Speeding Up

If velocity magnitude increases, acceleration acts in the same direction as the velocity vector.

Slowing Down

If velocity magnitude decreases, acceleration acts in the opposite direction to the velocity vector (negative acceleration/deceleration).

For an object dropped from a height, gravity causes a constant downward acceleration of $9.8\text{ m/s}^2$ (denoted as $g$), meaning velocity increases by exactly $9.8\text{ m/s}$ in every successive second.

Visual Kinematics

Graphical Representation of Motion

Line graphs provide a powerful visual tool to describe how position and velocity change with time. Plotting these values requires choosing suitable scales for horizontal (X-axis: Time) and vertical (Y-axis: Position or Velocity) axes.

1. Position-Time (s-t) Graphs

• Straight Line: Velocity is constant. The slope of the line represents the velocity: $$\text{Slope} = \frac{\Delta s}{\Delta t} = \frac{s_2 - s_1}{t_2 - t_1} = v$$ • Curve: Velocity is changing (accelerated motion).
• Horizontal Line: Object is stationary (velocity = $0$).

2. Velocity-Time (v-t) Graphs

• Slope of the line: Represents the acceleration of the object: $$\text{Slope} = \frac{\Delta v}{\Delta t} = \frac{v_2 - v_1}{t_2 - t_1} = a$$ • Area enclosed under the line: Represents the displacement of the object in that time interval.

Mathematical Equations

Kinematic Equations of Motion

For an object moving in a straight line with constant acceleration ($a$), we can relate displacement ($s$), time interval ($t$), initial velocity ($u$), final velocity ($v$), and acceleration ($a$) using three kinematic equations:

1. $$v = u + at$$ 2. $$s = ut + \frac{1}{2}at^2$$ 3. $$v^2 = u^2 + 2as$$

Graphical Derivations:

  • First Equation ($v = u + at$): Derived from the definition of acceleration as the slope of the v-t graph: $$a = \text{Slope} = \frac{v - u}{t} \implies v = u + at$$
  • Second Equation ($s = ut + \frac{1}{2}at^2$): Derived by integrating the area under the v-t line, split into a rectangle of area $ut$ and a triangle of area $\frac{1}{2}t(v-u) = \frac{1}{2}at^2$: $$s = \text{Area}_{\text{rectangle}} + \text{Area}_{\text{triangle}} = ut + \frac{1}{2}t(at) = ut + \frac{1}{2}at^2$$
  • Third Equation ($v^2 = u^2 + 2as$): Derived by substituting $t = \frac{v - u}{a}$ into the second equation and expanding the algebra.
Braking Distances & Safe Margins

When brakes are applied ($a = -4\text{ m/s}^2$), the stopping distance increases quadratically with speed:
• Initial speed = $54\text{ km/h } (15\text{ m/s}) \implies \text{Stopping Distance} = 28.1\text{ m}$.
• Initial speed = $108\text{ km/h } (30\text{ m/s}) \implies \text{Stopping Distance} = 112.5\text{ m}$ (4x increase!).
This highlights the extreme danger of tailgating and the need for V2V (vehicle-to-vehicle) warning systems.

2D Dynamics

Motion in a Plane & Circular Motion

Motion in two dimensions occurs when an object travels along a curved path in a plane (e.g. a kicked ball's trajectory, vehicle overtaking, or satellites in orbit).

Ready to Go Beyond: 3D Motion in Space

Motion in space, such as a car climbing up a winding mountain road, a bird flying in the sky, or an aircraft navigating through the clouds, is called motion in three dimensions.

Uniform Circular Motion

When an object moves in a circular path with constant (uniform) speed, it is in uniform circular motion.
• Distance covered in one complete revolution is equal to the circumference: $2\pi R$.
• Displacement is zero.
• Average Speed is constant: $v_{av} = \frac{2\pi R}{T}$.
• Average Velocity is zero.

Tangential Velocity Vector & Centripetal Acceleration

Even though speed remains constant, the direction of the velocity vector is changing continuously at every point. This velocity at any point is directed along the tangent to the circle. Because direction is constantly changing, circular motion is always accelerated.

What is a Tangent? A straight line that meets the circle at one and only one point is called a tangent.

Activity 4.5: Confinement and Tangential Release

When a marble is thrown inside a circular tape ring, it is forced to move in a circle. However, if you lift the ring, the boundary is removed. The marble immediately flies off in a straight line tangent to the circle. This is because once released, the marble continues moving in the direction it was headed at the exact instant the constraint was removed.
