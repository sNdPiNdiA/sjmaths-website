Q1. What is a scientific model? Give examples from physics and biology.

2 Marks
View Ideal Answer & Marking Scheme
Model Answer: A scientific model is a simplified representation of a complex real system that focuses only on what is most important to answer a specific question.

Examples:
  1. Physics: Modeling a moving car as a single point mass to calculate acceleration.
  2. Biology: Drawing a simplified cell diagram highlighting key organelles while ignoring molecular pathways.
Marking Scheme Tips: - 1 Mark for defining modeling as simplification.
- 1 Mark for providing valid physics and biology examples.

Q2. In Example 1.1, when modeling a cricket ball hit for a six, what details are essential and what can be ignored?

2 Marks
View Ideal Answer & Marking Scheme
Model Answer: - Essential Details to Keep: Mass of the ball, launch speed, and direction/angle of the hit. These directly determine trajectory.
- Details to Ignore: Brand of the bat, colour of the ball, grass on the field, and stitching on the seams (which have negligible effects on a simple model). Marking Scheme Tips: - 1 Mark for list of kept parameters.
- 1 Mark for list of ignored parameters.

Q3. Why did physicist Meghnad Saha ignore chemical processes while modeling starlight?

3 Marks
View Ideal Answer & Marking Scheme
Model Answer: Meghnad Saha wanted to explain why the colour of stars is connected to their temperature. To make the model simple enough to calculate:
1. He treated the matter in the star simply as a hot gas.
2. He ignored complex internal reactions and movement of stars.
3. He focused only on temperature, pressure, and atom ionization. This simplification allowed him to formulate the Saha Ionization Equation successfully. Marking Scheme Tips: - 1 Mark for stating his goal (linking colour and temperature).
- 1.5 Marks for listing what was ignored/simplified.
- 0.5 Mark for highlighting the outcome (Ionization Equation).

Q4. What is the danger of mixing unit systems? Use the passenger aircraft incident as an example.

3 Marks
View Ideal Answer & Marking Scheme
Model Answer: Mixing units leads to conversion errors that can cause system failures. In the passenger aircraft incident:
1. The aircraft needed 22,300 kg of fuel.
2. The ground crew used the density of fuel in pounds (lb) per litre instead of kilograms (kg) per litre.
3. Because pounds are lighter than kilograms, the aircraft was under-fueled by 15,000 litres, leading to fuel exhaustion mid-flight. Standardizing units avoids calculations and catastrophic human errors. Marking Scheme Tips: - 1 Mark for explaining the general danger of conversion mix-ups.
- 2 Marks for outlining the aircraft fuel (kg vs lb) scenario.

Q5. Differentiate between a Law, a Theory, and a Principle in science. Provide examples.

3 Marks
View Ideal Answer & Marking Scheme
Model Answer:
  1. Law: Describes a regular observed pattern in nature (e.g. Newton's laws of motion describing a bus stopping jerk).
  2. Theory: Provides a deep explanation of why those patterns occur, based on tested evidence (e.g. Atomic theory explaining molecules).
  3. Principle: Broad core ideas applied to solve problems in a given situation (e.g. Principle of Conservation of Energy).
Marking Scheme Tips: - 1 Mark for each correct term definition along with its corresponding example.

Q6. Meghna wants to check Varsha's prediction that "it will rain this afternoon because clouds look dark". Suggest three scientific questions Meghna can ask.

3 Marks
View Ideal Answer & Marking Scheme
Model Answer: Good scientific questions look for measurable evidence rather than mere opinions. Meghna could ask:
1. "What is today's relative humidity? Was it above 80% when it rained last time?"
2. "Is the air temperature dropping rapidly like it did before recent rain events?"
3. "What is the wind speed and direction today? Will it blow the clouds away?" Marking Scheme Tips: - 1 Mark for each query referencing measurable meteorological parameters (humidity, temperature change, wind).

Q7. Disprove the claim that "eating food during an eclipse is harmful" using scientific reasoning.

3 Marks
View Ideal Answer & Marking Scheme
Model Answer: The claim has no basis because:
1. Physical Nature: An eclipse is simply a play of shadows where one celestial body blocks light from another.
2. Environmental Changes: There is no significant radiation or chemical change during an eclipse that differs from standard twilight shadow.
3. Experimental Comparison: Leaving food in a standard shadow does not make it toxic. Therefore, no physical, chemical, or biological mechanism supports this claim. Marking Scheme Tips: - 1 Mark for clarifying that an eclipse is just a physical shadow.
- 2 Marks for demonstrating that no scientific mechanism (phys/chem/bio) supports food spoilage due to shadows.

Q8. Perform a Fermi estimation to calculate the approximate volume of air a human breathes in one day.

5 Marks
View Ideal Answer & Marking Scheme
Model Answer: To make a rough order-of-magnitude estimate:
1. Breaths per minute: At rest, an average human takes about 12 to 15 breaths per minute.
2. Minutes per day: 60 minutes × 24 hours = 1,440 minutes.
3. Total breaths per day: 15 breaths/min × 1,440 min/day ≈ 20,000 breaths.
4. Volume of one breath: Since it takes about 4 breaths to blow up a 2-litre balloon, the volume of one breath ≈ 0.5 Litres.
5. Calculation: 20,000 breaths × 0.5 Litres/breath = 10,000 Litres.

Conclusion: A human breathes in approximately 10,000 litres of air per day. Marking Scheme Tips: - 1 Mark for breathing rate assumption (12-15 breaths/min).
- 1 Mark for calculating daily minutes (1440).
- 1 Mark for estimating one breath volume (0.5 L) using balloon reference.
- 2 Marks for final multiplication and reasonable unit notation.

Q9. Explain how the design and functioning of a protective face mask connect physics, chemistry, biology, and mathematics.

5 Marks
View Ideal Answer & Marking Scheme
Model Answer: Solving complex real-world problems requires integrating multiple disciplines:
  • Physics: governs particle motion, drag forces, and the electrostatic attraction used by fibers to capture virus particles.
  • Chemistry: provides the material science for polymer fibers, such as non-woven polypropylene sheets.
  • Biology: analyzes virus size, transmission pathways, and inhalation capacities.
  • Mathematics: builds modeling equations for airflow pathways, filtration efficiency, and probability of virus capture.
Marking Scheme Tips: - 1 Mark for each discipline's contribution described correctly.
- 1 Mark for summarizing the conclusion (nature lacks boundaries).
