Probability

Addition theorem of probability

The addition theorem of probability quantifies the probability of the union of two or more events, bridging set-theoretic union with measure-theoretic additivity.

Definition and statement

General Addition Theorem of Probability
For any two events $A$ and $B$ defined on a sample space $\Omega$, the probability of their union is given by $$P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \cap B)$$. For three events $A, B, C$, $$P(A \cup B \cup C) = P(A) + P(B) + P(C) - P(A \cap B) - P(B \cap C) - P(C \cap A) + P(A \cap B \cap C)$$.

📐 Geometric Interpretation

Visualized via Venn diagrams, the sum of areas of sets $A$ and $B$ counts their intersection $A \cap B$ twice. Subtracting $P(A \cap B)$ exactly corrects this double-counting, yielding the true area of the union.

⚙️ Algebraic & Structural Meaning

Rooted in the measure-theoretic axioms of Kolmogorov, specifically countable additivity and the inclusion-exclusion principle over Boolean algebras of events.

Conditions and hypotheses

Check each condition and what changes when it is omitted:

Hypothesis / Condition Domain / Interval Necessity What Fails If Condition Dropped
Event space validity Subsets of sample space $\Omega$ Strictly Mandatory If $A$ and $B$ are not measurable subsets of $\Omega$, their probabilities are undefined.
Kolmogorov Axioms $0 \le P(E) \le 1$ Strictly Mandatory Probabilities outside $[0, 1]$ or violating $P(\Omega) = 1$ invalidate all derived union bounds.
Mutually Exclusive Special Case $A \cap B = \emptyset$ Optional (reduces formula) If assumed mutually exclusive when they are not, failure to subtract $P(A \cap B)$ leads to overcounting.

Useful results and shortcuts

Use these identities and results when their conditions apply:

01

Mutually Exclusive Events

$$P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B)$$

Rule: When $A \cap B = \emptyset$, the intersection term vanishes entirely.

Application: undefined
02

Probability of Neither Event ($A' \cap B'$)

$$P(A' \cap B') = 1 - P(A \cup B) = 1 - [P(A) + P(B) - P(A \cap B)]$$

Rule: Use De Morgan's Law combined with the addition theorem to find none-occurrences instantly.

03

Exactly One Event Occurring

$$P(A \Delta B) = P(A) + P(B) - 2P(A \cap B)$$

Rule: Symmetric difference formula accounts for elements in $A$ or $B$ but not both.

Application: Use this result in the stated type of problem.
04

Bonferroni's First Inequality (Upper/Lower Bounds)

$$P(A \cap B) \ge P(A) + P(B) - 1$$

Rule: Since $P(A \cup B) \le 1$, rearranging the addition theorem gives a strict lower bound for the intersection.

Application: Advanced TGT/PGT questions testing range of intersection given marginal probabilities.
05

Three-Event Union with Symmetry

$$P(A \cup B \cup C) = 3P(A) - 3P(A \cap B) + P(A \cap B \cap C)$$

Rule: When all individual probabilities and pairwise intersections are equal due to symmetry.

Application: Shortcut for symmetrical die-rolling or card-drawing problems.
06

Probability of at least two events occurring

$$P(A \cap B) + P(B \cap C) + P(C \cap A) - 2P(A \cap B \cap C)$$

Rule: Rapid calculation trick for multi-event overlap questions.

Application: High-level DSSSB and KVS PGT questions.

Common errors and counterexamples

These examples show how a conclusion can fail when a condition is missing:

⚠️ COMMON ERROR #01

Assuming Independence for General Addition

Common Pitfall: Candidates substitute $P(A \cap B) = P(A)P(B)$ into the addition theorem even when events are dependent.

Counterexample: $A$ and $B$ are dependent events where $P(A \cap B) \neq P(A)P(B)$

Why it fails: Using product formula for intersection on dependent events yields completely wrong union probabilities.

🛡️ Exam Defense: Check explicitly whether independence is stated in the problem before using $P(A)P(B)$.
⚠️ COMMON ERROR #02

Probability Exceeding Unity

Common Pitfall: Calculating $P(A) + P(B)$ and getting a value greater than 1 without realizing $P(A \cap B)$ must absorb the excess.

Counterexample: $P(A) = 0.7, P(B) = 0.6$

Why it fails: Sum is $1.3$. Without subtracting $P(A \cap B)$, the probability axiom $P \le 1$ is violated.

🛡️ Exam Defense: Always verify that $P(A) + P(B) - 1 \le P(A \cap B) \le \min(P(A), P(B))$.

Solved examples

Each problem includes a full solution and a shorter method:

CANONICAL PROBLEM 01 Dual-Method Breakdown

If $A$ and $B$ are two events such that $P(A) = 3/8$, $P(B) = 1/2$, and $P(A \cap B) = 1/4$, find $P(A \cup B)$.

(A) 5/8(B) 3/4(C) 1/2(D) 7/8
✓ Correct Answer: (A)
📚 Method A: Formal Textbook Derivation
Step 1: Write formula $P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \cap B)$. Step 2: Substitute values: $3/8 + 1/2 - 1/4 = 3/8 + 4/8 - 2/8 = 5/8$.
⚡ Method B: 25-Second Exam Shortcut
Direct mental arithmetic: $3/8 + 4/8 - 2/8 = 5/8$.
💡 Tip: Convert all fractions to a common denominator instantly.
CANONICAL PROBLEM 02 Dual-Method Breakdown

For two events $A$ and $B$, $P(A) = 0.4$, $P(B) = p$, and $P(A \cup B) = 0.7$. If $A$ and $B$ are mutually exclusive, find the value of $p$.

(A) 0.1(B) 0.2(C) 0.3(D) 0.4
✓ Correct Answer: (C)
📚 Method A: Formal Textbook Derivation
Step 1: Mutually exclusive means $P(A \cap B) = 0$. Step 2: $P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B)$. Step 3: $0.7 = 0.4 + p \implies p = 0.3$.
⚡ Method B: 25-Second Exam Shortcut
Since intersection is zero, simply subtract $P(A)$ from union: $0.7 - 0.4 = 0.3$.
💡 Tip: Spot the keyword 'mutually exclusive' to eliminate the intersection term.

Practice questions

Choose an answer to check it and see the solution:

Q1
MCQ 1: If $P(A) = 0.5$, $P(B) = 0.3$, and $P(A \cap B) = 0.1$, then $P(A' \cup B')$ is equal to:
A 0.9
B 0.8
C 0.7
D 0.6
Derivation & Analysis:

$P(A' \cup B') = 1 - P(A \cap B) = 1 - 0.1 = 0.9$ by De Morgan's Law.

Q2
MCQ 2: If $A$ and $B$ are mutually exclusive events, then $P(A \cap B)$ is:
A $P(A)P(B)$
B $1$
C $0$
D $P(A) + P(B)$
Derivation & Analysis:

By definition, mutually exclusive events cannot occur simultaneously, so their intersection is the null set, having probability $0$.

Q3
MCQ 3: For any two events $A$ and $B$, the probability that exactly one of them occurs is:
A $P(A) + P(B) - P(A \cap B)$
B $P(A) + P(B) - 2P(A \cap B)$
C $P(A) + P(B) + 2P(A \cap B)$
D $P(A \cap B') + P(A' \cap B)$
Derivation & Analysis:

Exactly one occurring is $(A \setminus B) \cup (B \setminus A)$, whose probability is $P(A) + P(B) - 2P(A \cap B)$.

Q4
MCQ 4: If $P(A) = 0.6$, $P(B) = 0.4$, and $P(A \cup B) = 0.8$, what is $P(A \cap B)$?
A 0.2
B 0.4
C 0.5
D 0.1
Derivation & Analysis:

$P(A \cap B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \cup B) = 0.6 + 0.4 - 0.8 = 0.2$.

Q5
MCQ 5: What is the maximum possible value of $P(A \cap B)$ given $P(A) = 0.3$ and $P(B) = 0.7$?
A 1.0
B 0.7
C 0.3
D 0.21
Derivation & Analysis:

$P(A \cap B) \le \min(P(A), P(B)) = \min(0.3, 0.7) = 0.3$.

Q6
MCQ 6: If $A \subset B$, then the value of $P(A \cup B)$ is:
A $P(A)$
B $P(B)$
C $P(A) + P(B)$
D $0$
Derivation & Analysis:

If $A \subset B$, their union is $B$, so $P(A \cup B) = P(B)$.

Q7
MCQ 7: If $A$ and $B$ are independent events with $P(A) = 0.4$ and $P(B) = 0.5$, then $P(A \cup B)$ is:
A 0.9
B 0.2
C 0.7
D 0.5
Derivation & Analysis:

For independent events, $P(A \cap B) = 0.4 \times 0.5 = 0.2$. Then $P(A \cup B) = 0.4 + 0.5 - 0.2 = 0.7$.

Q8
MCQ 8: Assertion (A): For any two events, $P(A \cup B) \ge \max(P(A), P(B))$. Reason (R): Union contains both individual sets as subsets.
A Both (A) and (R) are true and (R) is the correct explanation
B Both (A) and (R) are true but (R) is not the correct explanation
C (A) is true but (R) is false
D (A) is false but (R) is true
Derivation & Analysis:

Since $A \subset (A \cup B)$ and $B \subset (A \cup B)$, monotonicity of probability ensures $P(A \cup B) \ge P(A)$ and $P(A \cup B) \ge P(B)$.

Q9
MCQ 9: If $P(A) = 1/3$, $P(B) = 1/4$, and $P(A \cup B) = 5/12$, are $A$ and $B$ independent?
A Yes
B No
C Cannot be determined
D Mutually exclusive
Derivation & Analysis:

$P(A \cap B) = 1/3 + 1/4 - 5/12 = 7/12 - 5/12 = 2/12 = 1/6$. Since $P(A)P(B) = (1/3)(1/4) = 1/12 \neq 1/6$, wait... let us recalculate: $1/3 \times 1/4 = 1/12$. Here $P(A \cap B) = 2/12 = 1/6$. Thus they are dependent. Correct option is No (B).

Q10
MCQ 10: In a class of 100 students, 40 failed in Math, 30 failed in Physics, and 10 failed in both. A student is selected at random. What is the probability that the student failed in Math or Physics?
A 0.5
B 0.6
C 0.7
D 0.4
Derivation & Analysis:

Let $M$ be failing Math ($P(M) = 40/100$) and $P$ be failing Physics ($P(P) = 30/100$), $P(M \cap P) = 10/100$. $P(M \cup P) = 0.4 + 0.3 - 0.1 = 0.6$.
