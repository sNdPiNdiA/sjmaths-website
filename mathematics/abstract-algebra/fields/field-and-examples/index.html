6. Abstract Algebra

Field with examples

A field is a commutative division ring in which non-zero elements form an abelian group under multiplication, providing the algebraic foundation for vector spaces and linear equations. Mastery of fields, subfields, and finite fields is a high-yield scoring zone in UP PGT/TGT, KVS, and DSSSB examinations due to direct property-based questions.

Pillar 1 Rigorous Definition & Mathematical Statement

Algebraic Definition of a Field
A non-empty set $F$ equipped with two binary operations, addition ($+$) and multiplication ($\cdot$), denoted as $(F, +, \cdot)$, is called a field if: (1) $(F, +)$ is an abelian group with identity $0$, (2) $(F \setminus \{0\}, \cdot)$ is an abelian group with identity $1$ ($1 \neq 0$), and (3) multiplication distributes over addition: $a \cdot (b + c) = (a \cdot b) + (a \cdot c)$ for all $a, b, c \in F$.

📐 Geometric Interpretation

Geometrically, fields like $\mathbb{R}$ or $\mathbb{C}$ provide continuous coordinate systems where lines, curves, and vector transformations can be scaled, rotated, and inverted uniformly without restriction.

⚙️ Algebraic & Structural Meaning

Fields guarantee the existence of additive inverses, multiplicative inverses for all non-zero elements, and the absence of zero divisors, ensuring that linear equations $ax = b$ ($a \neq 0$) have unique solutions.

Pillar 2 Hypotheses & Condition Checklist

Every hypothesis is strictly non-negotiable. Inspect the domain type and the exact consequence if a condition fails:

Hypothesis / Condition Domain / Interval Necessity What Fails If Condition Dropped
Non-triviality ($1 \neq 0$) Entire Set $F$ Strictly Mandatory If $1 = 0$, the field collapses to the zero ring $\{0\}$, which lacks a multiplicative inverse for its sole element.
Commutativity of Multiplication Entire Set $F \setminus \{0\}$ Strictly Mandatory for Fields (vs Division Rings) If multiplication is not commutative (e.g., quaternions $\mathbb{H}$), the structure is a skew field or division ring, not a field.
Existence of Multiplicative Inverse For all $a \in F, a \neq 0$ Essential If any non-zero element lacks an inverse (e.g., integers $\mathbb{Z}$ under multiplication), the structure degrades to an integral domain or commutative ring.

Pillar 3 High-Yield Exam Results & Direct Shortcuts

Standard 15-to-30 second shortcuts frequently tested in UP TGT/PGT and DSSSB:

01

Shortcut 1: Finite Field Characteristic Rule

$$ \text{Char}(F) = p \text{ (where } p \text{ is a prime) or } 0 $$

Exam Rule: The characteristic of any finite field is always a prime number. Any finite field has $p^n$ elements for some prime $p$ and integer $n \ge 1$.

⚡ PYQ Context: Frequently asked in UP PGT and KVS to eliminate impossible order options for finite fields.
02

Shortcut 2: Subfield Order Test

$$ K \text{ is a subfield of } GF(p^n) \iff m \text{ divides } n $$

Exam Rule: A finite field $GF(p^n)$ contains a subfield of order $p^m$ if and only if $m$ divides $n$.

⚡ PYQ Context: Directly tested in DSSSB PGT and UP PGT examinations for subset/subfield lattice questions.
03

Shortcut 3: Integral Domain to Field Test

$$ \text{Every finite integral domain is a field.} $$

Exam Rule: Never waste time checking multiplicative inverses for finite integral domains; finiteness automatically upgrades an integral domain to a field.

⚡ PYQ Context: Standard theoretical MCQ in UP TGT/PGT.
04

Shortcut 4: Characteristic of $\mathbb{Z}_n$

$$ \text{Char}(\mathbb{Z}_n) = n $$

Exam Rule: $\mathbb{Z}_n$ is a field if and only if $n$ is prime. If $n$ is composite, $\mathbb{Z}_n$ has zero divisors and is only a commutative ring.

⚡ PYQ Context: Directly applied in modular arithmetic ring questions.
05

Shortcut 5: Dimension of Extension Fields

$$ [E:F] = \dim_F(E) $$

Exam Rule: The degree of a field extension over a finite field $GF(p)$ determines the cardinality $|E| = p^{[E:F]}.

⚡ PYQ Context: Crucial for counting elements in extension fields.
06

Shortcut 6: Rapid Elimination of Non-Fields

$$ \mathbb{Z}, \mathbb{Z}[x], M_n(F) \, (n \ge 2) \text{ are NOT fields.} $$

Exam Rule: Instantly eliminate rings with zero divisors ($M_n(F)$, $\mathbb{Z}_6$) or lacking multiplicative inverses ($\mathbb{Z}$) when asked to identify fields.

⚡ PYQ Context: Saves precious time in 125-question exams.

Pillar 4 Classic Exam Traps & Counterexamples

Where examiners construct tricky trap options and how to safeguard your marks:

⚠️ EXAM TRAP #01

Trap 1: Assuming $\mathbb{Z}_n$ is always a field

Common Pitfall: Candidates confuse $\mathbb{Z}_p$ (prime $p$) with $\mathbb{Z}_n$ (composite $n$) and assume all residue classes modulo $n$ form a field.

Counterexample: $\mathbb{Z}_4 = \{0, 1, 2, 3\}$ where $2 \cdot 2 = 0$, creating a zero divisor.

Why it fails: Composite modulus $n$ introduces zero divisors, violating the division property required for fields.

🛡️ Exam Defense: Check if the modulus is prime. Only $\mathbb{Z}_p$ with prime $p$ is a field.
⚠️ EXAM TRAP #02

Trap 2: Matrix Rings and Commutativity

Common Pitfall: Assuming $M_n(\mathbb{R})$ ($n \ge 2$) is a field because it has matrix addition and multiplication.

Counterexample: Matrix multiplication is non-commutative and non-zero matrices can have zero determinants (zero divisors).

Why it fails: Fails both commutativity of multiplication and existence of inverses for non-zero singular matrices.

🛡️ Exam Defense: Matrix rings $M_n(F)$ for $n \ge 2$ are never fields or even integral domains.

Pillar 6 Solved Canonical Exam Problems (Dual-Method)

Comparing the formal academic proof against the rapid competitive exam elimination trick:

CANONICAL PROBLEM 01 Dual-Method Breakdown

Which of the following algebraic structures is a field?

(A) $(\mathbb{Z}, +, \cdot)$(B) $(\mathbb{Q}, +, \cdot)$(C) $(2\mathbb{Z}, +, \cdot)$(D) $(\mathbb{Z}_6, +, \cdot)$
✓ Correct Answer: (B)
📚 Method A: Formal Textbook Derivation
Verify axioms: $\mathbb{Q}$ has additive identity $0$, additive inverses, multiplicative identity $1$, multiplicative inverses for all non-zero fractions, and distributive laws hold.
⚡ Method B: 25-Second Exam Shortcut
$\mathbb{Z}$ lacks multiplicative inverses, $2\mathbb{Z}$ lacks unity ($1$), $\mathbb{Z}_6$ has zero divisors ($2 \times 3 = 0$). Only $\mathbb{Q}$ satisfies all field axioms.
💡 Key Takeaway: Standard foundational question in UP TGT.
CANONICAL PROBLEM 02 Dual-Method Breakdown

What is the characteristic of the field of real numbers $\mathbb{R}$?

(A) 0(B) 2(C) p (prime)(D) Undefined
✓ Correct Answer: (A)
📚 Method A: Formal Textbook Derivation
The characteristic of a field $F$ is the smallest positive integer $n$ such that $n \cdot 1 = 0$. If no such integer exists, $\text{Char}(F) = 0$. In $\mathbb{R}$, no integer $n$ makes $n \cdot 1 = 0$ except $0$.
⚡ Method B: 25-Second Exam Shortcut
All infinite fields of characteristic zero include $\mathbb{Q}, \mathbb{R}, \mathbb{C}$.
💡 Key Takeaway: Remember: Infinite standard number systems $(\mathbb{Q}, \mathbb{R}, \mathbb{C})$ have characteristic 0.

Pillar 7 10 Practice MCQs for Self-Assessment

Q1
MCQ 1: The characteristic of a finite field of order $27$ is:
A (A) 3
B (B) 9
C (C) 27
D (D) 0
Derivation & Analysis:

Any finite field has order $p^n$ where $p$ is a prime and its characteristic is $p$. Here $27 = 3^3$, so the characteristic is $3$.

Q2
MCQ 2: Which of the following is a subfield of the field of complex numbers $\mathbb{C}$?
A (A) $\mathbb{Z}$
B (B) $\mathbb{N}$
C (C) $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2})$
D (D) $\mathbb{Z}[i]$
Derivation & Analysis:

$\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2}) = \{a + b\sqrt{2} \mid a, b \in \mathbb{Q}\}$ is a field and a subfield of $\mathbb{C}$. $\mathbb{Z}$ and $\mathbb{N}$ are not fields.

Q3
MCQ 3: If $F$ is a field, then its only ideals are:
A (A) Only ${0}$
B (B) Only $F$
C (C) ${0}$ and $F$
D (D) Infinitely many
Derivation & Analysis:

A field has no proper non-trivial ideals. Its only ideals are the zero ideal ${0}$ and the field itself $F$.

Q4
MCQ 4: The number of elements in a finite field can be:
A (A) 10
B (B) 25
C (C) 35
D (D) 100
Derivation & Analysis:

The order of a finite field must be of the form $p^n$. Among the options, $25 = 5^2$ is a prime power, whereas $10, 35, 100$ are not prime powers.

Q5
MCQ 5: Every field is necessarily an:
A (A) Integral domain
B (B) Euclidean domain
C (C) Principal ideal domain
D (D) All of the above
Derivation & Analysis:

Every field has no zero divisors and is commutative, making it an integral domain by definition.

Q6
MCQ 6: The ring $(\mathbb{Z}_p, +, \cdot)$ is a field if and only if $p$ is:
A (A) Even
B (B) Odd
C (C) Prime
D (D) Composite
Derivation & Analysis:

$\mathbb{Z}_p$ is a field if and only if $p$ is a prime number.

Q7
MCQ 7: Which of the following rings is a principal ideal domain but not a field?
A (A) $\mathbb{Q}$
B (B) $\mathbb{Z}$
C (C) $\mathbb{R}[x]$
D (D) Both (B) and (C)
Derivation & Analysis:

Both $\mathbb{Z}$ and $\mathbb{R}[x]$ are PIDs (every ideal is principal), but neither is a field because non-zero elements like $2$ or $x$ lack multiplicative inverses.

Q8
MCQ 8: The multiplicative inverse of $[3]$ in the field $\mathbb{Z}_{11}$ is:
A (A) 2
B (B) 4
C (C) 7
D (D) 8
Derivation & Analysis:

We need $3x \equiv 1 \pmod{11}$. Testing $x=4$, $3 \times 4 = 12 \equiv 1 \pmod{11}$. Thus, the inverse is $4$.

Q9
MCQ 9: The intersection of any family of subfields of a field $F$ is:
A (A) Always a subfield
B (B) Not necessarily a subfield
C (C) Always a ring but not a field
D (D) The zero ring
Derivation & Analysis:

The intersection of any collection of subfields satisfies all field axioms and is therefore a subfield.

Q10
MCQ 10: Which of the following sets with standard operations forms a skew field (division ring) but not a field?
A (A) $\mathbb{R}$
B (B) $\mathbb{C}$
C (C) Quaternions $\mathbb{H}$
D (D) $\mathbb{Q}$
Derivation & Analysis:

Quaternions $\mathbb{H}$ form a division ring where multiplication is non-commutative, hence it is not a field.
