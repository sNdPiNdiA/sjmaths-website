6. Abstract Algebra

Fundamental theorem of homomorphism

The Fundamental Theorem of Homomorphism for groups bridges group structures and quotient groups by establishing a canonical isomorphism between the image of a homomorphism and the domain modulo the kernel. Mastery of this theorem and its ring-theoretic counterpart forms a heavily tested scoring zone in UP PGT/TGT, KVS, and DSSSB examinations due to its direct application in quotient structure analysis.

Pillar 1 Rigorous Definition & Mathematical Statement

Fundamental Theorem of Group Homomorphism (First Isomorphism Theorem)
Let $\phi: G \to H$ be a group homomorphism. Then the kernel $\ker(\phi)$ is a normal subgroup of $G$, the image $\text{Im}(\phi)$ is a subgroup of $H$, and the quotient group $G/\ker(\phi)$ is isomorphic to $\text{Im}(\phi)$. Explicitly, the induced mapping $\bar{\phi}: G/\ker(\phi) \to \text{Im}(\phi)$ defined by $\bar{\phi}(x\ker(\phi)) = \phi(x)$ is a well-defined group isomorphism.

📐 Geometric Interpretation

Geometrically, a homomorphism collapses certain sub-structures (the kernel) to the identity element. The quotient group $G/\ker(\phi)$ treats each coset as a single point, effectively flattening the domain by factoring out all null-directions, leaving an exact replica (isomorphic copy) of the image space $\text{Im}(\phi)$.

⚙️ Algebraic & Structural Meaning

It provides a universal algebraic mechanism to decompose complex group structures into simpler quotient parts and homomorphic images, serving as the foundational engine for classification theorems in abstract algebra.

Pillar 3 High-Yield Exam Results & Direct Shortcuts

Standard 15-to-30 second shortcuts frequently tested in UP TGT/PGT and DSSSB:

01

Shortcut 1: Order Calculation via Homomorphism

$$|G| = |\ker(\phi)| \cdot |\text{Im}(\phi)|$$

Exam Rule: If a homomorphism $\phi: G \to H$ is given, the cardinality of the domain group equals the product of the order of the kernel and the order of the image.

⚡ PYQ Context: Frequently asked in UP PGT to quickly determine kernel size or image size without explicit coset listing.
02

Shortcut 2: Cyclic Group Image Rule

$$G \text{ is cyclic} \implies \text{Im}(\phi) \text{ is cyclic}$$

Exam Rule: Homomorphic images preserve cyclicity. If $G = \langle a \rangle$, then $\text{Im}(\phi) = \langle \phi(a) \rangle$.

⚡ PYQ Context: Directly tested in DSSSB and KVS when identifying possible homomorphic images of $(\mathbb{Z}, +)$.
03

Shortcut 3: Abelian Quotient Deduction

$$G \text{ is abelian} \implies G/N \text{ is abelian}$$

Exam Rule: Every quotient group of an abelian group is abelian. Since $\text{Im}(\phi) \cong G/\ker(\phi)$, the image is also abelian.

⚡ PYQ Context: Used for rapid elimination in multiple-choice questions concerning non-abelian target groups.
04

Shortcut 4: Index of Kernel for Finite Groups

$$[G : \ker(\phi)] = |\text{Im}(\phi)|$$

Exam Rule: The index of the kernel in $G$ equals the exact order of the image subgroup in $H$.

⚡ PYQ Context: Standard shortcut for Lagrange-based order constraints in UP TGT/PGT.
05

Shortcut 5: Modular Arithmetic Homomorphisms (\mathbb{Z}_n)

$$\phi: \mathbb{Z}_n \to \mathbb{Z}_m \implies \text{order of } \phi(1) \text{ divides } \gcd(n, m)$$

Exam Rule: The number of homomorphisms from $\mathbb{Z}_n$ to $\mathbb{Z}_m$ is equal to $\gcd(n, m)$.

⚡ PYQ Context: Direct formula question frequently appearing in PGT examinations.
06

Shortcut 6: Rapid Elimination via Cardinality

$$|\text{Im}(\phi)| \text{ must divide } |H| \text{ and } |G|_d$$

Exam Rule: Eliminate options where the claimed image order does not divide the order of the codomain group $H$.

⚡ PYQ Context: Saves 45 seconds on matching isomorphism questions in competitive exams.

Pillar 4 Classic Exam Traps & Counterexamples

Where examiners construct tricky trap options and how to safeguard your marks:

⚠️ EXAM TRAP #01

Trap 1: Assuming Every Subgroup is a Kernel

Common Pitfall: Candidates assume that for any subgroup $H \leq G$, there exists a homomorphism $\phi$ such that $\ker(\phi) = H$.

Counterexample: Subgroup $2\mathbb{Z}$ in $(\mathbb{Z}, +)$ is normal, but not every arbitrary subgroup in a non-abelian group is a kernel unless it is normal.

Why it fails: Only *normal* subgroups can act as kernels of group homomorphisms.

🛡️ Exam Defense: Never link non-normal subgroups to kernels of group homomorphisms.
⚠️ EXAM TRAP #02

Trap 2: Confusing Isomorphism with Equality

Common Pitfall: Conclude that $G/\ker(\phi) = \text{Im}(\phi)$ in a literal set-theoretic sense.

Counterexample: Cosets $x\ker(\phi)$ are sets, whereas elements of $\text{Im}(\phi)$ are group elements in $H$.

Why it fails: They are structurally isomorphic ($\cong$), not identically equal sets.

🛡️ Exam Defense: Look for the symbol $\cong$ rather than $=$ when dealing with quotient structures and images.

Pillar 6 Solved Canonical Exam Problems (Dual-Method)

Comparing the formal academic proof against the rapid competitive exam elimination trick:

CANONICAL PROBLEM 01 Dual-Method Breakdown

Let $\phi: (\mathbb{Z}, +) \to (\mathbb{Z}_n, +_n)$ be defined by $\phi(x) = x \pmod n$. What is the kernel of $\phi$?

(A) $\mathbb{Z}$(B) $n\mathbb{Z}$(C) \{0\}(D) $\mathbb{Z}_n$
✓ Correct Answer: (B)
📚 Method A: Formal Textbook Derivation
Step 1: Set $\phi(x) = 0$ in $\mathbb{Z}_n$. Step 2: This implies $x \equiv 0 \pmod n$. Step 3: Therefore, $x$ is a multiple of $n$. Step 4: $\ker(\phi) = \{nx : x \in \mathbb{Z}\} = n\mathbb{Z}$.
⚡ Method B: 25-Second Exam Shortcut
By the First Isomorphism Theorem, $\mathbb{Z}/\ker(\phi) \cong \mathbb{Z}_n$. Since order of $\mathbb{Z}_n$ is $n$, the index of $\ker(\phi)$ in $\mathbb{Z}$ must be $n$, giving $n\mathbb{Z}$.
💡 Key Takeaway: Standard canonical question in UP PGT. Always check the modulus.
CANONICAL PROBLEM 02 Dual-Method Breakdown

Let $\phi: (\mathbb{R}^*, \cdot) \to (\{1, -1}, \cdot)$ be defined by $\phi(x) = \frac{x}{|x|}$. What is the quotient group $\mathbb{R}^*/\ker(\phi)$?

(A) $(\mathbb{R}^+, \cdot)$(B) $(\{1, -1}, \cdot)$(C) $(\mathbb{Z}_2, +)$(D) Both (B) and (C) abstractly
✓ Correct Answer: (D)
📚 Method A: Formal Textbook Derivation
Step 1: Find kernel: $\phi(x) = 1 \implies x > 0$, so $\ker(\phi) = \mathbb{R}^+$. Step 2: Find image: $\text{Im}(\phi) = \{1, -1}$. Step 3: By First Isomorphism Theorem, $\mathbb{R}^+/\mathbb{R}^+ \cong \{1, -1} \cong \mathbb{Z}_2$.
⚡ Method B: 25-Second Exam Shortcut
Recognize that the image has order 2, and any group modulo its kernel is isomorphic to the image.
💡 Key Takeaway: Note that $(\{1, -1}, \cdot)$ is isomorphic to $(\mathbb{Z}_2, +)$.

Pillar 7 10 Practice MCQs for Self-Assessment

Q1
If $\phi: G \to H$ is a group homomorphism, then $\ker(\phi)$ is always:
A A cyclic subgroup of $G$
B A normal subgroup of $G$
C An abelian subgroup of $G$
D Equal to $H$
Derivation & Analysis:

The kernel of any group homomorphism is always a normal subgroup of the domain group $G$ by the fundamental properties of homomorphisms.

Q2
According to the First Isomorphism Theorem, if $\phi: G \to H$ is a homomorphism, then $G/\ker(\phi)$ is isomorphic to:
A $G$
B $H$
C $\text{Im}(\phi)$
D $\ker(\phi)$
Derivation & Analysis:

The fundamental theorem of homomorphism states that $G/\ker(\phi) \cong \text{Im}(\phi)$.

Q3
Let $G$ be a group of order 60 and $\phi: G \to H$ be a homomorphism with $|\text{Im}(\phi)| = 12$. What is the order of $\ker(\phi)$?
A 12
B 60
C 5
D 720
Derivation & Analysis:

Using $|G| = |\ker(\phi)| \cdot |\text{Im}(\phi)|$, we get $60 = |\ker(\phi)| \times 12$, which yields $|\ker(\phi)| = 5$.

Q4
How many homomorphisms exist from $(\mathbb{Z}_4, +)$ to $(\mathbb{Z}_2, +)$?
A 1
B 2
C 4
D 0
Derivation & Analysis:

The number of homomorphisms from $\mathbb{Z}_n$ to $\mathbb{Z}_m$ is $\gcd(n, m)$. Here $\gcd(4, 2) = 2$.

Q5
If $\phi: G \to H$ is an injective homomorphism, then:
A $\ker(\phi) = G$
B $\ker(\phi) = \{e_G\}$
C $\text{Im}(\phi) = \{e_H\}$
D $G \cong H$
Derivation & Analysis:

A homomorphism is injective if and only if its kernel is trivial, i.e., $\ker(\phi) = \{e_G\}$.

Q6
The quotient group $\mathbb{Z}/5\mathbb{Z}$ is isomorphic to:
A $\mathbb{Z}$
B $5\mathbb{Z}$
C $\mathbb{Z}_5$
D The trivial group
Derivation & Analysis:

By definition, the quotient group of integers modulo $n\mathbb{Z}$ is isomorphic to $\mathbb{Z}_n$. Thus $\mathbb{Z}/5\mathbb{Z} \cong \mathbb{Z}_5$.

Q7
Let $\phi: G \to H$ be a surjective group homomorphism. If $G$ is abelian, then $H$ must be:
A Non-abelian
B Abelian
C Cyclic
D Trivial
Derivation & Analysis:

Since $H = \text{Im}(\phi) \cong G/\ker(\phi)$, and quotient groups of abelian groups are abelian, $H$ is abelian.

Q8
Which of the following mapping conditions is mandatory for $\phi: G \to H$ to be a group homomorphism?
A $\phi(x+y) = \phi(x) + \phi(y)$
B $\phi(xy) = \phi(x)\phi(y)$
C $\phi(xy) = \phi(y)\phi(x)$
D $\phi(x^{-1}) = \phi(x)$
Derivation & Analysis:

The defining property of a group homomorphism is preservation of the group operation: $\phi(xy) = \phi(x)\phi(y)$. Note that group operations are often written multiplicatively.

Q9
If $G$ is a cyclic group of order 12, what is the maximum possible order of a homomorphic image of $G$?
A 6
B 12
C 1
D Infinite
Derivation & Analysis:

The image $\text{Im}(\phi)$ is a subgroup of the codomain $H$, but since $G$ is cyclic, $\text{Im}(\phi)$ is also cyclic and must divide $|G| = 12$. The maximum possible order is 12.

Q10
Let $\phi: \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{C}^*$ be defined by $\phi(x) = e^{ix}$. What is the kernel of $\phi$?
A \{0\}
B $2\pi\mathbb{Z}$
C $\mathbb{Z}$
D $\pi\mathbb{Z}$
Derivation & Analysis:

$\phi(x) = 1 \implies e^{ix} = 1 \implies x = 2k\pi$ for $k \in \mathbb{Z}$. Thus $\ker(\phi) = 2\pi\mathbb{Z}$.

Pillar 8 Frequently Asked Questions

For the quotient set $G/H$ to form a valid group under coset multiplication $(aH)(bH) = (ab)H$, the subgroup $H$ must be normal ($gHg^{-1} = H$ for all $g \in G$). Since the kernel of a homomorphism satisfies this condition naturally via $\phi(xgx^{-1}) = \phi(x)\phi(g)\phi(x^{-1}) = \phi(g)$, only normal subgroups can serve as kernels.

Candidates often confuse the order formula $|G| = |\ker(\phi)| \cdot |\text{Im}(\phi)|$ with Lagrange's theorem involving arbitrary subgroups. Remember that the image order is tied directly to the quotient group cardinality, not an arbitrary subgroup of the codomain.

Abstract algebra constitutes roughly 15-20% of the PGT Mathematics paper. Within this, group homomorphisms, normal subgroups, and the fundamental theorem account for 2 to 4 direct or application-based questions every year.

Use the order relation $|G| = |\ker(\phi)| \cdot |\text{Im}(\phi)|$ combined with the fact that $\text{Im}(\phi)$ must divide the order of the codomain. Checking divisibility eliminates wrong options instantly without calculating cosets.
