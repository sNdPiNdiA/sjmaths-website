6. Abstract Algebra

Fermat's theorem

Fermat's Theorem in abstract algebra connects group theory and number theory by stating that if $p$ is a prime number and $a$ is an integer not divisible by $p$, then $a^{p-1} \equiv 1 \pmod p$, which is also generalized via group actions. In the context of calculus and group-theoretic critical point analysis, Fermat's Interior Extremum Theorem states that the derivative at a local extremum in an open interval vanishes. Mastering both number-theoretic and analytical facets forms a high-yield scoring zone for UP TGT/PGT, DSSSB, and KVS examinations.

Pillar 1 Rigorous Definition & Mathematical Statement

Fermat's Little Theorem (Number Theory) & Fermat's Interior Extremum Theorem (Calculus)
(1) \text{ If } p \text{ is prime and } p \nmid a, \text{ then } a^{p-1} \equiv 1 \pmod p. \\ (2) \text{ If } f: (a, b) \to \mathbb{R} \text{ has a local extremum at } c \in (a, b) \text{ and } f'(c) \text{ exists, then } f'(c) = 0.

📐 Geometric Interpretation

In calculus, at any local maximum or minimum inside an open interval, the tangent line to the curve is perfectly horizontal, yielding a slope of zero ($f'(c) = 0$). In number theory, it reflects the cyclic group structure of the multiplicative group of integers modulo $p$, denoted as $\mathbb{Z}_p^*$.

⚙️ Algebraic & Structural Meaning

Fermat's Little Theorem is a direct corollary of Lagrange's Theorem applied to the finite multiplicative group $(\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z})^-$ of order $p-1$. It underpins modern public-key cryptography (RSA) and modular arithmetic simplification in competitive examinations.

Every hypothesis is strictly non-negotiable. Inspect the domain type and the exact consequence if a condition fails:

Hypothesis / Condition Domain / Interval Necessity What Fails If Condition Dropped
Primality of modulus $p$ (For Number Theory) Modular integers $\mathbb{Z}_p$ Strictly Mandatory If $p$ is composite, $a^{\phi(n)} \equiv 1 \pmod n$ applies instead (Euler's Totient Theorem), and $a^{n-1} \equiv 1 \pmod n$ may fail (e.g., base 2, composite 4).
Existence of Differentiability at Local Extremum (For Calculus) Open interval $(a, b)$ Strictly Mandatory If the function has a sharp corner (e.g., $f(x) = |x|$ at $x=0$), a local extremum exists, but the derivative does not exist, so $f'(c) = 0$ is inapplicable.

Pillar 4 Classic Exam Traps & Counterexamples

Where examiners construct tricky trap options and how to safeguard your marks:

⚠️ EXAM TRAP #01

Trap 1: Forgetting the Coprimality Condition

Common Pitfall: Applying $a^{p-1} \equiv 1 \pmod p$ when $\gcd(a, p) \neq 1$.

Counterexample: $a = 3, p = 3 \implies 3^{3-1} = 9 \equiv 0 \pmod 3 \neq 1 \pmod 3$

Why it fails: Fermat's Little Theorem requires $p \nmid a$. If $p$ divides $a$, the remainder modulo $p$ is $0$, not $1$.

🛡️ Exam Defense: Always check if $\gcd(a, p) = 1$ before reducing exponents by $p-1$.
⚠️ EXAM TRAP #02

Trap 2: Confusing Fermat's Little Theorem with Euler's Totient Theorem

Common Pitfall: Using $p-1$ when the modulus is a composite number $n$.

Counterexample: For $n = 6$ (composite), $2^{6-1} = 32 \equiv 2 \pmod 6 \neq 1 \pmod 6$

Why it fails: For composite moduli, Euler's totient theorem states $a^{\phi(n)} \equiv 1 \pmod n$, where $\phi(6) = 2 \neq 5$.

🛡️ Exam Defense: Check if the modulus is prime. If composite, calculate $\phi(n)$.

If $f(x) = x^3$ on $[-1, 1]$, does Fermat's Interior Extremum Theorem guarantee a point $c \in (-1, 1)$ where $f'(c) = 0$?
A Yes, at $c = 0$
B No, because $f(-1) \neq f(1)$
C No, because $f'(0) = 0$ is a point of inflection, not an extremum
D Yes, by Rolle's theorem linkage
Derivation & Analysis:

Fermat's theorem requires a local extremum. For $f(x) = x^3$, $x=0$ is a point of inflection, not a local extremum. (Note: Rolle's theorem requires $f(a)=f(b)$). Correct option is (C).

Q10
If $p$ is a prime number, the number of generators of the multiplicative cyclic group $\mathbb{Z}_p^*$ is given by:
A $p$
B $p-1$
C \phi(p-1)
D \phi(p)
Derivation & Analysis:

The multiplicative group $\mathbb{Z}_p^*$ is cyclic of order $p-1$. The number of generators of a cyclic group of order $m$ is $\phi(m)$, which here is $\phi(p-1)$. Correct option is (C).
