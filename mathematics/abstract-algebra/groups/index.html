6. Abstract Algebra

Group and examples

A group is a non-empty set equipped with a single binary operation that is associative, possesses an identity element, and contains an inverse for every element. Mastery of standard groups like $(\mathbb{Z}, +)$, $(\mathbb{R}^*, \cdot)$, modulo arithmetic groups, and matrix groups is a frequent high-scoring zone in UP TGT/PGT, KVS, and DSSSB examinations.

Pillar 1 Rigorous Definition & Mathematical Statement

Definition of a Group
A non-empty set $G$ together with a binary operation $*$, denoted by $(G, *)$, is called a group if the following axioms hold: (i) Closure: $\forall a, b \in G, a * b \in G$. (ii) Associativity: $\forall a, b, c \in G, (a * b) * c = a * (b * c)$. (iii) Identity Element: $\exists e \in G$ such that $\forall a \in G, a * e = e * a = a$. (iv) Inverse Element: $\forall a \in G, \exists a^{-1} \in G$ such that $a * a^{-1} = a^{-1} * a = e$.

📐 Geometric Interpretation

Geometrically, groups represent fundamental symmetries of geometric objects, where the binary operation corresponds to the composition of rigid transformations (such as rotations and reflections) that leave the object invariant.

⚙️ Algebraic & Structural Meaning

It serves as the foundational algebraic structure in abstract algebra, unifying diverse mathematical systems under a single set of axioms and enabling the systematic study of symmetry, solvability of equations, and structural classification.

Pillar 3 High-Yield Exam Results & Direct Shortcuts

Standard 15-to-30 second shortcuts frequently tested in UP TGT/PGT and DSSSB:

01

Shortcut 1: Finite Semigroup Cancellation

$$ \text{If } S \text{ is a finite semigroup satisfying left and right cancellation laws, then } S \text{ is a group.} $$

Exam Rule: In finite sets, checking closure, associativity, and cancellation is sufficient to guarantee a group, bypassing explicit inverse checks.

⚡ PYQ Context: Frequently tested in UP PGT to quickly identify finite groups among multiplicative or matrix subsets.
02

Shortcut 2: Order of Elements in Modular Arithmetic

$$ o(a) \text{ divides } |G| \text{ (Lagrange's Theorem corollary).} $$

Exam Rule: The order of any element in a finite group $G$ must divide the order of the group $|G|$.

⚡ PYQ Context: Directly applied in TGT/PGT questions asking for the order of elements in $(\mathbb{Z}_n, +)$ or $(\mathbb{U}(n), \cdot)$.
03

Shortcut 3: Matrix Group Determinants

$$ \det(AB) = \det(A)\det(B) \text{ and } \det(A^{-1}) = \frac{1}{\det(A)} $$

Exam Rule: To check if a set of matrices forms a group under multiplication, verify that the determinant is non-zero and closed under multiplication.

⚡ PYQ Context: Standard pattern in KVS PGT and DSSSB for $GL(n, \mathbb{R})$ and $SL(n, \mathbb{R})$.
04

Shortcut 4: Klein Four-Group & Cyclic Group Identification

$$ \text{If every non-identity element has order 2, the group is isomorphic to } V_4. $$

Exam Rule: If $a^2 = e$ for all elements in a group, the group is abelian and isomorphic to the Klein four-group (if of order 4).

⚡ PYQ Context: Scoring shortcut for structural identification problems in UP PGT Mathematics.
05

Shortcut 5: Direct Product Orders

$$ o((a,b)) = \text{lcm}(o(a), o(b)) \text{ in } G_1 \times G_2 $$

Exam Rule: Use the least common multiple of component element orders to find the order of elements in direct product groups.

⚡ PYQ Context: Asked in advanced PGT examinations for direct products of cyclic groups like $\mathbb{Z}_2 \times \mathbb{Z}_3$.
06

Shortcut 6: Rapid Elimination via Identity Check

$$ e * a = a * e = a $$

Exam Rule: Always test the candidate identity element first. If a proposed set lacks the standard or modified identity, eliminate the option instantly.

⚡ PYQ Context: Saves 40+ seconds in examination questions listing multiple algebraic structures.

Pillar 4 Classic Exam Traps & Counterexamples

Where examiners construct tricky trap options and how to safeguard your marks:

⚠️ EXAM TRAP #01

Trap 1: Confusing $(\mathbb{Z}, \cdot)$ with $(\mathbb{Z}, +)$

Common Pitfall: Candidates assume integers form a group under multiplication, forgetting that non-zero integers lack multiplicative inverses within $\mathbb{Z}$.

Counterexample: For $2 \in \mathbb{Z}$, there is no $b \in \mathbb{Z}$ such that $2 \cdot b = 1$.

Why it fails: The inverse axiom fails for all integers except $1$ and $-1$.

🛡️ Exam Defense: Always specify the operation explicitly; integers form a monoid, not a group, under multiplication.
⚠️ EXAM TRAP #02

Trap 2: Assuming Matrix Multiplication is Always Commutative

Common Pitfall: Assuming general linear groups $GL(n, \mathbb{R})$ for $n \ge 2$ are abelian.

Counterexample: For $A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ and $B = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$, $AB \neq BA$.

Why it fails: Matrix multiplication is non-commutative for $n \ge 2$, so $GL(n, \mathbb{R})$ is non-abelian.

🛡️ Exam Defense: Never apply commutativity rules to matrix groups unless restricted to diagonal or $n=1$ cases.

Pillar 6 Solved Canonical Exam Problems (Dual-Method)

Comparing the formal academic proof against the rapid competitive exam elimination trick:

CANONICAL PROBLEM 01 Dual-Method Breakdown

Which of the following sets forms a group under multiplication modulo $5$?

(A) $\{0, 1, 2, 3, 4\}$(B) $\{1, 2, 3, 4\}$(C) $\{2, 3, 4\}$(D) $\{1, 3, 4\}$
✓ Correct Answer: (B)
📚 Method A: Formal Textbook Derivation
Step 1: Check multiplicative identity $1$. Step 2: Verify non-zero elements have multiplicative inverses modulo 5 ($1^{-1}=1, 2^{-1}=3, 3^{-1}=2, 4^{-1}=4$). Step 3: Confirm closure. Thus, $\mathbb{Z}_5^*$ forms a group.
⚡ Method B: 25-Second Exam Shortcut
Exclude $0$ immediately for multiplicative groups, and check if remaining non-zero residues coprime to $5$ form $\mathbb{Z}_5^*$.
💡 Key Takeaway: For modulo $n$ multiplication, the group is always formed by integers coprime to $n$, of size $\phi(n)$.
CANONICAL PROBLEM 02 Dual-Method Breakdown

What is the order of the element $3$ in the group $(\mathbb{Z}_7^*, \cdot_{7})$?

(A) $3$(B) $6$(C) $2$(D) $4$
✓ Correct Answer: (B)
📚 Method A: Formal Textbook Derivation
Step 1: Compute powers of $3$ modulo $7$. $3^1 \equiv 3$, $3^2 \equiv 2$, $3^3 \equiv 6$, $3^4 \equiv 4$, $3^5 \equiv 5$, $3^6 \equiv 1$. Step 2: The smallest positive integer $n$ such that $3^n \equiv 1 \pmod 7$ is $6$.
⚡ Method B: 25-Second Exam Shortcut
By Fermat's Little Theorem, $a^{p-1} \equiv 1 \pmod p$. The order must divide $\phi(7) = 6$. Test divisors $1, 2, 3, 6$.
💡 Key Takeaway: Always test divisors of the order of the group when seeking element orders.

Pillar 7 10 Practice MCQs for Self-Assessment

Q1
MCQ 1: Which of the following is not a group under the given operation?
A (A) $(\mathbb{Z}, +)$
B (B) $(\mathbb{Q}, +)$
C (C) $(\mathbb{R}, \cdot)$
D (D) $(\mathbb{C}^*, \cdot)$
Derivation & Analysis:

In $(\mathbb{R}, \cdot)$, the element $0$ does not possess a multiplicative inverse ($0 \cdot x = 1$ has no solution in $\mathbb{R}$), hence it is not a group.

Q2
MCQ 2: The order of the additive group $\mathbb{Z}_n$ is:
A (A) $n-1$
B (B) $n$
C (C) $\infty$
D (D) $\phi(n)$
Derivation & Analysis:

The set $\mathbb{Z}_n = \{0, 1, 2, \dots, n-1\}$ contains exactly $n$ elements, so its order is $n$.

Q3
MCQ 3: If $G$ is a group such that $(ab)^2 = a^2 b^2$ for all $a, b \in G$, then $G$ is:
A (A) Cyclic
B (B) Infinite
C (C) Abelian
D (D) Trivial only
Derivation & Analysis:

Expanding $(ab)(ab) = aabb \implies abab = aabb$. By left and right cancellation of $a$ and $b$, $ba = ab$, proving $G$ is abelian.

Q4
MCQ 4: The number of generators of the cyclic group $(\mathbb{Z}_{10}, +)$ is:
A (A) $1$
B (B) $2$
C (C) $4$
D (D) $5$
Derivation & Analysis:

The number of generators of a finite cyclic group of order $n$ is $\phi(n)$. Here $\phi(10) = \phi(2) \cdot \phi(5) = 1 \cdot 4 = 4$.

Q5
MCQ 5: Which of the following matrix sets forms a group under matrix multiplication?
A (A) All $2 \times 2$ real matrices
B (B) All $2 \times 2$ matrices with $\det = 0$
C (C) All $2 \times 2$ matrices with $\det = 1$
D (D) All $2 \times 2$ symmetric matrices
Derivation & Analysis:

The set of $2 \times 2$ matrices with determinant $1$ forms the Special Linear Group $SL(2, \mathbb{R})$, which satisfies closure, associativity, identity, and inverse properties.

Q6
MCQ 6: In any group $G$, the solution of the equation $ax = b$ for $a, b \in G$ is:
A (A) $x = ba^{-1}$
B (B) $x = a^{-1}b$
C (C) $x = ab^{-1}$
D (D) $x = ba$
Derivation & Analysis:

Multiplying $ax = b$ on the left by $a^{-1}$ gives $a^{-1}(ax) = a^{-1}b \implies (a^{-1}a)x = a^{-1}b \implies x = a^{-1}b$.

Q7
MCQ 7: The inverse of the element $(2, 3)$ in the direct product group $\mathbb{Z}_5 \times \mathbb{Z}_4$ is:
A (A) $(2, 3)$
B (B) $(3, 1)$
C (C) $(2, 1)$
D (D) $(3, 2)$
Derivation & Analysis:

In $\mathbb{Z}_5$, $-2 \equiv 3 \pmod 5$. In $\mathbb{Z}_4$, $-3 \equiv 1 \pmod 4$. Thus, the inverse is $(3, 1)$.

Q8
MCQ 8: Assertion (A): Every cyclic group is abelian. Reason (R): If $a, b \in \langle x \rangle$, then $a = x^m, b = x^n$, so $ab = x^{m+n} = x^{n+m} = ba$.
A (A) Both (A) and (R) are true and (R) is the correct explanation of (A)
B (B) Both (A) and (R) are true but (R) is not the correct explanation
C (C) (A) is true but (R) is false
D (D) (A) is false but (R) is true
Derivation & Analysis:

Every cyclic group is generated by a single element, and powers of the same element commute under addition of exponents, confirming both assertion and reason.

Q9
MCQ 9: The order of the permutation $\sigma = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 2 & 3 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ in $S_3$ is:
A (A) $1$
B (B) $2$
C (C) $3$
D (D) $6$
Derivation & Analysis:

Writing $\sigma$ in disjoint cycle notation gives $(1 2 3)$, which is a 3-cycle of length 3, hence its order is 3.

Q10
MCQ 10: If $H$ and $K$ are two subgroups of a group $G$, then $H \cap K$ is:
A (A) Never a subgroup
B (B) Always a subgroup of $G$
C (C) A subgroup only if $|H| = |K|$
D (D) A subgroup only if $H \cup K = G$
Derivation & Analysis:

The intersection of any arbitrary collection of subgroups of a group $G$ is always a subgroup of $G$.
