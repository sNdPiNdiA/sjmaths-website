6. Abstract Algebra

Lagrange theorem

Lagrange's Theorem in Group Theory states that the order of any subgroup of a finite group divides the order of the group, serving as a cornerstone for algebraic classification. In teacher recruitment examinations like UP PGT, TGT, and KVS Mathematics, it is a high-yield scoring zone frequently tested via index calculations and element orders.

Pillar 1 Rigorous Definition & Mathematical Statement

Lagrange's Theorem for Finite Groups
Let $G$ be a finite group and $H$ be a subgroup of $G$. Then the order of $H$ divides the order of $G$. Symbolically, $|H| \mid |G|$, or equivalently, $|G| = [G : H] \cdot |H|$, where $[G : H]$ is the index of $H$ in $G$.

📐 Geometric Interpretation

While group theory is purely algebraic, the partition property can be visualized as tiling a space of size $|G|$ with disjoint, congruent blocks (cosets) of uniform size $|H|$.

⚙️ Algebraic & Structural Meaning

Establishes a fundamental restriction on the subgroup structure of finite groups, immediately implying that groups of prime order have no proper nontrivial subgroups and that the order of any element divides the group order.

Pillar 3 High-Yield Exam Results & Direct Shortcuts

Standard 15-to-30 second shortcuts frequently tested in UP TGT/PGT and DSSSB:

01

Shortcut 1: Order of Elements Divides Group Order

$$ \forall a \in G, \quad o(a) \mid |G| $$

Exam Rule: The order of any element in a finite group $G$ divides the order of $G$. Instantly eliminate options where element orders do not divide $|G|$.

⚡ PYQ Context: Directly tested in UP PGT/TGT to find possible element orders or nonexistent elements in cyclic/symmetric groups.
02

Shortcut 2: Prime Order Implies Cyclicity

$$ |G| = p \text{ (prime)} \implies G \cong \mathbb{Z}_p $$

Exam Rule: Any group of prime order is cyclic and has no proper nontrivial subgroups.

⚡ PYQ Context: Standard question in DSSSB and KVS PGT regarding classification of groups of order $p, p^2$, etc.
03

Shortcut 3: Fermat's Little Theorem Deduction

$$ a^{p-1} \equiv 1 \pmod p $$

Exam Rule: Derived by applying Lagrange's theorem to the multiplicative group $(\mathbb{Z}_p^*, \cdot)$ of order $p-1$.

⚡ PYQ Context: Frequently asked number theory/algebra overlap questions in UP PGT.
04

Shortcut 4: Subgroup Index Calculation

$$ [G : H] = \frac{|G|}{|H|} $$

Exam Rule: To find the number of left cosets, simply divide group order by subgroup order for finite groups.

⚡ PYQ Context: Commonly framed with alternating groups $A_n$ or symmetric groups $S_n$.
05

Shortcut 5: Non-existence of Subgroups

$$ \text{If } m \nmid n \implies \text{no subgroup of order } m \text{ in } G \text{ of order } n $$

Exam Rule: If an integer $m$ does not divide $|G|$, $G$ cannot possess a subgroup of order $m$.

⚡ PYQ Context: Used to quickly eliminate options in multiple-choice questions.
06

Shortcut 6: Converse Failure Trap

$$ m \mid |G| \not\implies \exists \text{ subgroup of order } m $$

Exam Rule: Lagrange's converse is false in general (e.g., $A_4$ has order 12, but no subgroup of order 6). Always check known alternating/dihedral structures.

⚡ PYQ Context: Favorite conceptual trap in PGT examinations.

Pillar 4 Classic Exam Traps & Counterexamples

Where examiners construct tricky trap options and how to safeguard your marks:

⚠️ EXAM TRAP #01

Trap 1: Assuming Lagrange's Converse is True

Common Pitfall: Candidates assume that if $m$ divides $|G|$, there must exist a subgroup of order $m$.

Counterexample: A_4 \text{ has order } 12, \text{ but has no subgroup of order } 6

Why it fails: Divisibility is necessary, but not sufficient for the existence of a subgroup of a given order.

🛡️ Exam Defense: Remember Cauchy's Theorem (prime divisors) and Sylow's Theorems ($p^k$ divisors), but never assume arbitrary divisors guarantee subgroups.
⚠️ EXAM TRAP #02

Trap 2: Confusing Left and Right Coset Count

Common Pitfall: Believing that left cosets and right cosets have different counts or indices.

Counterexample: [G : H]_l = [G : H]_r = \frac{|G|}{|H|}

Why it fails: The number of left cosets equals the number of right cosets, even though individual left and right cosets may not coincide unless $H$ is normal.

🛡️ Exam Defense: The index $[G : H]$ is uniquely defined regardless of left or right cosets.

Pillar 6 Solved Canonical Exam Problems (Dual-Method)

Comparing the formal academic proof against the rapid competitive exam elimination trick:

CANONICAL PROBLEM 01 Dual-Method Breakdown

If $G$ is a group of order 45, what are the possible orders of proper subgroups of $G$?

(A) 1, 3, 5, 9, 15, 45(B) 1, 3, 5, 9, 15(C) 3, 5, 9, 15(D) 1, 3, 9, 45
✓ Correct Answer: (B)
📚 Method A: Formal Textbook Derivation
Step 1: Identify $|G| = 45 = 3^2 \cdot 5$. Step 2: List all positive divisors of 45, which are 1, 3, 5, 9, 15, 45. Step 3: Proper subgroups exclude the improper subgroup $G$ itself (order 45). Thus, possible orders are divisors strictly less than 45: 1, 3, 5, 9, 15.
⚡ Method B: 25-Second Exam Shortcut
Divisors of 45 except 45 itself. Match options instantly.
💡 Key Takeaway: Read carefully whether 'subgroups' or 'proper subgroups' is asked.
CANONICAL PROBLEM 02 Dual-Method Breakdown

Which of the following orders cannot be the order of a subgroup of a group of order 60?

(A) 12(B) 15(C) 25(D) 30
✓ Correct Answer: (C)
📚 Method A: Formal Textbook Derivation
Step 1: Determine divisors of 60 ($60 = 2^2 \cdot 3 \cdot 5$). Divisors include 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 10, 12, 15, 20, 30, 60. Step 2: Check 25. Since 25 does not divide 60 ($25 \nmid 60$), by Lagrange's theorem, no subgroup of order 25 can exist.
⚡ Method B: 25-Second Exam Shortcut
Check divisibility of options into 60. 25 does not divide 60.
💡 Key Takeaway: Direct application of divisibility criteria.

Pillar 7 10 Practice MCQs for Self-Assessment

Q1
MCQ 1: If $H$ and $K$ are subgroups of a finite group $G$ of orders 12 and 18 respectively, what can we say about $|H \cap K|$?
A It divides 6
B It divides 12
C It divides 18
D It divides 36
Derivation & Analysis:

$H \cap K$ is a subgroup of both $H$ and $K$. Therefore, its order must divide both $|H| = 12$ and $|K| = 18$. Thus, $|H \cap K| \mid \gcd(12, 18) = 6$.

Q2
MCQ 2: The order of every element in a finite group $G$ of order $n$ is:
A Equal to $n$
B A multiple of $n$
C A divisor of $n$
D Prime
Derivation & Analysis:

By corollary to Lagrange's theorem, the order of an element $a$ equals the order of the cyclic subgroup $\langle a \rangle$, which must divide $|G| = n$.

Q3
MCQ 3: If $G$ is a group of prime order $p$, then the number of distinct proper subgroups of $G$ is:
A $p$
B $1$
C $0$
D $p-1$
Derivation & Analysis:

A group of prime order has no proper nontrivial subgroups. The only subgroups are the trivial subgroup and $G$ itself, meaning 0 proper subgroups.

Q4
MCQ 4: If $H$ is a subgroup of index 2 in a group $G$, then for all $x \in G$, we have:
A $x^2 \in H$
B $x \in H$
C $x^2 = e$
D $x$ has infinite order
Derivation & Analysis:

A subgroup of index 2 is always normal. Thus $G/H$ is a group of order 2, meaning $(xH)^2 = H \implies x^2 H = H \implies x^2 \in H$.

Q5
MCQ 5: Which of the following groups has no proper subgroups?
A (\mathbb{Z}_6, +)
B (\mathbb{Z}_5, +)
C (S_3, \circ)
D (\mathbb{Z}, +)
Derivation & Analysis:

(\mathbb{Z}_5, +) has prime order 5, hence no proper subgroups. Others have composite orders or infinite size.

Q6
MCQ 6: If $|G| = 100$ and $H$ is a subgroup of $G$ with $|H| = 25$, what is the index $[G : H]$?
A 25
B 4
C 100
D 5
Derivation & Analysis:

The index is given by $[G : H] = \frac{|G|}{|H|} = \frac{100}{25} = 4$.

Q7
MCQ 7: Lagrange's theorem states that if $H$ is a subgroup of a finite group $G$, then:
A $|H|$ equals $|G|$
B $|H|$ divides $|G|$
C $|G|$ divides $|H|$
D $|H| + |G|$ is even
Derivation & Analysis:

Direct statement of Lagrange's theorem: the order of a subgroup divides the order of the finite group.

Q8
MCQ 8: Assertion (A): The alternating group $A_4$ has no subgroup of order 6. Reason (R): Lagrange's theorem converse is not universally true.
A Both (A) and (R) are true and (R) is the correct explanation of (A)
B Both (A) and (R) are true but (R) is not the correct explanation
C (A) is true but (R) is false
D (A) is false but (R) is true
Derivation & Analysis:

6 divides $|A_4| = 12$, but $A_4$ has no subgroup of order 6 because Lagrange's converse fails.

Q9
MCQ 9: If $a \in G$ and $o(a) = 15$, then the possible orders of subgroups generated by powers of $a$ are:
A 1, 3, 5, 15
B 1, 2, 3, 5, 15
C 3, 5
D 1, 15
Derivation & Analysis:

The cyclic subgroup $\langle a \rangle$ has order 15. Its subgroups correspond to the divisors of 15, which are 1, 3, 5, 15.

Q10
MCQ 10: Let $G$ be a group of order $p^n$ where $p$ is prime. Every subgroup of $G$ has order of the form:
A $p^k$ where $0 \le k \le n$
B $n^k$
C $kp$
D $p+k$
Derivation & Analysis:

By Lagrange's theorem, the order of any subgroup of $G$ (order $p^n$) must divide $p^n$, hence its order must be a power of $p$, i.e., $p^k$ with $0 \le k \le n$.
