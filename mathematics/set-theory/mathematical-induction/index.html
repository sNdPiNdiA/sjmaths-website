Principle of mathematical induction

The Principle of Mathematical Induction is a fundamental axiom of natural numbers used to prove assertions for all $n \in \mathbb{N}$, anchored in the Well-Ordering Principle of $\mathbb{Z}$. In competitive exams like UP TGT/PGT, KVS, and DSSSB, it serves as a high-scoring zone for validating summation formulas, divisibility properties, and matrix powers.

Target Exams: UP PGT Mathematics UP TGT Mathematics DSSSB PGT / TGT KVS / NVS PGT LT Grade
📖 Self-Study Mastery Status
○ Mark Topic as Mastered

Pillar 1 Rigorous Definition & Mathematical Statement

Principle of Mathematical Induction (First Form)
Let $P(n)$ be a statement defined for all $n \in \mathbb{N}$. Suppose that: (i) $P(1)$ is true, and (ii) For any $k \in \mathbb{N}$, the truth of $P(k)$ implies the truth of $P(k+1)$. Then $P(n)$ is true for all $n \in \mathbb{N}$, symbolically expressed as $$\left[P(1) \land \forall k \in \mathbb{N}, (P(k) \implies P(k+1))\right] \implies \forall n \in \mathbb{N}, P(n)$$

📐 Geometric Interpretation

Visualize an infinite row of dominoes aligned in sequence. Condition (i) knocks down the first domino ($n=1$), and condition (ii) ensures that the fall of the $k$-th domino guarantees the fall of the $(k+1)$-th domino, resulting in a chain reaction across all positive integers.

⚙️ Algebraic & Structural Meaning

It formalizes the recursive nature of the natural numbers $\mathbb{N}$ and is equivalent to the Peano Axioms and the Well-Ordering Principle, establishing that no subset of $\mathbb{N}$ lacks a least element.

Pillar 4 Classic Exam Traps & Counterexamples

Where examiners construct tricky trap options and how to safeguard your marks:

⚠️ EXAM TRAP #01

Trap 1: The False Base Case Trap

Common Pitfall: Assuming an inequality or property holds for all $n \in \mathbb{N}$ when it actually only holds for $n \ge 4$ (e.g., $2^n > n^2$).

Counterexample: For $n=3$: $2^3 = 8$, but $3^2 = 9$ (so $2^3 > 3^3$ is false).

Why it fails: The inductive step might work algebraically for all $k$, but if the base case is checked at an incorrect starting index, the theorem fails.

🛡️ Exam Defense: Always test small values ($n=1, 2, 3, 4$) explicitly before accepting an inductive inequality.
⚠️ EXAM TRAP #02

Trap 2: Flawed Inductive Hypothesis Application

Common Pitfall: Proving $P(k+1)$ by assuming $P(n)$ for all numbers up to $k$ when using First Induction instead of Strong Induction, or vice versa.

Counterexample: Assuming $P(k+1)$ directly to prove $P(k)$ (circular reasoning).

Why it fails: Induction must strictly flow from $k$ to $k+1$, never backward or circularly.

🛡️ Exam Defense: Ensure the substitution of the inductive hypothesis happens precisely once during the derivation of $P(k+1)$.

CANONICAL PROBLEM 01 Dual-Method Breakdown

For all $n \in \mathbb{N}$, the sum $1 + 2 + 3 + \dots + n$ is equal to:

(A) \frac{n(n+1)}{2}(B) \frac{n(n-1)}{2}(C) n(n+1)(D) \frac{n(n+1)(2n+1)}{6}
✓ Correct Answer: (A)
📚 Method A: Formal Textbook Derivation
Step 1: Check for $n=1$: $1 = \frac{1(2)}{2} = 1$ (True). Step 2: Assume true for $n=k$: $1+...+k = \frac{k(k+1)}{2}$. Step 3: For $n=k+1$, add $k+1$ to both sides to get $\frac{(k+1)(k+2)}{2}$. Step 4: By induction, true for all $n$.
⚡ Method B: 25-Second Exam Shortcut
Substitute $n=1$: Option A gives $1$, Option B gives $0$ (eliminate B), Option C gives $2$ (eliminate C), Option D gives $1$. Substitute $n=2$: Option A gives $3$, Option D gives $5$ (eliminate D). Option A is correct.
💡 Key Takeaway: Use quick value substitution for $n=1, 2$ to eliminate distractors instantly.
CANONICAL PROBLEM 02 Dual-Method Breakdown

Which of the following statements is always divisible by $3$ for all $n \in \mathbb{N}$?

(A) $n^3 + 2n$(B) $3^n + 1$(C) $n^2 + n + 1$(D) $2^n + 1$
✓ Correct Answer: (A)
📚 Method A: Formal Textbook Derivation
Let $P(n) = n^3 + 2n$. For $n=1$, $1+2=3$ (divisible by 3). Assume $k^3+2k = 3m$. For $n=k+1$, $(k+1)^3 + 2(k+1) = k^3+3k^2+3k+1+2k+2 = (k^3+2k) + 3(k^2+k+1)$, which is divisible by 3.
⚡ Method B: 25-Second Exam Shortcut
Test $n=1$: (A) $1+2=3$ (Divisible), (B) $3+1=4$ (No), (C) $3$ (Yes), (D) $3$ (Yes). Test $n=2$: (A) $8+4=12$ (Yes), (C) $4+2+1=7$ (No), (D) $4+1=5$ (No). Thus only (A) holds for all $n$.
💡 Key Takeaway: Modular arithmetic testing with $n=1, 2$ eliminates incorrect options in seconds.

MCQ 5: If $A = \begin{pmatrix} 3 & -4 \\ 1 & -1 \end{pmatrix}$, then $A^n$ using induction is equal to:
A \begin{pmatrix} 1+2n & -4n \\ n & 1-2n \end{pmatrix}
B \begin{pmatrix} 3n & -4n \\ n & -n \end{pmatrix}
C \begin{pmatrix} 3^n & -4^n \\ 1 & -1 \end{pmatrix}
D \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}
Derivation & Analysis:

Checking $n=1$: \begin{pmatrix} 1+2(-4)? \dots \end{pmatrix}. Let's test option A for $n=1$: \begin{pmatrix} 3 & -4 \\ 1 & -1 \end{pmatrix}. For $n=2$: \begin{pmatrix} 5 & -8 \\ 2 & -3 \end{pmatrix}, which matches $A^2$. Option A is correct.

Q6
MCQ 6: The minimum value of $n$ for which the inequality $2^n \ge n^2$ holds for all $n \ge n_0$ is:
A $n_0 = 1$
B $n_0 = 2$
C $n_0 = 3$
D $n_0 = 4$
Derivation & Analysis:

For $n=1$: $2 \ge 1$ (True). For $n=2$: $4 \ge 4$ (True). For $n=3$: $8 \ge 9$ (False). For $n=4$: $16 \ge 16$ (True). Thus $n \ge 4$ is required for strict inequality, though it holds at $n=1, 2$ and $4$ onwards.

Q7
MCQ 7: The sum of the series $1\cdot 2 + 2\cdot 3 + 3\cdot 4 + \dots + n(n+1)$ is:
A \frac{n(n+1)(n+2)}{3}
B \frac{n(n+1)(2n+1)}{3}
C \frac{n(n+2)(n+3)}{4}
D n(n+1)(n+2)
Derivation & Analysis:

Using standard summation $\sum i(i+1) = \sum i^2 + \sum i = \frac{n(n+1)(2n+1)}{6} + \frac{n(n+1)}{2} = \frac{n(n+1)(n+2)}{3}$.

Q8
MCQ 8: Statement 1: Induction can prove statements for real numbers. Statement 2: Induction applies to countable discrete ordered sets like $\mathbb{N}$.
A Both Statement 1 and Statement 2 are true
B Statement 1 is true, Statement 2 is false
C Statement 1 is false, Statement 2 is true
D Both statements are false
Derivation & Analysis:

Mathematical induction relies on the discrete successor property of integers and does not apply directly to continuous real numbers $\mathbb{R}$. Thus Statement 1 is false and Statement 2 is true.

Q9
MCQ 9: For any natural number $n$, $7^{2n} - 48n - 1$ is divisible by:
A 24
B 48
C 2304
D 576
Derivation & Analysis:

For $n=1$: $49 - 48 - 1 = 0$, which is divisible by 576 (and any integer). For $n=2$: $7^4 - 96 - 1 = 2401 - 97 = 2304 = 4 \times 576$. Thus 576 is the correct divisor.

MCQ 10: If $P(n)$ is the statement ' $n^3 + (n+1)^3 + (n+2)^3$ is a multiple of 9', what is the base case check $P(1)$?
A $1+8+27 = 36$ (Multiple of 9)
B $1+4+9 = 14$
C $8+27+64 = 99$
D $0$
Derivation & Analysis:

For $n=1$, $1^3 + 2^3 + 3^3 = 1 + 8 + 27 = 36$, which is $4 \times 9$, confirming the base case.
