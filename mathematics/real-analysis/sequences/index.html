Real Analysis

Real sequences

A real sequence is a function mapping the set of natural numbers $\mathbb{N}$ into the set of real numbers $\mathbb{R}$, serving as the foundational stepping stone for limits, continuity, and convergence in Real Analysis. Mastering the convergence tests, Cauchy criteria, and monotonicity theorems is vital for scoring high-yield questions in UP PGT/TGT, KVS, and DSSSB examinations.

Pillar 1 Rigorous Definition & Mathematical Statement

Monotone Convergence Theorem (MCT) for Real Sequences
Every bounded monotone sequence of real numbers is convergent. Specifically, if $\langle x_n \rangle$ is monotonically increasing and bounded above, then \begin{equation*} \lim_{n \to \infty} x_n = \sup\{x_n : n \in \mathbb{N}\}. \end{equation*} If $\langle x_n \rangle$ is monotonically decreasing and bounded below, then \begin{equation*} \lim_{n \to \infty} x_n = \inf\{x_n : n \in \mathbb{N}\}. \end{equation*}

📐 Geometric Interpretation

Geometrically, points on the real number line move monotonically in one direction (either strictly increasing or decreasing) while being trapped by a finite upper or lower bound. Because the real number system $\mathbb{R}$ possesses the completeness property (supremum property), the sequence cannot wander indefinitely and must accumulate precisely at its least upper bound or greatest lower bound.

⚙️ Algebraic & Structural Meaning

Establishes the fundamental completeness axiom of the real number system in sequence form. It bridges order properties with topological limits, allowing the deduction of convergence without explicitly knowing or calculating the limiting value in advance.

Pillar 3 High-Yield Exam Results & Direct Shortcuts

Standard 15-to-30 second shortcuts frequently tested in UP TGT/PGT and DSSSB:

01

Shortcut 1: Algebraic Ratio Test for Sequences ($n$-th root or D'Alembert's ratio)

$$ \lim_{n \to \infty} \frac{x_{n+1}}{x_n} = L $$

Exam Rule: If $L < 1$, then $\lim_{n \to \infty} x_n = 0$. If $L > 1$, the sequence diverges to infinity. If $L = 1$, the test fails and further analysis (like Raabe's Test) is required.

⚡ PYQ Context: Frequently asked in UP PGT and KVS to find limits of factorial-heavy or exponential terms like $x_n = \frac{n!}{n^n}$.
02

Shortcut 2: Cauchy's First Limit Theorem (Arithmetic Mean of Sequences)

$$ \text{If } \lim_{n \to \infty} x_n = L, \text{ then } \lim_{n \to \infty} \frac{x_1 + x_2 + \dots + x_n}{n} = L $$

Exam Rule: The limit of the arithmetic mean of a convergent sequence is equal to the limit of the sequence itself. Useful when evaluating complex summation limits.

⚡ PYQ Context: Directly tested in DSSSB PGT and UP TGT to evaluate limits of averages such as $\frac{1}{n} \left(1 + \frac{1}{2} + \dots + \frac{1}{n}\right)$ or root-based combinations.
03

Shortcut 3: Cauchy's Second Limit Theorem (Geometric Mean)

$$ \text{If } x_n > 0 \text{ and } \lim_{n \to \infty} x_n = L, \text{ then } \lim_{n \to \infty} (x_1 x_2 \dots x_n)^{1/n} = L $$

Exam Rule: The limit of the geometric mean of a positive convergent sequence matches the sequence limit.

⚡ PYQ Context: Standard pattern in competitive examinations for product sequences.
04

Shortcut 4: Sandwich / Squeeze Theorem for Sequences

$$ \text{If } a_n \le x_n \le b_n \text{ and } \lim_{n \to \infty} a_n = \lim_{n \to \infty} b_n = L, \text{ then } \lim_{n \to \infty} x_n = L $$

Exam Rule: Squeeze complex fractional terms between two simpler geometric or power functions whose limits are easily evaluated as $0$ or a constant.

⚡ PYQ Context: Extensively used for evaluating limits involving $\frac{1}{n^p}$ sums or sine/cosine bounds.
05

Shortcut 5: Standard Growth Rate Hierarchy

$$ \ln n \ll n^p \ll a^n \ll n! \ll n^n \quad (a > 1, p > 0) $$

Exam Rule: In any rational combination of these standard sequences as $n \to \infty$, the faster-growing term dominates. Numerator dominates $\implies \infty$; Denominator dominates $\implies 0$.

⚡ PYQ Context: Saves 45 seconds on polynomial-exponential limit problems in UP PGT.
06

Shortcut 6: Rapid Elimination via Subsequences

$$ \text{If } \langle x_{2n} \rangle \text{ and } \langle x_{2n-1} \rangle \text{ converge to distinct limits, } \langle x_n \rangle \text{ diverges.} $$

Exam Rule: Instantly check even and odd terms. If they yield different limits, discard all options claiming convergence.

⚡ PYQ Context: Time-saving elimination trick for oscillating sequence questions in TGT/PGT.

Pillar 4 Classic Exam Traps & Counterexamples

Where examiners construct tricky trap options and how to safeguard your marks:

⚠️ EXAM TRAP #01

Trap 1: Boundedness Does Not Imply Convergence

Common Pitfall: Candidates assume that because a sequence remains within fixed bounds, it must converge to a specific single value.

Counterexample: $x_n = (-1)^n$

Why it fails: The sequence is bounded between $-1$ and $1$, but it oscillates indefinitely between $-1$ and $1$, failing to possess a unique limit.

🛡️ Exam Defense: Remember: Boundedness + Monotonicity = Convergence. Boundedness alone only guarantees the existence of at least one limit point (Bolzano-Weierstrass Theorem), not convergence.
⚠️ EXAM TRAP #02

Trap 2: Converse of Cauchy's First Theorem Failure

Common Pitfall: Assuming that if the arithmetic mean $\frac{1}{n}\sum x_n$ converges, the underlying sequence $\langle x_n \rangle$ must also converge.

Counterexample: $x_n = (-1)^n$

Why it fails: The arithmetic mean limit is $0$, but the sequence $\langle (-1)^n \rangle$ diverges.

🛡️ Exam Defense: The implication is strictly one-way: $x_n \to L \implies \text{Mean} \to L$. The converse is false.

Pillar 6 Solved Canonical Exam Problems (Dual-Method)

Comparing the formal academic proof against the rapid competitive exam elimination trick:

CANONICAL PROBLEM 01 Dual-Method Breakdown

Evaluate the limit of the sequence defined recursively by $x_1 = \sqrt{2}$ and $x_{n+1} = \sqrt{2 + x_n}$ for $n \ge 1$.

(A) $1$(B) $2$(C) $\sqrt{2}$(D) Does not exist
✓ Correct Answer: (B)
📚 Method A: Formal Textbook Derivation
Step 1: Show $x_n < 2$ by mathematical induction. Step 2: Show $x_{n+1} > x_n$ ($x_n$ is monotonically increasing). Step 3: By MCT, limit $L$ exists. Step 4: Solve $L = \sqrt{2 + L} \implies L^2 - L - 2 = 0 \implies L = 2$ (since $L > 0$).
⚡ Method B: 25-Second Exam Shortcut
Set $x_{n+1} = x_n = L$ directly in the recursive relation: $L = \sqrt{2 + L} \implies L^2 - L - 2 = 0 \implies L = 2$.
💡 Key Takeaway: For recursive relations of type $x_{n+1} = \sqrt{a + x_n}$, the positive root of $L^2 - L - a = 0$ is always the answer if $x_1 > 0$.
CANONICAL PROBLEM 02 Dual-Method Breakdown

Determine the limit of the sequence $\langle x_n \rangle$ where $x_n = \left(1 + \frac{1}{n}\sup\right)^n$.

(A) $1$(B) $e$(C) $0$(D) $\infty$
✓ Correct Answer: (B)
📚 Method A: Formal Textbook Derivation
Expand using the Binomial Theorem for positive integers and apply the Monotone Convergence Theorem to prove boundedness and monotonicity, establishing the standard definition of Euler's constant $e$.
⚡ Method B: 25-Second Exam Shortcut
Direct standard definition memorization from real analysis: $\lim_{n \to \infty} (1 + \frac{1}{n})^n = e$.
💡 Key Takeaway: Direct standard result often repeated verbatim in UP PGT exams.

Pillar 7 10 Practice MCQs for Self-Assessment

Q1
MCQ 1: Which of the following sequences is convergent?
A $x_n = (-1)^n n$
B $x_n = \frac{1}{n} \sin(n \frac{\pi}{2})$
C $x_n = n^{1/n}$
D $x_n = 1 + (-1)^n$
Derivation & Analysis:

By the Squeeze Theorem, $-\frac{1}{n} \le \frac{1}{n}\sin(\frac{n\pi}{2}) \le \frac{1}{n}$. Since limits of bounds are $0$, the sequence converges to $0$. Option A diverges to $\infty$ in magnitude, C converges to $1$, D oscillates between $0$ and $2$.

Q2
MCQ 2: The sequence $x_n = \frac{1}{n+1} + \frac{1}{n+2} + \dots + \frac{1}{2n}$ is:
A Monotone increasing and divergent
B Monotone increasing and convergent
C Monotone decreasing and convergent
D Not monotonic
Derivation & Analysis:

Computing $x_{n+1} - x_n = \frac{1}{2n+1} + \frac{1}{2n+2} - \frac{1}{n+1} = \frac{1}{2n+1} - \frac{1}{2n+2} > 0$, so it is monotonically increasing. It is bounded above by $1$. By MCT, it converges.

Q3
MCQ 3: If $x_n = \frac{n!}{n^n}$, then $\lim_{n \to \infty} x_n$ is equal to:
A $1$
B $e$
C $0$
D \infty
Derivation & Analysis:

Using D'Alembert's ratio test for sequences: $\frac{x_{n+1}}{x_n} = \frac{(n+1)!}{(n+1)^{n+1}} \cdot \frac{n^n}{n!} = \left(\frac{n}{n+1}\right)^n = \frac{1}{(1 + \frac{1}{n})^n} \to \frac{1}{e} < 1$. Thus, $\lim x_n = 0$.

Q4
MCQ 4: Every bounded sequence has:
A A unique limit
B At least one limit point
C No limit points
D Only rational limit points
Derivation & Analysis:

This is the direct statement of the Bolzano-Weierstrass Theorem for sequences in $\mathbb{R}$.

Q5
MCQ 5: The limit $\lim_{n \to \infty} \left( \frac{1}{\sqrt{n^2+1}} + \frac{1}{\sqrt{n^2+2}} + \dots + \frac{1}{\sqrt{n^2+n}} \right)$ is:
A $0$
B $1$
C $1/2$
D Does not exist
Derivation & Analysis:

Bounding the sum: $n \cdot \frac{1}{\sqrt{n^2+n}} \le x_n \le n \cdot \frac{1}{\sqrt{n^2+1}}$. Taking limits as $n \to \infty$, both lower and upper bounds approach $1$. By Squeeze Theorem, the limit is $1$.

Q6
MCQ 6: If $x_1 = 1$ and $x_{n+1} = \frac{1}{3}(x_n + 1)$, then $\lim_{n \to \infty} x_n$ is:
A $1/2$
B $1/3$
C $1$
D $0$
Derivation & Analysis:

Set $L = \frac{1}{3}(L + 1) \implies 3L = L + 1 \implies 2L = 1 \implies L = 1/2$.

Q7
MCQ 7: Which of the following is a Cauchy sequence?
A $x_n = \sum_{k=1}^n \frac{1}{k}$
B $x_n = \sum_{k=1}^n \frac{(-1)^k}{k}$
C $x_n = \ln n$
D $x_n = (-1)^n n$
Derivation & Analysis:

Since $\mathbb{R}$ is complete, a sequence is Cauchy if and only if it converges. The alternating harmonic series partial sums form a convergent sequence, hence a Cauchy sequence. Harmonic series (A) diverges.

Q8
MCQ 8: If $\lim_{n \to \infty} x_n = L$, then $\lim_{n \to \infty} x_{n+k}$ (where $k$ is a fixed positive integer) is:
A $L + k$
B $L$
C $kL$
D Undefined
Derivation & Analysis:

Shifting finite indices does not alter the tail limit of a convergent sequence.

Q9
MCQ 9: The sequence $x_n = \frac{\sin n}{n}$ satisfies:
A Converges to $1$
B Converges to $0$
C Diverges
D Oscillates finitely
Derivation & Analysis:

Since $-1 \le \sin n \le 1$, dividing by $n > 0$ gives $-\frac{1}{n} \le \frac{\sin n}{n} \le \frac{1}{n}$. By Squeeze Theorem, limit is $0$.

Q10
MCQ 10: Let $x_n = (-1)^n \left(1 + \frac{1}{n}\right)$. The upper and lower limits (limsup and liminf) of $\langle x_n \rangle$ are respectively:
A $1$ and $-1$
B $-1$ and $1$
C $+\infty$ and $-\infty$
D $0$ and $0$
Derivation & Analysis:

The even terms approach $1$, and odd terms approach $-1$. Thus, $\limsup x_n = 1$ and $\liminf x_n = -1$.
