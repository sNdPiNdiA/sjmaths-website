Real Analysis

Open sets

An open set in a metric space is a subset whose every point is an interior point, admitting an open ball entirely contained within the set. In real analysis and topology, open sets form the foundational bedrock for defining continuity, limits, and topological structures, making them a high-yield conceptual scoring zone in UP PGT/TGT and DSSSB examinations.

Pillar 1 Rigorous Definition & Mathematical Statement

Definition and Characterization of Open Sets in $\mathbb{R}$
A subset $S \subset \mathbb{R}$ is said to be an open set if for every point $x \in S$, there exists a positive real number $\epsilon > 0$ such that the open interval $(x - \epsilon, x + \epsilon) \subset S$. Formally, $\forall x \in S, \, \exists \epsilon > 0$ such that $N_\epsilon(x) = (x - \epsilon, x + \epsilon) \subseteq S$.

📐 Geometric Interpretation

Geometrically, around every point in an open set, you can draw a small symmetric neighborhood (an open interval) that stays completely inside the set without poking outside its boundaries. This means open sets cannot contain their boundary points.

⚙️ Algebraic & Structural Meaning

Algebraically and topologically, arbitrary unions of open sets are open, and finite intersections of open sets are open. They allow the rigorous transition from discrete inequalities to continuous limits.

Pillar 3 High-Yield Exam Results & Direct Shortcuts

Standard 15-to-30 second shortcuts frequently tested in UP TGT/PGT and DSSSB:

01

Shortcut 1: Open Intervals in $\mathbb{R}$

$$ (a, b) \text{ is open in } \mathbb{R}, \quad \forall a, b \in \mathbb{R} $$

Exam Rule: Every open interval $(a, b)$, unbounded intervals $(a, \infty)$, $(-\infty, b)$, and the entire real line $\mathbb{R}$ are instantly recognized as open sets.

⚡ PYQ Context: Directly tested in UP PGT to identify topological properties of standard number subsets.
02

Shortcut 2: Arbitrary Union of Open Sets

$$ \bigcup_{\alpha \in I} U_\alpha \text{ is open if each } U_\alpha \text{ is open} $$

Exam Rule: Any arbitrary (finite or infinite) union of open sets is always open. Look for union symbols in options for quick identification.

⚡ PYQ Context: Frequently framed in assertion-reason questions across DSSSB and KVS PGT.
03

Shortcut 3: Finite Intersection of Open Sets

$$ \bigcap_{i=1}^{n} U_i \text{ is open for finite } n $$

Exam Rule: Finite intersection of open sets is open. Caution: Infinite intersection of open sets need not be open (e.g., $(-1/n, 1/n)$ intersection is $\{0}$, which is closed).

⚡ PYQ Context: Classic trap question in UP TGT/PGT regarding countable intersections.
04

Shortcut 4: Complement of Closed Sets

$$ S \text{ is open} \iff S^c = \mathbb{R} \setminus S \text{ is closed} $$

Exam Rule: To prove or test if a set is open, check if its complement is a standard closed set (like finite points or closed intervals).

⚡ PYQ Context: Used to eliminate lengthy interior point proofs in 15 seconds.
05

Shortcut 5: Discrete Metric Space Property

$$ \text{In }(X, d_{\text{discrete}}), \text{ every subset is open.} $$

Exam Rule: Under the discrete metric, singleton sets and all subsets are both open and closed (clopen).

⚡ PYQ Context: Advanced conceptual questions in LT Grade and PGT exams.
06

Shortcut 6: Rapid Elimination via Boundary Check

$$ S \text{ contains its boundary } \implies \text{ Not Open} $$

Exam Rule: If a given set includes its endpoints or boundary points (such as $[a, b]$), instantly eliminate it from being an open set.

⚡ PYQ Context: Saves up to 45 seconds per question in 125-question exams.

Pillar 4 Classic Exam Traps & Counterexamples

Where examiners construct tricky trap options and how to safeguard your marks:

⚠️ EXAM TRAP #01

Trap 1: The Infinite Intersection Fallacy

Common Pitfall: Assuming that infinite intersections of open sets are open, mirroring the rule for finite intersections.

Counterexample: $$ \bigcap_{n=1}^{\infty} \left(-\frac{1}{n}, \frac{1}{n}\right) = \{0} $$

Why it fails: While each interval $(-1/n, 1/n)$ is open, their infinite intersection yields the singleton set $\{0}$, which is closed in $\mathbb{R}$, not open.

🛡️ Exam Defense: Remember: Arbitrary union = Open; Finite intersection = Open; Infinite intersection = NOT necessarily open.
⚠️ EXAM TRAP #02

Trap 2: Confusing Closed Intervals with Open Sets

Common Pitfall: Thinking that a set like $[0, 1)$ is open because it lacks the right endpoint.

Counterexample: $0 \in [0, 1)$, but no open interval around $0$ lies entirely in $[0, 1)$

Why it fails: Any $\epsilon$-neighborhood around $0$, i.e., $(-\epsilon, \epsilon)$, contains negative numbers which fall outside $[0, 1)$.

🛡️ Exam Defense: Check both left and right neighborhoods at every boundary point. If the boundary is included, the set is not open.

Pillar 6 Solved Canonical Exam Problems (Dual-Method)

Comparing the formal academic proof against the rapid competitive exam elimination trick:

CANONICAL PROBLEM 01 Dual-Method Breakdown

Which of the following subsets of $\mathbb{R}$ is an open set?

(A) $\mathbb{Z}$ (Set of integers)(B) $(0, 1]$(C) $(-\infty, 5)$(D) $\{x \in \mathbb{R} : x \ge 0 \}$
✓ Correct Answer: (C)
📚 Method A: Formal Textbook Derivation
Step 1: Check interval types. $\mathbb{Z}$ consists of isolated points with no interior intervals. $(0, 1]$ includes endpoint $1$. ${x \ge 0}$ includes $0$. Step 2: $(-\infty, 5)$ is a standard unbounded open interval. Step 3: Conclusion: $(-\infty, 5)$ is open.
⚡ Method B: 25-Second Exam Shortcut
Instantly recognize standard open intervals $(a, b)$ and $(-\infty, b)$. Eliminate sets with square brackets or discrete points.
💡 Key Takeaway: Look out for half-open intervals disguised in set-builder notation.
CANONICAL PROBLEM 02 Dual-Method Breakdown

Let $S = \left\{ \frac{1}{n} : n \in \mathbb{N} \right}$. In the standard topology of $\mathbb{R}$, the set $S$ is:

(A) An open set(B) A closed set(C) Neither open nor closed(D) Both open and closed
✓ Correct Answer: (C)
📚 Method A: Formal Textbook Derivation
Step 1: Examine limit points of $S$. The limit point is $0$, but $0 \notin S$. Thus $S$ is not closed. Step 2: For any element $1/n \in S$, no open interval around $1/n$ is contained in $S$ because it contains irrational and rational numbers outside $S$. Thus $S$ is not open. Step 3: Conclusion: Neither open nor closed.
⚡ Method B: 25-Second Exam Shortcut
Countable sets of isolated points with a limit point outside the set are classic examples of neither open nor closed sets.
💡 Key Takeaway: Do not confuse the openness of $S$ with the openness of intervals. Singleton points in $\mathbb{R}$ are closed.

Q1
MCQ 1: Which of the following is always an open set in $\mathbb{R}$ with standard topology?
A The set of rational numbers $\mathbb{Q}$
B Any finite subset of $\mathbb{R}$
C An open interval $(a, b)$
D The closed interval $[a, b]$
Derivation & Analysis:

By definition, an open interval $(a, b)$ contains an open neighborhood around each of its points, making it an open set.

Q2
MCQ 2: The union of an arbitrary family of open sets is:
A Always a closed set
B Always an open set
C Sometimes finite
D Never open
Derivation & Analysis:

A foundational theorem in topology states that arbitrary union of open sets is always open.

Q3
MCQ 3: The intersection of infinitely many open sets in $\mathbb{R}$ is:
A Always open
B Always closed
C Always empty
D Not necessarily open
Derivation & Analysis:

Infinite intersections of open sets can yield closed sets, such as $\bigcap_{n=1}^\infty (-1/n, 1/n) = \{0}$, which is closed.

Q4
MCQ 4: Which of the following sets is open in $\mathbb{R}$?
A $\mathbb{R} \setminus \{0\}$
B $\{0, 1, 2\}$
C $[0, 1)$
D $\mathbb{Q}$
Derivation & Analysis:

$\mathbb{R} \setminus \{0}$ is the complement of a closed set (a singleton), hence it is open.

Q5
MCQ 5: The interior of the set $S = [1, 2]$ in $\mathbb{R}$ is:
A $[1, 2]$
B (1, 2)
C $[1, 2)$
D $\emptyset$
Derivation & Analysis:

The largest open set contained in $[1, 2]$ is the open interval $(1, 2)$.

Q6
MCQ 6: In a discrete metric space $(X, d)$, every subset is:
A Open only
B Closed only
C Both open and closed (clopen)
D Neither open nor closed
Derivation & Analysis:

In a discrete space, singleton sets are open balls of radius $1$, making all subsets open and consequently closed.

Q7
MCQ 7: If $A$ and $B$ are open sets in $\mathbb{R}$, then $A \cap B$ is:
A Always open
B Always closed
C Empty
D Finite
Derivation & Analysis:

The intersection of a finite number of open sets (here two) is always open.

Q8
MCQ 8: Assertion (A): Every open interval is an open set. Reason (R): Every point in an open interval is an interior point.
A Both (A) and (R) are true and (R) is the correct explanation of (A)
B Both (A) and (R) are true but (R) is NOT the correct explanation of (A)
C (A) is true but (R) is false
D (A) is false but (R) is true
Derivation & Analysis:

Reason directly defines why an open interval is an open set, satisfying the interior point condition for all its elements.

Q9
MCQ 9: The set of all irrational numbers $\mathbb{R} \setminus \mathbb{Q}$ in $\mathbb{R}$ is:
A An open set
B A closed set
C Neither open nor closed
D Compact
Derivation & Analysis:

Neither $\mathbb{R} \setminus \mathbb{Q}$ nor its complement $\mathbb{Q}$ is open/closed alone; $\mathbb{Q}$ is dense and has empty interior, so its complement is dense and has empty interior.

Q10
MCQ 10: Which of the following is an open subset of $\mathbb{R}^2$?
A The unit circle $\{(x,y) : x^2 + y^2 = 1\}$
B The closed disc $\{(x,y) : x^2 + y^2 \le 1\}$
C The open disc $\{(x,y) : x^2 + y^2 < 1\}$
D The line segment $\{(x,0) : 0 \le x \le 1\}$
Derivation & Analysis:

An open disc defined by strict inequality ($< 1$) corresponds to an open ball in $\mathbb{R}^2$, hence it is an open set.
