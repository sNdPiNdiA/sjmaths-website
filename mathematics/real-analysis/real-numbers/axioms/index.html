Real Analysis

Axioms of real numbers

The real number system $\mathbb{R}$ is formally defined as a complete ordered field, combining algebraic, order, and completeness axioms. Mastery of these axioms underpins all of Real Analysis and forms a high-yield scoring foundation for UP TGT/PGT, KVS, and DSSSB examinations.

Target Exams: UP PGT Mathematics UP TGT Mathematics DSSSB PGT / TGT KVS / NVS PGT LT Grade
📖 Self-Study Mastery Status
○ Mark Topic as Mastered

Pillar 1 Rigorous Definition & Mathematical Statement

Axiom of Completeness (Least Upper Bound Property)
Every non-empty subset $S \subset \mathbb{R}$ that is bounded above possesses a supremum (least upper bound) in $\mathbb{R}$, i.e., \exists \sup S \in \mathbb{R}.

📐 Geometric Interpretation

Geometrically, $\mathbb{R}$ forms an unbroken, continuous continuum line without any 'gaps', holes, or missing points, unlike $\mathbb{Q}$.

⚙️ Algebraic & Structural Meaning

Completeness elevates an ordered field to a complete ordered field, ensuring the validity of limit processes, continuity theorems, and convergence of Cauchy sequences.

Pillar 2 Hypotheses & Condition Checklist

Every hypothesis is strictly non-negotiable. Inspect the domain type and the exact consequence if a condition fails:

Hypothesis / Condition Domain / Interval Necessity What Fails If Condition Dropped
Field Axioms (Algebraic Structure) Global on \mathbb{R} Strictly Mandatory / Necessary Failure of closure, associativity, commutativity, or existence of inverses leads to algebraic inconsistency.
Order Axioms Global on \mathbb{R} Strictly Mandatory Inability to define inequalities, positivity, and monotonicity; complex numbers \mathbb{C} lack a natural order.
Completeness Axiom (Lub Property) Bounded Subsets Essential for analysis In \mathbb{Q}$, the set $\{x \in \mathbb{Q} : x^2 < 2}$ is bounded above but lacks a supremum in \mathbb{Q}$, causing analysis to fail.

Pillar 3 High-Yield Exam Results & Direct Shortcuts

Standard 15-to-30 second shortcuts frequently tested in UP TGT/PGT and DSSSB:

01

Shortcut 1: Supremum and Infimum of Shifted Sets

$$\sup(a + S) = a + \sup S, \quad \inf(aS) = a \inf S \text{ (for } a > 0\text{)}$$

Exam Rule: Constants add directly to bounds, and positive scalar multipliers scale bounds directly.

⚡ PYQ Context: Frequently asked in UP PGT to quickly evaluate bounds of translated sets.
02

Shortcut 2: Bounds for Negative Scaling

$$\sup(-S) = -\inf S, \quad \inf(-S) = -\sup S$$

Exam Rule: Negating a set flips the supremum and infimum and swaps their roles with a negative sign.

⚡ PYQ Context: Directly tested in DSSSB and KVS PGT exams for set manipulation.
03

Shortcut 3: Supremum of Union of Bounded Sets

$$\sup(A \cup B) = \max(\sup A, \sup B)$$

Exam Rule: The least upper bound of a union is simply the maximum of the individual suprema.

⚡ PYQ Context: Standard conceptual shortcut to bypass epsilon-limit proofs.
04

Shortcut 4: Infimum of Union of Bounded Sets

$$\inf(A \cup B) = \min(\inf A, \inf B)$$

Exam Rule: The greatest lower bound of a union is the minimum of the individual infima.

⚡ PYQ Context: Essential property tested in multiple-choice set-theoretic questions.
05

Shortcut 5: Archimedean Property Application

$$ \forall x \in \mathbb{R}, \exists n \in \mathbb{N} \text{ s.t. } n > x $$

Exam Rule: The set of natural numbers is unbounded above in \mathbb{R}.

⚡ PYQ Context: Used to eliminate bounds options for unbounded sequences/sets.
06

Shortcut 6: Density of Q and R \setminus Q

$$ \forall a, b \in \mathbb{R} \text{ with } a < b, \exists r \in \mathbb{Q}, s \in \mathbb{R}\setminus\mathbb{Q} \text{ s.t. } a < r < b, a < s < b $$

Exam Rule: Between any two real numbers, infinitely many rational and irrational numbers exist.

⚡ PYQ Context: Direct theory question in UP TGT/PGT examinations.

Pillar 4 Classic Exam Traps & Counterexamples

Where examiners construct tricky trap options and how to safeguard your marks:

⚠️ EXAM TRAP #01

Trap 1: Confusing Maximum with Supremum

Common Pitfall: Assuming that every bounded set above must contain its supremum (i.e., a maximum).

Counterexample: Set $S = (0, 1)$, where $\sup S = 1$, but $1 \notin S$.

Why it fails: The supremum of a set may lie outside the set itself if the set is open on the upper end.

🛡️ Exam Defense: Always check if the boundary point belongs to the set before declaring it a maximum.
⚠️ EXAM TRAP #02

Trap 2: Assuming Rational Completeness

Common Pitfall: Applying completeness arguments directly inside the field of rational numbers \mathbb{Q}.

Counterexample: Set $S = \{x \in \mathbb{Q} : x^2 < 2 \}$

Why it fails: Although bounded above in \mathbb{Q}$, its supremum $\sqrt{2}$ does not exist in \mathbb{Q}.

🛡️ Exam Defense: Remember that \mathbb{Q} is not complete; completeness is an exclusive axiom of \mathbb{R}.

Pillar 6 Solved Canonical Exam Problems (Dual-Method)

Comparing the formal academic proof against the rapid competitive exam elimination trick:

CANONICAL PROBLEM 01 Dual-Method Breakdown

Find the supremum of the set $S = \left\{ 1 - \frac{1}{n} : n \in \mathbb{N} \right}$.

(A) 0(B) 1(C) \infty(D) Does not exist
✓ Correct Answer: (B)
📚 Method A: Formal Textbook Derivation
Step 1: Observe elements $0, \frac{1}{2}, \frac{2}{3}, \dots$. Step 2: Clearly bounded above by 1. Step 3: For any $\epsilon > 0$, choose $n$ large enough such that $1 - \frac{1}{n} > 1 - \epsilon$. Thus 1 is the least upper bound.
⚡ Method B: 25-Second Exam Shortcut
Evaluate $\lim_{n \to \infin} \left(1 - \frac{1}{n}\right) = 1$, which gives the supremum for monotonic sequences.
💡 Key Takeaway: For standard sequence-generated sets, the limit as $n \to \infty$ often gives the supremum or infimum.
CANONICAL PROBLEM 02 Dual-Method Breakdown

If $A$ and $B$ are non-empty bounded subsets of \mathbb{R}$, what is $\sup(A + B)$ where $A + B = \{a + b : a \in A, b \in B}$?

(A) \sup A \cdot \sup B(B) \sup A - \sup B(C) \sup A + \sup B(D) \max(\sup A, \sup B)
✓ Correct Answer: (C)
📚 Method A: Formal Textbook Derivation
Let $s_1 = \sup A$ and $s_2 = \sup B$. For any $a \in A, b \in B$, $a + b \le s_1 + s_2$, so $s_1 + s_2$ is an upper bound for $A + B$. By approximating elements within $\epsilon$, equality is established.
⚡ Method B: 25-Second Exam Shortcut
The supremum of a sum of sets is the sum of their suprema: $\sup(A+B) = \sup A + \sup B$.
💡 Key Takeaway: Direct standard theorem statement frequently tested in UP PGT.

Q1
MCQ 1: Which of the following is true for the set of rational numbers \mathbb{Q}?
A It is a complete ordered field
B It satisfies the least upper bound property
C It is an ordered field but not complete
D It possesses no order relation
Derivation & Analysis:

\mathbb{Q} satisfies field and order axioms, but fails the completeness axiom (e.g., bounded sets like \{x \in \mathbb{Q} : x^2 < 2\} have no supremum in \mathbb{Q}).

Q2
MCQ 2: The infimum of the set $S = \left\{ \frac{1}{n} : n \in \mathbb{N} \right}$ is:
A 1
B 0
C \frac{1}{2}
D Does not exist
Derivation & Analysis:

The set consists of positive terms decreasing toward 0. Since 0 is a lower bound and any number greater than 0 fails to be a lower bound, $\inf S = 0$.

Q3
MCQ 3: If $S = (0, 1)$, then $\sup S - \inf S$ equals:
A 0
B 1
C -1
D \infty
Derivation & Analysis:

For $S = (0, 1)$, $\sup S = 1$ and $\inf S = 0$. Thus $\sup S - \inf S = 1 - 0 = 1$.

Q4
MCQ 4: The Archimedean property of \mathbb{R}$ implies that for any positive real numbers $x$ and $y$:
A \exists n \in \mathbb{N} \text{ such that } nx > y
B nx < y \forall n \in \mathbb{N}
C x > ny \forall n \in \mathbb{N}
D nx = y for some n
Derivation & Analysis:

By the Archimedean property, given any $y > 0$ and $x > 0$, we can always find a natural number $n$ large enough such that $nx > y$.

Q5
MCQ 5: If $A$ and $B$ are non-empty bounded subsets of \mathbb{R}$ such that $A \subseteq B$, then:
A \sup A \ge \sup B
B \sup A \le \sup B
C \inf A \le \inf B
D \sup A = \inf B
Derivation & Analysis:

Since every upper bound of $B$ is also an upper bound of $A$, the least upper bound of $A$ must be less than or equal to the least upper bound of $B$.

Q6
MCQ 6: Which of the following sets does not have a supremum in \mathbb{R}?
A \{x \in \mathbb{R} : x < 5\}
B \{x \in \mathbb{R} : x^2 < 4\}
C \{n \in \mathbb{N} : n < 100\}
D \{x \in \mathbb{R} : x > 0\}
Derivation & Analysis:

The set $\{x \in \mathbb{R} : x > 0\}$ is not bounded above in \mathbb{R}$, hence it does not possess a supremum in \mathbb{R} (or its supremum is considered $+\infty$).

Q7
MCQ 7: The supremum of the set $S = \left\{ (-1)^n \left(1 + \frac{1}{n}\right) : n \in \mathbb{N} \right}$ is:
A 1
B \frac{3}{2}
C 2
D Does not exist
Derivation & Analysis:

For odd $n$, terms are $-(1 + 1/n)$ approaching $-1$. For even $n$, terms are $1 + 1/n$, with the maximum at $n=2$ giving $1 + 1/2 = 3/2$. Thus the supremum is $3/2$.

Q8
MCQ 8: Between any two distinct real numbers, there exist:
A Only rational numbers
B Only irrational numbers
C Infinitely many rational and irrational numbers
D A finite number of real numbers
Derivation & Analysis:

Due to the density property of both \mathbb{Q}$ and \mathbb{R}\setminus\mathbb{Q}$ in \mathbb{R}$, infinitely many rational and irrational numbers lie between any two distinct reals.

Q9
MCQ 9: If $S$ is a non-empty bounded subset of \mathbb{R}$ and $c > 0$, let $cS = \{cs : s \in S\}$. Then $\inf(cS)$ is equal to:
A c \inf S
B \inf S
C c \sup S
D -c \inf S
Derivation & Analysis:

Multiplying a set by a positive scalar $c > 0$ scales all lower bounds and elements proportionally, so $\inf(cS) = c \inf S$.

Q10
MCQ 10: The greatest lower bound of the set $S = \left\{ \frac{n}{n+1} : n \in \mathbb{N} \right}$ is:
A 1
B 0
C \frac{1}{2}
D Does not exist
Derivation & Analysis:

For $n=1$, the element is $1/2$. As $n$ increases, $n/(n+1)$ increases toward 1. The smallest element is at $n=1$, which is $1/2$, hence $\inf S = 1/2$.

Pillar 8 Frequently Asked Questions

Completeness guarantees that there are no 'gaps' in the number line. Without it, standard calculus concepts like limits, continuity, derivatives, and integration would break down because Cauchy sequences and bounded monotonic sequences would fail to converge.

A supremum is the least upper bound, which may or may not belong to the set. A maximum is a supremum that strictly belongs to the set. For example, in $(0, 1)$, the supremum is 1, but the maximum does not exist.

Typically, 2 to 4 direct or indirect questions appear, focusing on bounds of sets, supremum/infimum properties, and field/order axioms.

Evaluate the first few terms to check monotonicity and find the limit as $n \to \infty$. For monotonic sequences, the limit often gives the supremum or infimum.
