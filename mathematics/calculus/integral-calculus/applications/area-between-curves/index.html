Calculus

Area between curves

The computation of the area between curves relies on definite integration by partitioning regions into infinitesimal vertical or horizontal strips. Mastery of intersection points, curve sketching, and proper choice of integration variables (with respect to $x$ or $y$) forms a high-scoring, recurring pillar in UP TGT/PGT, DSSSB, and KVS examinations.

Target Exams: UP PGT Mathematics UP TGT Mathematics DSSSB PGT / TGT KVS / NVS PGT LT Grade
📖 Self-Study Mastery Status
○ Mark Topic as Mastered

Pillar 1 Rigorous Definition & Mathematical Statement

Fundamental Principle of Area Between Curves
Let $f(x)$ and $g(x)$ be continuous functions on the closed interval $[a, b]$ such that $f(x) \ge g(x)$ for all $x \in [a, b]$. The net area $A$ of the region bounded by the curves $y = f(x)$, $y = g(x)$, and the lines $x = a$ and $x = b$ is given by $$A = \int_{a}^{b} [f(x) - g(x)]\,dx$$.

📐 Geometric Interpretation

Geometrically, the integral accumulates the areas of infinitesimal vertical rectangles of height $f(x) - g(x)$ and width $dx$ from $x = a$ to $x = b$. If the curves intersect within $[a, b]$, the interval must be partitioned at the intersection roots where $f(x) = g(x)$, integrating $|f(x) - g(x)|$ across sub-intervals.

⚙️ Algebraic & Structural Meaning

Algebraically, finding the exact boundaries $a$ and $b$ requires solving the equation $f(x) = g(x)$ to determine the abscissae of the points of intersection. The absolute difference ensures non-negativity of the calculated geometric measure.

Pillar 2 Hypotheses & Condition Checklist

Every hypothesis is strictly non-negotiable. Inspect the domain type and the exact consequence if a condition fails:

Hypothesis / Condition Domain / Interval Necessity What Fails If Condition Dropped
Continuity of bounding functions [a, b] (Closed interval) Strictly Mandatory / Necessary If either function possesses a jump or infinite discontinuity, the Riemann integral fails to exist in the standard sense, rendering the net area undefined or requiring improper integration techniques.
Non-negativity of integrand magnitude [a, b] Strictly Mandatory Ignoring intersection points where the upper and lower curves switch roles results in negative cancellation, yielding signed area instead of total geometric area.
Finiteness of integration limits Finite bounds $a$ and $b$ Essential for conclusion Unbounded domains lead to improper integrals that may diverge, yielding infinite areas unless evaluated via limits.

Pillar 3 High-Yield Exam Results & Direct Shortcuts

Standard 15-to-30 second shortcuts frequently tested in UP TGT/PGT and DSSSB:

01

Shortcut 1: Area Bounded by a Parabola and a Line

$$A = \frac{(beta - \alpha)^3}{6a}$$

Exam Rule: For the area bounded by a parabola $y^2 = 4ax$ (or $y = ax^2 + bx + c$) and a line intersecting it at two points with abscissae $\alpha$ and $\beta$, the area is given directly by the cubic difference formula divided by $6a$ (where $a$ is the leading coefficient).

⚡ PYQ Context: Directly applied in UP PGT and TGT exams to bypass lengthy expansion and integration steps for parabolas and chords.
02

Shortcut 2: Archimedes' Parabola Theorem

$$A = \frac{2}{3} \times (base \times height)$$

Exam Rule: The area enclosed by a parabola and any chord is exactly two-thirds of the area of the circumscribed parallelogram formed by the chord and the tangent parallel to the chord.

⚡ PYQ Context: Frequently tested in KVS and NVS PGT mathematics for rapid visual calculation.
03

Shortcut 3: Area of Standard Ellipse

$$A = \pi ab$$

Exam Rule: The total area enclosed by the ellipse $\frac{x^2}{a^2} + \frac{y^2}{b^2} = 1$ is $\pi ab$. For shifted ellipses, the area remains invariant under translation.

⚡ PYQ Context: Standard baseline question appearing as a direct formula or preliminary step in composite curve areas.
04

Shortcut 4: Area Between Two Parabolas $y^2 = 4ax$ and $x^2 = 4by$

$$A = \frac{16ab}{3}$$

Exam Rule: The region enclosed by symmetric standard parabolas $y^2 = 4ax$ and $x^2 = 4by$ has a fixed area ratio calculated directly from parameters $a$ and $b$.

⚡ PYQ Context: Directly featured in DSSSB PGT Mathematics multiple-choice questions.
05

Shortcut 5: Symmetry Exploitation (Even/Odd Functions)

$$ \int_{-a}^{a} f(x)\,dx = 2 \int_{0}^{a} f(x)\,dx \text{ (if even)} $$

Exam Rule: When calculating areas bounded symmetrically about the $y$-axis, compute the integral from $0$ to $a$ and multiply by 2.

⚡ PYQ Context: Saves up to 45 seconds on trigonometric and polynomial curve bounding problems.
06

Shortcut 6: Integration with respect to $y$ ($dy$ strips)

$$A = \int_{c}^{d} [x_{right}(y) - x_{left}(y)]\,dy$$

Exam Rule: If curves are expressed more cleanly as functions of $y$ (e.g., parabolas opening left/right), integrate horizontally with respect to $y$ to avoid splitting into multiple $x$-integrals.

⚡ PYQ Context: Essential strategy to eliminate algebraic complexity in composite regions.

Pillar 4 Classic Exam Traps & Counterexamples

Where examiners construct tricky trap options and how to safeguard your marks:

⚠️ EXAM TRAP #01

Trap 1: Intersection Blindness (Crossing Curves)

Common Pitfall: Integrating $\int_a^b (f(x) - g(x))\,dx$ without checking if $f(x)$ and $g(x)$ cross each other inside $[a, b]$.

Counterexample: $y = \sin x$ and $y = \cos x$ on $[0, \pi/2]$

Why it fails: At $x = \pi/4$, $\sin x = \cos x$. Integrating without splitting gives a net signed area of zero instead of the true geometric area.

🛡️ Exam Defense: Always solve $f(x) = g(x)$ across the domain to locate interior intersection nodes and split integrals accordingly.
⚠️ EXAM TRAP #02

Trap 2: Confusing Vertical and Horizontal Strips

Common Pitfall: Forcing $dx$ integration on curves defined naturally as $x = f(y)$, leading to difficult square roots and multiple piecewise integrals.

Counterexample: $y^2 = x$ and $y = x - 2$

Why it fails: Using $dx$ requires splitting the region into two parts because the lower boundary of the region changes from the lower branch of the parabola to the upper branch. Using $dy$ unifies it into a single integral.

🛡️ Exam Defense: Examine whether $x$ is easily expressed as a function of $y$. If yes, integrate with respect to $y$ ($dy$).

Pillar 6 Solved Canonical Exam Problems (Dual-Method)

Comparing the formal academic proof against the rapid competitive exam elimination trick:

CANONICAL PROBLEM 01 Dual-Method Breakdown

Find the area of the region bounded by the parabola $y^2 = 4x$ and the line $y = 2x$.

(A) $\frac{1}{3}$ sq. units(B) $\frac{2}{3}$ sq. units(C) $\frac{4}{3}$ sq. units(D) $\frac{8}{3}$ sq. units
✓ Correct Answer: (A)
📚 Method A: Formal Textbook Derivation
Step 1: Find intersection points by substituting $y = 2x$ into $y^2 = 4x$: $(2x)^2 = 4x \implies 4x^2 - 4x = 0 \implies x = 0, 1$. Intersection points are $(0,0)$ and $(1,2)$. Step 2: Set up the integral with respect to $x$: $A = \int_{0}^{1} (2\sqrt{x} - 2x)\,dx$. Step 3: Integrate: $\left[ 2 \cdot \frac{2}{3}x^{3/2} - x^2 \right]_0^1 = \frac{4}{3} - 1 = \frac{1}{3}$.
⚡ Method B: 25-Second Exam Shortcut
Use Shortcut 1 for $y^2 = 4ax$ and $y = mx$: Here $4a = 4 \implies a = 1$. The line is $y = 2x \implies m = 2$. The area formula for $y^2 = 4ax$ and $y = mx$ is $A = \frac{8a^2}{3m^3} = \frac{8(1)^2}{3(2)^3} = \frac{8}{24} = \frac{1}{3}$.
💡 Key Takeaway: Memorize standard parameter formulas for parabola-line intersections to solve within 20 seconds.
CANONICAL PROBLEM 02 Dual-Method Breakdown

The area of the region bounded by the curve $y = x^3$ and the lines $y = x$ for $x \ge 0$ is:

(A) $\frac{1}{2}$(B) $\frac{1}{4}$(C) $\frac{1}{8}$(D) $\frac{1}{3}$
✓ Correct Answer: (B)
📚 Method A: Formal Textbook Derivation
Step 1: Find intersection points: $x^3 = x \implies x(x^2 - 1) = 0 \implies x = -1, 0, 1$. Given $x \ge 0$, integration limits are $0$ to $1$. Step 2: Check upper curve on $[0,1]$: For $x = 0.5$, $x = 0.5$ and $x^3 = 0.125$, so line $y = x$ is above $y = x^3$. Step 3: Compute integral: $A = \int_{0}^{1} (x - x^3)\,dx = \left[ \frac{x^2}{2} - \frac{x^4}{4} \right]_0^1 = \frac{1}{2} - \frac{1}{4} = \frac{1}{4}$.
⚡ Method B: 25-Second Exam Shortcut
Recognize polynomial intersection bounds. For $y = x^n$ and $y = x$, area between $0$ and $1$ is $\frac{1}{2} - \frac{1}{n+1} = \frac{1}{2} - \frac{1}{4} = \frac{1}{4}$.
💡 Key Takeaway: Pay close attention to domain restrictions like $x \ge 0$ to avoid integrating over negative regions unnecessarily.

Q1
MCQ 1: The area bounded by the curve $y = x^2$ and the line $y = 4$ is:
A (A) $\frac{32}{3}$
B (B) $\frac{16}{3}$
C (C) $\frac{8}{3}$
D (D) $\frac{64}{3}$
Derivation & Analysis:

The parabola $y = x^2$ intersects $y = 4$ at $x = -2$ and $x = 2$. By symmetry, $A = 2 \int_{0}^{2} (4 - x^2)\,dx = 2 \left[ 4x - \frac{x^3}{3} \right]_0^2 = 2 \left( 8 - \frac{8}{3} \right) = 2 \left( \frac{16}{3} \right) = \frac{32}{3}$.

Q2
MCQ 2: What is the area of the circle $x^2 + y^2 = a^2$?
A (A) $2\pi a^2$
B (B) $\pi a^2$
C (C) $\frac{1}{2}\pi a^2$
D (D) $\pi a$
Derivation & Analysis:

Using symmetry across four quadrants, $A = 4 \int_{0}^{a} \sqrt{a^2 - x^2}\,dx = 4 \left[ \frac{x}{2}\sqrt{a^2-x^2} + \frac{a^2}{2}\sin^{-1}\left(\frac{x}{a}\right) \right]_0^a = 4 \left( \frac{a^2}{2} \cdot \frac{\pi}{2} \right) = \pi a^2$.

Q3
MCQ 3: The area enclosed by the ellipse $\frac{x^2}{9} + \frac{y^2}{4} = 1$ is:
A (A) $6\pi$
B (B) $12\pi$
C (C) $3\pi$
D (D) $36\pi$
Derivation & Analysis:

Using the standard ellipse area formula $A = \pi ab$, where $a = 3$ and $b = 2$, we get $A = \pi(3)(2) = 6\pi$.

Q4
MCQ 4: The area of the region bounded by $y = \sin x$, $x = 0$, and $x = 2\pi$ is:
A (A) $0$
B (B) $2$
C (C) $4$
D (D) $2\pi$
Derivation & Analysis:

Sine wave area over one period $[0, \pi]$ is $2$, and over $[\pi, 2\pi]$ is $2$ (taking absolute value). Thus total geometric area is $\int_{0}^{\pi} \sin x\,dx + \int_{\pi}^{2\pi} -\sin x\,dx = 2 + 2 = 4$.

MCQ 5: The area of the region bounded by $y^2 = 9x$ and $y = 3x$ is:
A (A) $\frac{1}{2}$
B (B) $\frac{1}{3}$
C (C) $\frac{1}{4}$
D (D) $\frac{1}{6}$

Here $4a = 9 \implies a = 9/4$, and line slope $m = 3$. Limits $0$ to $1$. $A = \int_0^1 (3\sqrt{x} - 3x)\,dx = 3 [\frac{2}{3}x^{3/2} - \frac{x^2}{2}]_0^1 = 3(\frac{2}{3} - \frac{1}{2}) = 3(\frac{1}{6}) = \frac{1}{2}$. Q6
MCQ 6: The area bounded by the curves $y = \ln x$, $x = 1$, $x = e$, and the $x$-axis is:
A (A) $1$
B (B) $e - 1$
C (C) $e$
D (D) $1 - e$
Derivation & Analysis:

Area $A = \int_{1}^{e} \ln x\,dx = [x\ln x - x]_1^e = (e\ln e - e) - (1\ln 1 - 1) = (e - e) - (0 - 1) = 1$.

Q7
MCQ 7: The area of the region bounded by $|x| + |y| = 1$ is:
A (A) $1$
B (B) $2$
C (C) $\sqrt{2}$
D (D) $4$
Derivation & Analysis:

The equation $|x| + |y| = 1$ represents a square rotated by $45^\circ$ with vertices $(1,0), (0,1), (-1,0), (0,-1)$. The diagonals have lengths $d_1 = 2$ and $d_2 = 2$. Area of square = $\frac{1}{2} d_1 d_2 = \frac{1}{2}(2)(2) = 2$. Alternatively, $4 \times \text{Area in 1st quadrant} = 4 \int_0^1 (1-x)\,dx = 4(1 - 1/2) = 2$.

Q8
MCQ 8: The area bounded by the parabola $x^2 = 4y$ and the line $x = 4y - 2$ is:
A (A) $\frac{9}{8}$
B (B) $\frac{9}{4}$
C (C) $\frac{3}{4}$
D (D) $\frac{9}{2}$
Derivation & Analysis:

Rewrite line as $4y = x + 2$. Substitute $4y$ into parabola: $x^2 = x + 2 \implies x^2 - x - 2 = 0 \implies (x-2)(x+1) = 0 \implies x = -1, 2$. Using integration with respect to $x$: $A = \int_{-1}^{2} \left( \frac{x+2}{4} - \frac{x^2}{4} \right)\,dx = \frac{1}{4} \int_{-1}^{2} (2 + x - x^2)\,dx = \frac{1}{4} \left[ 2x + \frac{x^2}{2} - \frac{x^3}{3} \right]_{-1}^2 = \frac{1}{4} \left( \frac{9}{2} \right) = \frac{9}{8}$.

Q9
MCQ 9: The area of the region bounded by $y = |x|-1$ and $y = 1-|x|$ is:
A (A) $1$
B (B) $2$
C (C) $4$
D (D) $8$
Derivation & Analysis:

The curves form a rhombus with vertices $(0,1), (1,0), (0,-1), (-1,0)$. The diagonals are along the coordinate axes with lengths $2$ and $2$. Area = $\frac{1}{2} \times 2 \times 2 = 2$.

Q10
MCQ 10: The area enclosed by the astroid $x^{2/3} + y^{2/3} = a^{2/3}$ is:
A (A) $\frac{3}{8}\pi a^2$
B (B) $\frac{3}{4}\pi a^2$
C (C) $\frac{3}{8}\pi^2 a^2$
D (D) $\frac{3}{8} a^2$
Derivation & Analysis:

By parametric integration with $x = a\cos^3\theta, y = a\sin^3\theta$, the total area is $4 \int_0^{\pi/2} y \frac{dx}{d\theta}\,d\theta = 4 \int_0^{\pi/2} (a\sin^3\theta)(3a\cos^2\theta(-\sin\theta))\,d\theta = \frac{3}{8}\pi a^2$.

Use Shortcut 1 for $y^2 = 4ax$ and $y = mx$: Here $4a = 4 \implies a = 1$. The line is $y = 2x \implies m = 2$. The area formula for $y^2 = 4ax$ and $y = mx$ is $A = \frac{8a^2}{3m^3} = \frac{8(1)^2}{3(2)^3} = \frac{8}{24} = \frac{1}{3}$.
💡 Key Takeaway: Memorize standard parameter formulas for parabola-line intersections to solve within 20 seconds.
CANONICAL PROBLEM 02 Dual-Method Breakdown

The area of the region bounded by the curve $y = x^3$ and the lines $y = x$ for $x \ge 0$ is:

(A) $\frac{1}{2}$(B) $\frac{1}{4}$(C) $\frac{1}{8}$(D) $\frac{1}{3}$
✓ Correct Answer: (B)
📚 Method A: Formal Textbook Derivation
Step 1: Find intersection points: $x^3 = x \implies x(x^2 - 1) = 0 \implies x = -1, 0, 1$. Given $x \ge 0$, integration limits are $0$ to $1$. Step 2: Check upper curve on $[0,1]$: For $x = 0.5$, $x = 0.5$ and $x^3 = 0.125$, so line $y = x$ is above $y = x^3$. Step 3: Compute integral: $A = \int_{0}^{1} (x - x^3)\,dx = \left[ \frac{x^2}{2} - \frac{x^4}{4} \right]_0^1 = \frac{1}{2} - \frac{1}{4} = \frac{1}{4}$.
⚡ Method B: 25-Second Exam Shortcut
Recognize polynomial intersection bounds. For $y = x^n$ and $y = x$, area between $0$ and $1$ is $\frac{1}{2} - \frac{1}{n+1} = \frac{1}{2} - \frac{1}{4} = \frac{1}{4}$.
💡 Key Takeaway: Pay close attention to domain restrictions like $x \ge 0$ to avoid integrating over negative regions unnecessarily.
