Volume of cylinder, cone and sphere by integration

The calculation of solid volumes via definite integration relies on the slicing method (Disk and Washer methods) rooted in Riemann sums. Mastery of these surface-of-revolution formulas is essential for securing high scores in UP TGT/PGT, KVS, and DSSSB examinations where rotational geometry frequently appears.

Pillar 1 Rigorous Definition & Mathematical Statement

Disk Method for Volume of Revolution
Let $f$ be continuous and non-negative on $[a, b]$. The volume $V$ of the solid generated by revolving the region bounded by $y = f(x)$, $y = 0$, $x = a$, and $x = b$ about the $x$-axis is given by $$V = \pi \int_{a}^{b} [f(x)]^2 \, dx$$.

📐 Geometric Interpretation

The solid is approximated by an infinite stack of thin cylindrical disks of radius $r = f(x)$ and infinitesimal thickness $\Delta x$. As $\Delta x \to 0$, the Riemann sum converges to the exact definite integral of the cross-sectional area $A(x) = \pi [f(x)]^2$.

⚙️ Algebraic & Structural Meaning

Translates a geometric limit problem into a standard single-variable Riemann integrable function, enabling exact evaluation via the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus.

Pillar 2 Hypotheses & Condition Checklist

Every hypothesis is strictly non-negotiable. Inspect the domain type and the exact consequence if a condition fails:

Hypothesis / Condition Domain / Interval Necessity What Fails If Condition Dropped
Continuity of generator function $f(x) [a, b] (Closed interval) Strictly Mandatory / Necessary If $f(x)$ is discontinuous, Riemann integrability of $[f(x)]^2$ may fail, rendering the area and volume undefined.
Non-negativity $f(x) \ge 0$ [a, b] (Closed interval) Strictly Mandatory for radius interpretation If $f(x)$ oscillates between positive and negative values without squaring, the net integral would cancel out, misrepresenting physical volume.
Rectifiability / Smoothness of boundaries (a, b) (Open interval) Essential for well-behaved solids Pathological boundaries (like non-measurable sets) prevent standard Riemann integration.

Pillar 3 High-Yield Exam Results & Direct Shortcuts

Standard 15-to-30 second shortcuts frequently tested in UP TGT/PGT and DSSSB:

01

Shortcut 1: Direct Volume of a Right Circular Cone

$$V = \frac{1}{3} \pi r^2 h$$

Exam Rule: Generated by revolving the line $y = \frac{r}{h}x$ from $x = 0$ to $x = h$. Memorize the $\frac{1}{3}$ factor to skip integration in standard cone problems.

⚡ PYQ Context: Frequently tested in UP PGT/TGT for quick verification or combined solid problems.
02

Shortcut 2: Direct Volume of a Sphere

$$V = \frac{4}{3} \pi R^3$$

Exam Rule: Generated by revolving the semicircle $y = \sqrt{R^2 - x^2}$ from $x = -R$ to $x = R$. Use symmetry: $2 \pi \int_{0}^{R} (R^2 - x^2) \, dx$.

⚡ PYQ Context: Standard baseline question in all teaching recruitment exams (KVS, NVS, LT Grade).
03

Shortcut 3: Volume of a Cylinder

$$V = \pi r^2 h$$

Exam Rule: Generated by revolving the constant function $y = r$ from $x = 0$ to $x = h$.

⚡ PYQ Context: Base case tested in foundational calculus sets.
04

Shortcut 4: Pappus's Centroid Theorem (First Theorem)

$$V = 2\pi \bar{y} A$$

Exam Rule: The volume of a solid of revolution is equal to the product of the area $A$ of the generating region and the distance $2\pi \bar{y}$ traveled by its geometric centroid during a full rotation.

⚡ PYQ Context: Saves 2-3 minutes on complex composite regions in PGT exams.
05

Shortcut 5: Washer Method Quick Setup

$$V = \pi \int_{a}^{b} \left( [R(x)]^2 - [r(x)]^2 \right) \, dx$$

Exam Rule: For hollow solids (outer radius $R(x)$, inner radius $r(x)$), square outer and inner radii separately before subtracting.

⚡ PYQ Context: Appears in bounded region rotation problems (e.g., between a parabola and a line).
06

Shortcut 6: Parabolic Segment Volume (Archimedes' Formula)

$$V = \frac{1}{2} \text{(Cylinder Volume)}$$ or specific paraboloid bounds

Exam Rule: Volume of a paraboloid generated by $y^2 = 4ax$ up to $x = h$ is $V = 2 \pi a h^2$.

⚡ PYQ Context: Direct time-saver for UP PGT calculus questions.

Pillar 4 Classic Exam Traps & Counterexamples

Where examiners construct tricky trap options and how to safeguard your marks:

⚠️ EXAM TRAP #01

Trap 1: Squaring the Function vs. Squaring the Integral

Common Pitfall: Candidates mistakenly compute $\pi \left( \int f(x) \, dx \right)^2$ instead of the correct $\pi \int [f(x)]^2 \, dx$.

Counterexample: $f(x) = x$ on $[0, 1]$: $\left(\int_0^1 x dx\right)^2 = \frac{1}{4}$, whereas $\int_0^1 x^2 dx = \frac{1}{3}$.

Why it fails: The volume is the sum of disk volumes ($\pi r^2 \Delta x$), not the square of the accumulated area.

🛡️ Exam Defense: Always square the radius function inside the integrand before integrating.
⚠️ EXAM TRAP #02

Trap 2: Axis of Revolution Not the Coordinate Axis

Common Pitfall: Integrating with respect to $x$ when revolving about a line parallel to the $y$-axis, or failing to adjust radius for rotation about $y = k$ or $x = h$.

Counterexample: Revolving $y = \sqrt{x}$ about $y = 2$.

Why it fails: The radius is not $\sqrt{x}$, but $2 - \sqrt{x}$. Using $y$ directly leads to complete calculation failure.

🛡️ Exam Defense: Always draw a quick sketch and measure the perpendicular distance from the generating curve to the axis of rotation to establish the correct radius.

Pillar 6 Solved Canonical Exam Problems (Dual-Method)

Comparing the formal academic proof against the rapid competitive exam elimination trick:

CANONICAL PROBLEM 01 Dual-Method Breakdown

Find the volume of the solid generated by revolving the region bounded by $y = \sqrt{x}$, $y = 0$, and $x = 4$ about the $x$-axis.

(A) $4\pi$ cubic units(B) $8\pi$ cubic units(C) $16\pi$ cubic units(D) $2\pi$ cubic units
✓ Correct Answer: (B)
📚 Method A: Formal Textbook Derivation
Step 1: Identify radius $r(x) = \sqrt{x}$ and interval $[0, 4]$. Step 2: Apply Disk formula $V = \pi \int_{0}^{4} (\sqrt{x})^2 \, dx$. Step 3: Evaluate integral $\pi \int_{0}^{4} x \, dx = \pi \left[ \frac{x^2}{2} \right]_0^4 = \frac{16\pi}{2} = 8\pi$.
⚡ Method B: 25-Second Exam Shortcut
Recognize the parabola $y^2 = x$ up to $x=4$; volume of paraboloid slice $V = \frac{1}{2} \pi r^2 h = \frac{1}{2} \pi (2)^2 (4) = 8\pi$.
💡 Key Takeaway: Direct substitution test in UP TGT; ensure limits are properly squared.
CANONICAL PROBLEM 02 Dual-Method Breakdown

Find the volume of a sphere of radius $R$ generated by revolving the circle $x^2 + y^2 = R^2$ about the $x$-axis.

(A) $\frac{4}{3}\pi R^3$(B) $\frac{2}{3}\pi R^3$(C) $2\pi R^3$(D) $\frac{4}{5}\pi R^3$
✓ Correct Answer: (A)
📚 Method A: Formal Textbook Derivation
Step 1: Upper semicircle equation is $y = \sqrt{R^2 - x^2}$. Step 2: Set up integral $V = 2\pi \int_{0}^{R} (R^2 - x^2) \, dx$. Step 3: Integrate term by term: $2\pi \left[ R^2 x - \frac{x^3}{3} \right]_0^R = 2\pi \left( R^3 - \frac{R^3}{3} \right) = \frac{4}{3}\pi R^3$.
⚡ Method B: 25-Second Exam Shortcut
Standard derived result; recall $\frac{4}{3}\pi R^3$ instantly for time management.
💡 Key Takeaway: Memorize standard derivation steps as direct theoretical questions appear in PGT exams.

Q1
MCQ 1: The volume of the solid generated by revolving the region bounded by $y = x^2$ and $y = x$ about the $x$-axis is:
A (A) $\frac{\pi}{15}$
B (B) $\frac{2\pi}{15}$
C (C) $\frac{\pi}{3}$
D (D) $\frac{4\pi}{15}$
Derivation & Analysis:

Intersection points are $x = 0$ and $x = 1$. Using the washer method: $V = \pi \int_{0}^{1} \left(x^2 - (x^2)^2\right) dx = \pi \int_{0}^{1} (x^2 - x^4) dx = \pi \left[ \frac{x^3}{3} - \frac{x^5}{5} \right]_0^1 = \pi \left(\frac{1}{3} - \frac{1}{5}\right) = \frac{2\pi}{15}$.

Q2
MCQ 2: What is the volume of a cone generated by revolving the line $y = 3x$ from $x = 0$ to $x = 2$ about the $x$-axis?
A (A) $6\pi$
B (B) $12\pi$
C (C) $18\pi$
D (D) $24\pi$
Derivation & Analysis:

Using $V = \pi \int_{0}^{2} (3x)^2 dx = \pi \int_{0}^{2} 9x^2 dx = 9\pi \left[ \frac{x^3}{3} \right]_0^2 = 3\pi (8) = 24\pi$. Alternatively, radius $r = 6$, height $h = 2$, $V = \frac{1}{3}\pi r^2 h = \frac{1}{3}\pi (36)(2) = 24\pi$.

Q3
MCQ 3: The volume generated by revolving the curve $y = \sin x$ from $x = 0$ to $x = \pi$ about the $x$-axis is:
A (A) $\frac{\pi^2}{2}$
B (B) $\pi^2$
C (C) $\frac{\pi}{2}$
D (D) $2\pi^2$
Derivation & Analysis:

Using $V = \pi \int_{0}^{\pi} \sin^2 x \, dx = \pi \int_{0}^{\pi} \frac{1 - \cos(2x)}{2} dx = \frac{\pi}{2} \left[ x - \frac{\sin(2x)}{2} \right]_0^\pi = \frac{\pi^2}{2}$.

Q4
MCQ 4: The volume of the solid formed by revolving the region bounded by $y = e^x$, $y = 0$, $x = 0$, and $x = 1$ about the $x$-axis is:
A (A) $\pi(e^2 - 1)$
B (B) $\frac{\pi}{2}(e^2 - 1)$
C (C) $\pi e^2$
D (D) $\frac{\pi}{2} e^2$
Derivation & Analysis:

Using $V = \pi \int_{0}^{1} (e^x)^2 dx = \pi \int_{0}^{1} e^{2x} dx = \pi \left[ \frac{e^{2x}}{2} \right]_0^1 = \frac{\pi}{2}(e^2 - 1)$.

Q5
MCQ 5: If the region bounded by $y = \sqrt{x}$ and $y = x$ is rotated about the $x$-axis, the volume is:
A (A) $\frac{\pi}{6}$
B (B) $\frac{\pi}{3}$
C (C) $\frac{\pi}{2}$
D (D) $\frac{\pi}{5}$
Derivation & Analysis:

Intersection points are $0$ and $1$. $V = \pi \int_{0}^{1} \left((\sqrt{x})^2 - x^2\right) dx = \pi \int_{0}^{1} (x - x^2) dx = \pi \left[ \frac{x^2}{2} - \frac{x^3}{3} \right]_0^1 = \pi \left(\frac{1}{6}\right) = \frac{\pi}{6}$.

Q6
MCQ 6: The volume of the cylinder generated by revolving $y = r$ from $x = 0$ to $x = h$ about the $x$-axis is given by:
A (A) $\frac{1}{3}\pi r^2 h$
B (B) $\pi r^2 h$
C (C) $2\pi r^2 h$
D (D) $\frac{4}{3}\pi r^2 h$
Derivation & Analysis:

Using $V = \pi \int_{0}^{h} r^2 dx = \pi r^2 [x]_0^h = \pi r^2 h$.

Q7
MCQ 7: Which of the following integrals represents the volume of a sphere of radius $R$ generated by revolving $x^2 + y^2 = R^2$?
A (A) $\pi \int_{-R}^{R} (R^2 - x^2) dx$
B (B) $2\pi \int_{-R}^{R} \sqrt{R^2 - x^2} dx$
C (C) $\pi \int_{-R}^{R} \sqrt{R^2 - x^2} dx$
D (D) $2\pi \int_{0}^{R} \sqrt{R^2 - x^2} dx$
Derivation & Analysis:

By the disk method, $y^2 = R^2 - x^2$, so $V = \pi \int_{-R}^{R} (R^2 - x^2) dx$.

Q8
MCQ 8: Assertion (A): The volume of a solid of revolution can be negative if the curve lies below the $x$-axis. Reason (R): Integration accumulates signed areas.
A (A) Both (A) and (R) are true and (R) is the correct explanation of (A).
B (B) Both (A) and (R) are true but (R) is NOT the correct explanation.
C (C) (A) is true but (R) is false.
D (D) Both (A) and (R) are false.
Derivation & Analysis:

Since the radius function is squared ($[f(x)]^2 \ge 0$), the volume is always non-negative regardless of whether the curve lies above or below the $x$-axis. Thus both Assertion and Reason are false.

Q9
MCQ 9: The volume generated by revolving the ellipse $\frac{x^2}{a^2} + \frac{y^2}{b^2} = 1$ about the $x$-axis (forming a prolate/oblate spheroid) is:
A (A) $\frac{4}{3}\pi a b^2$
B (B) $\frac{4}{3}\pi a^2 b$
C (C) $\frac{4}{3}\pi a b$
D (D) $\pi a b^2$
Derivation & Analysis:

Using $y^2 = b^2 \left(1 - \frac{x^2}{a^2}\right)$, $V = 2\pi \int_{0}^{a} b^2 \left(1 - \frac{x^2}{a^2}\right) dx = 2\pi b^2 \left[ x - \frac{x^3}{3a^2} \right]_0^a = 2\pi b^2 \left(a - \frac{a}{3}\right) = \frac{4}{3}\pi a b^2$.

Q10
MCQ 10: If a solid is generated by revolving $y = \frac{1}{x}$ from $x = 1$ to $x = \infty$ about the $x$-axis (Gabriel's Horn), its volume is:
A (A) Infinite
B (B) $\pi$ cubic units
C (C) $2\pi$ cubic units
D (D) $\frac{\pi}{2}$
Derivation & Analysis:

Using improper integration: $V = \pi \int_{1}^{\infty} \frac{1}{x^2} dx = \lim_{t \to \infty} \pi \left[ -\frac{1}{x} \right]_1^t = \pi \left(0 - (-1)\right) = \pi$.

Pillar 8 Frequently Asked Questions

The disk method sums individual cylindrical disk volumes given by $\pi r^2 h$. Here, the radius $r$ is the height of the curve $f(x)$, and the thickness $h$ is the infinitesimal element $\dx$. Squaring ensures that negative function values contribute positively to physical volume.

The Disk method is used for solid figures generated by a single bounding curve touching or forming the axis of rotation (no internal hollow space). The Washer method is used when there is a gap or hole between the axis of rotation and the generating region, requiring the subtraction of the inner radius squared from the outer radius squared.

Typically 1 to 3 direct or application-based questions appear across the calculus section, often combining integration techniques with standard curves like parabolas, circles, and lines.

Always sketch the region roughly, clearly identify intersection points by equating curves, and verify whether the rotation is about the $x$-axis, $y$-axis, or a shifted line before setting up the integral limits.
