Differentiation of trigonometric functions

The differentiation of trigonometric functions forms the bedrock of differential calculus, establishing standard rate-of-change formulas for sine, cosine, tangent, and their reciprocals.

Definition and statement

First Principles & Standard Derivatives of Trigonometric Functions
Let $f(x) = \sin x$. Then $f'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{\sin(x+h) - \sin x}{h} = \cos x$ for all $x \in \mathbb{R}$. Similarly, $\frac{d}{dx}(\cos x) = -\sin x$, $\frac{d}{dx}(\tan x) = \sec^2 x$, $\frac{d}{dx}(\cot x) = -\csc^2 x$, $\frac{d}{dx}(\sec x) = \sec x \tan x$, and $\frac{d}{dx}(\csc x) = -\csc x \cot x$.

📐 Geometric Interpretation

The derivative represents the instantaneous slope of the tangent line to the trigonometric curve at any point $x$. For instance, the slope of $y = \sin x$ at $x = 0$ is $\cos(0) = 1$, matching the slope of the line $y = x$ near the origin.

⚙️ Algebraic & Structural Meaning

These formulas provide the analytical machinery required to solve higher-order differential equations, optimization problems involving periodic phenomena, and Taylor series expansions of transcendental functions.

Useful results and shortcuts

Use these identities and results when their conditions apply:

01

Chain Rule for Powers of Sine/Cosine

$$\frac{d}{dx}[\sin^n x] = n \sin^{n-1} x \cos x$$

Rule: Instantly bring down the power, reduce the exponent by 1, and multiply by the derivative of the inner function.

Application: Directly used in UP PGT/TGT questions asking for higher-order derivatives or tangents of power functions.
02

Logarithmic Differentiation for Product/Quotient Towers

$$y = [f(x)]^{g(x)} \implies \frac{1}{y} \frac{dy}{dx} = g'(x)\ln f(x) + g(x)\frac{f'(x)}{f(x)}$$

Rule: Take natural logarithms on both sides before differentiating functions of the form $(\sin x)^{\cos x}$.

Application: Standard pattern in DSSSB PGT Mathematics for functions raised to trigonometric powers.
03

Inverse Trigonometric Simplification

$$\frac{d}{dx}\left[\sin^{-1}\left(\frac{2x}{1+x^2}\right)\right] = \frac{2}{1+x^2} \quad (\text{for } |x| \le 1)$$

Rule: Substitute $x = \tan \theta$ to simplify inverse trigonometric expressions algebraically before differentiating.

Application: Extremely frequent in UP TGT/PGT to bypass lengthy chain rule calculations.
04

nth Derivative of Exponential-Trigonometric Products

$$y = e^{ax} \sin(bx + c) \implies y_n = (a^2 + b^2)^{n/2} e^{ax} \sin(bx + c + n \tan^{-1}\frac{b}{a})$$

Rule: Use the standard $n$-th derivative formula directly instead of repeated differentiation.

05

Parametric Trigonometric Differentiation

$$x = a(\theta - \sin \theta), \, y = a(1 - \cos \theta) \implies \frac{dy}{dx} = \tan\left(\frac{\theta}{2}\right)$$

Rule: Divide $\frac{dy}{d\theta}$ by $\frac{dx}{d\theta}$ and use half-angle identities to simplify instantly.

06

Rapid Elimination via Domain & Periodicity

$$f(x+T) = f(x) \implies f'(x+T) = f'(x)$$

Rule: If a function is periodic, its derivative shares the same or related periodicity. Eliminate options with incorrect signs or frequencies.

Common errors and counterexamples

These examples show how a conclusion can fail when a condition is missing:

⚠️ COMMON ERROR #01

Sign Error in Cofunction Derivatives

Common Pitfall: Forgetting the negative sign when differentiating cosine, cotangent, and cosecant functions (e.g., writing $\frac{d}{dx}(\cos x) = \sin x$).

Counterexample: \frac{d}{dx}(\cos x) = -\sin x \neq \sin x

Why it fails: Violates the fundamental limit definition where $\cos(x+h) - \cos x = -2\sin(x+h/2)\sin(h/2)$.

🛡️ Exam Defense: Remember: All derivatives of co-functions (starting with 'co-') carry a negative sign.
⚠️ COMMON ERROR #02

Ignoring Domain Restrictions in Inverse Trig Derivatives

Common Pitfall: Applying derivative formulas for inverse trigonometric functions outside their valid principal domains.

Counterexample: \frac{d}{dx}(\sin^{-1} x) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1-x^2}} \text{ only for } |x| < 1

Why it fails: At $x = \pm 1$, the denominator becomes zero, resulting in a vertical tangent where the derivative is undefined.

🛡️ Exam Defense: Always check boundary values of the argument before asserting differentiability.

Solved examples

Each problem includes a full solution and a shorter method:

CANONICAL PROBLEM 01 Dual-Method Breakdown

If $y = \sqrt{\sin x + \sqrt{\sin x + \sqrt{\sin x + \dots \to \infty}}}$, then $\frac{dy}{dx}$ is equal to:

(A) \frac{\cos x}{2y - 1}(B) \frac{\sin x}{2y - 1}(C) \frac{\cos x}{2y + 1}(D) \frac{-\cos x}{2y - 1}
✓ Correct Answer: (A)
📚 Method A: Formal Textbook Derivation
Step 1: Write $y = \sqrt{\sin x + y}$. Step 2: Square both sides to get $y^2 = \sin x + y$. Step 3: Differentiate implicitly with respect to $x$: $2y \frac{dy}{dx} = \cos x + \frac{dy}{dx}$. Step 4: Rearrange to get $\frac{dy}{dx}(2y - 1) = \cos x \implies \frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{\cos x}{2y - 1}$.
⚡ Method B: 25-Second Exam Shortcut
Use infinite series derivative formula: for $y = \sqrt{f(x) + y}$, $\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{f'(x)}{2y - 1}$.
💡 Tip: Recognize infinite nesting structures immediately to write the answer.
CANONICAL PROBLEM 02 Dual-Method Breakdown

If $y = \tan^{-1}\left(\frac{\cos x - \sin x}{\cos x + \sin x}\right)$, then $\frac{dy}{dx}$ is:

(A) 1(B) -1(C) \frac{1}{1+x^2}(D) \frac{-1}{1+x^2}
✓ Correct Answer: (B)
📚 Method A: Formal Textbook Derivation
Step 1: Divide numerator and denominator by $\cos x$ to get $\tan^{-1}\left(\frac{1 - \tan x}{1 + \tan x}\right)$. Step 2: Express as $\tan^{-1}\left(\tan(\frac{\pi}{4} - x)\right)$. Step 3: Simplify to $y = \frac{\pi}{4} - x$. Step 4: Differentiate to obtain $\frac{dy}{dx} = -1$.
⚡ Method B: 25-Second Exam Shortcut
Standard trigonometric reduction: $\frac{\cos x - \sin x}{\cos x + \sin x} = \tan(\frac{\pi}{4} - x)$.
💡 Tip: Look for linear combinations of sine and cosine inside inverse tangent; they always reduce to $\frac{\pi}{4} \pm x$.

Practice questions

Choose an answer to check it and see the solution:

Q1
MCQ 1: If $f(x) = \log_e(\tan x)$, then $f'\left(\frac{\pi}{4}\right)$ is equal to:
A 0
B 1
C 2
D \sqrt{2}
Derivation & Analysis:

Given $f(x) = \ln(\tan x)$. By chain rule, $f'(x) = \frac{1}{\tan x} \cdot \sec^2 x = \frac{\cos x}{\sin x} \cdot \frac{1}{\cos^2 x} = \frac{1}{\sin x \cos x} = \frac{2}{\sin 2x}$. At $x = \frac{\pi}{4}$, $f'(\frac{\pi}{4}) = \frac{2}{\sin(\pi/2)} = 2$.

Q2
MCQ 2: The derivative of $\sin(x^2)$ with respect to $x^2$ is:
A \cos(x^2)
B 2x \cos(x^2)
C -\cos(x^2)
D \sin(x^2)
Derivation & Analysis:

Let $u = x^2$. We need to find $\frac{d}{du}(\sin u) = \cos u = \cos(x^2)$. Alternatively, $\frac{d/dx(\sin(x^2))}{d/dx(x^2)} = \frac{2x \cos(x^2)}{2x} = \cos(x^2)$.

Q3
MCQ 3: If $y = \sec(\tan^{-1} x)$, then $\frac{dy}{dx}$ at $x = 1$ is:
A \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}
B \sqrt{2}
C 1
D 2
Derivation & Analysis:

Let $\theta = \tan^{-1} x$, so $\tan \theta = x$ and $\sec \theta = \sqrt{1 + \tan^2 \theta} = \sqrt{1 + x^2}$. Thus $y = \sqrt{1 + x^2}$. Differentiating with respect to $x$, $\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{x}{\sqrt{1+x^2}}$. At $x = 1$, $\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}$? Wait, $\sec(\theta) = \sqrt{1+x^2}$, derivative is $\frac{x}{\sqrt{1+x^2}}$. At $x=1$, value is $\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}$? Let us re-verify: $\frac{d}{dx}(\sec(\tan^{-1} x)) = \sec(\tan^{-1}x)\tan(\tan^{-1}x) \cdot \frac{1}{1+x^2} = \frac{\sqrt{1+x^2} \cdot x}{1+x^2} = \frac{x}{\sqrt{1+x^2}}$. At $x=1$, it is $\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}$. Correct option is (A).

Q4
MCQ 4: If $y = \sin^{-1} x + \cos^{-1} x$, then $\frac{dy}{dx}$ is equal to:
A \frac{2}{\sqrt{1-x^2}}
B 0
C 1
D -1
Derivation & Analysis:

We know that $\sin^{-1} x + \cos^{-1} x = \frac{\pi}{2}$ for all $x \in [-1, 1]$. Differentiating a constant gives $\frac{dy}{dx} = 0$.

Q5
MCQ 5: If $y = e^{\tan x}$, then $\frac{dy}{dx}$ is:
A e^{\tan x}
B e^{\tan x} \sec^2 x
C \sec^2 x
D e^{\tan x} \tan x
Derivation & Analysis:

By the chain rule, $\frac{dy}{dx} = e^{\tan x} \cdot \frac{d}{dx}(\tan x) = e^{\tan x} \sec^2 x$.

Q6
MCQ 6: The 10th derivative of $\cos x$ with respect to $x$ is:
A \cos x
B -\cos x
C \sin x
D -\sin x
Derivation & Analysis:

The derivatives of $\cos x$ cycle every 4 steps: $-\sin x, -\cos x, \sin x, \cos x$. For $n=10$, $10 = 4(2) + 2$, which corresponds to the second derivative, $-\cos x$.

Q7
MCQ 7: If $x = \theta + \sin \theta$ and $y = 1 - \cos \theta$, then $\frac{dy}{dx}$ at $\theta = \frac{\pi}{2}$ is:
A 0
B 1
C -1
D \frac{1}{2}
Derivation & Analysis:

$\frac{dy}{d\theta} = \sin \theta$, $\frac{dx}{d\theta} = 1 + \cos \theta$. Thus $\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{\sin \theta}{1 + \cos \theta} = \tan(\theta/2)$. At $\theta = \frac{\pi}{2}$, $\tan(\pi/4) = 1$.

Q8
MCQ 8: Assertion (A): $\frac{d}{dx}(\tan^{-1} x + \cot^{-1} x) = 0$. Reason (R): $\tan^{-1} x + \cot^{-1} x = \frac{\pi}{2}$ for all $x \in \mathbb{R}$.
A Both (A) and (R) are true and (R) is the correct explanation of (A)
B Both (A) and (R) are true but (R) is not the correct explanation of (A)
C (A) is true but (R) is false
D (A) is false but (R) is true
Derivation & Analysis:

The identity $\tan^{-1} x + \cot^{-1} x = \frac{\pi}{2}$ holds for all real $x$, and its derivative is identically zero.

Q9
MCQ 9: If $y = \sqrt{\frac{1 - \cos 2x}{1 + \cos 2x}}$, then $\frac{dy}{dx}$ is:
A \sec^2 x
B -\sec^2 x
C \csc^2 x
D 2\sec^2 x
Derivation & Analysis:

Using trigonometric identities, $\frac{1-\cos 2x}{1+\cos 2x} = \frac{2\sin^2 x}{2\cos^2 x} = \tan^2 x$. Thus $y = \sqrt{\tan^2 x} = |\tan x|$. For principal domain, $y = \tan x$, and $\frac{dy}{dx} = \sec^2 x$.

Q10
MCQ 10: If $y = \sin(\ln x)$, then $x^2 y_2 + x y_1$ is equal to:
A y
B -y
C 0
D 1
Derivation & Analysis:

Let $y = \sin(\ln x)$. $y_1 = \cos(\ln x) \cdot \frac{1}{x} \implies x y_1 = \cos(\ln x)$. Differentiating again: $x y_2 + y_1 = -\sin(\ln x) \cdot \frac{1}{x} \implies x^2 y_2 + x y_1 = -\sin(\ln x) = -y$.

Common questions

The negative sign arises in co-functions (like $\cos^{-1} x$, $\cot^{-1} x$, and $\csc^{-1} x$) due to the strictly decreasing nature of these inverse functions over their principal domains, reflecting a negative instantaneous rate of change.

Forgetting to apply the Chain Rule to the interior argument (e.g., writing $\frac{d}{dx}(\sin(2x)) = \cos(2x)$ instead of $2\cos(2x)$).

Mastering standard trigonometric identities to simplify expressions before differentiation (e.g., half-angle substitutions) bypasses cumbersome quotient and product rules entirely.
