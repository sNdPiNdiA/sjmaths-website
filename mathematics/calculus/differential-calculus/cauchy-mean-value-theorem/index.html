Cauchy mean value theorem

Cauchy's Mean Value Theorem generalizes Lagrange's theorem by accommodating two functions, providing a fundamental tool for evaluating indeterminate forms via L'Hôpital's rule. Mastery of its parametric form and hypotheses is a high-yield scoring area in UP PGT/TGT, KVS, and DSSSB examinations.

Pillar 1 Rigorous Definition & Mathematical Statement

Cauchy's Generalized Mean Value Theorem
Let $f$ and $g$ be two real-valued functions defined on the closed interval $[a, b]$ such that: (1) $f$ and $g$ are continuous on $[a, b]$; (2) $f$ and $g$ are differentiable on $(a, b)$; (3) $g'(x) \neq 0$ for all $x \in (a, b)$. Then there exists at least one real number $c \in (a, b)$ such that $$\frac{f'(c)}{g'(c)} = \frac{f(b) - f(a)}{g(b) - g(a)}$$.

📐 Geometric Interpretation

Parametrically, it represents the slope ratio of secant to tangent vectors on curves defined by $(g(t), f(t))$, ensuring a point where the tangent vector is parallel to the chord joining the endpoints.

⚙️ Algebraic & Structural Meaning

Serves as the rigorous backbone for establishing L'Hôpital's rule and proving Taylor's theorem with Cauchy's form of remainder.

Pillar 2 Hypotheses & Condition Checklist

Every hypothesis is strictly non-negotiable. Inspect the domain type and the exact consequence if a condition fails:

Hypothesis / Condition Domain / Interval Necessity What Fails If Condition Dropped
Continuity on $[a, b] [a, b] (Closed interval) Strictly Mandatory / Necessary If either function has a jump or infinite discontinuity in $[a, b]$, the quotient ratio of increments may not be bounded or attained.
Differentiability on $(a, b) (a, b) (Open interval) Strictly Mandatory Failure of differentiability (e.g., sharp corners) implies $f'(c)$ or $g'(c)$ does not exist, destroying the relation.
Non-vanishing derivative $g'(x) \neq 0$ on $(a, b) (a, b) (Open interval) Essential to prevent division by zero If $g'(x) = 0$ at some point, the denominator $g(b) - g(a)$ might be zero, or the fraction $\frac{f'(c)}{g'(c)}$ becomes undefined.

Pillar 4 Classic Exam Traps & Counterexamples

Where examiners construct tricky trap options and how to safeguard your marks:

⚠️ EXAM TRAP #01

Trap 1: Division by Zero in $g(b) - g(a) = 0$

Common Pitfall: Applying Cauchy's theorem when $g(b) = g(a)$, which violates Rolle's theorem application to $g$, causing $g'(c) = 0$ for some $c$.

Counterexample: $f(x) = x^2, g(x) = x^3 - 3x$ on $[-1, 1]$ where $g(-1) = g(1) = 2$

Why it fails: If $g(a) = g(b)$, Rolle's theorem implies $g'(c) = 0$ for some $c \in (a, b)$, rendering the Cauchy quotient $\frac{f'(c)}{g'(c)}$ undefined or meaningless.

🛡️ Exam Defense: Always check that $g(b) \neq g(a)$ before setting up Cauchy's formula.
⚠️ EXAM TRAP #02

Trap 2: Assuming $g'(x)$ can be zero anywhere in $(a, b)$

Common Pitfall: Forgetting that $g'(x) \neq 0$ must hold strictly across the entire open interval $(a, b)$.

Counterexample: $g(x) = x^3$ on $[-1, 1]$, where $g'(0) = 0$

Why it fails: At $x = 0$, $g'(0) = 0$, which violates the denominator condition of Cauchy's theorem.

🛡️ Exam Defense: Verify $g'(x) = 0$ has no roots in $(a, b)$.

CANONICAL PROBLEM 01 Dual-Method Breakdown

Find the value of $c$ guaranteed by Cauchy's Mean Value Theorem for functions $f(x) = e^x$ and $g(x) = e^{-x}$ on the interval $[0, 1].$

(A) $\frac{1}{2} \ln \left(\frac{e-1}{e}\right)$(B) $\frac{1}{2}$(C) $\ln \left(\frac{e+1}{2}\right)$(D) $\frac{1}{2} \ln \left(\frac{e^2-1}{2e}\right)$
✓ Correct Answer: (B)
📚 Method A: Formal Textbook Derivation
Step 1: Calculate $f(0) = 1, f(1) = e$ and $g(0) = 1, g(1) = e^{-1}$. Step 2: Compute derivatives $f'(x) = e^x, g'(x) = -e^{-x}$. Step 3: Apply Cauchy's formula: $\frac{e^c}{-e^{-c}} = \frac{e - 1}{e^{-1} - 1}$. Step 4: Simplify $-e^{2c} = \frac{e-1}{(1-e)/e} = -e$, giving $e^{2c} = e \implies 2c = 1 \implies c = 1/2$.
⚡ Method B: 25-Second Exam Shortcut
Recognize exponential symmetry: $c$ is the midpoint $\frac{0+1}{2} = \frac{1}{2}$ for reciprocal exponential structures.
💡 Key Takeaway: Do not expand blindly; look for algebraic cancellation in exponential ratios.
CANONICAL PROBLEM 02 Dual-Method Breakdown

For $f(x) = x^2$ and $g(x) = x^3$ on the interval $[1, 2]$, the value of $c$ given by Cauchy's Mean Value Theorem is:

(A) $\frac{7}{9}$(B) $\frac{14}{9}$(C) $\frac{28}{9}$(D) $\frac{7}{3}$
✓ Correct Answer: (B)
📚 Method A: Formal Textbook Derivation
Step 1: $f(1)=1, f(2)=4 \implies f(2)-f(1)=3$. $g(1)=1, g(2)=8 \implies g(2)-g(1)=7$. Step 2: $f'(x)=2x, g'(x)=3x^2$. Step 3: $\frac{2c}{3c^2} = \frac{3}{7} \implies \frac{2}{3c} = \frac{3}{7}$. Step 4: $9c = 14 \implies c = \frac{14}{9}$. Verify $1 < \frac{14}{9} < 2$.
⚡ Method B: 25-Second Exam Shortcut
Equate ratio of derivatives to secant slope: $\frac{2}{3c} = \frac{4-1}{8-1} = \frac{3}{7} \implies c = \frac{14}{9}.
💡 Key Takeaway: Always check that the derived value of $c$ lies strictly inside $[1, 2]$; $\frac{14}{9} \approx 1.555$ is valid.

$\frac{f(e)-f(1)}{g(e)-g(1)} = \frac{1-0}{e-1} = \frac{1}{e-1}$. Derivative ratio: $\frac{1/c}{1} = \frac{1}{c}$. Thus $\frac{1}{c} = \frac{1}{e-1} \implies c = e - 1$.

MCQ 7: If $f(x) = e^x$ and $g(x) = e^{2x}$ on $[0, 1]$, the value of $c$ satisfying Cauchy's theorem is:

Notice $g(x) = (f(x))^2$. The denominator $g(1) - g(0) = e^2 - 1$ and $f(1)-f(0) = e-1$. Derivative ratio $\frac{e^c}{2e^{2c}} = \frac{1}{2e^c}$. Secant ratio $\frac{e-1}{e^2-1} = \frac{1}{e+1}$. D D) $f(x) = g(x)$
Derivation & Analysis:

If $g(x) = x$, then $g'(x) = 1$ and $g(b) - g(a) = b - a$, transforming Cauchy's formula directly into Lagrange's MVT formula.

Q2
MCQ 2: For $f(x) = \sin x$ and $g(x) = \cos x$ on $[0, \pi/2]$, the value of $c$ by Cauchy's theorem is:
A A) $\frac{\pi}{4}$
B B) $\frac{\pi}{3}$
C C) $\frac{\pi}{6}$
D D) $\frac{\pi}{2}$
Derivation & Analysis:

Here $f(0)=0, f(\pi/2)=1$ and $g(0)=1, g(\pi/2)=0$. Thus $\frac{f(b)-f(a)}{g(b)-g(a)} = \frac{1-0}{0-1} = -1$. Derivatives give $\frac{\cos c}{-\sin c} = -1 \implies \cot c = 1 \implies c = \pi/4$.

Q3
MCQ 3: Which of the following is a necessary condition for Cauchy's Mean Value Theorem on $[a, b]$?
A A) $f'(x)$ must be continuous on $[a, b]$
B B) $g'(x) \neq 0$ for any $x \in (a, b)$
C C) $f(a) = f(b)$
D D) $g(a) = g(b)$
Derivation & Analysis:

Condition $g'(x) \neq 0$ on $(a, b)$ is strictly required to avoid division by zero in $\frac{f'(c)}{g'(c)}$.

Q4
MCQ 4: If $f(x) = x^3$ and $g(x) = x^2$ on $[1, 2]$, the value of $c$ is:
A A) $\frac{7}{3}$
B B) $\frac{14}{9}$
C C) $\frac{3}{2}$
D D) $\frac{19}{9}$
Derivation & Analysis:

Secant slope ratio: $\frac{8-1}{4-1} = \frac{7}{3}$. Derivative ratio: $\frac{3c^2}{2c} = \frac{3c}{2}$. Equating: $\frac{3c}{2} = \frac{7}{3} \implies c = \frac{14}{9}.

Q5
MCQ 5: Cauchy's Mean Value Theorem cannot be applied to $f(x) = x^2$ and $g(x) = x^3 - x$ on $[-1, 1]$ because:
A A) $f$ is not continuous
B B) $g'(x) = 0$ at $x = \frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}$ inside $(-1, 1)$
C C) $f(a) \neq f(b)$
D D) $g(-1) \neq g(1)$
Derivation & Analysis:

Here $g'(x) = 3x^2 - 1$. Setting $g'(x) = 0$ gives $x = \pm \frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}$, which lie inside $(-1, 1)$, violating the non-vanishing derivative condition.

Q6
MCQ 6: For $f(x) = \ln x$ and $g(x) = x$ on $[1, e]$, the value of $c$ is:
A A) $e - 1$
B B) $\ln e$
C C) $\frac{1}{e-1}$
D D) $1$
Derivation & Analysis:

$\frac{f(e)-f(1)}{g(e)-g(1)} = \frac{1-0}{e-1} = \frac{1}{e-1}$. Derivative ratio: $\frac{1/c}{1} = \frac{1}{c}$. Thus $\frac{1}{c} = \frac{1}{e-1} \implies c = e - 1$.

Q7
MCQ 7: If $f(x) = e^x$ and $g(x) = e^{2x}$ on $[0, 1]$, the value of $c$ satisfying Cauchy's theorem is:
A A) $0$
B B) $\frac{1}{2}$
C C) Does not exist / invalid
D D) $1$
Derivation & Analysis:

Notice $g(x) = (f(x))^2$. The denominator $g(1) - g(0) = e^2 - 1$ and $f(1)-f(0) = e-1$. Derivative ratio $\frac{e^c}{2e^{2c}} = \frac{1}{2e^c}$. Secant ratio $\frac{e-1}{e^2-1} = \frac{1}{e+1}$. So $2e^c = e+1 \implies e^c = \frac{e+1}{2} \implies c = \ln\left(\frac{e+1}{2}\right)$, which is valid and lies in $(0, 1)$.

Q8
MCQ 8: Assertion (A): Cauchy's Mean Value Theorem is used to prove L'Hôpital's Rule. Reason (R): It relates the ratio of increments of two functions to the ratio of their derivatives at an interior point.
A A) Both (A) and (R) are true and (R) is the correct explanation of (A)
B B) Both (A) and (R) are true but (R) is not the correct explanation
C C) (A) is true but (R) is false
D D) (A) is false but (R) is true
Derivation & Analysis:

Cauchy's theorem provides the exact mathematical framework to evaluate limits yielding $0/0$ or $\infty/\infty$ in L'Hôpital's rule.

Q9
MCQ 9: For linear functions $f(x) = ax + b$ and $g(x) = cx + d$ with $ad - bc \neq 0$ on any interval $[u, v]$, the value of $c$ guaranteed by CMVT:
A A) Exists uniquely at the midpoint
B B) Cannot be determined
C C) Holds trivially for all $x$ in $(u, v)$ as both sides are constant
D D) Fails because derivatives are constant
Derivation & Analysis:

For linear functions, $f'(c) = a$ and $g'(c) = c$, and the secant ratio is also $\frac{a}{c}$, so $\frac{a}{c} = \frac{a}{c}$ is identically true for all $x \in (u, v)$.

Q10
MCQ 10: If $f(x) = x^2$ and $g(x) = x$ on $[a, b]$ where $a > 0$, the Cauchy MVT point $c$ is:
A A) $\frac{a+b}{2}$
B B) $\sqrt{ab}$
C C) $\frac{2ab}{a+b}$
D D) $\frac{a^2+ab+b^2}{3}$
Derivation & Analysis:

Since $g(x) = x$, CMVT reduces to LMVT: $2c = \frac{b^2-a^2}{b-a} = b+a \implies c = \frac{a+b}{2}$.

Pillar 8 Frequently Asked Questions

Lagrange's theorem is merely a special case of Cauchy's theorem where $g(x) = x$. Cauchy's theorem handles two independent functions simultaneously, making it indispensable for proofs involving indeterminate forms and Taylor's remainders.

Failing to check whether $g'(x) = 0$ anywhere in the open interval $(a, b)$. If the derivative of the denominator function vanishes at an interior point, the theorem's hypotheses are violated.

Calculus questions regularly feature 2 to 3 items on mean value theorems, with CMVT specifically targeted in PGT examinations for testing derivative quotient properties.

Memorize standard polynomial and exponential pairings where $c$ has closed-form algebraic relations (such as arithmetic or logarithmic means) to bypass lengthy algebraic manipulation.
