Polynomials and factors of second-degree polynomials

Second-degree polynomials form the bedrock of algebraic analysis and equation theory frequently tested in competitive examinations like UP TGT/PGT, KVS, and DSSSB. Mastery over factorization techniques, discriminant properties, and root-coefficient relations guarantees high-speed accuracy under strict time constraints.

Definition and statement

Fundamental Factorization Theorem for Second-Degree Polynomials
Let $P(x) = ax^2 + bx + c$ be a quadratic polynomial with coefficients $a, b, c \in \mathbb{C}$ (or \mathbb{R}) and $a \neq 0$. If $\alpha$ and $\beta$ are the roots of the equation $ax^2 + bx + c = 0$, then $P(x)$ can be uniquely factored into linear factors as $P(x) = a(x - \alpha)(x - \beta)$.

📐 Geometric Interpretation

Geometrically, $y = ax^2 + bx + c$ represents a parabola whose orientation and width are governed by leading coefficient $a$. The roots $\alpha$ and $\beta$ designate the exact x-intercepts of the parabolic curve when intersecting the real x-axis, provided the discriminant $\Delta = b^2 - 4ac \ge 0$.

⚙️ Algebraic & Structural Meaning

Establishes the fundamental link between polynomial roots and ideal factorization in polynomial rings $\mathbb{R}[x]$ and $\mathbb{C}[x]$, directly connecting Vieta's formulas with linear factor decomposition.

Useful results and shortcuts

Use these identities and results when their conditions apply:

01

Vieta's Rapid Sum and Product

$$ \alpha + \beta = -\frac{b}{a}, \quad \alpha\beta = \frac{c}{a} $$

Rule: Instantly construct a quadratic polynomial or find symmetric functions of roots without calculating individual roots.

Application: Directly tested in UP PGT/TGT to find values like $\alpha^3 + \beta^3$ or $\frac{1}{\alpha} + \frac{1}{\beta}$.
02

Nature of Roots via Discriminant Sign

$$ \Delta = b^2 - 4ac $$

Rule: If $a, b, c$ are rational and $\Delta$ is a perfect square, roots are rational; if $\Delta < 0$, roots are complex conjugates.

Application: Frequent MCQ items asking for parameter ranges for real/imaginary roots.
03

Condition for Common Roots

$$ \frac{a_1}{a_2} = \frac{b_1}{b_2} = \frac{c_1}{c_2} \quad \text{(both roots)} \quad \text{or cross-multiplication for one root} $$

Rule: Use cross-multiplication determinant to find unknown parameters when two quadratics share a root.

04

Maximum/Minimum Value of Quadratic Expression

$$ \text{Extremum at } x = -\frac{b}{2a}, \quad \text{Extreme value} = \frac{4ac - b^2}{4a} $$

Rule: Avoid calculus for quadratic extrema; directly read off vertex coordinates.

Application: Essential for range determination and optimization problems in UP PGT.
05

Sign of Quadratic Expression

$$ ax^2 + bx + c \text{ has same sign as } a \; \forall x \in \mathbb{R} \iff \Delta < 0 $$

Rule: Instantly determine inequality solution sets and parameter bounds without sketching.

Application: Appears in range and domain analysis problems.
06

Reciprocal Equation Property

$$ \text{If } \alpha \text{ is a root of } ax^2+bx+c=0, \text{ then } \frac{1}{\alpha} \text{ is a root of } cx^2+bx+a=0 $$

Rule: Swap coefficients $a$ and $c$ when roots are reciprocals.

Common errors and counterexamples

These examples show how a conclusion can fail when a condition is missing:

⚠️ COMMON ERROR #01

Forgetting the Leading Coefficient $a$ in Factorization

Common Pitfall: Writing $P(x) = (x - \alpha)(x - \beta)$ instead of $P(x) = a(x - \alpha)(x - \beta)$ when $a \neq 1$.

Counterexample: $P(x) = 2x^2 - 8x + 6 = 2(x-1)(x-3)$

Why it fails: Dropping $a$ alters the polynomial scaling and leads to incorrect coefficient comparisons in parameter determination.

🛡️ Exam Defense: Always preserve the leading coefficient $a$ when expressing a polynomial in factored form.
⚠️ COMMON ERROR #02

Assuming Real Roots Imply Rational Factors

Common Pitfall: Attempting middle-term splitting when $\Delta > 0$ is not a perfect square.

Counterexample: $x^2 - 3x + 1 = 0 \implies \Delta = 5$

Why it fails: Middle-term splitting fails for irrational roots; quadratic formula must be deployed.

🛡️ Exam Defense: Check $\Delta = b^2 - 4ac$ for squareness before attempting integer factorization.

Solved examples

Each problem includes a full solution and a shorter method:

CANONICAL PROBLEM 01 Dual-Method Breakdown

If the roots of the quadratic equation $x^2 - px + q = 0$ differ by 1, then which of the following relations holds true?

(A) $p^2 = 4q + 1$(B) $p^2 = 4q - 1$(C) $p^2 = 4q$(D) $q^2 = 4p + 1$
✓ Correct Answer: (A)
📚 Method A: Formal Textbook Derivation
Let roots be $\alpha$ and $\alpha + 1$. Then sum of roots $2\alpha + 1 = p \implies \alpha = \frac{p-1}{2}$. Product of roots $\alpha(\alpha+1) = q$. Substituting $\alpha$, we get $\frac{p-1}{2}\frac{p+1}{2} = q \implies \frac{p^2-1}{4} = q \implies p^2 = 4q + 1$.
⚡ Method B: 25-Second Exam Shortcut
Use $(\alpha - \beta)^2 = (\alpha + \beta)^2 - 4\alpha\beta \implies 1^2 = p^2 - 4q \implies p^2 = 4q + 1$.
💡 Tip: Memorize $(\alpha - \beta)^2 = \frac{\Delta}{a^2}$ for instant 10-second solutions.
CANONICAL PROBLEM 02 Dual-Method Breakdown

For what real values of $k$ does the expression $kx^2 - 6x + 1$ remain strictly positive for all $x \in \mathbb{R}$?

(A) $k > 9$(B) $k < 9$(C) $k \ge 9$(D) $k = 9$
✓ Correct Answer: (A)
📚 Method A: Formal Textbook Derivation
For a quadratic $ax^2 + bx + c > 0$ for all $x$, we must have $a > 0$ and $\Delta < 0$. Here $a = k > 0$ and $\Delta = (-6)^2 - 4(k)(1) = 36 - 4k < 0 \implies 4k > 36 \implies k > 9$.
⚡ Method B: 25-Second Exam Shortcut
Direct application of sign rule: $a > 0$ and $b^2 - 4ac < 0$.
💡 Tip: Watch out for strict inequality vs. non-strict inequality ($>$ vs $\ge$) which decides boundary inclusion.

Practice questions

Choose an answer to check it and see the solution:

Q1
If $\alpha$ and $\beta$ are the roots of $2x^2 - 5x + 3 = 0$, the value of $\frac{1}{\alpha} + \frac{1}{\beta}$ is:
A $\frac{5}{3}$
B $\frac{3}{5}$
C $-\frac{5}{3}$
D $\frac{2}{3}$
Derivation & Analysis:

We know $\alpha + \beta = \frac{5}{2}$ and $\alpha\beta = \frac{3}{2}$. Thus $\frac{1}{\alpha} + \frac{1}{\beta} = \frac{\alpha + \beta}{\alpha\beta} = \frac{5/2}{3/2} = \frac{5}{3}$.

Q2
If one root of the equation $x^2 - px + 12 = 0$ is 4, then the value of $p$ is:
A 7
B 8
C -7
D 6
Derivation & Analysis:

Substitute $x = 4$ into the equation: $4^2 - 4p + 12 = 0 \implies 16 - 4p + 12 = 0 \implies 4p = 28 \implies p = 7$.

Q3
The quadratic equation whose roots are reciprocal to the roots of $2x^2 + 5x + 2 = 0$ is:
A $2x^2 + 5x + 2 = 0$
B $2x^2 - 5x + 2 = 0$
C $x^2 + 5x + 2 = 0$
D $2x^2 + 5x - 2 = 0$
Derivation & Analysis:

Replacing $x$ by $\frac{1}{x}$ in $2x^2 + 5x + 2 = 0$ gives $2\left(\frac{1}{x}\right)^2 + 5\left(\frac{1}{x}\right) + 2 = 0 \implies \frac{2}{x^2} + \frac{5}{x} + 2 = 0 \implies 2 + 5x + 2x^2 = 0$, which simplifies to $2x^2 + 5x + 2 = 0$.

Q4
If the equations $x^2 + ax + b = 0$ and $x^2 + bx + a = 0$ ($a \neq b$) have a common root, then $a + b$ equals:
A 1
B -1
C 0
D 2
Derivation & Analysis:

Let common root be $\alpha$. Then $\alpha^2 + a\alpha + b = 0$ and $\alpha^2 + b\alpha + a = 0$. Subtracting the two gives $(a-b)\alpha + (b-a) = 0 \implies (a-b)(\alpha - 1) = 0$. Since $a \neq b$, we must have $\alpha = 1$. Substituting $\alpha = 1$ in $1 + a + b = 0 \implies a + b = -1$.

Q5
The minimum value of the expression $4x^2 - 4x + 3$ is:
A 2
B 3
C 1
D $\frac{1}{2}$
Derivation & Analysis:

Using formula $\frac{4ac - b^2}{4a}$ where $a=4, b=-4, c=3$: Minimum value = $\frac{4(4)(3) - (-4)^2}{4(4)} = \frac{48 - 16}{16} = \frac{32}{16} = 2$.

Q6
If $\alpha, \beta$ are roots of $x^2 - 3x + 2 = 0$, then the quadratic equation with roots $\alpha^2$ and $\beta^2$ is:
A $x^2 - 5x + 4 = 0$
B $x^2 - 9x + 4 = 0$
C $x^2 + 5x + 4 = 0$
D $x^2 - 5x - 4 = 0$
Derivation & Analysis:

Roots of $x^2 - 3x + 2 = 0$ are $\alpha = 1, \beta = 2$. Their squares are $\alpha^2 = 1, \beta^2 = 4$. Sum of new roots = $1 + 4 = 5$, product = $1 \times 4 = 4$. Equation: $x^2 - 5x + 4 = 0$.

Q7
The expression $x^2 - 8x + 15$ can be factored as:
A $(x-3)(x-5)$
B $(x+3)(x+5)$
C $(x-3)(x+5)$
D $(x+3)(x-5)$
Derivation & Analysis:

Splitting the middle term: $-8x = -3x - 5x$, and $(-3)(-5) = 15$. Thus $x^2 - 3x - 5x + 15 = x(x-3) - 5(x-3) = (x-3)(x-5)$.

Q8
If the discriminant of $ax^2 + bx + c = 0$ is zero, then the roots are:
A Real and equal
B Real and distinct
C Complex conjugate
D Purely imaginary
Derivation & Analysis:

By definition, $\Delta = 0$ implies both roots are real and coincide (multiplicity 2).

Q9
For what value of $m$ is $x^2 - 2(1 + 3m)x + 7(3 + 2m) = 0$ having equal roots?
A $2, \frac{10}{9}$
B $1, -1$
C $3, 2$
D $0, 1$
Derivation & Analysis:

Set $\Delta = 0 \implies [-2(1+3m)]^2 - 4(1)[7(3+2m)] = 0 \implies 4(1 + 6m + 9m^2) - 28(3 + 2m) = 0 \implies 4 + 24m + 36m^2 - 84 - 56m = 0 \implies 36m^2 - 32m - 80 = 0 \implies 9m^2 - 8m - 20 = 0 \implies (9m + 10)(m - 2) = 0 \implies m = 2$ or $m = -10/9$.

Q10
If the sum of the roots of $ax^2 + bx + c = 0$ is equal to the sum of their squares, then:
A $2ab = b^2 + ac$
B $b^2 = 2ac - ab$
C $2ac = ab + b^2$
D $a^2 + b^2 = c^2$
Derivation & Analysis:

Given $\alpha + \beta = \alpha^2 + \beta^2$. Since $\alpha^2 + \beta^2 = (\alpha + \beta)^2 - 2\alpha\beta$, we have $-\frac{b}{a} = \left(-\frac{b}{a}\right)^2 - 2\left(\frac{c}{a}\right) \implies -\frac{b}{a} = \frac{b^2}{a^2} - \frac{2c}{a}$. Multiplying by $a^2$: $-ab = b^2 - 2ac \implies 2ac = b^2 + ab$ or $2ab = b^2 + ac$ depending on formulation. Wait, $-ab = b^2 - 2ac \implies 2ac = b^2 + ab$. Let's check option A: $2ab = b^2 + ac$. Actually $2ac = b^2 + ab$ corresponds to $2ac = b(a+b)$.

Common questions

The leading coefficient $a$ determines the vertical stretch and scaling of the polynomial. Omitting $a$ changes the polynomial identity from $ax^2 + bx + c$ to $(x-\alpha)(x-\beta)$, which is only valid when $a = 1$.

Sign errors during middle-term splitting and misinterpreting strict versus non-strict inequalities (e.g., using $>$ instead of $\ge$ when ensuring expressions are non-negative) are the primary culprits.

Memorize standard discriminant conditions, symmetric root identities (like $\alpha^3 + \beta^3 = (\alpha+\beta)^3 - 3\alpha\beta(\alpha+\beta)$), and vertex formulas to bypass lengthy algebraic manipulation.
