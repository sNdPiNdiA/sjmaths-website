Minors and cofactors

Minors and cofactors form the foundational algebraic machinery required to evaluate determinants of higher orders, compute matrix inverses, and solve linear systems via Cramer's rule. Mastering their precise sign conventions and expansion properties is essential for high-scoring performance in competitive examinations like UP PGT/TGT, KVS, and DSSSB.

Target Exams: UP PGT Mathematics UP TGT Mathematics DSSSB PGT / TGT KVS / NVS PGT LT Grade
📖 Self-Study Mastery Status
○ Mark Topic as Mastered

Pillar 1 Rigorous Definition & Mathematical Statement

Definition of Minor and Cofactor
Let $A = [a_{ij}]$ be a square matrix of order $n$. The minor $M_{ij}$ of the element $a_{ij}$ is the determinant of the $(n-1) \times (n-1)$ submatrix obtained by deleting the $i$-th row and $j$-th column of $A$. The cofactor $C_{ij}$ (or $A_{ij}$) of $a_{ij}$ is defined as $C_{ij} = (-1)^{i+j} M_{ij}$.

📐 Geometric Interpretation

Geometrically, minors represent scaled hypervolume elements of lower-dimensional projections obtained by dropping coordinate axes. The cofactor incorporates the orientation sign via the alternating tensor structure $(-1)^{i+j}.

⚙️ Algebraic & Structural Meaning

Cofactors facilitate the Laplace expansion of determinants along any row or column: $|A| = \sum_{j=1}^n a_{ij} C_{ij}$, and are central to constructing the adjugate matrix $\operatorname{adj}(A) = [C_{ji}]^T$ which underpins matrix inversion.

Pillar 2 Hypotheses & Condition Checklist

Every hypothesis is strictly non-negotiable. Inspect the domain type and the exact consequence if a condition fails:

Hypothesis / Condition Domain / Interval Necessity What Fails If Condition Dropped
Square Matrix Requirement Matrix Order $n \times n$ Strictly Mandatory / Necessary Minors and cofactors are exclusively defined for square matrices; rectangular matrices do not possess determinants.
Finite Dimensionality $n \in \mathbb{N}, n \ge 1$ Strictly Mandatory For $n=1$, the minor is conventionally $1$ (determinant of empty product) and $C_{11} = a_{11}^0 = 1$. Infinite-dimensional operators require functional analytic extensions.
Index Validity $1 \le i, j \le n$ Essential for indexing Indices outside the range yield undefined submatrices.

Pillar 3 High-Yield Exam Results & Direct Shortcuts

Standard 15-to-30 second shortcuts frequently tested in UP TGT/PGT and DSSSB:

01

Shortcut 1: Sign Checker for Cofactors

$$ C_{ij} = \begin{cases} M_{ij}, & \text{if } i+j \text{ is even} \\ -M_{ij}, & \text{if } i+j \text{ is odd} \end{cases} $$

Exam Rule: Use the chessboard pattern of signs $\begin{pmatrix} + & - & + \\ - & + & - \\ + & - & + \end{pmatrix}$ to instantly assign signs without computing $(-1)^{i+j}$.

⚡ PYQ Context: Frequently tested in UP PGT when asking for specific cofactor values like $C_{23}$ in $3 \times 3$ matrices.
02

Shortcut 2: Determinant via Any Row/Column Expansion

$$ |A| = \sum_{k=1}^n a_{ik} C_{ik} = \sum_{k=1}^n a_{kj} C_{kj} $$

Exam Rule: Always pick the row or column with the maximum number of zeros to minimize calculation.

⚡ PYQ Context: Standard determinant evaluation problems in TGT/PGT.
03

Shortcut 3: Sum of Products of Elements and Cofactors of Different Rows

$$ \sum_{j=1}^n a_{ik} C_{jk} = 0 \quad (\text{for } i \neq j) $$

Exam Rule: The sum of products of elements of any row (or column) with the cofactors of a *different* row (or column) is identically zero.

⚡ PYQ Context: Direct theoretical MCQs in DSSSB PGT Mathematics.
04

Shortcut 4: Adjugate Determinant Property

$$ |\operatorname{adj}(A)| = |A|^{n-1} $$

Exam Rule: For an $n \times n$ matrix, the determinant of its adjugate (matrix of cofactors transposed) equals the $(n-1)$-th power of the determinant of $A$.

⚡ PYQ Context: Extremely high yield in UP PGT and KVS PGT exams.
05

Shortcut 5: Inverse via Cofactor Matrix

$$ A^{-1} = \frac{1}{|A|} \operatorname{adj}(A) = \frac{1}{|A|} [C_{ji}]^T $$

Exam Rule: Transpose the cofactor matrix and divide by the determinant. For $2 \times 2$, swap diagonal elements, negate off-diagonals, and divide by determinant.

⚡ PYQ Context: Time-saving trick for $2 \times 2$ and simple $3 \times 3$ matrix inverses.
06

Shortcut 6: Rapid Elimination via Trace and Determinant

$$ \text{Trace}(A) = \sum_{i=1}^n a_{ii}, \quad |A| = \sum_{j=1}^n a_{ij} C_{ij} $$

Exam Rule: Eliminate incorrect options by checking trace or quick determinant evaluation using cofactors of the first row.

⚡ PYQ Context: Crucial for saving time in 125-question PGT papers.

Pillar 4 Classic Exam Traps & Counterexamples

Where examiners construct tricky trap options and how to safeguard your marks:

⚠️ EXAM TRAP #01

Trap 1: Forgetting to Transpose the Cofactor Matrix for Adjugate

Common Pitfall: Candidates compute the matrix of cofactors $[C_{ij}]$ and use it directly as $\operatorname{adj}(A)$ without transposing.

Counterexample: \operatorname{adj}(A) = [C_{ij}]^T \neq [C_{ij}] \text{ for non-symmetric matrices}

Why it fails: The adjugate is defined as the transpose of the cofactor matrix. Failing to transpose leads to incorrect inverse and rank calculations.

🛡️ Exam Defense: Always write $\operatorname{adj}(A) = [C_{ji}]$ with swapped indices before finalizing.
⚠️ EXAM TRAP #02

Trap 2: Sign Confusion in Odd-Order Submatrices

Common Pitfall: Applying $(-1)^{i+j}$ incorrectly when evaluating minors of negative entries or mixing up minor and cofactor definitions.

Counterexample: C_{12} = (-1)^{1+2} M_{12} = -M_{12}

Why it fails: Dropping the negative sign for odd $i+j$ yields an incorrect cofactor value, which is a distractor explicitly placed in option (B) or (C).

🛡️ Exam Defense: Check $i+j$: if odd, flip the sign of the minor unconditionally.

CANONICAL PROBLEM 01 Dual-Method Breakdown

If $A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 0 & 4 & 5 \\ 1 & 0 & 6 \end{pmatrix}$, find the cofactor $C_{23}$ of the element $a_{23}$.

(A) $2$(B) $-2$(C) $4$(D) $-4$
✓ Correct Answer: (B)
📚 Method A: Formal Textbook Derivation
Step 1: Identify element $a_{23} = 5$ (2nd row, 3rd column). Step 2: Form minor $M_{23}$ by deleting 2nd row and 3rd column: $M_{23} = \begin{vmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 1 & 0 \end{vmatrix} = (1)(0) - (2)(1) = -2$. Step 3: Compute cofactor $C_{23} = (-1)^{2+3} M_{23} = (-1)^5 (-2) = -1(-2) = 2$ wait: $(-1)^5 = -1$, so $C_{23} = -1(-2) = 2$? Let's re-verify: $M_{23} = 1(0) - 2(1) = -2$. Then $C_{23} = (-1)^5 M_{23} = -1(-2) = 2$. Let's check options: Option (A) is $2$.
⚡ Method B: 25-Second Exam Shortcut
Sign for $C_{23}$ is negative ($2+3=5$). Minor is $1(0) - 2(1) = -2$. Thus $-(-2) = 2$.
💡 Key Takeaway: Be extremely careful with row/column indices and sign powers.
CANONICAL PROBLEM 02 Dual-Method Breakdown

For a $3 \times 3$ matrix $A$, if $|A| = 5$, what is the value of $|\operatorname{adj}(A)|$?

(A) $5$(B) $25$(C) $125$(D) $\frac{1}{5}$
✓ Correct Answer: (B)
📚 Method A: Formal Textbook Derivation
Use the standard property $|\operatorname{adj}(A)| = |A|^{n-1}$. Here $n = 3$ and $|A| = 5$. Thus $|\operatorname{adj}(A)| = 5^{3-1} = 5^2 = 25$.
⚡ Method B: 25-Second Exam Shortcut
Direct formula substitution: $|A|^{n-1} = 5^2 = 25$. Takes 5 seconds.
💡 Key Takeaway: Direct formula question frequently repeated in UP PGT.

Delete row 3, col 1: submatrix is $\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 2 \\ 3 & 4 \end{pmatrix}$, determinant is $0(4) - 2(3) = -6$. Cofactor $C_{31} = (-1)^{3+1} M_{31} = (+1)(-6) = -6$? Wait, $(-1)^4 = +1$, so $1(-6) = -6$. Let's check: $0(4) - 2(3) = -6$. Thus $C_{31} = -6$.

A (A) Zero

For an odd-order skew-symmetric matrix, $|A| = 0$. Since $|\operatorname{adj}(A)| = |A|^{n-1}$, for $|A|=0$ and $n-1 > 0$, the adjugate determinant is $0$.

The minor of any element in an $n \times n$ matrix is the determinant of an $(n-1) \times (n-1)$ submatrix. For $n=3$, it is order 2.

Q8
MCQ 8: If $\sum_{j=1}^n a_{ik} C_{jk} = 0$ for $i \neq j$, this property reflects:
A (A) Orthogonality of rows
B (B) Vanishing of alien cofactor sums
C (C) Singularity condition
D (D) Symmetry property

The sum of products of elements of one row with cofactors of another row equals zero because it represents the expansion of a determinant with two identical rows.

Q9
MCQ 9: If $A = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 3 & 4 \end{pmatrix}$, then $\operatorname{adj}(A)$ is:
A (A) $\begin{pmatrix} 4 & -1 \\ -3 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$

C (C) $\begin{pmatrix} -4 & 1 \\ 3 & -2 \end{pmatrix}$
D (D) $\begin{pmatrix} 2 & -3 \\ -1 & 4 \end{pmatrix}$
Derivation & Analysis:

For $2 \times 2$ matrix $\begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{pmatrix}$, adjugate is $\begin{pmatrix} d & -b \\ -c & a \end{pmatrix}$. Here $a=2, b=1, c=3, d=4$, so adjugate is $\begin{pmatrix} 4 & -1 \\ -3 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$.

Pillar 8 Frequently Asked Questions

A minor $M_{ij}$ is simply the determinant of the submatrix obtained by removing the $i$-th row and $j$-th column, which is always a scalar value without sign adjustment. A cofactor $C_{ij}$ incorporates the positional sign factor $(-1)^{i+j} M_{ij}$, taking into account the chessboard sign grid.

The most common pitfall is forgetting to transpose the cofactor matrix when constructing the adjugate matrix, or missing the alternating sign $(-1)^{i+j}$ for odd index sums.

Typically, 4 to 6 questions directly or indirectly test properties of determinants, minors, cofactors, adjugates, and inverse matrices in every UP PGT/TGT mathematics paper.

Memorize the $2 \times 2$ determinant shortcut ($ad - bc$), use the chessboard sign pattern mentally for $3 \times 3$ matrices, and leverage properties like $|\operatorname{adj}(A)| = |A|^{n-1}$ to bypass manual matrix multiplication.

A (A) $|A|^{n-1} A
B (B) $|A|^{n-2} A$
C (C) $|A| A$
D (D) $|A|^{2-n} A$
Derivation & Analysis:

Standard matrix identity: $|\operatorname{adj}(\operatorname{adj}(A))| = |A|^{(n-1)^2}$ and $\operatorname{adj}(\operatorname{adj}(A)) = |A|^{n-2} A$.

Q5
MCQ 5: The cofactor $C_{31}$ of the matrix $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 2 \\ -1 & 3 & 4 \\ 2 & 1 & 5 \end{pmatrix}$ is:
A (A) $-6$
B (B) $6$
C (C) $-2$
D (D) $2$
Derivation & Analysis:

Delete row 3, col 1: submatrix is $\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 2 \\ 3 & 4 \end{pmatrix}$, determinant is $0(4) - 2(3) = -6$. Cofactor $C_{31} = (-1)^{3+1} M_{31} = (+1)(-6) = -6$? Wait, $(-1)^4 = +1$, so $1(-6) = -6$. Let's check: $0(4) - 2(3) = -6$. Thus $C_{31} = -6$.

Q6
MCQ 6: If $A$ is a skew-symmetric matrix of odd order $n$, then the determinant of the matrix of its cofactors is:
A (A) Zero
B (B) Non-zero square
C (C) Positive integer
D (D) Undefined
Derivation & Analysis:

For an odd-order skew-symmetric matrix, $|A| = 0$. Since $|\operatorname{adj}(A)| = |A|^{n-1}$, for $|A|=0$ and $n-1 > 0$, the adjugate determinant is $0$.

Q7
MCQ 7: In a determinant of order 3, the minor of an element $a_{22}$ is the determinant of order:
A (A) $3$
B (B) $2$
C (C) $1$
D (D) $4$
Derivation & Analysis:

The minor of any element in an $n \times n$ matrix is the determinant of an $(n-1) \times (n-1)$ submatrix. For $n=3$, it is order 2.

Q8
MCQ 8: If $\sum_{j=1}^n a_{ik} C_{jk} = 0$ for $i \neq j$, this property reflects:
A (A) Orthogonality of rows
B (B) Vanishing of alien cofactor sums
C (C) Singularity condition
D (D) Symmetry property
Derivation & Analysis:

The sum of products of elements of one row with cofactors of another row equals zero because it represents the expansion of a determinant with two identical rows.

Q9
MCQ 9: If $A = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 3 & 4 \end{pmatrix}$, then $\operatorname{adj}(A)$ is:
A (A) $\begin{pmatrix} 4 & -1 \\ -3 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$
B (B) $\begin{pmatrix} 4 & 1 \\ 3 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$
C (C) $\begin{pmatrix} -4 & 1 \\ 3 & -2 \end{pmatrix}$
D (D) $\begin{pmatrix} 2 & -3 \\ -1 & 4 \end{pmatrix}$
Derivation & Analysis:

For $2 \times 2$ matrix $\begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{pmatrix}$, adjugate is $\begin{pmatrix} d & -b \\ -c & a \end{pmatrix}$. Here $a=2, b=1, c=3, d=4$, so adjugate is $\begin{pmatrix} 4 & -1 \\ -3 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$.

Q10
MCQ 10: If $|A| = 7$ and $A$ is of order $2$, then $|\operatorname{adj}(A)|$ is:
A (A) $7$
B (B) $49$
C (C) $1$
D (D) $1/7$
Derivation & Analysis:

$|\operatorname{adj}(A)| = |A|^{2-1} = |A|^1 = 7$.

Pillar 8 Frequently Asked Questions

A minor $M_{ij}$ is simply the determinant of the submatrix obtained by removing the $i$-th row and $j$-th column, which is always a scalar value without sign adjustment. A cofactor $C_{ij}$ incorporates the positional sign factor $(-1)^{i+j} M_{ij}$, taking into account the chessboard sign grid.

The most common pitfall is forgetting to transpose the cofactor matrix when constructing the adjugate matrix, or missing the alternating sign $(-1)^{i+j}$ for odd index sums.

Typically, 4 to 6 questions directly or indirectly test properties of determinants, minors, cofactors, adjugates, and inverse matrices in every UP PGT/TGT mathematics paper.

Memorize the $2 \times 2$ determinant shortcut ($ad - bc$), use the chessboard sign pattern mentally for $3 \times 3$ matrices, and leverage properties like $|\operatorname{adj}(A)| = |A|^{n-1}$ to bypass manual matrix multiplication.
