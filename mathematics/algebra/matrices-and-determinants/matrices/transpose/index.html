Transpose of a matrix

The transpose of a matrix is formed by interchanging its rows and columns, serving as the foundational operator for symmetric, skew-symmetric, and orthogonal matrices. This topic covers transpose properties.

Definition and statement

Fundamental Properties of Matrix Transposition
Let $A = [a_{ij}]$ be an $m \times n$ matrix and $B = [b_{ij}]$ be an $n \times p$ matrix. The transpose of $A$, denoted by $A^T$ or $A'$, is the $n \times m$ matrix whose $(i, j)$-th entry is $a_{ji}$. For any matrices $A, B$ and scalar $k \in \mathbb{C}$, the following hold: (1) $(A^T)^T = A$, (2) $(A + B)^T = A^T + B^T$, (3) $(kA)^T = k A^T$, (4) $(AB)^T = B^T A^T$.

📐 Geometric Interpretation

Geometrically, transposition corresponds to reflecting the elements across the main diagonal of the matrix array. In linear transformations, if $T: V \to W$ is a linear map represented by matrix $A$ relative to orthonormal bases, then $A^T$ represents the adjoint transformation relative to the dual spaces.

⚙️ Algebraic & Structural Meaning

Algebraically, transposition defines an involution on the ring of square matrices. It establishes the structural classification of matrices into symmetric ($A^T = A$) and skew-symmetric ($A^T = -A$) components, which span the entire vector space of square matrices.

Useful results and shortcuts

Use these identities and results when their conditions apply:

01

Reversal Law for Products of Multiple Matrices

$$ (A_1 A_2 A_3 \dots A_n)^T = A_n^T A_{n-1}^T \dots A_2^T A_1^T $$

Rule: Reverse the order of every matrix and apply transpose to each individually.

Application: Frequently asked in UP PGT/TGT to evaluate transpose of complex matrix products.
02

Transpose of Matrix Polynomials

$$ P(A)^T = P(A^T) \quad \text{where } P(x) = a_0 + a_1 x + a_2 x^2 + \dots + a_n x^n $$

Rule: Substitute $A^T$ directly into the polynomial expression without expanding individual powers repeatedly.

Application: Standard pattern in KVS PGT and DSSSB for finding powers of symmetric or nilpotent transposed matrices.
03

Determinant Invariance

$$ |A^T| = |A| $$

Rule: Never recalculate a determinant after transposing; the determinant value remains strictly invariant.

Application: Directly tested in matching matrix properties and eigenvalue problems.
04

Trace Invariance

$$ \text{Tr}(A^T) = \text{Tr}(A) $$

Rule: The sum of principal diagonal elements is unaffected by transposition.

Application: Used in quick elimination of options in matrix trace equations.
05

Symmetric & Skew-Symmetric Decomposition

$$ A = \frac{1}{2}(A + A^T) + \frac{1}{2}(A - A^T) $$

Rule: Any square matrix is uniquely expressible as the sum of a symmetric matrix and a skew-symmetric matrix.

06

Inverse and Transpose Commutativity

$$ (A^{-1})^T = (A^T)^{-1} $$

Rule: Inversion and transposition operations commute for any non-singular square matrix.

Common errors and counterexamples

These examples show how a conclusion can fail when a condition is missing:

⚠️ COMMON ERROR #01

The Product Reversal Omission

Common Pitfall: Candidates write $(AB)^T = A^T B^T$ instead of the correct reversal law $B^T A^T$.

Counterexample: Let $A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 4 \end{pmatrix}, B = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$

Why it fails: Matrix multiplication is non-commutative ($AB \neq BA$), hence $(AB)^T = B^T A^T \neq A^T B^T$ in general.

🛡️ Exam Defense: Always reverse the sequence of matrices when distributing transpose over a product.
⚠️ COMMON ERROR #02

Skew-Symmetric Odd-Order Determinant Trap

Common Pitfall: Assuming non-zero determinants for odd-order skew-symmetric matrices.

Counterexample: Let $A$ be a skew-symmetric matrix of odd order $n$ ($n = 3, 5, \dots$).

Why it fails: $|A^T| = |-A| = (-1)^n |A| = -|A|$ since $n$ is odd, which implies $|A| = -|A| \implies 2|A| = 0 \implies |A| = 0$.

🛡️ Exam Defense: Any skew-symmetric matrix of odd order has a determinant identically equal to zero. Never calculate it manually!

Solved examples

Each problem includes a full solution and a shorter method:

CANONICAL PROBLEM 01 Dual-Method Breakdown

If $A$ is a square matrix such that $A^2 = I$, then what is the simplified value of $(I - A)^T + (I + A)^T$?

(A) $2I$(B) $2A$(C) $I$(D) $0$
✓ Correct Answer: (A)
📚 Method A: Formal Textbook Derivation
Step 1: Apply linearity of transpose: $(I - A)^T + (I + A)^T = I^T - A^T + I^T + A^T$. Step 2: Since $I^T = I$, this simplifies to $I - A^T + I + A^T = 2I$. Notice that $A$ being equal to $I$ or not does not alter the algebraic cancellation of $-A^T$ and $+A^T$.
⚡ Method B: 25-Second Exam Shortcut
Observe that $+A^T$ and $-A^T$ cancel out immediately, leaving $I^T + I^T = 2I$. Done in 10 seconds.
💡 Tip: Do not waste time substituting numerical values for $A$; rely purely on algebraic cancellation of transpose terms.
CANONICAL PROBLEM 02 Dual-Method Breakdown

If $A$ and $B$ are symmetric matrices of the same order, then $AB - BA$ is a:

(A) Symmetric matrix(B) Skew-symmetric matrix(C) Diagonal matrix(D) Zero matrix
✓ Correct Answer: (B)
📚 Method A: Formal Textbook Derivation
Step 1: Take the transpose of the expression: $(AB - BA)^T$. Step 2: Apply subtraction and product rules: $(AB)^T - (BA)^T = B^T A^T - A^T B^T$. Step 3: Use the given condition that $A$ and $B$ are symmetric ($A^T = A, B^T = B$): $BA - AB$. Step 4: Factor out negative sign: $-(AB - BA)$. Since the transpose equals its negative, $AB - BA$ is skew-symmetric.
⚡ Method B: 25-Second Exam Shortcut
Standard standard result: The commutator of two symmetric matrices is always skew-symmetric.
💡 Tip: Memorize this standard result: Symmetric $\pm$ Symmetric is symmetric, but commutator of symmetric matrices is skew-symmetric.

Practice questions

Choose an answer to check it and see the solution:

Q1
MCQ 1: For any square matrix $A$, the matrix $A + A^T$ is always:
A (A) Symmetric
B (B) Skew-symmetric
C (C) Diagonal
D (D) Identity
Derivation & Analysis:

Let $P = A + A^T$. Then $P^T = (A + A^T)^T = A^T + (A^T)^T = A^T + A = A + A^T = P$. Thus, $P$ is symmetric.

Q2
MCQ 2: If $A$ is a skew-symmetric matrix of order $n$, then $A^T$ is equal to:
A (A) $A$
B (B) $-A$
C (C) $I$
D (D) $0$
Derivation & Analysis:

By definition, a skew-symmetric matrix satisfies $A^T = -A$.

Q3
MCQ 3: If $A$ and $B$ are invertible matrices of the same order, then $(AB)^{-1}$ is equal to:
A (A) $A^{-1} B^{-1}$
B (B) $B^{-1} A^{-1}$
C (C) $B^T A^T$
D (D) $(BA)^{-1}$
Derivation & Analysis:

The reversal law for inverses states that $(AB)^{-1} = B^{-1} A^{-1}$. This parallels the transpose reversal law $(AB)^T = B^T A^T$.

Q4
MCQ 4: If $A = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ 3 & 4 \end{pmatrix}$, then $A^T$ is:
A (A) $\begin{pmatrix} 2 & 3 \\ -1 & 4 \end{pmatrix}$
B (B) $\begin{pmatrix} -2 & 1 \\ -3 & -4 \end{pmatrix}$
C (C) $\begin{pmatrix} 4 & -1 \\ 3 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$
D (D) $\begin{pmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ 3 & 4 \end{pmatrix}$
Derivation & Analysis:

Interchanging rows and columns of $A$ gives the first row $(2, -1)$ as the first column and the second row $(3, 4)$ as the second column.

Q5
MCQ 5: The determinant of a skew-symmetric matrix of odd order $5$ is always:
A (A) $1$
B (B) $-1$
C (C) $0$
D (D) Depends on matrix entries
Derivation & Analysis:

For any skew-symmetric matrix $A$ of odd order $n$, $|A| = |-A| = (-1)^n |A| = -|A| \implies 2|A| = 0 \implies |A| = 0$.

Q6
MCQ 6: If $A$ is a symmetric matrix, then $A^3$ is:
A (A) Skew-symmetric
B (B) Symmetric
C (C) Diagonal
D (D) Null matrix
Derivation & Analysis:

Since $A^T = A$, we have $(A^3)^T = (A^T)^3 = A^3$, which proves $A^3$ is symmetric.

Q7
MCQ 7: If $A$ is any square matrix, then $A - A^T$ is always a:
A (A) Symmetric matrix
B (B) Skew-symmetric matrix
C (C) Identity matrix
D (D) Zero matrix
Derivation & Analysis:

Let $Q = A - A^T$. Then $Q^T = (A - A^T)^T = A^T - (A^T)^T = A^T - A = -(A - A^T) = -Q$. Thus, $Q$ is skew-symmetric.

Q8
MCQ 8: Assertion (A): If $A$ is an orthogonal matrix, then $|A| = \pm 1$. Reason (R): $A A^T = I \implies |A||A^T| = |I| \implies |A|^2 = 1$.
A (A) Both (A) and (R) are true and (R) is the correct explanation of (A)
B (B) Both (A) and (R) are true but (R) is not the correct explanation of (A)
C (C) (A) is true but (R) is false
D (D) (A) is false but (R) is true
Derivation & Analysis:

Both assertion and reason are mathematically rigorous and directly connected via determinant multiplicative property and $|A^T| = |A|$.

Q9
MCQ 9: If $A$ is a matrix of order $3 \times 4$, what is the order of $A^T A$?
A (A) $3 \times 3$
B (B) $4 \times 4$
C (C) $3 \times 4$
D (D) Not defined
Derivation & Analysis:

$A^T$ has order $4 \times 3$. Multiplying $A^T$ ($4 \times 3$) by $A$ ($3 \times 4$) yields a resulting matrix of order $4 \times 4$.

Q10
MCQ 10: If $A = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 2 & -3 \\ -2 & 0 & 5 \\ 3 & -5 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$, then $A$ is classified as:
A (A) Symmetric matrix
B (B) Skew-symmetric matrix
C (C) Diagonal matrix
D (D) Scalar matrix
Derivation & Analysis:

Checking $A^T$: the diagonal elements are all zero and $a_{ij} = -a_{ji}$ for all $i \neq j$ (e.g., $a_{12} = 2, a_{21} = -2$). Thus $A^T = -A$.

Common questions

Dimension compatibility dictates the order. If $A$ is $m \times n$ and $B$ is $n \times p$, then $AB$ is $m \times p$, making $(AB)^T$ of order $p \times m$. For $B^T A^T$, $B^T$ is $p \times n$ and $A^T$ is $n \times m$, so their product $B^T A^T$ is valid and results in a $p \times m$ matrix. $A^T B^T$ would have incompatible inner dimensions ($n \times m$ and $p \times n$).

Forgetting to apply the reversal law in matrix products or confusing symmetric and skew-symmetric matrix properties, especially regarding diagonal elements and odd-order determinants.

Memorize standard algebraic identities such as $(AB)^T = B^T A^T$, trace invariance, determinant invariance, and the decomposition formula $A = \frac{1}{2}(A + A^T) + \frac{1}{2}(A - A^T)$, avoiding full matrix expansion whenever possible.
