1. Algebra — Matrices and Determinants

Symmetric matrices

A square matrix $A$ is called symmetric if it is equal to its transpose, i.e., $A^T = A$, which implies that its elements satisfy $a_{ij} = a_{ji}$ for all $i$ and $j$. These matrices form a cornerstone of linear algebra because of their real eigenvalues and orthogonal diagonalizability, making them a high-yield scoring zone in UP TGT/PGT, DSSSB, and KVS examinations.

Target Exams: UP PGT Mathematics UP TGT Mathematics DSSSB PGT / TGT KVS / NVS PGT LT Grade
📖 Self-Study Mastery Status
○ Mark Topic as Mastered

Pillar 1 Rigorous Definition & Mathematical Statement

Fundamental Characterization and Spectral Property of Symmetric Matrices
Let $A$ be a real square matrix of order $n$. Then $A$ is symmetric ($A^T = A$) if and only if $A$ is orthogonally diagonalizable, and all the eigenvalues of a real symmetric matrix are strictly real.

📐 Geometric Interpretation

Geometrically, a real symmetric matrix represents a self-adjoint linear transformation corresponding to orthogonal principal axes. The eigenvectors associated with distinct eigenvalues are mutually orthogonal, representing perpendicular stretching or compressing axes in $\mathbb{R}^n$.

⚙️ Algebraic & Structural Meaning

Algebraically, the space of $n \times n$ matrices decomposes into the direct sum of the subspace of symmetric matrices and skew-symmetric matrices. The trace of $A^2$ equals the sum of squares of all its entries, and quadratic forms $x^T A x$ are fully characterized by symmetric matrices.

Pillar 3 High-Yield Exam Results & Direct Shortcuts

Standard 15-to-30 second shortcuts frequently tested in UP TGT/PGT and DSSSB:

01

Shortcut 1: Number of Independent Elements

$$ \frac{n(n+1)}{2} $$

Exam Rule: The total number of free (independent) scalar variables required to construct an $n \times n$ symmetric matrix is given by the triangular number formula.

⚡ PYQ Context: Directly tested in UP PGT and KVS to find the dimension of the vector space of symmetric matrices.
02

Shortcut 2: Trace and Sum of Eigenvalues

$$ \text{Tr}(A) = \sum_{i=1}^n \lambda_i $$

Exam Rule: Since real symmetric matrices are diagonalizable over $\mathbb{R}$, the trace is identically the sum of its real eigenvalues, and the determinant is the product of eigenvalues.

⚡ PYQ Context: Frequent in DSSSB PGT for finding unknown parameters in characteristic equations.
03

Shortcut 3: Skew-Symmetric Decomposition

$$ A = \frac{1}{2}(A + A^T) + \frac{1}{2}(A - A^T) $$

Exam Rule: Every square matrix can be uniquely expressed as the sum of a symmetric matrix ($S = \frac{A+A^T}{2}$) and a skew-symmetric matrix ($V = \frac{A-A^T}{2}$).

⚡ PYQ Context: Standard question pattern in UP TGT examining decomposition components.
04

Shortcut 4: Powers and Transposition

$$ (A^k)^T = (A^T)^k = A^k $$

Exam Rule: Any positive integer power $A^k$ of a symmetric matrix $A$ is also a symmetric matrix.

⚡ PYQ Context: Used in matrix polynomial evaluation questions in UP PGT.
05

Shortcut 5: Product of Two Symmetric Matrices

$$ (AB)^T = B^T A^T = BA $$

Exam Rule: The product of two symmetric matrices $A$ and $B$ is symmetric if and only if they commute, i.e., $AB = BA$.

⚡ PYQ Context: High-frequency conceptual trap in competitive examinations.
06

Shortcut 6: Eigenvectors of Distinct Eigenvalues

$$ X_i^T X_j = 0 \quad (\text{for } \lambda_i \neq \lambda_j) $$

Exam Rule: Eigenvectors corresponding to distinct eigenvalues of a real symmetric matrix are mutually orthogonal.

⚡ PYQ Context: Crucial for advanced linear algebra matching questions in LT Grade exams.

Pillar 4 Classic Exam Traps & Counterexamples

Where examiners construct tricky trap options and how to safeguard your marks:

⚠️ EXAM TRAP #01

Trap 1: Product Symmetry Assumption

Common Pitfall: Candidates assume that if $A$ and $B$ are symmetric, then $AB$ is always symmetric.

Counterexample: $A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 2 \end{pmatrix}, B = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$

Why it fails: Matrix multiplication is non-commutative; $(AB)^T = BA \neq AB$ unless $AB = BA$.

🛡️ Exam Defense: Never assume $AB$ is symmetric unless explicitly given that $A$ and $B$ commute ($AB = BA$).
⚠️ EXAM TRAP #02

Trap 2: Skew-Symmetric Diagonal Confusion

Common Pitfall: Confusing symmetric matrix properties with skew-symmetric matrix properties, particularly regarding diagonal elements.

Counterexample: Skew-symmetric matrices have zeros on the main diagonal, whereas symmetric matrices have no such restriction.

Why it fails: For a skew-symmetric matrix $A^T = -A$, setting $i=j$ gives $a_{ii} = -a_{ii} \implies a_{ii} = 0$. Symmetric matrices require $a_{ii} = a_{ii}$ which gives no constraint.

🛡️ Exam Defense: Check diagonal elements: arbitrary reals for symmetric, strictly zero for skew-symmetric.

Pillar 6 Solved Canonical Exam Problems (Dual-Method)

Comparing the formal academic proof against the rapid competitive exam elimination trick:

CANONICAL PROBLEM 01 Dual-Method Breakdown

If $A = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & x \\ 3 & 4 \end{pmatrix}$ is a symmetric matrix, then the value of $x$ is:

(A) 2(B) 3(C) 4(D) 6
✓ Correct Answer: (B)
📚 Method A: Formal Textbook Derivation
Step 1: Compute $A^T = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 3 \\ x & 4 \end{pmatrix}$. Step 2: Use the symmetry condition $A = A^T$. Step 3: Equate corresponding elements: $x = 3$. Step 4: Verify that all diagonal and off-diagonal conditions hold.
⚡ Method B: 25-Second Exam Shortcut
Instantly equate the off-diagonal element $a_{12}$ to $a_{21}$. Hence $x = 3$. Elapsed time: 5 seconds.
💡 Key Takeaway: Look out for algebraic expressions in place of numbers; equate them directly.
CANONICAL PROBLEM 02 Dual-Method Breakdown

The dimension of the vector space of all symmetric matrices of order $n \times n$ over the field of real numbers $\mathbb{R}$ is:

(A) $n^2$(B) $\frac{n(n-1)}{2}$(C) $\frac{n(n+1)}{2}$(D) $n^2 - n$
✓ Correct Answer: (C)
📚 Method A: Formal Textbook Derivation
A symmetric matrix has $n$ independent elements on the main diagonal and $\frac{n(n-1)}{2}$ independent elements above the main diagonal. Summing these gives $n + \frac{n(n-1)}{2} = \frac{n(n+1)}{2}$.
⚡ Method B: 25-Second Exam Shortcut
Recall standard result for symmetric matrix dimension: $\frac{n(n+1)}{2}$. For skew-symmetric, it is $\frac{n(n-1)}{2}$.
💡 Key Takeaway: Memorize dimensions of standard matrix spaces for rapid answering in PGT exams.

Pillar 7 10 Practice MCQs for Self-Assessment

Q1
If $A$ is a square matrix, then $A + A^T$ is always a:
A Symmetric matrix
B Skew-symmetric matrix
C Diagonal matrix
D Identity matrix
Derivation & Analysis:

Let $P = A + A^T$. Then $P^T = (A + A^T)^T = A^T + (A^T)^T = A^T + A = A + A^T = P$. Thus $P$ is symmetric.

Q2
If $A$ is a square matrix, then $A - A^T$ is always a:
A Symmetric matrix
B Skew-symmetric matrix
C Scalar matrix
D Null matrix
Derivation & Analysis:

Let $Q = A - A^T$. Then $Q^T = (A - A^T)^T = A^T - (A^T)^T = A^T - A = -(A - A^T) = -Q$. Thus $Q$ is skew-symmetric.

Q3
If $A$ and $B$ are symmetric matrices of the same order, then $AB - BA$ is a:
A Symmetric matrix
B Skew-symmetric matrix
C Zero matrix
D Identity matrix
Derivation & Analysis:

Let $X = AB - BA$. Then $X^T = (AB - BA)^T = (AB)^T - (BA)^T = B^T A^T - A^T B^T = BA - AB = -(AB - BA) = -X$. Hence $X$ is skew-symmetric.

Q4
The eigenvalues of a real symmetric matrix are always:
A Purely imaginary
B Real numbers
C Zero
D Complex conjugates with modulus 1
Derivation & Analysis:

A foundational spectral theorem states that every real symmetric matrix has exclusively real eigenvalues.

Q5
If $A$ is a symmetric matrix such that $A^2 = I$, then $A^{-1}$ is equal to:
A $-A$
B $A$
C $I$
D $2A$
Derivation & Analysis:

Given $A^2 = I$, multiplying by $A^{-1}$ (or noting $A^T = A$ and $A A = I$) yields $A^{-1} = A$.

Q6
What is the number of independent elements in a symmetric matrix of order $4 \times 4$?
A 16
B 6
C 10
D 12
Derivation & Analysis:

Using formula $\frac{n(n+1)}{2}$ for $n=4$: $\frac{4(5)}{2} = 10$.

Q7
If $A$ is a skew-symmetric matrix of odd order $n$, then its determinant $\det(A)$ is:
A 1
B -1
C 0
D Depends on entries
Derivation & Analysis:

For any skew-symmetric matrix of odd order, $\det(A) = \det(A^T) = \det(-A) = (-1)^n \det(A) = -\det(A) \implies 2\det(A) = 0 \implies \det(A) = 0$.

Q8
If $A$ and $B$ are symmetric matrices of the same order, then $AB$ is symmetric if and only if:
A $A = B$
B $AB = -BA$
C $AB = BA$
D $A+B = 0$
Derivation & Analysis:

$(AB)^T = B^T A^T = BA$. For $AB$ to be symmetric, we need $(AB)^T = AB$, which means $BA = AB$.

Q9
If $A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 2 & 4 & 5 \\ 3 & 5 & 6 \end{pmatrix}$, then $A$ is:
A Skew-symmetric
B Symmetric
C Orthogonal
D Diagonal
Derivation & Analysis:

Checking off-diagonal elements: $a_{12} = 2 = a_{21}$, $a_{13} = 3 = a_{31}$, $a_{23} = 5 = a_{32}$. Thus $A = A^T$, so $A$ is symmetric.

Q10
The trace of a skew-symmetric matrix is always:
A Positive
B Negative
C Zero
D One
Derivation & Analysis:

The diagonal elements of a skew-symmetric matrix are all zero. Therefore, their sum (the trace) is zero.

Pillar 8 Frequently Asked Questions

No. Eigenvalues can have algebraic multiplicities greater than 1. However, the spectral theorem guarantees that even with repeated eigenvalues, we can always find an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors.

Candidates frequently assume that the product of two symmetric matrices is symmetric. It is vital to remember that $(AB)^T = BA$, so $AB$ is symmetric if and only if $A$ and $B$ commute.

Typically, 4 to 6 questions appear directly from matrices, determinants, and properties of special matrices (symmetric, skew-symmetric, orthogonal) in each exam cycle.

Use direct element-wise comparison ($a_{ij} = a_{ji}$) for symmetric matrices and avoid calculating full determinants or inverses unless specifically required.
