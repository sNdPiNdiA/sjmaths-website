Inverse of a square matrix

The inverse of a square matrix $A$ of order $n$, denoted by $A^{-1}$, is the unique matrix satisfying $A A^{-1} = A^{-1} A = I_n$, existing if and only if $A$ is non-singular ($\det(A) \neq 0$). This topic covers matrix inversion properties, adjugate relations, and elementary transformations.

Definition and statement

Theorem of Matrix Invertibility and Adjugate Formula
Let $A$ be a square matrix of order $n$. $A$ is invertible (non-singular) if and only if $\det(A) \neq 0$. Furthermore, the inverse is given explicitly by $$A^{-1} = \frac{1}{\det(A)} \text{adj}(A)$$ where $\text{adj}(A)$ is the transpose of the cofactor matrix of $A$.

📐 Geometric Interpretation

Geometrically, a square matrix represents a linear transformation of vector space $\mathbb{R}^n$. The determinant measures the scaling factor of volumes. A non-zero determinant ensures the transformation is bijective (one-to-one and onto), meaning no dimensions collapse. The inverse matrix $A^{-1}$ represents the undoing or reversal of this geometric transformation.

⚙️ Algebraic & Structural Meaning

Algebraically, invertible matrices form a group under matrix multiplication known as the General Linear Group $\text{GL}_n(\mathbb{F})$. Inverting matrices allows for the systematic solution of consistent systems of linear equations $AX = B$ via $X = A^{-1}B$ when $\det(A) \neq 0$.

Useful results and shortcuts

Use these identities and results when their conditions apply:

01

Inverse of a 2x2 Matrix

$$ \text{If } A = \begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{pmatrix}, \text{ then } A^{-1} = \frac{1}{ad - bc} \begin{pmatrix} d & -b \\ -c & a \end{pmatrix} $$

Rule: Swap the principal diagonal elements, change the signs of the off-diagonal elements, and divide by the determinant ($ad - bc$).

Application: Directly tested in UP TGT/PGT for rapid calculation of matrix inverses and parameter determination.
02

Inverse of Diagonal Matrices

$$ \text{If } D = \text{diag}(d_1, d_2, \dots, d_n), \text{ then } D^{-1} = \text{diag}\left(\frac{1}{d_1}, \frac{1}{d_2}, \dots, \frac{1}{d_n}\right) $$

Rule: Invert each diagonal entry directly, provided none of them equals zero.

03

Reversal Law for Inverses

$$ (AB)^{-1} = B^{-1}A^{-1} $$

Rule: The inverse of a product is the product of the inverses in reverse order. Extends to $(ABC)^{-1} = C^{-1}B^{-1}A^{-1}$.

04

Determinant and Transpose Inverse Properties

$$ \det(A^{-1}) = (\det(A))^{-1}, \quad (A^T)^{-1} = (A^{-1})^T $$

Rule: Taking the inverse commutes with transposition and inverts the determinant value.

Application: Used in properties-based assertion-reason and rapid calculation questions.
05

Inverse of Scalar Multiples and Powers

$$ (kA)^{-1} = \frac{1}{k}A^{-1}, \quad (A^k)^{-1} = (A^{-1})^k = A^{-k} $$

Rule: Scalar scales reciprocally; matrix power inverts alongside the matrix.

Application: Appears in polynomial matrix equations (Cayley-Hamilton theorem applications).
06

Adjugate Determinant & Power Rules

$$ \det(\text{adj}(A)) = (\det(A))^{n-1}, \quad \text{adj}(\text{adj}(A)) = (\det(A))^{n-2}A $$

Rule: Direct exponent rules for adjugates of order $n$ matrices saving minutes of manual expansion.

Common errors and counterexamples

These examples show how a conclusion can fail when a condition is missing:

⚠️ COMMON ERROR #01

Distributive Fallacy of Inverses

Common Pitfall: Assuming $(A + B)^{-1} = A^{-1} + B^{-1}$, which is generally false in matrix algebra.

Counterexample: Let $A = I, B = -I$, then $A+B = 0$ (singular, non-invertible), but $A^{-1} + B^{-1}$ exists.

Why it fails: Matrix addition and multiplication do not satisfy standard real number distributive laws for inverses.

🛡️ Exam Defense: Never split the inverse across a sum. Only apply $(AB)^{-1} = B^{-1}A^{-1}$ across products.
⚠️ COMMON ERROR #02

Singular Matrix Division Trap

Common Pitfall: Attempting to find $A^{-1}$ when $\det(A) = 0$ or canceling matrices without checking if they are invertible.

Counterexample: If $AB = AC$ and $A \neq 0$, we cannot conclude $B = C$ unless $A$ is invertible.

Why it fails: Zero divisors exist in matrix rings; singular matrices destroy information, making left/right cancellation invalid.

🛡️ Exam Defense: Always compute $\det(A)$ first before attempting any inversion or cancellation step.

Solved examples

Each problem includes a full solution and a shorter method:

CANONICAL PROBLEM 01 Dual-Method Breakdown

If $A = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 3 \\ 1 & 4 \end{pmatrix}$, find $A^{-1}$.

(A) $\frac{1}{5} \begin{pmatrix} 4 & -3 \\ -1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$(B) $\frac{1}{5} \begin{pmatrix} 2 & -3 \\ -1 & 4 \end{pmatrix}$(C) $\begin{pmatrix} 4 & -3 \\ -1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$(D) $\frac{1}{5} \begin{pmatrix} -4 & 3 \\ 1 & -2 \end{pmatrix}$
✓ Correct Answer: (A)
📚 Method A: Formal Textbook Derivation
Step 1: Compute $\det(A) = (2)(4) - (3)(1) = 8 - 3 = 5 \neq 0$. Step 2: Find adjugate by swapping diagonal elements and negating off-diagonal elements: $\text{adj}(A) = \begin{pmatrix} 4 & -3 \\ -1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$. Step 3: Apply $A^{-1} = \frac{1}{5} \begin{pmatrix} 4 & -3 \\ -1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$.
⚡ Method B: 25-Second Exam Shortcut
Use 2x2 formula instantly: determinant is 5, swap 2 and 4, negate 3 and 1.
💡 Tip: Check determinant sign carefully to avoid sign errors in options.
CANONICAL PROBLEM 02 Dual-Method Breakdown

If $A$ is a square matrix of order $3$ such that $\det(A) = 5$, find $\det(\text{adj}(A))$.

(A) 5(B) 25(C) 125(D) 1/5
✓ Correct Answer: (B)
📚 Method A: Formal Textbook Derivation
Use the standard property $\det(\text{adj}(A)) = (\det(A))^{n-1}$. Here $n = 3$, so $\det(\text{adj}(A)) = (5)^{3-1} = 5^2 = 25$.
⚡ Method B: 25-Second Exam Shortcut
Direct formula application: $(\det(A))^{n-1} = 5^2 = 25$. Takes 5 seconds.
💡 Tip: Memorize the order $n$ dependency in adjugate determinant formulas.

Practice questions

Choose an answer to check it and see the solution:

Q1
MCQ 1: If $A$ is an invertible square matrix of order $n$, then $\det(A^{-1})$ is equal to:
A (A) $\det(A)$
B (B) $(\det(A))^{-1}$
C (C) $n \det(A)$
D (D) $0$
Derivation & Analysis:

Since $A A^{-1} = I$, taking determinants gives $\det(A)\det(A^{-1}) = \det(I) = 1$, hence $\det(A^{-1}) = \frac{1}{\det(A)} = (\det(A))^{-1}$.

Q2
MCQ 2: The inverse of the matrix $A = \begin{pmatrix} \cos \theta & -\sin \theta \\ \sin \theta & \cos \theta \end{pmatrix}$ is:
A (A) $\begin{pmatrix} \cos \theta & \sin \theta \\ -\sin \theta & \cos \theta \end{pmatrix}$
B (B) $\begin{pmatrix} -\cos \theta & \sin \theta \\ -\sin \theta & -\cos \theta \end{pmatrix}$
C (C) $\begin{pmatrix} \cos \theta & -\sin \theta \\ \sin \theta & \cos \theta \end{pmatrix}$
D (D) $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$
Derivation & Analysis:

The determinant is $\cos^2 \theta + \sin^2 \theta = 1$. Using the 2x2 inverse formula, we swap diagonal elements and negate off-diagonal elements, yielding the transpose which is $A^T$ (orthogonal matrix property $A^{-1} = A^T$).

Q3
MCQ 3: If $A$ is a non-singular matrix of order $n$, then $\text{adj}(\text{adj}(A))$ is equal to:
A (A) $(\det(A))^{n-1} A$
B (B) $(\det(A))^{n-2} A$
C (C) $(\det(A))^{n} A^{-1}$
D (D) $\text{adj}(A)
Derivation & Analysis:

By standard matrix identity, $\text{adj}(\text{adj}(A)) = (\det(A))^{n-2} A$ for any non-singular square matrix of order $n \ge 2$.

Q4
MCQ 4: If $A^2 - 3A + I = 0$, then the inverse matrix $A^{-1}$ is:
A (A) $3I - A$
B (B) $A - 3I$
C (C) $3I + A$
D (D) $I - 3A$
Derivation & Analysis:

Given $A^2 - 3A + I = 0$, multiply by $A^{-1}$: $A - 3I + A^{-1} = 0 \implies A^{-1} = 3I - A$.

Q5
MCQ 5: Which of the following matrices is always symmetric if $A$ is invertible and symmetric?
A (A) $\text{adj}(A)$
B (B) $A^{-1}$
C (C) Both (A) and (B)
D (D) None of these
Derivation & Analysis:

If $A$ is symmetric ($A^T = A$), then $A^{-1}$ is also symmetric because $(A^{-1})^T = (A^T)^{-1} = A^{-1}$, and $\text{adj}(A)$ is symmetric as well.

Q6
MCQ 6: If $A$ and $B$ are invertible matrices of the same order, then $(AB)^{-1}$ is:
A (A) $A^{-1}B^{-1}$
B (B) $B^{-1}A^{-1}$
C (C) $AB$
D (D) $BA$
Derivation & Analysis:

By the reversal law of matrix inversion, $(AB)^{-1} = B^{-1}A^{-1}$ since $(B^{-1}A^{-1})(AB) = B^{-1}(A^{-1}A)B = B^{-1}IB = B^{-1}B = I$.

Q7
MCQ 7: If $\det(A) = 3$ and order of $A$ is $3$, then $\det(5A^{-1})$ is:
A (A) $\frac{125}{3}$
B (B) $\frac{25}{3}$
C (C) $125$
D (D) $\frac{5}{3}$
Derivation & Analysis:

For an order $n$ matrix, $\det(kA) = k^n \det(A)$. Thus $\det(5A^{-1}) = 5^3 \det(A^{-1}) = 125 \times \frac{1}{3} = \frac{125}{3}$.

Q8
MCQ 8: A square matrix $A$ is orthogonal if and only if:
A (A) $A^{-1} = A^T$
B (B) $A^{-1} = A$
C (C) $A^2 = I$
D (D) $\det(A) = 0$
Derivation & Analysis:

By definition, an orthogonal matrix satisfies $A^T A = I$, which implies $A^{-1} = A^T$.

Q9
MCQ 9: If $A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 4 \end{pmatrix}$, what is the trace of $A^{-1}$?
A (A) $-2$
B (B) $2$
C (C) $-\frac{5}{2}$
D (D) $\frac{5}{2}$
Derivation & Analysis:

$\det(A) = 4 - 6 = -2$. $A^{-1} = \frac{1}{-2} \begin{pmatrix} 4 & -2 \\ -3 & 1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} -2 & 1 \\ 3/2 & -1/2 \end{pmatrix}$. Trace = $-2 - 1/2 = -5/2$.

Q10
MCQ 10: If $A$ is a skew-symmetric matrix of odd order $n$, then $A$ is:
A (A) Invertible
B (B) Singular / Non-invertible
C (C) Identity matrix
D (D) Orthogonal
Derivation & Analysis:

The determinant of a skew-symmetric matrix of odd order is always $0$. Since $\det(A) = 0$, $A$ is singular and thus non-invertible.

Common questions

No. By definition, matrix inversion requires the matrix to be square ($n \times n$) so that left and right inverses coincide and the determinant is defined.

If time permits, use the adjugate formula $A^{-1} = \frac{\text{adj}(A)}{\det(A)}$. However, in competitive exams, questions on 3x3 inverses are usually structured around the Cayley-Hamilton theorem or simple structural properties where full cofactors are not required.

Matrix multiplication is non-commutative ($AB \neq BA$). To undo the combined transformation $AB$, you must reverse the operations in opposite order: first undo $B$ (using $B^{-1}$), then undo $A$ (using $A^{-1}$).
