1. Algebra — Matrices and Determinants

Non-singular matrices

A square matrix $A$ of order $n$ is termed non-singular if and only if its determinant is non-zero, i.e., $\det(A) \neq 0$. Geometrically, this signifies that the linear transformation represented by $A$ is bijective and preserves full dimensional volume without collapsing space. This topic covers non-singular matrices.

Definition and statement

Invertibility and Determinant Criterion for Non-Singular Matrices
Let $A$ be a square matrix of order $n$ over a field $F$. The following statements are equivalent: (1) $A$ is non-singular, i.e., $|A| \neq 0$; (2) $A$ is invertible, meaning there exists a unique matrix $A^{-1}$ such that $AA^{-1} = A^{-1}A = I_n$; (3) The rank of $A$ is equal to $n$ ($\text{rank}(A) = n$); (4) The homogeneous system $AX = 0$ possesses only the trivial solution $X = 0$.

📐 Geometric Interpretation

Geometrically, a non-singular matrix represents an invertible linear transformation of $\mathbb{R}^n$ into itself. It maps linearly independent vectors to linearly independent vectors, scales volumes by a non-zero factor $|\det(A)|$, and never collapses an $n$-dimensional region into a lower-dimensional subspace.

⚙️ Algebraic & Structural Meaning

Algebraically, non-singular matrices form a group under matrix multiplication known as the General Linear Group $GL(n, F)$. They ensure uniqueness of solutions to linear systems via Cramer's Rule and matrix inversion.

Useful results and shortcuts

Use these identities and results when their conditions apply:

01

Inverse of a 2x2 Matrix

$$A = \begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{pmatrix} \implies A^{-1} = \frac{1}{ad - bc} \begin{pmatrix} d & -b \\ -c & a \end{pmatrix}$$

Rule: Swap principal diagonal elements, change signs of off-diagonal elements, and divide by the determinant $ad - bc \neq 0$.

Application: Frequently asked in UP PGT for quick calculation of matrix inverses or satisfying polynomial equations.
02

Determinant of Product and Powers

$$|AB| = |A||B|, \quad |A^k| = (|A|)^k, \quad |A^{-1}| = \frac{1}{|A|}$$

Rule: Never compute matrices explicitly for powers or products when evaluating determinants or non-singularity; evaluate scalar determinants independently.

03

Adjoint Matrix Properties

$$|\text{adj}(A)| = |A|^{n-1}, \quad \text{adj}(\text{adj}(A)) = |A|^{n-2}A$$

Rule: Use the order reduction formula for the determinant of an adjugate matrix to solve parameter values instantly.

Application: Directly tested in UP TGT/PGT matching matrix dimensions.
04

Inverse of Diagonal and Triangular Matrices

$$A = \text{diag}(d_1, d_2, \dots, d_n) \implies A^{-1} = \text{diag}(d_1^{-1}, d_2^{-1}, \dots, d_n^{-1})$$

Rule: For diagonal or triangular matrices, the determinant is the product of diagonal entries. Non-singularity requires all diagonal entries to be non-zero.

05

Trace and Eigenvalue Determinant Link

$$|A| = \prod_{i=1}^{n} \lambda_i \neq 0 \iff \lambda_i \neq 0 \quad \forall i$$

Rule: A matrix is non-singular if and only if none of its eigenvalues are zero.

Application: Crucial for advanced PGT questions involving characteristic equations.
06

Rapid Elimination via Zero Rows/Columns

$$\text{If any row/column of } A \text{ is zero, } |A| = 0$$

Rule: Immediately classify matrices with proportional rows or zero rows as singular without full expansion.

Common errors and counterexamples

These examples show how a conclusion can fail when a condition is missing:

⚠️ COMMON ERROR #01

Confusion Between Singular and Zero Matrix

Common Pitfall: Candidates assume a singular matrix must be a zero matrix.

Counterexample: $A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 4 \end{pmatrix}$

Why it fails: Here $|A| = (1)(4) - (2)(2) = 0$, so $A$ is singular, yet $A \neq 0$.

🛡️ Exam Defense: Remember that singularity only requires $|A| = 0$, not that all entries are zero.
⚠️ COMMON ERROR #02

Linearity Property of Determinants

Common Pitfall: Assuming $|A + B| = |A| + |B|$.

Counterexample: $A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}, B = \begin{pmatrix} -1 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 \end{pmatrix}$

Why it fails: $|A| = 1, |B| = 1$, but $|A + B| = |0| = 0 \neq |A| + |B| = 2$.

🛡️ Exam Defense: Only multiplicative property $|AB| = |A||B|$ holds universally for determinants.

Solved examples

Each problem includes a full solution and a shorter method:

CANONICAL PROBLEM 01 Dual-Method Breakdown

For what value of $k$ is the matrix $A = \begin{pmatrix} k & 2 \\ 3 & 6 \end{pmatrix}$ singular?

(A) $k = 1$(B) $k = 2$(C) $k = 0$(D) $k = 3$
✓ Correct Answer: (B)
📚 Method A: Formal Textbook Derivation
Step 1: Set determinant to zero for singularity. Step 2: $|A| = (k)(6) - (2)(3) = 6k - 6$. Step 3: $6k - 6 = 0 \implies k = 1$. Wait, let's re-verify: $(k)(6) - (2)(3) = 6k - 6 = 0 \implies k = 1$. Let's check options: if $k=1$, matrix is $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 6 \end{pmatrix}$, determinant is $6 - 6 = 0$. Hence option (A) is correct.
⚡ Method B: 25-Second Exam Shortcut
Observe that column 2 is twice column 1 if $k=1$ (since $\begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ 6 \end{pmatrix} = 2 \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 3 \end{pmatrix}$), so $k=1$.
💡 Tip: Check for proportional rows/columns for instant answers in $2 \times 2$ matrices.
CANONICAL PROBLEM 02 Dual-Method Breakdown

If $A$ is a non-singular square matrix of order $3$ with $|A| = 5$, then find $|3A|$.

(A) $15$(B) $45$(C) $135$(D) $27$
✓ Correct Answer: (C)
📚 Method A: Formal Textbook Derivation
Use the property $|kA| = k^n |A|$ where $n$ is the order of the matrix. Here $k = 3$, $n = 3$, and $|A| = 5$. Thus, $|3A| = 3^3 \times |A| = 27 \times 5 = 135$.
⚡ Method B: 25-Second Exam Shortcut
Instantly compute $3^3 \times 5 = 27 \times 5 = 135$ using scalar multiple determinant rule.
💡 Tip: Do not multiply every entry by 3 before taking the determinant; apply the scalar power formula directly.

Practice questions

Choose an answer to check it and see the solution:

Q1
MCQ 1: If $A$ and $B$ are non-singular square matrices of the same order, then $(AB)^{-1}$ is equal to:
A $A^{-1}B^{-1}$
B $B^{-1}A^{-1}$
C $AB$
D $B A$
Derivation & Analysis:

By standard matrix inverse reversal law, $(AB)^{-1} = B^{-1}A^{-1}$ because $(B^{-1}A^{-1})(AB) = B^{-1}(A^{-1}A)B = B^{-1}IB = B^{-1}B = I$.

Q2
MCQ 2: If $A$ is a square matrix such that $A^2 - A + I = 0$, then $A^{-1}$ equals:
A $A$
B $I - A$
C $A - I$
D $-A$
Derivation & Analysis:

Given $A^2 - A + I = 0$. Multiply by $A^{-1}$: $A - I + A^{-1} = 0 \implies A^{-1} = I - A$. Wait, let's check: $A - I + A^{-1} = 0 \implies A^{-1} = I - A$. Let's verify with options: if option is $I - A$, then $A^{-1} = I - A$.

Q3
MCQ 3: The determinant of an orthogonal matrix is always:
A $0$
B $1$
C $-1$
D $1$ or $-1$
Derivation & Analysis:

For an orthogonal matrix $A$, $A^T A = I$. Taking determinants: $|A^T A| = |I| \implies |A^T||A| = 1 \implies |A|^2 = 1 \implies |A| = \pm 1$.

Q4
MCQ 4: If $A$ is a non-singular matrix of order $n$, then $|\text{adj}(A)|$ is equal to:
A $|A|$
B $|A|^{n-1}$
C $|A|^n$
D $|A|^{1-n}$
Derivation & Analysis:

The standard adjugate determinant property states that $|\text{adj}(A)| = |A|^{n-1}$ for an $n \times n$ non-singular matrix.

Q5
MCQ 5: Which of the following matrices is singular?
A $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 4 \end{pmatrix}$
B $\begin{pmatrix} 2 & 3 \\ 4 & 6 \end{pmatrix}$
C $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$
D $\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$
Derivation & Analysis:

For $\begin{pmatrix} 2 & 3 \\ 4 & 6 \end{pmatrix}$, determinant is $(2)(6) - (3)(4) = 12 - 12 = 0$. Thus it is singular.

Q6
MCQ 6: If $A$ is a non-singular matrix, then $\text{rank}(A)$ is:
A $0$
B $n-1$
C $n$
D Depends on entries
Derivation & Analysis:

A non-singular matrix of order $n$ has full rank, i.e., $\text{rank}(A) = n$.

Q7
MCQ 7: If $A$ is a skew-symmetric matrix of odd order $n$, then $A$ is always:
A Non-singular
B Singular
C Identity
D Diagonal
Derivation & Analysis:

The determinant of a skew-symmetric matrix of odd order is always zero ($|A| = |-A^T| = (-1)^n |A| = -|A| \implies 2|A| = 0 \implies |A| = 0$). Hence it is always singular.

Q8
MCQ 8: If $|A| = 5$ and order of $A$ is $3$, then $|\text{adj}(\text{adj}(A))|$ is:
A $5$
B $5^2$
C $5^4$
D $5^8$
Derivation & Analysis:

Using formula $|\text{adj}(\text{adj}(A))| = |A|^{(n-1)^2}$. For $n=3$, $(3-1)^2 = 4$. Thus $|A|^4 = 5^4$.

Q9
MCQ 9: If $A$ is a non-singular matrix, then $(A^T)^{-1}$ is equal to:
A $A$
B $A^T$
C $(A^{-1})^T$
D $-A^{-1}$
Derivation & Analysis:

Transposition and inversion commute: $(A^T)^{-1} = (A^{-1})^T$.

Q10
MCQ 10: Let $A$ be a $3 \times 3$ matrix such that $|A| = 4$. Then the value of $|A \cdot \text{adj}(A)|$ is:
A $4$
B $16$
C $64$
D $256$
Derivation & Analysis:

We know $A \cdot \text{adj}(A) = |A|I_n$. Taking determinant of both sides: $|A \cdot \text{adj}(A)| = ||A|I_n| = |A|^n |I_n| = 4^3 \times 1 = 64$.

Candidates frequently confuse scalar multiplication formulas such as $|kA| = k^n|A|$ with matrix addition or misapply adjugate determinant properties like $|\text{adj}(A)| = |A|^{n-1}$ by forgetting to adjust for the matrix order $n$.

Candidates must memorize standard formulas for adjugates, inverse properties, and block matrix determinants, and actively look for row/column dependencies to spot singular matrices instantly without brute-force expansion.
