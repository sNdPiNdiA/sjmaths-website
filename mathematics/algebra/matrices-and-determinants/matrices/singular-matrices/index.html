1. Algebra — Matrices and Determinants

Singular matrices

A square matrix is termed singular if and only if its determinant vanishes identically, signifying algebraic degeneracy and linear dependence among its row or column vectors. Mastery of singular matrices, their rank deficiency, and characteristic root properties forms a high-yield scoring cornerstone in UP PGT/TGT, DSSSB, and KVS competitive examinations.

Target Exams: UP PGT Mathematics UP TGT Mathematics DSSSB PGT / TGT KVS / NVS PGT LT Grade
📖 Self-Study Mastery Status
○ Mark Topic as Mastered

Pillar 1 Rigorous Definition & Mathematical Statement

Characterization Theorem for Singular Matrices
Let $A$ be an $n \times n$ square matrix over a field $\mathbb{F}$. The following statements are equivalent: (1) $A$ is singular, i.e., $\det(A) = 0$; (2) $\text{rank}(A) < n$; (3) The homogeneous system $AX = 0$ possesses a non-trivial solution $X \neq 0$; (4) Zero is an eigenvalue of $A$, i.e., $\det(A - 0I) = 0$; (5) $A$ is non-invertible, meaning no matrix $A^{-1}$ exists such that $AA^{-1} = A^{-1}A = I$.

📐 Geometric Interpretation

Geometrically, an $n \times n$ singular matrix represents a linear transformation that collapses the entire $n$-dimensional space $\mathbb{R}^n$ into a lower-dimensional subspace (e.g., mapping 3D space onto a plane or line), resulting in zero volume scaling factor.

⚙️ Algebraic & Structural Meaning

Algebraically, singular matrices form a non-ideal algebraic subvariety defined by a single polynomial equation $\det(A) = 0$ within the vector space of matrices $M_n(\mathbb{F})$, destroying multiplicative group structure under inversion.

Pillar 2 Hypotheses & Condition Checklist

Every hypothesis is strictly non-negotiable. Inspect the domain type and the exact consequence if a condition fails:

Pillar 3 High-Yield Exam Results & Direct Shortcuts

Standard 15-to-30 second shortcuts frequently tested in UP TGT/PGT and DSSSB:

01

Shortcut 1: Parameter Finding via Determinant Zero

$$ \det \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & \lambda \\ 1 & 1 & 3 \\ 2 & 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix} = 0 \implies \lambda = \text{solve linear equation} $$

Exam Rule: To find an unknown parameter making a matrix singular, expand the determinant directly along the row/column with variables and equate to zero in under 20 seconds.

⚡ PYQ Context: Frequently asked in UP PGT and TGT Mathematics to test parameter calculation for non-invertibility.
02

Shortcut 2: Row/Column Linear Dependence

$$ R_3 = k_1 R_1 + k_2 R_2 \implies \det(A) = 0 $$

Exam Rule: If any row (or column) can be expressed as a linear combination of other rows (or columns), or if two rows are identical/proportional, the matrix is instantly singular.

⚡ PYQ Context: Directly tested in KVS PGT and DSSSB matrix property MCQs to bypass heavy cofactor expansions.
03

Shortcut 3: Eigenvalue Product Property

$$ \det(A) = \prod_{i=1}^n \lambda_i = 0 \iff \text{at least one } \lambda_i = 0 $$

Exam Rule: A matrix is singular if and only if at least one of its eigenvalues is zero. Look for triangular matrices or inspect trace/determinant relations.

⚡ PYQ Context: Standard UP PGT pattern for higher-order matrix singularity analysis.
04

Shortcut 4: Skew-Symmetric Matrix of Odd Order

$$ A^T = -A, \quad \text{order } n \text{ is odd} \implies \det(A) = 0 $$

Exam Rule: Any skew-symmetric matrix of odd dimension is always singular because $\det(-A) = (-1)^n \det(A) = - \det(A) \implies \det(A) = 0$.

⚡ PYQ Context: A classic 5-second conceptual question appearing across all teaching recruitment exams.
05

Shortcut 5: Rank-Nullity Theorem Application

$$ \text{Nullity}(A) = n - \text{rank}(A) \ge 1 $$

Exam Rule: For a singular matrix, the dimension of the null space (solution space of $AX=0$) is at least 1, guaranteeing non-zero solutions.

⚡ PYQ Context: Tested in advanced PGT examinations focusing on linear transformations.
06

Shortcut 6: Product of Singular Matrices

$$ \det(AB) = \det(A)\det(B) = 0 \text{ if either } \det(A)=0 \text{ or } \det(B)=0 $$

Exam Rule: The product of two square matrices is singular if at least one factor is singular, without needing to compute matrix multiplication $AB$.

⚡ PYQ Context: Time-saving elimination trick for multi-matrix operator questions.

Pillar 4 Classic Exam Traps & Counterexamples

Where examiners construct tricky trap options and how to safeguard your marks:

⚠️ EXAM TRAP #01

Trap 1: Assuming Addition Preserves Singularity

Common Pitfall: Candidates often assume that if $A$ and $B$ are singular, then $A + B$ is also singular.

Counterexample: $A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \quad B = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$

Why it fails: Both $A$ and $B$ are singular ($\det=0$), but $A + B = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} = I$, which is non-singular ($\det=1$).

🛡️ Exam Defense: Never apply linearity properties to determinants or singularity under matrix addition; $\det(A+B) \neq \det(A) + \det(B)$.
⚠️ EXAM TRAP #02

Trap 2: Confusing Non-Singular with Positive Definite

Common Pitfall: Assuming that a non-singular matrix must have positive eigenvalues or positive determinant.

Counterexample: $A = \begin{pmatrix} -1 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 \end{pmatrix}$

Why it fails: $A$ is non-singular ($\det(A) = 1 \neq 0$), but its eigenvalues are negative ($-1, -1$), proving non-singularity only requires $\det \neq 0$.

🛡️ Exam Defense: Remember that singularity depends strictly on whether the determinant equals zero, regardless of the signs of the entries or eigenvalues.

CANONICAL PROBLEM 01 Dual-Method Breakdown

If the matrix $A = \begin{pmatrix} k & 4 \\ 1 & k \end{pmatrix}$ is singular, then the possible values of $k$ are:

(A) $\pm 2$(B) $2, -2$(C) $\pm 4$(D) $0, 4$
✓ Correct Answer: (C)
📚 Method A: Formal Textbook Derivation
Step 1: Set $\det(A) = 0$. Step 2: Compute $k(k) - (4)(1) = 0 \implies k^2 - 4 = 0$. Step 3: Solve $k^2 = 4 \implies k = \pm 2$. Wait, recomputing: $k \cdot k - 4(1) = k^2 - 4 = 0 \implies k = \pm 2$. Let us correct option check: $\det = k^2 - 4 = 0 \implies k = \pm 2$. Let's set matrix as $\begin{pmatrix} k & 2 \\ 2 & k \end{pmatrix}$ so $\det = k^2 - 4 = 0 \implies k = \pm 2$. For $A = \begin{pmatrix} k & 4 \\ 1 & k \end{pmatrix}$, $\det = k^2 - 4 = 0 \implies k = \pm 2$. Thus option (A) is correct.
⚡ Method B: 25-Second Exam Shortcut
Instantly write $\det(A) = k^2 - 4 = 0 \implies k = \pm 2$. Mark option (A).
💡 Key Takeaway: Check arithmetic carefully when expanding $2 \times 2$ determinants under exam pressure.
CANONICAL PROBLEM 02 Dual-Method Breakdown

For what value of $\lambda$ is the matrix $A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & -2 & 3 \\ 1 & 2 & 1 \\ \lambda & -2 & 3 \end{pmatrix}$ singular?

(A) $1$(B) $0$(C) $2$(D) $-1$
✓ Correct Answer: (A)
📚 Method A: Formal Textbook Derivation
Step 1: Compute determinant $\det(A) = 0$. Notice that row 1 and row 3 have identical second and third columns if $\lambda = 1$. Specifically, if $R_1 = R_3$, then $\det(A) = 0$. Thus $\lambda = 1$.
⚡ Method B: 25-Second Exam Shortcut
Observe row 1: $(1, -2, 3)$ and row 3: $(\lambda, -2, 3)$. For linear dependence (identical rows/proportional rows), $\lambda = 1$.
💡 Key Takeaway: Always scan rows and columns for identical or proportional elements before expanding.

A $5$

Using the standard property $\det(adj(A)) = (\det(A))^{n-1}$, for $n=3$ and $\det(A)=5$, we get $\det(adj(A)) = 5^{3-1} = 5^2 = 25$.

A A symmetric matrix of even order
B A skew-symmetric matrix of odd order
C An orthogonal matrix
D An identity matrix
Derivation & Analysis:

A skew-symmetric matrix of odd order always has a determinant of zero because $\det(-A) = (-1)^n \det(A) = -\det(A)$ for odd $n$, forcing $\det(A) = 0$.

Q3
MCQ 3: If $A$ and $B$ are non-singular matrices of the same order, then $AB$ is:
A Always singular
B Always non-singular
C Always symmetric
D Always skew-symmetric
Derivation & Analysis:

By the multiplicative property of determinants, $\det(AB) = \det(A)\det(B)$. Since $\det(A) \neq 0$ and $\det(B) \neq 0$, $\det(AB) \neq 0$, meaning $AB$ is non-singular.

A No solution
B Unique solution

A singular matrix has $\det(A) = 0$, which implies the homogeneous system $AX = 0$ has a non-zero null space, yielding infinitely many non-trivial solutions.

Q8
MCQ 8: Assertion (A): Every diagonal matrix with at least one zero diagonal entry is singular. Reason (R): The determinant of a diagonal matrix is the product of its principal diagonal elements.
A Both (A) and (R) are true and (R) is the correct explanation of (A)

The determinant of a diagonal matrix is the product of its diagonal entries. If one entry is zero, the product is zero, making the matrix singular.

We have the fundamental identity $A(adj(A)) = (\det(A))I$. Since $A$ is singular, $\det(A) = 0$, which yields $0 \cdot I = 0$, the zero matrix.

Derivation & Analysis:

A singular matrix has $\det(A) = 0$, which implies the homogeneous system $AX = 0$ has a non-zero null space, yielding infinitely many non-trivial solutions.

Q6
MCQ 6: The determinant of a singular matrix of order $n$ is:
A $1$
B $0$
C $n$
D $(-1)^n$
Derivation & Analysis:

By definition, a square matrix is singular if and only if its determinant is exactly zero.

Q7
MCQ 7: If $A$ is a singular matrix of order $3$, what is the rank of $A$?
A $\text{rank}(A) = 3$
B $\text{rank}(A) \le 2$
C $\text{rank}(A) = 0$
D $\text{rank}(A) = 1$
Derivation & Analysis:

Since $A$ is singular of order 3, its determinant is zero, meaning its rank is strictly less than 3, i.e., $\text{rank}(A) \le 2$.

Q8
MCQ 8: Assertion (A): Every diagonal matrix with at least one zero diagonal entry is singular. Reason (R): The determinant of a diagonal matrix is the product of its principal diagonal elements.
A Both (A) and (R) are true and (R) is the correct explanation of (A)
B Both (A) and (R) are true but (R) is not the correct explanation
C (A) is true but (R) is false
D (A) is false but (R) is true
Derivation & Analysis:

The determinant of a diagonal matrix is the product of its diagonal entries. If one entry is zero, the product is zero, making the matrix singular.

Q9
MCQ 9: If $A$ is a $2 \times 2$ matrix with $\det(A) = 0$, which of the following must be true?
A $A = 0$
B The rows of $A$ are linearly dependent
C $A$ is symmetric
D $A$ is invertible
Derivation & Analysis:

A vanishing determinant in a square matrix signifies that its row vectors (and column vectors) are linearly dependent.

Q10
MCQ 10: If $A$ is a singular matrix, then $A(adj(A))$ is equal to:
A $I$
B $\det(A)I$
C The zero matrix $0$
D $A^{-1}$
Derivation & Analysis:

We have the fundamental identity $A(adj(A)) = (\det(A))I$. Since $A$ is singular, $\det(A) = 0$, which yields $0 \cdot I = 0$, the zero matrix.
