Composition of Velocity Vectors

The composition of velocity vectors governs how velocities combine under Galilean transformations, serving as a cornerstone of relative motion in vector analysis. This topic covers vector addition, parallelogram law, and relative velocity formulas.

Definition and statement

Parallelogram Law of Vector Addition & Relative Velocity Composition
Let $\mathbf{v}_1$ and $\mathbf{v}_2$ be two velocity vectors acting simultaneously on a particle, represented in magnitude and direction by the two adjacent sides of a parallelogram drawn from a point. Their resultant velocity $\mathbf{R} = \mathbf{v}_1 + \mathbf{v}_2$ is represented in magnitude and direction by the diagonal of the parallelogram passing through that point, with magnitude given by $$R = \sqrt{v_1^2 + v_2^2 + 2v_1v_2 \cos\theta}$$ where $\theta$ is the angle between $\mathbf{v}_1$ and $\mathbf{v}_2$.

📐 Geometric Interpretation

Geometrically, vector composition corresponds to the head-to-tail placement of directed line segments or completing the parallelogram. The resultant vector forms the diagonal, while the relative velocity vector $\mathbf{v}_{AB} = \mathbf{v}_A - \mathbf{v}_B$ represents the displacement vector difference in unit time.

⚙️ Algebraic & Structural Meaning

Algebraically, velocities obey commutative and associative laws under vector addition over the field of real scalars $\mathbb{R}$, allowing decomposition into orthogonal Cartesian components ($v_x \mathbf{i} + v_y \mathbf{j} + v_z \mathbf{k}$) for linear algebraic manipulation.

Useful results and shortcuts

Use these identities and results when their conditions apply:

01

Equal Magnitude Resultant Velocity

$$R = 2v \cos\left(\frac{\theta}{2}\right)$$

Rule: When two component velocities have equal magnitude $v_1 = v_2 = v$, the resultant magnitude simplifies immediately using half-angle trigonometric identity.

Application: Use this result in the stated type of problem.
02

Maximum and Minimum Resultants

$$R_{\text{max}} = v_1 + v_2, \quad R_{\text{min}} = |v_1 - v_2고$$

Rule: The extreme values of composite velocity occur when angle $\theta = 0^\circ$ (aligned) and $\theta = 180^\circ$ (opposite).

Application: Direct numerical questions in DSSSB and TGT regarding bounds of resultant speeds.
03

Perpendicular Component Direction Angle

$$\tan\alpha = \frac{v_2 \sin\theta}{v_1 + v_2 \cos\theta}$$

Rule: Use this direct tangent formula to find the angle $\alpha$ that the resultant vector makes with the first velocity vector $\mathbf{v}_1$.

Application: Standard river-swimmer crossing problems (shortest path vs. shortest time).
04

Shortest Path River Crossing Time

$$t = \frac{d}{\sqrt{v_s^2 - v_r^2}}$$ (when $v_s > v_r$)

Rule: For a swimmer/boat crossing a river of width $d$ orthogonally against current $v_r$ with swimming speed $v_s$, time is minimized via this exact formula.

Application: A classic recurring item in UP PGT Mathematics kinematics section.
05

Orthogonal Velocity Vectors

$$R = \sqrt{v_1^2 + v_2^2}, \quad \theta = 90^\circ$$

Rule: When two velocity vectors are perpendicular, drop the cosine term instantly from the general magnitude formula.

06

Rain-Man Relative Velocity Angle

$$\tan\theta = \frac{v_m}{v_r}$$

Rule: If rain falls vertically at $v_r$ and a man walks horizontally at $v_m$, he must hold his umbrella inclined at angle $\theta$ to the vertical given by this ratio.

Application: Instant option elimination in TGT/PGT vector application questions.

Common errors and counterexamples

These examples show how a conclusion can fail when a condition is missing:

⚠️ COMMON ERROR #01

Confusing Angle Between Vectors with Angle with Resultant

Common Pitfall: Candidates plug the angle made with the resultant ($\alpha$) into the magnitude formula instead of the angle between the two vectors ($\theta$).

Counterexample: Given $\alpha = 30^\circ$ with $\mathbf{v}_1$, but $\theta$ between vectors is $60^\circ$.

Why it fails: Using $\alpha$ instead of $\theta$ in $R = \sqrt{v_1^2 + v_2^2 + 2v_1v_2 \cos\theta}$ yields incorrect magnitude.

🛡️ Exam Defense: Carefully read whether the given angle is between the two constituent vectors or between one vector and the resultant.
⚠️ COMMON ERROR #02

Relativistic vs. Galilean Velocity Addition

Common Pitfall: Applying simple vector addition ($v = v_1 + v_2$) when velocities approach the speed of light in high-level physics-linked math questions.

Counterexample: Particle velocities near $c$

Why it fails: Galilean composition assumes absolute time and linear addition, which breaks down in Lorentz transformations ($v = \frac{v_1 + v_2}{1 + v_1v_2/c^2}$).

🛡️ Exam Defense: Unless specified as relativistic, standard UP TGT/PGT vector exams always assume classical Galilean Newtonian mechanics.

Solved examples

Each problem includes a full solution and a shorter method:

CANONICAL PROBLEM 01 Dual-Method Breakdown

Two velocity vectors of magnitudes $3\text{ m/s}$ and $4\text{ m/s}$ act at an angle of $60^\circ$ to each other. Find the magnitude of their resultant velocity.

(A) $\sqrt{13}\text{ m/s}$(B) $\sqrt{37}\text{ m/s}$(C) $7\text{ m/s}$(D) $5\text{ m/s}$
✓ Correct Answer: (B)
📚 Method A: Formal Textbook Derivation
Step 1: Identify given values: $v_1 = 3$, $v_2 = 4$, $\theta = 60^\circ$. Step 2: Apply magnitude formula $R = \sqrt{v_1^2 + v_2^2 + 2v_1v_2 \cos\theta}$. Step 3: Compute $R = \sqrt{3^2 + 4^2 + 2(3)(4)(\frac{1}{2})} = \sqrt{9 + 16 + 12} = \sqrt{37}$.
⚡ Method B: 25-Second Exam Shortcut
Recognize that $3$ and $4$ are legs of a right triangle if $\theta=90^\circ$ ($5$). With $\cos 60^\circ = 1/2$, add the extra cross term $2(3)(4)(1/2) = 12$ to $3^2+4^2=25$, yielding $\sqrt{37}$ instantly.
💡 Tip: Do not confuse $60^\circ$ with $90^\circ$. Always evaluate $\cos\theta$ carefully.
CANONICAL PROBLEM 02 Dual-Method Breakdown

A boat rows at $5\text{ km/h}$ in still water across a river $1\text{ km}$ wide flowing at $3\text{ km/h}$. If the boat heads in the direction to minimize crossing time, what is the time taken?

(A) $12$ minutes(B) $15$ minutes(C) $20$ minutes(D) $30$ minutes
✓ Correct Answer: (A)
📚 Method A: Formal Textbook Derivation
Step 1: For minimum time, the boat must head perpendicular to the river flow, so effective velocity across is $v_s = 5\text{ km/h}$. Step 2: Time $t = \frac{d}{v_s} = \frac{1\text{ km}}{5\text{ km/h}} = \frac{1}{5}\text{ hours}$. Step 3: Convert to minutes: $\frac{1}{5} \times 60 = 12\text{ minutes}$.
⚡ Method B: 25-Second Exam Shortcut
Minimum time depends only on width and component perpendicular to flow: $t = d / v_{\text{perpendicular}} = 1 / 5\text{ hr} = 12\text{ min}$. Ignore river speed for time calculation.
💡 Tip: Remember: river speed affects drift distance in minimum time problems, but NOT the crossing time itself!

Practice questions

Choose an answer to check it and see the solution:

Q1
MCQ 1: If the resultant of two forces (or velocities) equal in magnitude $P$ is also equal in magnitude to $P$, the angle between them is:
A (A) $60^\circ$
B (B) $90^\circ$
C (C) $120^\circ$
D (D) $180^\circ$
Derivation & Analysis:

Set $R = P, v_1 = P, v_2 = P$. Then $P = \sqrt{P^2 + P^2 + 2P^2\cos\theta} \implies P^2 = 2P^2(1 + \cos\theta) \implies 1 + \cos\theta = 1/2 \implies \cos\theta = -1/2 \implies \theta = 120^\circ$.

Q2
MCQ 2: The maximum and minimum resultant of two velocity vectors are $17\text{ m/s}$ and $7\text{ m/s}$. The magnitude of the two vectors are:
A (A) $10\text{ m/s}, 7\text{ m/s}$
B (B) $12\text{ m/s}, 5\text{ m/s}$
C (C) $14\text{ m/s}, 3\text{ m/s}$
D (D) $15\text{ m/s}, 2\text{ m/s}$
Derivation & Analysis:

Let vectors be $v_1, v_2$. $v_1 + v_2 = 17$ and $v_1 - v_2 = 7$ (assuming $v_1 > v_2$). Solving yields $2v_1 = 24 \implies v_1 = 12$, and $v_2 = 5$.

Q3
MCQ 3: Rain is falling vertically with a speed of $10\text{ m/s}$. A person is running eastward with a speed of $10\text{ m/s}$. The velocity of rain relative to the person is:
A (A) $10\sqrt{2}\text{ m/s}$ at $45^\circ$ to vertical
B (B) $10\text{ m/s}$ vertical
C (C) $20\text{ m/s}$ horizontal
D (D) $10\sqrt{2}\text{ m/s}$ horizontal
Derivation & Analysis:

$\mathbf{v}_r = -10\mathbf{j}$, $\mathbf{v}_m = 10\mathbf{i}$. Relative velocity $\mathbf{v}_{rm} = \mathbf{v}_r - \mathbf{v}_m = -10\mathbf{i} - 10\mathbf{j}$. Magnitude is $\sqrt{(-10)^2 + (-10)^2} = 10\sqrt{2}\text{ m/s}$ at $45^\circ$ to the vertical.

Q4
MCQ 4: Two velocities $\mathbf{v}_1$ and $\mathbf{v}_2$ are inclined at an angle $\theta$. If the angle is changed to $180^\circ - \theta$, the magnitude of their resultant:
A (A) Remains unchanged
B (B) Increases
C (C) Decreases
D (D) Becomes zero
Derivation & Analysis:

Resultant magnitude $R = \sqrt{v_1^2 + v_2^2 + 2v_1v_2\cos\theta}$. Since $\cos(180^\circ - \theta) = -\cos\theta$, replacing $\theta$ with its supplement decreases the cosine term, hence decreasing the resultant magnitude.

Q5
MCQ 5: A boatman can row $4\text{ km/h}$ in still water. If the river flows at $2\text{ km/h}$, at what angle to the stream should he head to cross the river straight across?
A (A) $30^\circ$
B (B) $60^\circ$
C (C) $90^\circ$
D (D) $120^\circ$
Derivation & Analysis:

To cross straight across, the component of boat velocity along river flow must cancel the river speed: $v_b \cos\theta + v_r = 0 \implies 4\cos\theta + 2 = 0 \implies \cos\theta = -1/2 \implies \theta = 120^\circ$ upstream.

Q6
MCQ 6: If $\mathbf{a}$ and $\mathbf{b}$ are two velocity vectors such that $|\mathbf{a} + \mathbf{b}| = |\mathbf{a} - \mathbf{b}|$, the angle between $\mathbf{a}$ and $\mathbf{b}$ is:
A (A) $0^\circ$
B (B) $45^\circ$
C (C) $90^\circ$
D (D) $180^\circ$
Derivation & Analysis:

Squaring both sides: $(\mathbf{a} + \mathbf{b}) \cdot (\mathbf{a} + \mathbf{b}) = (\mathbf{a} - \mathbf{b}) \cdot (\mathbf{a} - \mathbf{b}) \implies a^2 + b^2 + 2\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b} = a^2 + b^2 - 2\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b} \implies 4\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b} = 0 \implies \mathbf{a} \perp \mathbf{b}$ ($\theta = 90^\circ$).

Q7
MCQ 7: A particle moves with velocities $\mathbf{v}_1 = 3\mathbf{i} + 4\mathbf{j}$ and $\mathbf{v}_2 = 6\mathbf{i} + 8\mathbf{j}$. The magnitude of the composite velocity vector is:
A (A) $5\text{ units}$
B (B) $10\text{ units}$
C (C) $15\text{ units}$
D (D) $25\text{ units}$
Derivation & Analysis:

Composite velocity $\mathbf{R} = \mathbf{v}_1 + \mathbf{v}_2 = (3+6)\mathbf{i} + (4+8)\mathbf{j} = 9\mathbf{i} + 12\mathbf{j}$. Magnitude $= \sqrt{9^2 + 12^2} = \sqrt{81 + 144} = \sqrt{225} = 15$.

Q8
MCQ 8: Assertion (A): The resultant of two velocities can never be less than the absolute difference of their magnitudes. Reason (R): The minimum value of $\cos\theta$ is $-1$.
A (A) Both (A) and (R) are true and (R) is the correct explanation of (A)
B (B) Both (A) and (R) are true but (R) is not the correct explanation of (A)
C (C) (A) is true but (R) is false
D (D) (A) is false but (R) is true
Derivation & Analysis:

The formula $R = \sqrt{v_1^2 + v_2^2 + 2v_1v_2\cos\theta} = \sqrt{(v_1+v_2)^2 - 2v_1v_2(1-\cos\theta)}$ attains its minimum when $\cos\theta = -1$, yielding $R_{\text{min}} = |v_1 - v_2|$. Thus both statement and reason are correct and linked.

Q9
MCQ 9: If vector $\mathbf{v}_1$ has magnitude $3$ and vector $\mathbf{v}_2$ has magnitude $4$, and their scalar dot product $\mathbf{v}_1 \cdot \mathbf{v}_2 = 6$, the angle between them is:
A (A) $30^\circ$
B (B) $45^\circ$
C (C) $60^\circ$
D (D) $90^\circ$
Derivation & Analysis:

$\mathbf{v}_1 \cdot \mathbf{v}_2 = v_1 v_2 \cos\theta \implies 6 = (3)(4)\cos\theta \implies 6 = 12\cos\theta \implies \cos\theta = \frac{1}{2} \implies \theta = 60^\circ$.

Q10
MCQ 10: An airplane is flying in wind with air speed $200\text{ km/h}$ directed due North. A steady wind is blowing from West to East at $50\text{ km/h}$. The actual ground speed of the airplane is:
A (A) $150\text{ km/h}$
B (B) $200\text{ km/h}$
C (C) $50\sqrt{17}\text{ km/h}$
D (D) $250\text{ km/h}$
Derivation & Analysis:

Airplane velocity $\mathbf{v}_p = 200\mathbf{j}$, wind velocity $\mathbf{v}_w = 50\mathbf{i}$. Ground speed $R = \sqrt{200^2 + 50^2} = \sqrt{40000 + 2500} = \sqrt{42500} = 50\sqrt{17}\text{ km/h}$.

Common questions

Velocity is a vector quantity possessing both magnitude (speed) and direction. Because directions operate in a multi-dimensional Euclidean space, composition must account for the angle of inclination between trajectories, leading to vector addition.

Confusing 'shortest path' (which requires steering upstream against the current so that drift is zero) with 'shortest time' (which requires steering directly perpendicular across the river regardless of downstream drift).

Memorize standard Pythagorean triplets ($3-4-5$, $5-12-13$) and standard trigonometric values ($\cos 60^\circ = 1/2$, $\cos 120^\circ = -1/2$) to instantly compute dot products and resultants without full manual expansion.
