5. Three-Dimensional Geometry

Skew lines

Skew lines are two lines in three-dimensional space that are neither intersecting nor parallel, existing in strictly non-coplanar planes. Mastering their shortest distance, equations of the line of shortest distance, and coplanarity conditions is essential for scoring high-yield questions in UP PGT/TGT, KVS, and DSSSB examinations.

Pillar 1 Rigorous Definition & Mathematical Statement

Shortest Distance Between Two Skew Lines
Let two skew lines be given vectorially by $\mathbf{r} = \mathbf{a}_1 + \lambda \mathbf{b}_1$ and $\mathbf{r} = \mathbf{a}_2 + \mu \mathbf{b}_2$. The shortest distance $d$ between them is uniquely given by $$d = \left| \frac{(\mathbf{a}_2 - \mathbf{a}_1) \cdot (\mathbf{b}_1 \times \mathbf{b}_2)}{|\mathbf{b}_1 \times \mathbf{b}_2|} \right|$$

📐 Geometric Interpretation

Geometrically, skew lines lie in two distinct parallel planes. The shortest distance is the length of the unique common perpendicular segment that simultaneously intersects both lines at right angles.

⚙️ Algebraic & Structural Meaning

Algebraically, the non-zero value of the scalar triple product $(\mathbf{a}_2 - \mathbf{a}_1, \mathbf{b}_1, \mathbf{b}_2) \neq 0$ serves as the definitive algebraic test for non-coplanarity and skewness of two lines in $\mathbb{R}^3$.

Pillar 3 High-Yield Exam Results & Direct Shortcuts

Standard 15-to-30 second shortcuts frequently tested in UP TGT/PGT and DSSSB:

01

Shortcut 1: Determinant Formula for Shortest Distance

$$d = \frac{|\begin{vmatrix} x_2 - x_1 & y_2 - y_1 & z_2 - z_1 \\ a_1 & b_1 & c_1 \\ a_2 & b_2 & c_2 \end{vmatrix}|}{\sqrt{(b_1c_2 - b_2c_1)^2 + (c_1a_2 - c_2a_1)^2 + (a_1b_2 - a_2b_1)^2}}$$

Exam Rule: Substitute Cartesian points $(x_1, y_1, z_1)$ and $(x_2, y_2, z_2)$ and direction ratios $(a_1, b_1, c_1)$, $(a_2, b_2, c_2)$ directly into the determinant without converting to vector form.

⚡ PYQ Context: Directly tested in UP PGT and TGT examinations to compute numerical shortest distance in under 45 seconds.
02

Shortcut 2: Condition for Coplanarity

$$\begin{vmatrix} x_2 - x_1 & y_2 - y_1 & z_2 - z_1 \\ a_1 & b_1 & c_1 \\ a_2 & b_2 & c_2 \end{vmatrix} = 0$$

Exam Rule: Two lines are coplanar if and only if the determinant formed by the difference of their position vectors and their direction ratios is zero.

⚡ PYQ Context: Extremely frequent in KVS and DSSSB PGT questions asking to find an unknown parameter $k$ for which two lines intersect or are coplanar.
03

Shortcut 3: Equation of Line of Shortest Distance (Symmetric Form)

$$\frac{x - x_0}{L} = \frac{y - y_0}{M} = \frac{z - z_0}{N}$$ where $(L, M, N) = \mathbf{b}_1 \times \mathbf{b}_2$

Exam Rule: The direction ratios of the line of shortest distance are always given by the cross product of the direction vectors of the two skew lines.

⚡ PYQ Context: Asked in UP PGT to identify the direction cosines or symmetric equations of the common perpendicular.
04

Shortcut 4: Distance Between Parallel Lines

$$d = \frac{|(\mathbf{a}_2 - \mathbf{a}_1) \times \mathbf{b}|}{|\mathbf{b}|}$$

Exam Rule: If direction vectors are identical ($\mathbf{b}_1 = \mathbf{b}_2 = \mathbf{b}$), use the simplified cross-product magnitude formula instead of the skew determinant.

⚡ PYQ Context: Trap question in exams where candidates mistakenly apply the 3-line skew determinant to parallel lines.
05

Shortcut 5: Intersection Point Check

$$x_1 + \lambda a_1 = x_2 + \mu a_2, \quad y_1 + \lambda b_1 = y_2 + \mu b_2, \quad z_1 + \lambda c_1 = z_2 + \mu c_2$$

Exam Rule: Solve any two equations for $\lambda$ and $\mu$, then substitute into the third. If it holds, they intersect; if inconsistent, they are skew.

⚡ PYQ Context: Used in TGT/PGT matching questions to classify pair of lines.
06

Shortcut 6: Elimination via Options using Coplanarity

$$(\mathbf{a}_2 - \mathbf{a}_1) \cdot (\mathbf{b}_1 \times \mathbf{b}_2) \neq 0$$

Exam Rule: If the options for distance are zero or have specific radical forms, quickly evaluate the numerator determinant to eliminate non-matching options.

⚡ PYQ Context: Saves crucial time in 125-question UP PGT paper.

Pillar 4 Classic Exam Traps & Counterexamples

Where examiners construct tricky trap options and how to safeguard your marks:

⚠️ EXAM TRAP #01

Trap 1: Confusing Parallel Lines with Skew Lines

Common Pitfall: Applying the skew line distance formula when the direction ratios are proportional ($a_1/a_2 = b_1/b_2 = c_1/c_2$).

Counterexample: $\frac{x-1}{2} = \frac{y-2}{3} = \frac{z-3}{4}$ and $\frac{x}{2} = \frac{y}{3} = \frac{z}{4}$

Why it fails: The cross product of parallel direction vectors is zero ($\mathbf{b}_1 \times \mathbf{b}_2 = \mathbf{0}$), resulting in a zero denominator and undefined formula.

🛡️ Exam Defense: Always check whether direction ratios are proportional before selecting the distance formula.
⚠️ EXAM TRAP #02

Trap 2: Mistaking Intersecting Lines for Skew Lines

Common Pitfall: Assuming all non-parallel lines in space are skew without testing for intersection.

Counterexample: Lines intersecting at a single point have shortest distance $d = 0$.

Why it fails: If the scalar triple product is zero, the lines intersect and are coplanar, not skew.

🛡️ Exam Defense: Always verify that the determinant is non-zero before concluding lines are skew.

Pillar 6 Solved Canonical Exam Problems (Dual-Method)

Comparing the formal academic proof against the rapid competitive exam elimination trick:

CANONICAL PROBLEM 01 Dual-Method Breakdown

Find the shortest distance between the lines given by $\frac{x-1}{2} = \frac{y-2}{3} = \frac{z-3}{4}$ and $\frac{x-2}{3} = \frac{y-4}{4} = \frac{z-5}{5}$.

(A) $\frac{1}{\sqrt{6}}$(B) $\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}$(C) $\frac{2}{\sqrt{3}}$(D) $0$
✓ Correct Answer: (A)
📚 Method A: Formal Textbook Derivation
Step 1: Identify points $(1, 2, 3)$ and $(2, 4, 5)$. Difference vector: $(1, 2, 2)$. Step 2: Direction vectors are $\mathbf{b}_1 = (2, 3, 4)$ and $\mathbf{b}_2 = (3, 4, 5)$. Step 3: Compute cross product $\mathbf{b}_1 \times \mathbf{b}_2 = (-1, 2, -1)$. Magnitude is $\sqrt{1+4+1} = \sqrt{6}$. Step 4: Compute dot product of difference vector and cross product: $(1)( -1) + (2)(2) + (2)(-1) = -1 + 4 - 2 = 1$. Step 5: Distance $d = |1| / \sqrt{6} = 1/\sqrt{6}$.
⚡ Method B: 25-Second Exam Shortcut
Set up the 3x3 determinant in numerator: $\begin{vmatrix} 1 & 2 & 2 \\ 2 & 3 & 4 \\ 3 & 4 & 5 \end{vmatrix} = 1(15-16) - 2(10-12) + 2(8-9) = -1 + 2 - 2 = -1$. Denominator magnitude of $( -1, 2, -1)$ is $\sqrt{6}$. Result is $1/\sqrt{6}$.
💡 Key Takeaway: Be extremely careful with sign arithmetic inside 3x3 determinants.
CANONICAL PROBLEM 02 Dual-Method Breakdown

For what value of $k$ are the lines $\frac{x-1}{2} = \frac{y+1}{3} = \frac{z}{1}$ and $\frac{x}{1} = \frac{y-k}{2} = \frac{z+1}{2}$ coplanar?

Q1
MCQ 1: The shortest distance between the lines $\mathbf{r} = (\mathbf{i} + 2\mathbf{j} + \mathbf{3\mathbf{k}}) + \lambda(\mathbf{2\mathbf{i}} + 3\mathbf{j} + 4\mathbf{k})$ and $\mathbf{r} = (2\mathbf{i} + 4\mathbf{j} + 5\mathbf{k}) + \mu(3\mathbf{i} + 4\mathbf{j} + 5\mathbf{k})$ is:
A (A) $\frac{1}{\sqrt{6}}$
B (B) $\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}$
C (C) $0$
D (D) $\sqrt{6}$
Derivation & Analysis:

Using the shortest distance formula with difference vector $(1, 2, 2)$ and direction vectors $(2,3,4)$ and $(3,4,5)$, the cross product is $(-1, 2, -1)$, yielding $d = 1/\sqrt{6}$.

Q2
MCQ 2: Two lines in space are called skew lines if they:
A (A) intersect at a single point
B (B) are parallel
C (C) are neither intersecting nor parallel
D (D) lie in the same plane
Derivation & Analysis:

By definition, skew lines are non-coplanar lines that do not intersect and are not parallel.

Q3
MCQ 3: If two lines are coplanar, the shortest distance between them is:
A (A) 1
B (B) $\infty$
C (C) 0
D (D) dependent on direction
Derivation & Analysis:

If lines are coplanar, they either intersect or are parallel. If they intersect, the distance is 0. If parallel, distance is measured between them, but coplanar includes intersecting where shortest distance is zero.

Q4
MCQ 4: The direction ratios of the line of shortest distance between two skew lines with direction vectors $\mathbf{b}_1$ and $\mathbf{b}_2$ are given by:
A (A) $\mathbf{b}_1 + \mathbf{b}_2$
B (B) $\mathbf{b}_1 \times \mathbf{b}_2$
C (C) $\mathbf{b}_1 \cdot \mathbf{b}_2$
D (D) $\frac{\mathbf{b}_1}{|\mathbf{b}_1|} + \frac{\mathbf{b}_2}{|\mathbf{b}_2|}$
Derivation & Analysis:

The line of shortest distance is perpendicular to both skew lines, hence its direction vector is parallel to $\mathbf{b}_1 \times \mathbf{b}_2$.

Q5
MCQ 5: The condition that the lines $\frac{x-x_1}{a_1} = \frac{y-y_1}{b_1} = \frac{z-z_1}{c_1}$ and $\frac{x-x_2}{a_2} = \frac{y-y_2}{b_2} = \frac{z-z_2}{c_2}$ are coplanar is:
A (A) $\begin{vmatrix} x_2-x_1 & y_2-y_1 & z_2-z_1 \\ a_1 & b_1 & c_1 \\ a_2 & b_2 & c_2 \end{vmatrix} = 0$
B (B) $a_1 a_2 + b_1 b_2 + c_1 c_2 = 0$
C (C) $\frac{a_1}{a_2} = \frac{b_1}{b_2} = \frac{c_1}{c_2}$
D (D) $(x_2-x_1)a_1 + (y_2-y_1)b_1 + (z_2-z_1)c_1 = 0$
Derivation & Analysis:

The vanishing of the scalar triple product of the difference of position vectors and the two direction vectors is the exact condition for coplanarity.

Q6
MCQ 6: The shortest distance between parallel lines $\mathbf{r} = \mathbf{a}_1 + \lambda \mathbf{b}$ and $\mathbf{r} = \mathbf{a}_2 + \mu \mathbf{b}$ is:
A (A) $\frac{|(\mathbf{a}_2 - \mathbf{a}_1) \cdot \mathbf{b}|}{|\mathbf{b}|}$
B (B) $\frac{|(\mathbf{a}_2 - \mathbf{a}_1) \times \mathbf{b}|}{|\mathbf{b}|}$
C (C) $|\mathbf{a}_2 - \mathbf{a}_1|$
D (D) $\frac{\mathbf{a}_2 - \mathbf{a}_1}{|\mathbf{b}|}$
Derivation & Analysis:

For parallel lines, the distance formula simplifies to the projection of the vector joining the base points onto the plane perpendicular to $\mathbf{b}$, which is $\frac{|(\mathbf{a}_2 - \mathbf{a}_1) \times \mathbf{b}|}{|\mathbf{b}|}$.

Q7
MCQ 7: If the lines $\frac{x}{1} = \frac{y}{2} = \frac{z}{3}$ and $\frac{x-1}{k} = \frac{y-1}{2} = \frac{z-1}{3}$ are coplanar, then $k$ equals:
A (A) 1
B (B) 0
C (C) 2
D (D) Any real number except 1
Derivation & Analysis:

Since the direction vectors are identical $(1,2,3)$, the lines are parallel. Parallel lines are always coplanar regardless of the starting points (as long as they are distinct), but if $k=1$ they are identical. For distinct parallel lines, any $k$ gives coplanar lines.

Q8
MCQ 8: Assertion (A): Skew lines can exist in 2-dimensional Cartesian space. Reason (R): Two lines in a plane must either intersect or be parallel.
A (A) Both (A) and (R) are true and (R) is the correct explanation of (A)
B (B) Both (A) and (R) are true but (R) is not the correct explanation of (A)
C (C) (A) is true but (R) is false
D (D) (A) is false but (R) is true
Derivation & Analysis:

Assertion (A) is false because skew lines require 3 dimensions. Reason (R) is true as two lines in a 2D plane always intersect or are parallel.

Q9
MCQ 9: The number of lines that can be mutually skew to each other in 3-dimensional space is:
A (A) exactly 2
B (B) exactly 3
C (C) at most 4
D (D) arbitrarily many
Derivation & Analysis:

One can construct infinitely many mutually skew lines in $\in \mathbb{R}^3$ (e.g., family of lines twisted in space).

Q10
MCQ 10: If the shortest distance between two lines is zero, then the lines:
A (A) are skew
B (B) intersect or are coincident
C (C) are strictly parallel
D (D) are orthogonal
Derivation & Analysis:

Zero shortest distance implies the lines share at least one common point (intersecting) or are identical (coincident).
