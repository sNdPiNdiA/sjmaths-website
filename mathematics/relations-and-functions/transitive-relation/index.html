1. Algebra — Relations and Functions

Transitive relation

A relation $R$ on a set $A$ is termed transitive if whenever an element is related to a second, and the second to a third, then the first is necessarily related to the third. Mastery of transitivity, along with reflexivity and symmetry, forms the core of equivalence relation testing frequently featured in UP TGT/PGT, DSSSB, and KVS competitive examinations.

Target Exams: UP PGT Mathematics UP TGT Mathematics DSSSB PGT / TGT KVS / NVS PGT LT Grade
📖 Self-Study Mastery Status
○ Mark Topic as Mastered

Pillar 1 Rigorous Definition & Mathematical Statement

Definition of Transitive Relation
Let $A$ be a non-empty set. A relation $R$ on $A$ ($R \subset A \times A$) is said to be transitive if \forall a, b, c \in A, \quad (a, b) \in R \text{ and } (b, c) \in R \implies (a, c) \in R.

📐 Geometric Interpretation

Graphically, if directed edges exist from vertex $a$ to vertex $b$ and from vertex $b$ to vertex $c$ in the digraph of the relation, transitivity requires the presence of a direct directed edge from $a$ to $c$.

⚙️ Algebraic & Structural Meaning

Transitivity enforces hierarchical, order-like, or divisibility consistency across algebraic structures, ensuring that compositional chains do not break the underlying relational property.

Pillar 3 High-Yield Exam Results & Direct Shortcuts

Standard 15-to-30 second shortcuts frequently tested in UP TGT/PGT and DSSSB:

01

Shortcut 1: Universal Transitivity of Equality and Inequalities

$$a = b \land b = c \implies a = c \quad (\text{Transitive})$$

Exam Rule: Standard relations like $=$, $\le$, $\ge$, $<$, $>$, divides ($|$) on $\mathbb{Z}$, and subset ($\subseteq$) on $\mathcal{P}(S)$ are universally transitive.

⚡ PYQ Context: Directly tested in UP PGT to quickly identify equivalence or partial order relations without manual pair-checking.
02

Shortcut 2: Congruence Modulo $n$

$$a \equiv b \pmod n \text{ and } b \equiv c \pmod n \implies a \equiv c \pmod n$$

Exam Rule: Congruence relations are always equivalence relations, hence transitive.

⚡ PYQ Context: Frequently asked in number theory sections of UP TGT/PGT mathematics.
03

Shortcut 3: Perpendicularity and Parallelism

$$L_1 \parallel L_2 \land L_2 \parallel L_3 \implies L_1 \parallel L_3 \quad (\text{Transitive})$$

Exam Rule: Parallelism of lines is transitive, whereas perpendicularity is NOT transitive ($L_1 \perp L_2$ and $L_2 \perp L_3 \implies L_1 \parallel L_3$).

⚡ PYQ Context: Classic trap question in KVS/DSSSB PGT exams regarding geometric relations.
04

Shortcut 4: Subset Inclusion Relation

$$A \subseteq B \land B \subseteq C \implies A \subseteq C$$

Exam Rule: Inclusion is always transitive. Strict inclusion ($A \subset B$) is also transitive.

⚡ PYQ Context: Set theory relation questions in LT Grade and UP PGT exams.
05

Shortcut 5: Function Composition Transitivity

$$f: A \to B, \ g: B \to C \implies \text{ relational properties transfer}$$

Exam Rule: Relations defined via functional equations (e.g., $f(x) = f(y)$) inherit transitivity from equality in the codomain.

⚡ PYQ Context: Advanced relation problems in UP PGT.
06

Shortcut 6: Rapid Elimination Trick for Non-Transitivity

$$(a,b) \in R, (b,c) \in R \text{ but } (a,c) \notin R$$

Exam Rule: To disprove transitivity, you only need to find a single counterexample triplet $(a, b, c)$ satisfying the premise but violating the conclusion.

⚡ PYQ Context: Saves 2 minutes per relation-checking question in 125-question exams.

Pillar 4 Classic Exam Traps & Counterexamples

Where examiners construct tricky trap options and how to safeguard your marks:

⚠️ EXAM TRAP #01

Trap 1: The Vacuous Truth Fallacy

Common Pitfall: Candidates assume a relation with very few elements cannot be transitive.

Counterexample: R = \{(1, 2)\} \text{ on } A = \{1, 2, 3}

Why it fails: There is no pair $(b, c)$ starting with $2$. Thus, the implication $(1,2) \land (2,c) \implies (1,c)$ is vacuously true. The relation IS transitive.

🛡️ Exam Defense: Check if any chain $(a,b)$ and $(b,c)$ actually exists before declaring failure.
⚠️ EXAM TRAP #02

Trap 2: Mistaking Perpendicularity for Transitivity

Common Pitfall: Assuming symmetric geometric relations are automatically transitive.

Counterexample: R = \{(L_1, L_2) : L_1 \perp L_2\}

Why it fails: If $L_1 \perp L_2$ and $L_2 \perp L_3$, then $L_1$ and $L_3$ are parallel, not perpendicular. Hence $(L_1, L_3) \notin R$.

🛡️ Exam Defense: Never extrapolate symmetry to transitivity in geometric orthogonality.

Pillar 6 Solved Canonical Exam Problems (Dual-Method)

Comparing the formal academic proof against the rapid competitive exam elimination trick:

CANONICAL PROBLEM 01 Dual-Method Breakdown

Let $R$ be a relation on the set of real numbers $\mathbb{R}$ defined by $R = \{(a, b) : a \le b^2\}$. Examine $R$ for transitivity.

(A) Transitive(B) Not transitive(C) Symmetric but not transitive(D) Equivalence relation
✓ Correct Answer: (B)
📚 Method A: Formal Textbook Derivation
Step 1: Test with real numbers. Let $a = 2, b = -3, c = -1$. Step 2: Check $(2, -3)$: $2 \le (-3)^2 = 9$ (True). Step 3: Check $(-3, -1)$: $-3 \le (-1)^2 = 1$ (True). Step 4: Check $(2, -1)$: $2 \le (-1)^2 = 1$ (False). Step 5: Since $(2,-3)\in R$ and $(-3,-1)\in R$ but $(2,-1)\notin R$, $R$ is not transitive.
⚡ Method B: 25-Second Exam Shortcut
Use small integer counterexamples involving negative numbers or fractions.
💡 Key Takeaway: Inequalities involving squares or absolute values often fail transitivity.
CANONICAL PROBLEM 02 Dual-Method Breakdown

Let $R$ be the relation defined on the set $N$ of natural numbers by $xRy \iff x$ divides $y$. Then $R$ is:

(A) Reflexive and symmetric only(B) Transitive and symmetric only(C) Equivalence relation(D) Reflexive and transitive, but not symmetric
✓ Correct Answer: (D)
📚 Method A: Formal Textbook Derivation
Step 1: Reflexivity: $x$ divides $x$ ($\true$). Step 2: Symmetry: $2$ divides $4$, but $4$ does not divide $2$ (Not symmetric). Step 3: Transitivity: If $x$ divides $y$ and $y$ divides $z$, then $x$ divides $z$ ($\true$).
⚡ Method B: 25-Second Exam Shortcut
Divisibility relation on $\mathbb{N}$ is a classic partial order (reflexive, antisymmetric, transitive).
💡 Key Takeaway: Memorize standard order relations on $\mathbb{N}$ or $\mathbb{R}$ for instant answers.
