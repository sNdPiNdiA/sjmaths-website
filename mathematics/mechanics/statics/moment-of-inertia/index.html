9. Mechanics — Statics

Moment of inertia

Moment of inertia is the scalar measure of a rigid body's rotational inertia, representing how its mass is distributed relative to a rotational axis. In competitive examinations like UP TGT/PGT, KVS, and DSSSB, it forms a high-yield scoring zone governed by standard integration formulas and transfer theorems.

Target Exams: UP PGT Mathematics UP TGT Mathematics DSSSB PGT / TGT KVS / NVS PGT LT Grade
📖 Self-Study Mastery Status
○ Mark Topic as Mastered

Pillar 1 Rigorous Definition & Mathematical Statement

Parallel Axes Theorem (Steiner's Theorem)
The moment of inertia of a body about any axis is equal to the sum of the moment of inertia about a parallel axis through its center of mass and the product of the total mass of the body and the square of the perpendicular distance between the two axes: $$I = I_{cm} + M h^2$$

📐 Geometric Interpretation

It bridges any arbitrary axis in space to the unique central axis, visually decomposing rotational inertia into an intrinsic part ($I_{cm}$) and an extrinsic spatial shift ($M h^2$).

⚙️ Algebraic & Structural Meaning

Provides a linear-quadratic decomposition that simplifies complex rotational dynamics into standard center-of-mass calculations.

Pillar 2 Hypotheses & Condition Checklist

Every hypothesis is strictly non-negotiable. Inspect the domain type and the exact consequence if a condition fails:

Hypothesis / Condition Domain / Interval Necessity What Fails If Condition Dropped
Rigid Body Continuum / Discrete Mass Distribution Domain of integration / summation Strictly Mandatory / Necessary If the body deforms, mass distribution changes dynamically, invalidating static spatial integration.
Parallel Axis Alignment Spatial orientation vector Strictly Mandatory If axes are non-parallel, the $M h^2$ scalar shift term fails; product of inertia tensors must be invoked.
Perpendicular Axis Condition Planar lamina condition ($z = 0$) Essential for Perpendicular Axes Theorem If the body is three-dimensional, $I_z = I_x + I_y$ does not hold without planar mass confinement.

02

Shortcut 2: Circular Ring vs. Disc (Diametral Axis)

$$I_{\text{ring}} = \frac{1}{2} M R^2, \quad I_{\text{disc}} = \frac{1}{4} M R^2$$

⚡ PYQ Context: Frequently asked in comparative matching questions in KVS/NVS PGT.
03

Shortcut 3: Solid Sphere about Tangent

$$I_{\text{tangent}} = \frac{2}{5} M R^2 + M R^2 = \frac{7}{5} M R^2$$

⚡ PYQ Context: Standard recurring formula in DSSSB PGT Mathematics.
04

Shortcut 4: Rectangular Lamina about Center

$$I = \frac{M(a^2 + b^2)}{12}$$

Exam Rule: Sum of perpendicular dimensions squared times mass over 12 for an axis perpendicular to the plane through the center.

⚡ PYQ Context: Tested in LT Grade mechanics sections.
05

Shortcut 5: Hollow Cylinder (Shell) vs. Solid Cylinder

$$I_{\text{shell}} = M R^2, \quad I_{\text{solid}} = \frac{1}{2} M R^2$$

Exam Rule: Cylindrical mass concentrated at radius $R$ gives $MR^2$; uniform distribution halves it.

⚡ PYQ Context: Quick elimination tool in multiple-choice questions.
06

Shortcut 6: Radius of Gyration ($k$) Quick Relation

$$k = \sqrt{\frac{I}{M}}$$

Exam Rule: If moment of inertia is given as a multiple of $MR^2$, immediately drop $M$ and take the square root of the coefficient multiplied by $R$.

⚡ PYQ Context: Saves up to 40 seconds in calculation-heavy exam items.

Pillar 4 Classic Exam Traps & Counterexamples

Where examiners construct tricky trap options and how to safeguard your marks:

⚠️ EXAM TRAP #01

Trap 1: Confusing Axis Orientation in Parallel Axes Theorem

Common Pitfall: Applying $I = I_{cm} + Mh^2$ when the axis is not passing through the center of mass or when axes are not parallel.

Counterexample: Using $h$ as the distance from an arbitrary random point instead of the center of mass.

Why it fails: The theorem mandates that one of the two parallel axes *must* pass through the center of mass.

🛡️ Exam Defense: Always verify that $I_{cm}$ is explicitly the moment of inertia about the center of mass before adding $M h^2$.
⚠️ EXAM TRAP #02

Trap 2: Misapplying Perpendicular Axes Theorem to 3D Bodies

Common Pitfall: Using $I_z = I_x + I_y$ for solid spheres or cylinders.

Counterexample: Solid sphere where $I_z = \frac{2}{5}MR^2$, but $I_x + I_y = \frac{2}{5}MR^2 + \frac{2}{5}MR^2 = \frac{4}{5}MR^2 \neq I_z$.

Why it fails: The perpendicular axes theorem is strictly valid only for two-dimensional planar laminas lying in the $xy$-plane.

🛡️ Exam Defense: Check if the object is flat (lamina) before applying $I_z = I_x + I_y$.

Pillar 6 Solved Canonical Exam Problems (Dual-Method)

Comparing the formal academic proof against the rapid competitive exam elimination trick:

CANONICAL PROBLEM 01 Dual-Method Breakdown

Find the moment of inertia of a uniform circular disc of mass $M$ and radius $R$ about a tangent in its own plane.

(A) $\frac{1}{2}MR^2$(B) $\frac{3}{2}MR^2$(C) $\frac{5}{4}MR^2$(D) $\frac{3}{4}MR^2$
✓ Correct Answer: (C)
📚 Method A: Formal Textbook Derivation
Step 1: Moment of inertia of disc about a diametral axis is $I_{cm} = \frac{1}{4}MR^2$. Step 2: Distance to parallel tangent in its plane is $h = R$. Step 3: Apply Parallel Axes Theorem: $I = I_{cm} + MR^2 = \frac{1}{4}MR^2 + MR^2 = \frac{5}{4}MR^2$.
⚡ Method B: 25-Second Exam Shortcut
Recall diametral $I = 0.25 MR^2$, add $1.0 MR^2$ for the parallel shift to get $1.25 MR^2 = \frac{5}{4}MR^2$.
💡 Key Takeaway: Note whether the tangent is *in its own plane* or *perpendicular to its plane* (which would give $\frac{3}{2}MR^2$).
CANONICAL PROBLEM 02 Dual-Method Breakdown

If the radius of gyration of a circular ring of radius $R$ about an axis passing through its center and perpendicular to its plane is $k$, what is $k$?

(A) $R$(B) $\frac{R}{\sqrt{2}}$(C) $2R$(D) $\frac{R}{2}$
✓ Correct Answer: (A)
📚 Method A: Formal Textbook Derivation
Step 1: For a circular ring, $I = MR^2$. Step 2: By definition of radius of gyration, $I = Mk^2$. Step 3: Equating gives $Mk^2 = MR^2 \implies k = R$.
⚡ Method B: 25-Second Exam Shortcut
For a ring about polar axis, all mass is at distance $R$, hence radius of gyration is identically $R$.
💡 Key Takeaway: Do not confuse ring ($k=R$) with disc ($k=R/\sqrt{2}$).

Pillar 8 Frequently Asked Questions

Strictly speaking, moment of inertia is neither a simple scalar nor a vector; it is a second-rank symmetric tensor. However, for a fixed axis of rotation, it behaves and can be treated as a scalar quantity.

Candidates frequently confuse the formulas for solid vs. hollow bodies and misapply the perpendicular axes theorem to three-dimensional solids instead of strictly two-dimensional laminas.

In UP TGT and PGT Mathematics examinations, mechanics accounts for 15-20% of the paper, with 2 to 3 direct questions dedicated to moments of inertia and standard transfer theorems.

Memorize standard standard results ($I_{cm}$ and radii of gyration for rods, rings, discs, spheres, and cylinders) and master the quick application of Parallel and Perpendicular Axes Theorems without re-deriving via integration.

Shell has $I = \frac{2}{3}MR^2 \approx 0.67 MR^2$, whereas solid sphere has $I = \frac{2}{5}MR^2 = 0.4 MR^2$. Thus, shell has greater moment of inertia.

Pillar 8 Frequently Asked Questions

Strictly speaking, moment of inertia is neither a simple scalar nor a vector; it is a second-rank symmetric tensor. However, for a fixed axis of rotation, it behaves and can be treated as a scalar quantity.

Candidates frequently confuse the formulas for solid vs. hollow bodies and misapply the perpendicular axes theorem to three-dimensional solids instead of strictly two-dimensional laminas.

In UP TGT and PGT Mathematics examinations, mechanics accounts for 15-20% of the paper, with 2 to 3 direct questions dedicated to moments of inertia and standard transfer theorems.

Memorize standard standard results ($I_{cm}$ and radii of gyration for rods, rings, discs, spheres, and cylinders) and master the quick application of Parallel and Perpendicular Axes Theorems without re-deriving via integration.
