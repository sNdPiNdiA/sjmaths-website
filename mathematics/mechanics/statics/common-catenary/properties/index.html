9. Mechanics — Statics

Properties of common catenary

The common catenary is the curve assumed by a perfectly flexible, inextensible uniform heavy string hanging freely under its own weight between two fixed points. Mastering its intrinsic and Cartesian equations, tension distribution, and geometric properties is vital for scoring high in UP PGT/TGT, KVS, and DSSSB examinations.

Pillar 1 Rigorous Definition & Mathematical Statement

Fundamental Equation and Intrinsic Properties of the Common Catenary
The intrinsic equation of a common catenary is given by $s = c \tan \psi$, and its Cartesian equation with respect to vertex-origin axes is $y = c \cosh\left(\frac{x}{c}\right)$, where $c$ is the parameter representing the span-to-tension ratio at the lowest point.

📐 Geometric Interpretation

The curve is symmetric about the $y$-axis passing through its lowest point (vertex), where the tension is minimum and purely horizontal. The parameter $c$ represents the length of the string whose weight equals the tension at the vertex.

⚙️ Algebraic & Structural Meaning

The relation between arc length $s$, intrinsic angle $\psi$, and Cartesian coordinates $(x,y)$ involves hyperbolic functions, establishing deep links between exponential growth and equilibrium under uniform gravity.

Pillar 3 High-Yield Exam Results & Direct Shortcuts

Standard 15-to-30 second shortcuts frequently tested in UP TGT/PGT and DSSSB:

01

Shortcut 1: Tension at any point

$$T = wy = w(c + s) = wc \sec \psi$$

Exam Rule: The tension at any point is directly proportional to its height $y$ above the directrix, or to $c + s$ measured from the vertex.

⚡ PYQ Context: Direct numerical questions asking for tension at a support or lowest point in UP PGT/TGT exams.
02

Shortcut 2: Cartesian coordinates and Hyperbolic identity

$$y^2 = c^2 + s^2$$

Exam Rule: Instantly relate the ordinate $y$, parameter $c$, and arc length $s$ without integrating.

⚡ PYQ Context: Frequently tested in KVS PGT for rapid calculation of arc lengths given coordinates.
03

Shortcut 3: Relation between span ($2l$), dip ($d$), and parameter $c$

$$d = c \left[ \cosh\left(\frac{l}{c}\right) - 1 \right] \approx \frac{l^2}{2c}$$

Exam Rule: Use the parabolic approximation $d \approx \frac{l^2}{2c}$ when the sag is small compared to the span.

⚡ PYQ Context: Standard approximation shortcut tested in DSSSB and LT Grade examinations.
04

Shortcut 4: Radius of curvature

$$\rho = \frac{y^2}{c} = c \sec^2 \psi$$

Exam Rule: The radius of curvature at any point equals the normal component intercepted between the curve and the directrix.

⚡ PYQ Context: Used in combined statics and differential geometry questions in PGT exams.
05

Shortcut 5: Directrix distance from vertex

$$y_{\text{vertex}} = c$$

Exam Rule: The height of the lowest point above the directrix is always equal to the parameter $c$.

⚡ PYQ Context: Quick conceptual check in multiple-choice questions.
06

Shortcut 6: Angle of friction or slope at support

$$\tan \psi = \frac{s}{c}$$

Exam Rule: The slope at any point is given by the ratio of the arc length from the vertex to the parameter $c$.

⚡ PYQ Context: Standard elimination tool for slope-related options.

Pillar 4 Classic Exam Traps & Counterexamples

Where examiners construct tricky trap options and how to safeguard your marks:

⚠️ EXAM TRAP #01

Trap 1: Confusing Directrix with Axis of Symmetry

Common Pitfall: Candidates take $y=0$ as the directrix instead of $y=c$ when measuring coordinates from the vertex.

Counterexample: Standard form $y = c \cosh(x/c)$ has directrix at $y = 0$, so vertex is at $(0, c)$.

Why it fails: Leads to incorrect height measurements and wrong tension values ($T = wy$ vs $T = w(y-c)$).

🛡️ Exam Defense: Always remember that the directrix lies at a distance $c$ below the lowest point (vertex).
⚠️ EXAM TRAP #02

Trap 2: Misapplying Parabolic Approximation

Common Pitfall: Using $d = \frac{l^2}{2c}$ when the dip $d$ is large or comparable to span $l$.

Counterexample: When $d$ is large, the exact relation $d = c(\cosh(l/c) - 1)$ must be used.

Why it fails: Approximation breaks down for steep catenaries, yielding significant calculation errors.

🛡️ Exam Defense: Check if sag is small ($d \ll l$) before applying parabolic formulas.

Pillar 6 Solved Canonical Exam Problems (Dual-Method)

Comparing the formal academic proof against the rapid competitive exam elimination trick:

CANONICAL PROBLEM 01 Dual-Method Breakdown

A uniform heavy string of length $2l$ hangs from two fixed points at the same level. If the sag is $d$, find the parameter $c$ of the catenary.

(A) $\frac{l^2 - d^2}{2d}$(B) $\frac{l^2 + d^2}{2d}$(C) $\frac{2l^2}{d}$(D) $\frac{l^2}{d}$
✓ Correct Answer: (B)
📚 Method A: Formal Textbook Derivation
Step 1: Half span is $l$, total height at support is $c + d$. Step 2: Use $(c+d)^2 = c^2 + l^2$. Step 3: Expand $c^2 + 2cd + d^2 = c^2 + l^2 \implies 2cd = l^2 - d^2$ or $c = \frac{l^2 - d^2}{2d}$ (Wait, let's verify exact relation: support is at $(l, c+d)$. Equation is $c+d = c \cosh(l/c)$. Alternatively, using $s^2 + c^2 = y^2$, with $s=l$ and $y=c+d$, we get $l^2 + c^2 = (c+d)^2 = c^2 + 2cd + d^2 \implies 2cd = l^2 - d^2 \implies c = \frac{l^2 - d^2}{2d}$.
⚡ Method B: 25-Second Exam Shortcut
Apply $s^2 = y^2 - c^2 \implies l^2 = (c+d)^2 - c^2 = 2cd + d^2$, hence $c = \frac{l^2-d^2}{2d}$.
💡 Key Takeaway: Memorize this standard derivation as it frequently appears directly in UP PGT.
CANONICAL PROBLEM 02 Dual-Method Breakdown

If $T_1$ and $T_2$ are the tensions at the lowest point and at the support of a common catenary respectively, and $w$ is the weight per unit length, what is the weight of the portion of the string between the lowest point and the support?

(A) $\sqrt{T_2^2 - T_1^2}$(B) $T_2 - T_1$(C) $\sqrt{T_1^2 + T_2^2}$(D) $\frac{T_1 + T_2}{2}$
✓ Correct Answer: (A)
📚 Method A: Formal Textbook Derivation
Step 1: Tension at lowest point $T_1 = wc$. Step 2: Tension at support $T_2 = w(c+s) = wc + ws = T_1 + W$, where $W = ws$ is the weight of the half string. Thus $W = T_2 - T_1$ for vertical component, but total tension relation is $T_2^2 = T_1^2 + (ws)^2 \implies ws = \sqrt{T_2^2 - T_1^2}$.
⚡ Method B: 25-Second Exam Shortcut
Recall force triangle at support: horizontal tension $T_1 = wc$, vertical tension $ws$, resultant tension $T_2$. By Pythagoras theorem, $(ws)^2 = T_2^2 - T_1^2$.
💡 Key Takeaway: Distinguish carefully between vertical tension component ($T_2 \sin \psi = ws = W$) and total tension magnitude ($T_2 = \sqrt{T_1^2 + W^2}$). Option B is a common trap for vertical component when magnitude is asked.

Pillar 7 10 Practice MCQs for Self-Assessment

Q1
The intrinsic equation of a common catenary is given by $s = c \tan \psi$. What represents the parameter $c$?
A Length of arc from vertex
B Ratio of horizontal tension to weight per unit length
C Radius of curvature at support
D Maximum sag of the string
Derivation & Analysis:

At the vertex, $\psi = 0$, and $T_0 = wc$, so $c = \frac{T_0}{w}$, representing the length of string whose weight equals tension at the vertex.

Q2
In a common catenary, the tension at any point $(x, y)$ is given by:
A $wy$
B $wc \cosh(x/c)$
C $w \sqrt{x^2 + y^2}$
D $wc \tan \psi$
Derivation & Analysis:

The fundamental property of a common catenary is that the tension $T$ at any point is equal to the weight of the portion of the string hanging from the directrix to that point, which is $wy$.

Q3
The Cartesian equation of a common catenary referred to its directrix as the $x$-axis is:
A $y = c \sinh(x/c)$
B $y = c \cosh(x/c)$
C $y = c \tanh(x/c)$
D $y = c \ln(\sec(x/c))$
Derivation & Analysis:

When the directrix is chosen as the $x$-axis and the $y$-axis passes through the vertex, the equation is $y = c \cosh(x/c)$.

Q4
If $\rho$ is the radius of curvature at any point $(x, y)$ on a common catenary, then $\rho$ in terms of $y$ and $c$ is:
A $\frac{y}{c}$
B $\frac{c^2}{y}$
C $\frac{y^2}{c}$
D $\sqrt{y^2 - c^2}$
Derivation & Analysis:

We know $\rho = c \sec^2 \psi$. Since $\sec \psi = \frac{y}{c}$, we have $\rho = c \left(\frac{y}{c}\right)^2 = \frac{y^2}{c}$.

Q5
The relation between the arc length $s$ measured from the vertex and the Cartesian coordinate $y$ is:
A $y^2 = c^2 + s^2$
B $y = c + s$
C $y^2 = s^2 - c^2$
D $s = \sqrt{y^2 + c^2}$
Derivation & Analysis:

From $y = c \cosh(x/c)$ and $s = c \sinh(x/c)$, using $\cosh^2\theta - \sinh^2\theta = 1$, we get $\left(\frac{y}{c}\right)^2 - \left(\frac{s}{c}\right)^2 = 1 \implies y^2 = c^2 + s^2$.

Q6
If the span of a catenary is $2l$ and dip is $d$, the length of the endless chain or total string length $2s$ can be approximated for small $d$ by:
A $2l + \frac{8d^2}{3l}$
B $2l + \frac{d^2}{l}$
C $l + \frac{d^2}{2l}$
D $2l$
Derivation & Analysis:

For small dip $d$, the length of half the string is $s \approx l + \frac{d^2}{2l}$ (or using standard expansion $s = l(1 + \frac{8}{3}\frac{d^2}{l^2})$ for total length).

Q7
At the vertex of a common catenary, the tangent is:
A Vertical
B Horizontal
C Inclined at $45^\circ$
D Undefined
Derivation & Analysis:

By definition, the vertex is the lowest point of the catenary where the tangent is horizontal (i.e., $\psi = 0$). basin of symmetry.

Q8
The normal at any point $(x, y)$ of a common catenary of parameter $c$ cuts the directrix at an angle equal to:
A $\psi$
B $90^\circ - \psi$
C $180^\circ - \psi$
D $2\psi$
Derivation & Analysis:

The angle between the normal and the vertical is $\psi$, which equals the angle the tangent makes with the horizontal.

Q9
If the tension at the lowest point of a catenary is equal to the weight of length $c_0$ of the string, then $c_0$ is equal to:
A $c$
B $2c$
C $c/2$
D $0$
Derivation & Analysis:

The parameter $c$ is defined precisely as the length of string whose weight equals the tension at the lowest point.

Q10
What is the shape formed by a uniform heavy cable hanging under its own weight between two supports?
A Parabola
B Catenary
C Ellipse
D Cycloid
Derivation & Analysis:

A cable hanging under its own weight forms a catenary. If loaded uniformly along the horizontal span, it forms a parabola.

Pillar 8 Frequently Asked Questions

A common catenary is formed when a string supports only its own weight (load distributed uniformly along the arc length). A parabola is formed when the load is distributed uniformly along the horizontal span (such as in a suspension bridge deck).

It depends on the choice of origin. When the origin is taken at the directrix, $y$ is the height from the directrix, and $T = wy$. When the origin is at the vertex, the height above the vertex is $y'$, and $T = w(c + y')$. Confusing the coordinate axes leads to sign and formula errors.

Master the three core relations: $s = c \tan \psi$, $y = c \cosh(x/c)$, and $y^2 = c^2 + s^2$. Using these identities avoids lengthy integration during the exam.
