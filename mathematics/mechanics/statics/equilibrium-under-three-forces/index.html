9. Mechanics — Statics

Equilibrium of a body under three forces

The study of a rigid body or particle in static equilibrium under the action of precisely three coplanar forces rests upon fundamental vector principles, Lami's Theorem, and the Triangle Law of Forces. Mastering these geometric and algebraic conditions provides an exceptionally high-scoring avenue in UP TGT/PGT, KVS, and DSSSB examinations.

Pillar 1 Rigorous Definition & Mathematical Statement

Lami's Theorem and Conditions for Three-Force Equilibrium
If a rigid body is in equilibrium under the action of three coplanar, concurrent forces $\vec{F}_1$, $\vec{F}_2$, and $\vec{F}_3$, then: (1) The lines of action of the three forces must be concurrent or parallel. (2) The magnitude of each force is directly proportional to the sine of the angle between the other two forces: $$\frac{F_1}{\sin \alpha} = \frac{F_2}{\sin \beta} = \frac{F_3}{\sin \gamma}$$ where $\alpha$, $\beta$, and $\gamma$ are the angles opposite to $\vec{F}_1$, $\vec{F}_2$, and $\vec{F}_3$ respectively.

📐 Geometric Interpretation

Geometrically, the vectors representing the three forces can be arranged in magnitude and direction to form a closed triangle (Triangle Law of Forces). The interior angles of this force triangle are supplementary to the angles between the lines of action of the forces.

⚙️ Algebraic & Structural Meaning

Algebraically, the vector sum of the forces must vanish ($\sum \vec{F} = 0$), and the algebraic sum of their moments about any point in their plane must also vanish ($\sum \vec{\tau} = 0$), reducing the static equilibrium equations to scalar trigonometric ratios.

Pillar 2 Hypotheses & Condition Checklist

Every hypothesis is strictly non-negotiable. Inspect the domain type and the exact consequence if a condition fails:

Hypothesis / Condition Domain / Interval Necessity What Fails If Condition Dropped
Coplanarity of Forces Two-dimensional plane ($$\mathbb{R}^2$$) Strictly Mandatory / Necessary If the forces are non-coplanar and non-concurrent, they constitute a general spatial force system or wrench, and Lami's Theorem fails completely.
Concurrence of Lines of Action Point intersection requirement Strictly Mandatory If three forces are coplanar and parallel (non-concurrent), they form a couple or produce net rotation, violating equilibrium unless balanced by a third intersecting force.
Exact count of three forces $N = 3$ Essential for Lami's ratios For $N \neq 3$, Lami's direct sine-ratio formula cannot be applied directly without resolving into orthogonal components ($$\sum F_x = 0, \sum F_y = 0$$).

Pillar 3 High-Yield Exam Results & Direct Shortcuts

Standard 15-to-30 second shortcuts frequently tested in UP TGT/PGT and DSSSB:

01

Shortcut 1: Lami's Ratio Direct Application

$$F_1 : F_2 : F_3 = \sin \alpha : \sin \beta : \sin \gamma$$

Exam Rule: When given angles between three concurrent forces in equilibrium, instantly equate force magnitudes to sines of opposite angles without setting up full $x$-$y$ equilibrium equations.

⚡ PYQ Context: Directly asked in UP PGT/TGT to find unknown tension in strings or weight suspended by strings.
02

Shortcut 2: Lami's Theorem with Right Triangles

$$\frac{T_1}{\sin(90^\circ + \theta)} = \frac{T_2}{\sin(90^\circ + \phi)} = \frac{W}{\sin(180^\circ - (\theta + \phi))}$$

Exam Rule: Simplify sine terms using reduction formulas ($$\sin(90^\circ + \theta) = \cos \theta$$) to instantly obtain trigonometric ratios of string tensions.

⚡ PYQ Context: Standard recurring pattern in KVS PGT and DSSSB mechanics portions.
03

Shortcut 3: Triangle of Forces & Sine Rule Equivalence

$$\frac{a}{\sin A} = \frac{b}{\sin B} = \frac{c}{\sin C}$$

Exam Rule: Recognize that the force polygon is a triangle whose side lengths are proportional to the magnitudes of the forces, allowing geometric similarity shortcuts.

⚡ PYQ Context: Frequently tested in problems involving weights on inclined planes.
04

Shortcut 4: Orthogonal Resolution Alternative ($$\Sigma F_x = 0, \Sigma F_y = 0$$)

$$F_1 \cos \theta_1 + F_2 \cos \theta_2 + F_3 \cos \theta_3 = 0$$

Exam Rule: If angles are cumbersome, align the $x$-axis along one of the unknown forces to eliminate it instantly from the $$\Sigma F_y = 0$$ equation.

⚡ PYQ Context: Used in complex ladder or string equilibrium problems where Lami's angles are obtuse or difficult to compute.
05

Shortcut 5: Lami's Theorem for Equal Forces

$$F_1 = F_2 = F_3 \implies \alpha = \beta = \gamma = 120^\circ$$

Exam Rule: If three forces in equilibrium are equal in magnitude, the angle between any two of them is strictly $120^\circ$.

⚡ PYQ Context: Quick conceptual elimination in multiple-choice questions.
06

Shortcut 6: Rapid Elimination via Triangle Inequality

$$F_1 + F_2 > F_3$$

Exam Rule: Any three forces in equilibrium must form a closed triangle, meaning the sum of any two force magnitudes must strictly exceed the third.

⚡ PYQ Context: Instantly eliminates 2 absurd options in numerical force magnitude questions.

Pillar 4 Classic Exam Traps & Counterexamples

Where examiners construct tricky trap options and how to safeguard your marks:

⚠️ EXAM TRAP #01

Trap 1: Non-Concurrent Coplanar Forces (Parallel Forces)

Common Pitfall: Applying Lami's theorem blindly to three parallel coplanar forces (e.g., three parallel beam loads).

Counterexample: Three parallel forces $F_1, F_2, F_3$ acting vertically downwards on a beam.

Why it fails: The angle between parallel forces is $0^\circ$ or $180^\circ$, making $\sin \theta = 0$, leading to division by zero in Lami's formula.

🛡️ Exam Defense: Check concurrence: Lami's theorem requires lines of action to intersect at a single point. If parallel, use moment equilibrium ($$\sum M = 0$$).
⚠️ EXAM TRAP #02

Trap 2: Incorrect Angle Identification in Lami's Theorem

Common Pitfall: Taking the angle adjacent to the force instead of the angle between the *other two* forces.

Counterexample: Using the angle between $F_1$ and $F_2$ as the denominator for $F_1$.

Why it fails: Results in inverted or completely incorrect trigonometric ratios, leading to wrong option selection.

🛡️ Exam Defense: Always draw a clear free-body diagram showing vectors acting outwards from the concurrent point, and use angles between adjacent force vectors.

Pillar 6 Solved Canonical Exam Problems (Dual-Method)

Comparing the formal academic proof against the rapid competitive exam elimination trick:

CANONICAL PROBLEM 01 Dual-Method Breakdown

A weight of $$W$$ is suspended by two strings of lengths $$l_1$$ and $$l_2$$ fixed to two points at the same horizontal level at a distance $$l$$ apart. If the strings make angles $\theta$ and $\phi$ with the vertical, find the tension $$T_1$$ in the first string.

(A) $\frac{W \cos \phi}{\sin(\theta + \phi)}$(B) $\frac{W \sin \phi}{\sin(\theta + \phi)}$(C) $\frac{W \sin \theta}{\sin(\theta + \phi)}$(D) $\frac{W \cos \theta}{\sin(\theta + \phi)}$
✓ Correct Answer: (B)
📚 Method A: Formal Textbook Derivation
Step 1: Draw free-body diagram at the knot where weight $$W$$ is suspended. Step 2: Identify angles between forces: angle between $$T_2$$ and vertical downward weight $$W$$ is $180^\circ - \phi$ (or relative angles). Step 3: Apply Lami's Theorem: $\frac{T_1}{\sin(180^\circ - \phi)} = \frac{W}{\sin(180^\circ - (\theta + \phi))}$ (using appropriate angle relations). Step 4: Simplify to get $T_1 = \frac{W \sin \phi}{\sin(\theta + \phi)}$.
⚡ Method B: 25-Second Exam Shortcut
Use Lami's direct ratio: Tension in string 1 ($T_1$) is proportional to the sine of the angle that string 2 makes with the vertical (i.e., $\sin \phi$), divided by the sine of the included angle between the two strings ($$\sin(\theta + \phi)$$).
💡 Key Takeaway: Memorize this standard string suspension result as it appears directly without numerical values in UP PGT.
CANONICAL PROBLEM 02 Dual-Method Breakdown

Three forces acting on a particle are in equilibrium. If their magnitudes are $$10\text{ N}$$, $$10\text{ N}$$, and $10\sqrt{2}\text{ N}$$, the angle between the two smaller forces is:

(A) $30^\circ$(B) $45^\circ$(C) $90^\circ$(D) $120^\circ$
✓ Correct Answer: (C)
📚 Method A: Formal Textbook Derivation
Step 1: Check triangle condition: $10^2 + 10^2 = 100 + 100 = 200 = (10\sqrt{2})^2$. Step 2: The force vector triangle is a right-angled isosceles triangle. Step 3: The angle between the two equal forces of magnitude $10\text{ N}$ forming the right angle is $90^\circ$.
⚡ Method B: 25-Second Exam Shortcut
Recognize the Pythagorean triplet in magnitudes ($$1^2 + 1^2 = (\sqrt{2})^2$$), which instantly confirms a right-angled triangle of forces, meaning the angle between the orthogonal equal forces is $90^\circ$.
💡 Key Takeaway: Always check if force magnitudes satisfy Pythagoras theorem before applying general sine formulas.
