9. Mechanics — Dynamics

Work, power and energy

Mechanics in dynamics builds upon Newton's laws to study work, conservative and non-conservative forces, kinetic and potential energies, and power via scalar and vector field integrations. Mastery of the Work-Energy Principle and conservation laws allows rapid resolution of complex particle dynamics without integrating full differential equations of motion, making it a high-scoring zone in UP TGT/PGT, DSSSB, and KVS examinations.

Pillar 1 Rigorous Definition & Mathematical Statement

Work-Energy Principle & Conservation of Mechanical Energy
The total work done by all forces (conservative and non-conservative) acting on a particle equals the change in its kinetic energy, given by $$W_{\text{total}} = \Delta T = T_2 - T_1 = \int_{\mathbf{r}_1}^{\mathbf{r}_2} \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{r}$$. If only conservative forces act, the total mechanical energy $E = T + V$ remains constant, where $V$ is the potential energy such that $\mathbf{F} = -\nabla V$.

📐 Geometric Interpretation

Work represents the line integral of a force vector field along a directed path $\mathcal{C}$. Geometrically, it is the area under the curve when plotting the tangential component of force $F_s$ against displacement $s$, or the accumulated scalar product along the trajectory.

⚙️ Algebraic & Structural Meaning

Translates vector differential equations of motion ($\mathbf{F} = m \dot{\mathbf{v}}$) into scalar algebraic conservation equations, drastically simplifying integration when potential functions exist.

Pillar 3 High-Yield Exam Results & Direct Shortcuts

Standard 15-to-30 second shortcuts frequently tested in UP TGT/PGT and DSSSB:

01

Shortcut 1: Conservative Force from Potential Energy

$$F_x = -\frac{\partial V}{\partial x}, \quad F_y = -\frac{\partial V}{\partial y}, \quad F_z = -\frac{\partial V}{\partial z}$$

Exam Rule: To find force components from a given potential $V(x,y,z)$, take partial derivatives with respect to position coordinates and negate them immediately without full vector expansion.

⚡ PYQ Context: Frequently asked in UP PGT to test gradient-force relations.
02

Shortcut 2: Work Done by Constant Force

$$W = \mathbf{F} \cdot \mathbf{s} = F s \cos\theta$$

Exam Rule: For uniform gravitational or electric fields, ignore intermediate path geometry; compute only the net displacement vector $\mathbf{s} = \mathbf{r}_2 - \mathbf{r}_1$.

⚡ PYQ Context: Standard 5-second question in TGT/PGT examinations.
03

Shortcut 3: Power-Velocity-Force Relation

$$P = \frac{dW}{dt} = \mathbf{F} \cdot \mathbf{v}$$

Exam Rule: When an engine pulls a load at constant speed or constant acceleration, instantaneous power is the direct dot product of tractive force and velocity vector.

⚡ PYQ Context: Appears in vehicle traction and pump rate problems in DSSSB PGT.
04

Shortcut 4: Equilibrium Stability Test via Potential Energy

$$\frac{dV}{dx} = 0 \quad \text{and} \quad \frac{d^2V}{dx^2} \begin{cases} > 0 & \text{(Stable)} \\ < 0 & \text{(Unstable)} \end{cases}$$

Exam Rule: Differentiate potential $V(x)$ once to find equilibrium positions, then check the sign of the second derivative to classify stability instantly.

⚡ PYQ Context: Directly tested across all state PGT mathematics papers.
05

Shortcut 5: Conservation of Energy for Projectiles / Oscillators

$$\frac{1}{2}m v_1^2 + V_1 = \frac{1}{2}m v_2^2 + V_2$$

Exam Rule: Equate initial and final total mechanical energy to find velocity at any height or elongation without solving kinematics differential equations.

⚡ PYQ Context: Core technique for particle-on-string or spring-mass problems.
06

Shortcut 6: Central Forces and Angular Momentum Conservation

$$L = m r^2 \dot{\theta} = \text{constant}$$

Exam Rule: Under central forces, use areal velocity conservation $\frac{dA}{dt} = \frac{L}{2m}$ to link radial distance and transverse speed.

⚡ PYQ Context: Standard elimination tool for orbital and central orbit mechanics questions.

Pillar 4 Classic Exam Traps & Counterexamples

Where examiners construct tricky trap options and how to safeguard your marks:

⚠️ EXAM TRAP #01

Trap 1: Ignoring Non-Conservative Forces (Friction)

Common Pitfall: Applying total mechanical energy conservation ($E_1 = E_2$) when frictional or dissipative forces are explicitly present in the system.

Counterexample: A block sliding on a rough horizontal plane with coefficient of friction $\mu$.

Why it fails: Mechanical energy decreases because non-conservative work $W_{\text{nc}} = -f_k s = \Delta E \neq 0$. Total energy is conserved only if thermal dissipation is included.

🛡️ Exam Defense: Always scan the question for terms like 'rough', 'resistance', 'dissipative', or 'viscous' which invalidate pure mechanical energy conservation.
⚠️ EXAM TRAP #02

Trap 2: Curl Test Omission for Potential Existence

Common Pitfall: Assuming any given force vector $\mathbf{F}(x,y)$ has a potential energy function without verifying if $\nabla \times \mathbf{F} = 0$.

Counterexample: $\mathbf{F} = -y\mathbf{i} + x\mathbf{j}$ (Rotational field)

Why it fails: Here $\nabla \times \mathbf{F} = 2\mathbf{k} \neq 0$. The force is non-conservative; work done around a closed loop is non-zero, so $V$ does not exist.

🛡️ Exam Defense: Check $\frac{\partial F_y}{\partial x} = \frac{\partial F_x}{\partial y}$ in 2D before attempting to integrate $V = -\int \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{r}$.

Pillar 6 Solved Canonical Exam Problems (Dual-Method)

Comparing the formal academic proof against the rapid competitive exam elimination trick:

CANONICAL PROBLEM 01 Dual-Method Breakdown

A particle of mass $2\text{ kg}$ moves in a conservative force field with potential energy $V(x, y) = 3x^2 + 4y^2$. If the particle starts from rest at $(1, 2)$, what is its kinetic energy when it reaches the origin $(0, 0)$?

(A) $19\text{ J}$(B) $23\text{ J}$(C) $27\text{ J}$(D) $31\text{ J}$
✓ Correct Answer: (B)
📚 Method A: Formal Textbook Derivation
Step 1: Compute initial potential energy $V_1 = V(1, 2) = 3(1)^2 + 4(2)^2 = 3 + 16 = 19\text{ J}$. Step 2: Compute final potential energy $V_2 = V(0, 0) = 0$. Step 3: Use conservation of mechanical energy $T_1 + V_1 = T_2 + V_2$. Since $T_1 = 0$ (starts from rest), $0 + 19 = T_2 + 0 \implies T_2 = 19\text{ J}$? Wait, let's re-evaluate: $\Delta T = -\Delta V \implies T_2 - 0 = -(0 - 19) = 19$. Wait, let's check values: $V_1 = 3(1) + 4(4) = 19$. $V_2 = 0$. $\Delta V = -19$. Thus $\Delta T = 19\text{ J}$. Option (A) is $19\text{ J}$. Let's adjust options so correct is clear: (A) $19\text{ J}$, (B) $38\text{ J}$, (C) $9.5\text{ J}$, (D) $0\text{ J}$. Correct option (A).
⚡ Method B: 25-Second Exam Shortcut
Instant energy conservation: $T_2 = V(1,2) - V(0,0) = (3+16) - 0 = 19\text{ J}$.
💡 Key Takeaway: Do not compute line integrals of force; evaluate potential energy directly at endpoints.
CANONICAL PROBLEM 02 Dual-Method Breakdown

An engine develops a constant power $P$ while pulling a train of mass $m$ along a horizontal track against a constant resistance $R$. If the maximum uniform speed of the train is $v_{\max}$, what is the tractive force at speed $v$?

(A) $\frac{P}{v}$(B) $R \frac{v_{\max}}{v}$(C) $\frac{P}{v_{\max}}$(D) $mv$
✓ Correct Answer: (A)
📚 Method A: Formal Textbook Derivation
Step 1: Power delivered by the engine is $P = F v$, where $F$ is the total forward tractive force. Step 2: Therefore, $F = \frac{P}{v}$. At maximum speed, tractive force equals resistance $R$, so $P = R v_{\max}$.
⚡ Method B: 25-Second Exam Shortcut
Power equals force times velocity ($P = Fv$), hence $F = P/v$ regardless of resistance for ideal engine output.
💡 Key Takeaway: Distinguish carefully between total tractive force and net accelerating force ($F - R$).

Pillar 7 10 Practice MCQs for Self-Assessment

Q1
MCQ 1: If the work done by a force in moving a particle around any closed path is zero, then the force is:
A Conservative
B Non-conservative
C Central only
D Zero
Derivation & Analysis:

By definition, a force is conservative if and only if the line integral around any closed loop vanishes: $\oint \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{r} = 0$.

Q2
MCQ 2: A force $\mathbf{F} = (2xy + z^3)\mathbf{i} + x^2\mathbf{j} + 3xz^2\mathbf{k}$ is conservative. Its potential energy function $V(x,y,z)$ such that $\mathbf{F} = -\nabla V$ is:
A $-(x^2 y + x z^3) + C$
B $x^2 y + x z^3 + C$
C $2xy + z^3 + C$
D $x^2 y^2 + z^3 + C$
Derivation & Analysis:

Integrating $-\frac{\partial V}{\partial x} = 2xy + z^3$ gives $V = -(x^2 y + x z^3) + f(y,z)$. Checking partial derivatives confirms $V = -(x^2 y + x z^3) + C$.

Q3
MCQ 3: The potential energy of a one-dimensional conservative system is given by $V(x) = x^3 - 3x^2 + 2$. The position of stable equilibrium is at:
A $x = 0$
B $x = 2$
C $x = 1$
D $x = 3$
Derivation & Analysis:

First derivative $V'(x) = 3x^2 - 6x = 3x(x-2) = 0 \implies x = 0, 2$. Second derivative $V''(x) = 6x - 6$. At $x = 2$, $V''(2) = 6 > 0$, giving stable equilibrium.

Q4
MCQ 4: A pump is required to lift $600\text{ kg}$ of water per minute from a well $20\text{ m}$ deep. The power of the pump is ($g = 9.8\text{ m/s}^2$):
A $1.96\text{ kW}$
B $3.92\text{ kW}$
C $117.6\text{ kW}$
D $980\text{ W}$
Derivation & Analysis:

Power $P = \frac{mgh}{t} = \frac{600 \times 9.8 \times 20}{60} = 10 \times 196 = 1960\text{ W} = 1.96\text{ kW}$.

Q5
MCQ 5: If kinetic energy of a particle increases by $300\%$, the percentage increase in its momentum is:
A $100\%$
B ($\sqrt{3} - 1) \times 100\%$
C $200\%$
D $300\%$
Derivation & Analysis:

Kinetic energy $T = \frac{p^2}{2m}$. If $T_2 = 4 T_1$ ($300\%$ increase means final is $100\% + 300\% = 400\%$ of initial), then $p_2 = \sqrt{4} p_1 = 2 p_1$. Percentage increase in $p$ is $\frac{2p_1 - p_1}{p_1} \times 100\% = 100\%$.

Q6
MCQ 6: Work done by a uniform gravitational force $\mathbf{mg}$ on a particle moving from $(x_1, y_1, z_1)$ to $(x_2, y_2, z_2)$ is:
A $-mg(z_2 - z_1)$
B $mg(z_2 - z_1)$
C $mg(x_2 - x_1)$
D 0
Derivation & Analysis:

Work $W = -\Delta V = -(mg z_2 - mg z_1) = -mg(z_2 - z_1)$. Gravity is conservative and acts downward along the $z$-axis.

Q7
MCQ 7: A particle of mass $m$ moves under the influence of a spring with stiffness $k$. The angular frequency of oscillation $\omega$ is:
A $\sqrt{\frac{k}{m}}$
B $\frac{k}{m}$
C $\sqrt{\frac{m}{k}}$
D $\frac{m}{k}$
Derivation & Analysis:

From potential energy $V = \frac{1}{2}kx^2$, restoring force $F = -kx = m\ddot{x}$, leading to $\ddot{x} + \frac{k}{m}x = 0$, giving $\omega = \sqrt{k/m}.

Q8
MCQ 8: Which of the following forces is non-conservative?
A Gravitational force
B Electrostatic force
C Viscous drag force
D Spring elastic force
Derivation & Analysis:

Viscosity and friction dissipate mechanical energy into heat, making work path-dependent, hence non-conservative.

Q9
MCQ 9: If a force $\mathbf{F} = 3\mathbf{i} + 4\mathbf{j} + 5\mathbf{k}$ displaces a body from $\mathbf{r}_1 = \mathbf{i} + 2\mathbf{j} + 3\mathbf{k}$ to $\mathbf{r}_2 = 3\mathbf{i} + 4\mathbf{j} + 5\mathbf{k}$, the work done is:
A $12\text{ J}$
B $26\text{ J}$
C $20\text{ J}$
D $50\text{ J}$
Derivation & Analysis:

Displacement $\mathbf{s} = \mathbf{r}_2 - \mathbf{r}_1 = (3-1)\mathbf{i} + (4-2)\mathbf{j} + (5-3)\mathbf{k} = 2\mathbf{i} + 2\mathbf{j} + 2\mathbf{k}$. Work $W = \mathbf{F} \cdot \mathbf{s} = 3(2) + 4(2) + 5(2) = 6 + 8 + 10 = 24\text{ J}$? Wait: $3(2)+4(2)+5(2) = 6+8+10 = 24\text{ J}$. Let's check options: (A) $12$, (B) $24$, (C) $20$, (D) $50$. Let's make correct option (B) $24\text{ J}$.

Q10
MCQ 10: The instantaneous power $P$ delivered to a body of mass $m$ starting from rest under a constant force $F$ at time $t$ is:
A $\frac{F^2 t}{m}$
B $\frac{Ft}{m}$
C $Fmt$
D $F^2 t^2 m$
Derivation & Analysis:

Velocity at time $t$ is $v = \frac{F}{m}t$. Power $P = F v = F \left(\frac{F}{m}t\right) = \frac{F^2 t}{m}$.
