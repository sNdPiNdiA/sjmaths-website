9. Mechanics — Dynamics

Central orbits

Central orbits describe the motion of a particle under a force always directed towards or away from a fixed point (the center of force), governed by fundamental conservation laws of angular momentum and energy. Mastering pedal equations, polar differential equations, and apsidal distances is essential for scoring high in competitive examinations like UP PGT/TGT, KVS, and DSSSB.

Target Exams: UP PGT Mathematics UP TGT Mathematics DSSSB PGT / TGT KVS / NVS PGT LT Grade
📖 Self-Study Mastery Status
○ Mark Topic as Mastered

Pillar 1 Rigorous Definition & Mathematical Statement

Differential Equation of a Central Orbit (Binet's Form and Polar Form)
Let a particle of unit mass move under a central force $P$ per unit mass directed towards the pole $O$. The differential equation of the central orbit in polar coordinates $(r, \theta)$ is given by $$h^2 \left[ \frac{d^2r}{d\theta^2} - \frac{2}{r} \left(\frac{dr}{d\theta}\right)^2 + r \right] = \frac{P}{u^2}$$ or in terms of $u = \frac{1}{r}$ and pedal variable $p$: $$h^2 \left( \frac{d^2u}{d\theta^2} + u \right) = P = \frac{h^2}{p^3} \frac{dp}{dr}$$, where $h = r^2 \dot{\theta}$ is the constant areal velocity (angular momentum per unit mass).

📐 Geometric Interpretation

The trajectory is a plane curve where the radius vector sweeps out equal areas in equal times ($\frac{1}{2} r^2 \dot{\theta} = \frac{h}{2} = \text{constant}$). The pedal equation $p = f(r)$ links the perpendicular from the pole to the tangent with the radius vector.

⚙️ Algebraic & Structural Meaning

Transforms a complex vector equation of motion into a second-order nonlinear ordinary differential equation in $u$ and $\theta$, or a first-order separable relation in $p$ and $r$.

Pillar 2 Hypotheses & Condition Checklist

Every hypothesis is strictly non-negotiable. Inspect the domain type and the exact consequence if a condition fails:

Hypothesis / Condition Domain / Interval Necessity What Fails If Condition Dropped
Central Force Condition Domain of motion $r \in (0, \infty)$ Strictly Mandatory If the line of action of the force does not pass through a fixed center, torque is non-zero, and angular momentum $h$ is not conserved.
Smoothness of Orbit Curve Differentiable on trajectory interval Strictly Mandatory Discontinuities or sharp corners in $r(\theta)$ lead to undefined second derivatives $\frac{d^2u}{d\theta^2}$, rendering Binet's equation inapplicable.
Non-zero Areal Velocity $h \neq 0$ Essential for conclusion If $h = 0$, motion is purely rectilinear (radial), causing the denominator terms involving $h$ to degenerate into radial free-fall.

Pillar 3 High-Yield Exam Results & Direct Shortcuts

Standard 15-to-30 second shortcuts frequently tested in UP TGT/PGT and DSSSB:

01

Shortcut 1: Inverse-Square Law Force ($P = \frac{\mu}{r^2}$)

$$u = \frac{1}{l} [1 + e \cos(\theta - \alpha)]$$

Exam Rule: For a force inversely proportional to $r^2$ (like gravity), the orbit is always a conic section with origin at a focus, where $l$ is semi-latus rectum and $e$ is eccentricity.

⚡ PYQ Context: Frequently asked in UP PGT to immediately identify conic orbits from force laws.
02

Shortcut 2: Velocity at any point in a Central Orbit

$$v^2 = \hbar^2 \left[ \left(\frac{du}{d\theta}\right)^2 + u^2 \right] = \frac{2}{m} \int P dr + C$$

Exam Rule: Velocity can be found directly from the energy integral without integrating the full differential equation.

⚡ PYQ Context: Direct numerical questions in KVS/DSSSB PGT calculating speed at periapsis and apoapsis.
03

Shortcut 3: Apsidal Distance Condition

$$\frac{dr}{d\theta} = 0 \quad \text{or} \quad \frac{du}{d\theta} = 0$$

Exam Rule: At apses (nearest or furthest points from the center), the tangent is perpendicular to the radius vector, so $p = r$.

⚡ PYQ Context: Standard question pattern: 'Find the apsidal distance for the given central orbit'.
04

Shortcut 4: Pedal Equation to Force Law

$$P = \frac{h^2}{p^3} \frac{dp}{dr}$$

Exam Rule: Given $p = f(r)$, take logarithmic derivative or differentiate directly to find $P$ in seconds.

⚡ PYQ Context: Saves 3 full minutes in UP TGT/PGT exams compared to converting to polar $u-\theta$ form.
05

Shortcut 5: Law of Force for Equiangular Spiral ($r = a e^{\theta \cot \alpha}$)

$$P = \frac{h^2 \csc^2 \alpha}{r^3}$$

Exam Rule: Memorize standard curves: For $r^n = a^n \cos(n\theta)$, $P \propto r^{2n+3}$ or $u^{2n+3}$. For $r = a e^{\theta \cot \alpha}$, $P \propto \frac{1}{r^3}$.

⚡ PYQ Context: Direct memory-based question in UP PGT Mathematics.
06

Shortcut 6: Apsidal Angle $\psi$

$$\psi = \int_{\theta_1}^{\theta_2} d\theta = \frac{\pi}{\sqrt{2k + 2}} \quad \text{(for } P = \mu u^n \text{)}$$

Exam Rule: Angle between two consecutive apses determines whether the orbit is apsidally stable or closed.

⚡ PYQ Context: Advanced PGT question testing apsidal angle formula.

Pillar 4 Classic Exam Traps & Counterexamples

Where examiners construct tricky trap options and how to safeguard your marks:

⚠️ EXAM TRAP #01

Trap 1: Confusing $u$ with $r$ in Binet's Equation

Common Pitfall: Substituting $r$ directly into $h^2(\frac{d^2u}{d\theta^2} + u) = P$ without taking $u = \frac{1}{r}$.

Counterexample: Taking $\frac{d^2r}{d\theta^2}$ instead of $\frac{d^2u}{d\theta^2}$ when $P$ is given in terms of $r$.

Why it fails: Leads to completely erroneous differential equations and mismatch with options.

🛡️ Exam Defense: Always explicitly write $u = 1/r$, $dr = -u^{-2} du$ before substituting into Binet's equation.
⚠️ EXAM TRAP #02

Trap 2: Misinterpreting Apsidal Distance as Mean Radius

Common Pitfall: Assuming apsidal distances occur when $\frac{d^2u}{d\theta^2} = 0$ instead of $\frac{du}{d\theta} = 0$.

Counterexample: Equating curvature instead of radial velocity extrema.

Why it fails: Apses are turning points of radius vector ($dr/d\theta = 0$), not points of inflection.

🛡️ Exam Defense: Always set $\frac{du}{d\theta} = 0$ to find apsidal distances $r$.

CANONICAL PROBLEM 01 Dual-Method Breakdown

If the law of force towards the pole is $\frac{\mu}{r^3}$, find the differential equation of the orbit and the form of the orbit.

(A) Circle(B) Equiangular spiral(C) Parabola(D) Ellipse
✓ Correct Answer: (B)
📚 Method A: Formal Textbook Derivation
Step 1: Substitute $P = \mu u^3$ into Binet's equation $h^2(\frac{d^2u}{d\theta^2} + u) = \frac{\mu u^3}{u^2} = \mu u$. Step 2: Simplify to $\frac{d^2u}{d\theta^2} + (1 - \frac{\mu}{h^2})u = 0$. Step 3: Solving this linear differential equation yields exponential/spiral solutions for appropriate constants.
⚡ Method B: 25-Second Exam Shortcut
Recognize the standard result: Force proportional to $r^{-3}$ always corresponds to the equiangular spiral $r = a e^{\theta \cot \alpha}$ in central orbits.
💡 Key Takeaway: Memorize force laws mapped to curves: $r^{-2} \to \text{Conic}$, $r^{-3} \to \text{Spiral}$, $r \to \text{Ellipse/Harmonic}$. Target 10-second recall.
CANONICAL PROBLEM 02 Dual-Method Breakdown

Find the law of force towards the pole for the orbit $r^n = a^n \cos(n\theta)$.

(A) $P \propto u^2$(B) $P \propto u^3$(C) $P \propto u^{2n+3}$(D) $P \propto u^{n+1}$
✓ Correct Answer: (C)
📚 Method A: Formal Textbook Derivation
Step 1: Take log of $r^n = a^n \cos(n\theta) \implies n \log r = n \log a + \log(\cos(n\theta))$. Step 2: Use pedal equation conversion $p = r \sin\psi$. Step 3: Relate $p$ and $r$ to get $p^2 = \frac{r^{n+1}}{a^n}$ or similar, then apply $P = \frac{h^2}{p^3} \frac{dp}{dr}$ to arrive at $P \propto r^{-(2n+3)} = u^{2n+3}$.
⚡ Method B: 25-Second Exam Shortcut
Standard formula for $r^n = a^n \cos(n\theta)$ or $r^n = a^n \sin(n\theta)$ is always $P \propto u^{2n+3}$.
💡 Key Takeaway: Direct standard textbook result often asked verbatim in UP PGT.

Q1
MCQ 1: The differential equation of a central orbit in polar coordinates using $u = \frac{1}{r}$ is given by:
A $h^2(\frac{d^2u}{d\theta^2} + u) = P$
B $h^2(\frac{d^2u}{d\theta^2} - u) = P$
C $h(\frac{d^2u}{d\theta^2} + u) = P/u^2$
D $h^2\frac{d^2u}{d\theta^2} = P$
Derivation & Analysis:

Binet's standard differential equation for central orbits is $h^2(\frac{d^2u}{d\theta^2} + u) = \frac{P}{u^2} = P r^2$, which simplifies to $h^2(\frac{d^2u}{d\theta^2} + u) = P$ when $P$ denotes force per unit mass divided by $u^2$ (or directly expressed as $P$). Option (A) is the standard textbook form.

Q2
MCQ 2: If the central orbit is a circle passing through the center of force (pole) with diameter $d$, the law of force is proportional to:
A $r$
B $r^{-2}$
C $r^{-3}$
D $r^{-5}$
Derivation & Analysis:

For a circle passing through the pole, $r = a \cos\theta$. Using Binet's equation or pedal equation $p = \frac{r^2}{a}$, we find $P = \frac{h^2}{p^3}\frac{dp}{dr} \propto r^{-5}$. Thus $P \propto u^5$ or $r^{-5}$.

Q3
MCQ 3: The areal velocity $h$ of a particle moving in a central orbit is given by:
A $r^2 \dot{\theta}$
B $r \dot{\theta}^2$
C $r \ddot{\theta}$
D $\frac{1}{2} r^2 \theta$
Derivation & Analysis:

Areal velocity is defined as $\frac{dA}{dt} = \frac{1}{2} r^2 \dot{\theta} = \text{constant} = \frac{h}{2}$, so $h = r^2 \dot{\theta}$.

Q4
MCQ 4: At an apse of a central orbit, the value of $\frac{dr}{d\theta}$ is:
A 1
B 0
C Infinity
D Undefined
Derivation & Analysis:

An apse is a point where the radius vector is a maximum or minimum, which mathematically corresponds to $\frac{dr}{d\theta} = 0$ (or $\frac{du}{d\theta} = 0$).

Q5
MCQ 5: If the central force is $\mu u^2$ ($P = \frac{\mu}{r^2}$), the orbit is a:
A Circle
B Parabola
C Conic section (Ellipse/Parabola/Hyperbola)
D Equiangular spiral
Derivation & Analysis:

An inverse-square law of force ($P = \mu / r^2$) always yields a conic section with focus at the center of force.

Q6
MCQ 6: The pedal equation of a central orbit is given by $\frac{1}{p^2} = \frac{1}{a^2} + \frac{1}{b^2} - \frac{r}{a^2b^2}$... wait, more simply, if $p^2 = ar$, then the law of force $P$ is proportional to:
A $r^{-3}$
B $r^{-4}$
C $r^{-2}$
D $r$
Derivation & Analysis:

Using $P = \frac{h^2}{p^3} \frac{dp}{dr}$. If $p^2 = ar$, then $2p \frac{dp}{dr} = a \implies \frac{dp}{dr} = \frac{a}{2p}$. Thus $P = \frac{h^2}{p^3} \left(\frac{a}{2p}\right) \propto \frac{1}{p^4} \propto r^{-2}$? Wait, let's check: if $p^2 = ar$, then $p^3 = a^{3/2} r^{3/2}$ or similar. More precisely, $p^4 = a^2 r^2 \implies P \propto r^{-4}$ when evaluated carefully.

Q7
MCQ 7: If the orbit is a circle $r = 2a \cos\theta$ with center of force at the pole, the law of force is:
A $\frac{\mu}{r^2}$
B $\frac{\mu}{r^3}$
C $\frac{\mu}{r^5}$
D $\mu r$
Derivation & Analysis:

For $r = 2a \cos\theta$, $u = \frac{1}{2a}\sec\theta$. Applying Binet's equation yields $P \propto u^5 = \frac{1}{r^5}$.

Q8
MCQ 8: Assertion (A): In any central orbit, angular momentum about the center of force is conserved. Reason (R): The central force passes through the center, producing zero torque.
A Both (A) and (R) are true and (R) is the correct explanation of (A)
B Both (A) and (R) are true but (R) is not the correct explanation
C (A) is true but (R) is false
D (A) is false but (R) is true
Derivation & Analysis:

Since the line of action of the central force passes through the pole, the torque $\vec{\tau} = \vec{r} \times \vec{F} = \vec{0}$, which implies conservation of angular momentum ($\vec{h} = \text{constant}$). Thus both are true and (R) explains (A).

Q9
MCQ 9: The angle $\psi$ between the radius vector and the tangent to the central orbit is given by:
A $\sin\psi = \frac{p}{r}$
B $\cos\psi = \frac{p}{r}$
C $\tan\psi = r \frac{d\theta}{dr}$
D $\psi = \theta$
Derivation & Analysis:

By definition of the pedal equation, $p = r \sin\psi$, which gives $\sin\psi = \frac{p}{r}$.

Q10
MCQ 10: If the velocity of a particle in a central orbit at distance $r$ from the center is $v$, then the transverse component of velocity is:
A $r \dot{\theta}$
B $\dot{r}$
C $\sqrt{\dot{r}^2 + r^2\dot{\theta}^2}$
D $r^2 \dot{\theta}$
Derivation & Analysis:

In polar coordinates, velocity has two orthogonal components: radial component $\dot{r}$ along the radius vector, and transverse component $r \dot{\theta}$ perpendicular to the radius vector.

Q2
MCQ 2: If the central orbit is a circle passing through the center of force (pole) with diameter $d$, the law of force is proportional to:
A $r$
B $r^{-2}$
C $r^{-3}$
D $r^{-5}$
Derivation & Analysis:

For a circle passing through the pole, $r = a \cos\theta$. Using Binet's equation or pedal equation $p = \frac{r^2}{a}$, we find $P = \frac{h^2}{p^3}\frac{dp}{dr} \propto r^{-5}$. Thus $P \propto u^5$ or $r^{-5}$.

Q3
MCQ 3: The areal velocity $h$ of a particle moving in a central orbit is given by:
A $r^2 \dot{\theta}$
B $r \dot{\theta}^2$
C $r \ddot{\theta}$
D $\frac{1}{2} r^2 \theta$
Derivation & Analysis:

Areal velocity is defined as $\frac{dA}{dt} = \frac{1}{2} r^2 \dot{\theta} = \text{constant} = \frac{h}{2}$, so $h = r^2 \dot{\theta}$.

Q4
MCQ 4: At an apse of a central orbit, the value of $\frac{dr}{d\theta}$ is:
A 1
B 0
C Infinity
D Undefined
Derivation & Analysis:

An apse is a point where the radius vector is a maximum or minimum, which mathematically corresponds to $\frac{dr}{d\theta} = 0$ (or $\frac{du}{d\theta} = 0$).

Q5
MCQ 5: If the central force is $\mu u^2$ ($P = \frac{\mu}{r^2}$), the orbit is a:
A Circle
B Parabola
C Conic section (Ellipse/Parabola/Hyperbola)
D Equiangular spiral
Derivation & Analysis:

An inverse-square law of force ($P = \mu / r^2$) always yields a conic section with focus at the center of force.

Q6
MCQ 6: The pedal equation of a central orbit is given by $\frac{1}{p^2} = \frac{1}{a^2} + \frac{1}{b^2} - \frac{r}{a^2b^2}$... wait, more simply, if $p^2 = ar$, then the law of force $P$ is proportional to:
A $r^{-3}$
B $r^{-4}$
C $r^{-2}$
D $r$
Derivation & Analysis:

Using $P = \frac{h^2}{p^3} \frac{dp}{dr}$. If $p^2 = ar$, then $2p \frac{dp}{dr} = a \implies \frac{dp}{dr} = \frac{a}{2p}$. Thus $P = \frac{h^2}{p^3} \left(\frac{a}{2p}\right) \propto \frac{1}{p^4} \propto r^{-2}$? Wait, let's check: if $p^2 = ar$, then $p^3 = a^{3/2} r^{3/2}$ or similar. More precisely, $p^4 = a^2 r^2 \implies P \propto r^{-4}$ when evaluated carefully.

Q7
MCQ 7: If the orbit is a circle $r = 2a \cos\theta$ with center of force at the pole, the law of force is:
A $\frac{\mu}{r^2}$
B $\frac{\mu}{r^3}$
C $\frac{\mu}{r^5}$
D $\mu r$
Derivation & Analysis:

For $r = 2a \cos\theta$, $u = \frac{1}{2a}\sec\theta$. Applying Binet's equation yields $P \propto u^5 = \frac{1}{r^5}$.

Q8
MCQ 8: Assertion (A): In any central orbit, angular momentum about the center of force is conserved. Reason (R): The central force passes through the center, producing zero torque.
A Both (A) and (R) are true and (R) is the correct explanation of (A)
B Both (A) and (R) are true but (R) is not the correct explanation
C (A) is true but (R) is false
D (A) is false but (R) is true
Derivation & Analysis:

Since the line of action of the central force passes through the pole, the torque $\vec{\tau} = \vec{r} \times \vec{F} = \vec{0}$, which implies conservation of angular momentum ($\vec{h} = \text{constant}$). Thus both are true and (R) explains (A).

Q9
MCQ 9: The angle $\psi$ between the radius vector and the tangent to the central orbit is given by:
A $\sin\psi = \frac{p}{r}$
B $\cos\psi = \frac{p}{r}$
C $\tan\psi = r \frac{d\theta}{dr}$
D $\psi = \theta$
Derivation & Analysis:

By definition of the pedal equation, $p = r \sin\psi$, which gives $\sin\psi = \frac{p}{r}$.

Q10
MCQ 10: If the velocity of a particle in a central orbit at distance $r$ from the center is $v$, then the transverse component of velocity is:
A $r \dot{\theta}$
B $\dot{r}$
C $\sqrt{\dot{r}^2 + r^2\dot{\theta}^2}$
D $r^2 \dot{\theta}$
Derivation & Analysis:

In polar coordinates, velocity has two orthogonal components: radial component $\dot{r}$ along the radius vector, and transverse component $r \dot{\theta}$ perpendicular to the radius vector.

Pillar 8 Frequently Asked Questions

Binet's form converts reciprocal radius $u = 1/r$ into a linear second-order differential equation with constant coefficients for standard force laws, making integration and solution identification much faster than dealing with $r$ and its derivatives directly.

Confusing apsidal distance conditions ($\frac{du}{d\theta} = 0$) with general orbital inflection points, or miscalculating the exponent of $r$ when converting pedal equations to force laws.

Mechanics typically contributes 8 to 12 questions out of 125 in UP PGT Mathematics, with 2 to 3 questions specifically dedicated to central orbits, force laws, and apsidal distances.

Memorize the standard force law mappings: $P \propto r^{-2}$ (Conics/Kepler), $P \propto r^{-3}$ (Equiangular Spiral), $P \propto r$ (Harmonic oscillator/Ellipse centered at origin), and $P \propto r^{-5}$ (Circle through pole).
