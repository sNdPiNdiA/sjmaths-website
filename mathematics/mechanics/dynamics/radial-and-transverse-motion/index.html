9. Mechanics — Dynamics

Radial and transverse velocity and acceleration

Radial and transverse velocity and acceleration resolve a particle's motion into components along and perpendicular to the radius vector in polar coordinates. Mastery of these kinematic formulas is essential for solving central orbit and planetary motion problems frequently tested in UP PGT/TGT, KVS, and DSSSB examinations.

Target Exams: UP PGT Mathematics UP TGT Mathematics DSSSB PGT / TGT KVS / NVS PGT LT Grade
📖 Self-Study Mastery Status
○ Mark Topic as Mastered

Pillar 1 Rigorous Definition & Mathematical Statement

Kinematic Relations in Polar Coordinates
Let a particle move in a plane curve such that its position at time $t$ is given in polar coordinates by $(r, \theta)$. The radial velocity is $v_r = \dot{r}$, the transverse velocity is $v_t = r\dot{\theta}$, the radial acceleration is $f_r = \ddot{r} - r\dot{\theta}^2$, and the transverse acceleration is $f_t = \frac{1}{r}\frac{d}{dt}(r^2\dot{\theta}) = r\ddot{\theta} + 2\dot{r}\dot{\theta}$.

📐 Geometric Interpretation

The radial velocity represents the rate of change of the distance from the origin, while the transverse velocity represents the linear speed due to angular rotation perpendicular to the radius vector. Similarly, radial and transverse accelerations capture the net inward/outward and Coriolis-like rotational acceleration components.

⚙️ Algebraic & Structural Meaning

Deriving these expressions relies on differentiating unit vectors $\hat{r} = \cos\theta \hat{i} + \sin\theta \hat{j}$ and $\hat{\theta} = -\sin\theta \hat{i} + \cos\theta \hat{j}$ with respect to time $t$, utilizing the chain rule where $\dot{\theta} = \frac{d\theta}{dt}$.

Pillar 4 Classic Exam Traps & Counterexamples

Where examiners construct tricky trap options and how to safeguard your marks:

⚠️ EXAM TRAP #01

Trap 1: Forgetting the $r\dot{\theta}^2$ term in Radial Acceleration

Common Pitfall: Candidates often write radial acceleration simply as $\ddot{r}$, forgetting the centripetal component $-r\dot{\theta}^2$ arising from the rotation of the unit vector $\hat{r}$.

Counterexample: $r = a, \theta = \omega t \implies f_r = -a\omega^2$

Why it fails: Ignoring $-r\dot{\theta}^2$ leads to missing the centripetal acceleration in circular motion.

🛡️ Exam Defense: Memorize the exact formula: $f_r = \ddot{r} - r\dot{\theta}^2$.
⚠️ EXAM TRAP #02

Trap 2: Misinterpreting Transverse Acceleration Formula

Common Pitfall: Confusing transverse acceleration $r\ddot{\theta} + 2\dot{r}\dot{\theta}$ with $\frac{1}{r}\frac{d}{dt}(r\dot{\theta})$.

Counterexample: $f_t = \frac{1}{r}\frac{d}{dt}(r^2\dot{\theta}) \neq \frac{d}{dt}(r\dot{\theta})$

Why it fails: Leads to incorrect expansion and sign errors during exams.

🛡️ Exam Defense: Always expand $f_t$ as $\frac{1}{r} \frac{d}{dt} (r^2 \dot{\theta}) = r\ddot{\theta} + 2\dot{r}\dot{\theta}$.

Specifically, $\frac{d\hat{r}}{dt} = \dot{\theta}\hat{\theta}$ and $\frac{d\hat{\theta}}{dt} = -\dot{\theta}\hat{r}$.

Confusing partial differentiation with total time derivatives or missing the factor of $2$ in the Coriolis-like term $2\dot{r}\dot{\theta}$ of transverse acceleration.
