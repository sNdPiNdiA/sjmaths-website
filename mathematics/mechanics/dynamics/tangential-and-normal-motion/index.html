9. Mechanics — Dynamics

Tangential and normal velocity and acceleration

Tangential and normal components of velocity and acceleration resolve a particle's motion along its instantaneous trajectory, separating rate changes in speed from directional changes. Mastery of these orthogonal components provides a high-scoring edge in UP TGT/PGT and DSSSB examinations by transforming complex curvilinear kinematics into manageable scalar equations.

Pillar 1 Rigorous Definition & Mathematical Statement

Kinematic Decomposition of Velocity and Acceleration along Intrinsic Coordinates
Let a particle move along a plane curve $s = f(t)$ where $s$ is the arc length measured from a fixed point. The velocity vector $\mathbf{v}$ and acceleration vector $\mathbf{a}$ at any point on the trajectory are resolved into tangential ($t$) and normal ($n$) components as $\mathbf{v} = v\hat{\mathbf{t}}$ and $\mathbf{a} = a_t \hat{\mathbf{t}} + a_n \hat{\mathbf{n}}$, where $v = \dot{s}$, $a_t = \dot{v} = v\frac{dv}{ds} = \ddot{s}$, and $a_n = \frac{v^2}{\rho} = \kappa v^2$, with $\rho$ being the radius of curvature and $\kappa = \frac{1}{\rho}$ the curvature.

📐 Geometric Interpretation

The tangential component $\hat{\mathbf{t}}$ points along the tangent line of the curve indicating the direction of motion, while the normal component $\hat{\mathbf{n}}$ points inward toward the center of curvature along the principal normal, capturing the rate at which the direction of motion changes.

⚙️ Algebraic & Structural Meaning

By projecting vector equations onto the moving Frenet-Serret frame $(\hat{\mathbf{t}}, \hat{\mathbf{n}})$, vector differential equations of motion decouple into independent scalar differential equations for arc length and turning rate.

Pillar 3 High-Yield Exam Results & Direct Shortcuts

Standard 15-to-30 second shortcuts frequently tested in UP TGT/PGT and DSSSB:

01

Shortcut 1: Direct Tangential Acceleration Formula

$$a_t = \frac{d}{dt}(v) = v \frac{dv}{ds} = \ddot{s}$$

Exam Rule: When speed is given as a function of arc length $s$, immediately use $v \frac{dv}{ds}$ instead of converting to time $t$.

⚡ PYQ Context: Frequently tested in UP PGT when particle moves under resistance proportional to a power of velocity.
02

Shortcut 2: Normal Acceleration & Curvature Link

$$a_n = \frac{v^2}{\rho} = v^2 \left| \frac{d^2 y / dx^2}{[1 + (dy/dx)^2]^{3/2}} \right|$$

Exam Rule: Substitute the standard radius of curvature formula directly into $a_n = v^2 / \rho$ for cartesian trajectories.

⚡ PYQ Context: Direct numerical calculation questions in DSSSB and KVS PGT Mathematics.
03

Shortcut 3: Uniform Circular Motion Special Case

$$a_t = 0, \quad a_n = \frac{v^2}{r} = r\omega^2$$

Exam Rule: If speed $v$ is constant, tangential acceleration vanishes entirely, leaving only centripetal normal acceleration.

⚡ PYQ Context: Standard conceptual check in UP TGT mechanics sections.
04

Shortcut 4: Magnitude of Total Acceleration

$$|\mathbf{a}| = \sqrt{a_t^2 + a_n^2} = \sqrt{(\ddot{s})^2 + \left(\frac{v^2}{\rho}\right)^2}$$

Exam Rule: Since $\hat{\mathbf{t}}$ and $\hat{\mathbf{n}}$ are orthogonal, never add scalar components algebraically; always use Pythagoras theorem.

⚡ PYQ Context: Commonly framed to test vector magnitude calculations in LT Grade exams.
05

Shortcut 5: Central Orbit Velocity Components

$$v_r = \dot{r}, \quad v_\theta = r\dot{\theta}$$

Exam Rule: For polar coordinates, radial and transverse velocities provide the instantaneous breakdown without full Cartesian mapping.

⚡ PYQ Context: Direct formula lookup in UP PGT dynamics papers.
06

Shortcut 6: Rapid Dimension & Unit Elimination

$$[a_t] = LT^{-2}, \quad [a_n] = LT^{-2}$$

Exam Rule: Eliminate options where dimensional homogeneity fails or where $v$ and $s$ exponents are mismatched.

⚡ PYQ Context: Instant elimination trick for 15-second answers in competitive exams.

Pillar 4 Classic Exam Traps & Counterexamples

Where examiners construct tricky trap options and how to safeguard your marks:

⚠️ EXAM TRAP #01

Trap 1: Confusing Tangential Acceleration with Total Acceleration

Common Pitfall: Candidates equate total acceleration magnitude with $\frac{dv}{dt}$, ignoring the normal centrifugal/centripetal component.

Counterexample: Particle moving on a circle of radius $r$ with constant speed $v$: $\frac{dv}{dt} = 0$, but $a_n = \frac{v^2}{r} \neq 0$.

Why it fails: Neglecting normal acceleration ignores the turning effect of the velocity vector.

🛡️ Exam Defense: Always check if the path is curved; if curvature exists, total acceleration has a normal component.
⚠️ EXAM TRAP #02

Trap 2: Sign Error in Retardation / Deceleration

Common Pitfall: Failing to attach a negative sign when speed decreases with respect to arc length or time.

Counterexample: A particle slowing down has $\ddot{s} < 0$, so $a_t$ is negative.

Why it fails: Results in incorrect vector directions and wrong numerical answers in work-energy problems.

🛡️ Exam Defense: Treat $a_t$ as algebraic: positive for acceleration, negative for deceleration.

Pillar 6 Solved Canonical Exam Problems (Dual-Method)

Comparing the formal academic proof against the rapid competitive exam elimination trick:

CANONICAL PROBLEM 01 Dual-Method Breakdown

A particle moves along a plane curve such that its tangential acceleration is constant $f$, and its normal acceleration is proportional to the square of its speed with constant proportionality $k$. If the initial speed is $u$, find the speed at arc length $s$.

(A) $v = \sqrt{u^2 + 2fs}$(B) $v = u + fs$(C) $v = u e^{fs}$(D) $v = \sqrt{u^2 + fs}$
✓ Correct Answer: (A)
📚 Method A: Formal Textbook Derivation
Step 1: Write tangential acceleration equation: $a_t = v \frac{dv}{ds} = f$. Step 2: Separate variables: $v dv = f ds$. Step 3: Integrate from $0$ to $s$ with speed from $u$ to $v$: $\int_{u}^{v} v dv = \int_{0}^{s} f ds$. Step 4: Obtain $\frac{v^2 - u^2}{2} = fs \implies v = \sqrt{u^2 + 2fs}$.
⚡ Method B: 25-Second Exam Shortcut
Recognize this as a direct kinematic analogue of linear motion under constant acceleration $v^2 = u^2 + 2as$ by replacing distance with arc length $s$.
💡 Key Takeaway: Direct formula recall saves 60 seconds in UP PGT.
CANONICAL PROBLEM 02 Dual-Method Breakdown

If a particle describes a circle of radius $a$ with a uniform angular velocity $\omega$, the magnitude of its total acceleration is:

(A) $a\omega^2$(B) $a^2\omega$(C) Zero(D) $a\omega$
✓ Correct Answer: (A)
📚 Method A: Formal Textbook Derivation
Step 1: Uniform angular velocity implies $\dot{\omega} = 0 \implies a_t = 0$. Step 2: Speed $v = a\omega$. Step 3: Normal acceleration $a_n = \frac{v^2}{a} = \frac{(a\omega)^2}{a} = a\omega^2$. Step 4: Total acceleration magnitude $|\mathbf{a}| = \sqrt{0^2 + (a\omega^2)^2} = a\omega^2$.
⚡ Method B: 25-Second Exam Shortcut
For uniform circular motion, tangential acceleration is zero and total acceleration is identically the centripetal acceleration $a\omega^2$.
💡 Key Takeaway: Do not confuse angular velocity $\omega$ with linear velocity $v$.

Pillar 7 10 Practice MCQs for Self-Assessment

Click on your selected answer to receive immediate feedback and unlock the step-by-step mathematical derivation:

Q1
MCQ 1: The tangential component of acceleration for a particle moving along a curve is given by:
A $\frac{dv}{dt}$
B $\frac{v^2}{\rho}$
C $\frac{d^2s}{dt^2}$
D Both (A) and (C)
Derivation & Analysis:

Tangential acceleration is $a_t = \frac{dv}{dt} = \frac{d^2s}{dt^2} = v\frac{dv}{ds}$. Thus both (A) and (C) represent $a_t$.

Q2
MCQ 2: The normal component of acceleration of a particle moving with speed $v$ on a curve of radius of curvature $\rho$ is:
A $\frac{v}{\rho}$
B $\frac{v^2}{\rho}$
C $\rho v^2$
D $\frac{\rho}{v^2}$
Derivation & Analysis:

By definition, the normal component of acceleration directed toward the center of curvature is $a_n = \frac{v^2}{\rho}$.

Q3
MCQ 3: If the tangential acceleration of a particle is zero throughout its motion, then:
A The path must be a straight line
B The speed of the particle is constant
C The acceleration vector is zero
D The particle is at rest
Derivation & Analysis:

Tangential acceleration $a_t = \frac{dv}{dt} = 0$ implies that the speed $v$ remains constant with respect to time.

Q4
MCQ 4: A particle moves along a circle of radius $r$ with speed $v$. The angle between its velocity vector and acceleration vector is $\theta$. Then $\tan\theta$ equals:
A $\frac{a_t}{a_n}$
B $\frac{a_n}{a_t}$
C $\frac{v}{\rho}$
D 1
Derivation & Analysis:

The velocity vector is purely tangential ($\hat{\mathbf{t}}$). The acceleration vector is $\mathbf{a} = a_t \hat{\mathbf{t}} + a_n \hat{\mathbf{n}}$. The angle $\theta$ between $\hat{\mathbf{t}}$ and $\mathbf{a}$ satisfies $\tan\theta = \frac{a_n}{a_t}$.

Q5
MCQ 5: If the velocity of a particle moving along a straight line is $v = s^2$, its tangential acceleration in terms of $s$ is:
A $2s^2$
B $2s^3$
C $2s$
D $s^4$
Derivation & Analysis:

Tangential acceleration is $a_t = v \frac{dv}{ds}$. Given $v = s^2$, we have $\frac{dv}{ds} = 2s$. Thus $a_t = (s^2)(2s) = 2s^3$.

Q6
MCQ 6: In a central orbit, if the transverse acceleration is zero, then:
A Angular momentum per unit mass is constant
B The path is a circle
C Radial acceleration is zero
D Speed is constant
Derivation & Analysis:

Transverse acceleration is $\frac{1}{r}\frac{d}{dt}(r^2\dot{\theta}) = 0 \implies r^2\dot{\theta} = h$ (constant), which represents conservation of angular momentum.

Q7
MCQ 7: If the normal acceleration of a particle is zero at all points, the trajectory of the particle is:
A A circle
B A parabola
C A straight line
D An ellipse
Derivation & Analysis:

Normal acceleration $a_n = \frac{v^2}{\rho} = 0$. Since $v \neq 0$, this requires $\frac{1}{\rho} = 0$, meaning curvature is zero, so the path is a straight line.

Q8
MCQ 8: A particle moves along a parabola $y^2 = 4ax$ with constant speed $v$. At the vertex, the normal acceleration is:
A $\frac{v^2}{2a}$
B $\frac{v^2}{a}$
C Zero
D $\frac{2v^2}{a}$
Derivation & Analysis:

For $y^2 = 4ax$, the radius of curvature at the vertex $(0,0)$ is $\rho = 2a$. Thus normal acceleration $a_n = \frac{v^2}{2a}$.

Q9
MCQ 9: If speed $v$ is directly proportional to arc length $s$, i.e., $v = ks$, then the tangential acceleration is proportional to:
A $s$
B $s^2$
C $\sqrt{s}$
D Constant
Derivation & Analysis:

$a_t = v \frac{dv}{ds} = (ks)(k) = k^2 s$. Thus $a_t$ is proportional to $s$.

Q10
MCQ 10: The unit vectors along the tangent and normal to a plane curve satisfy which of the following relations?
A $\frac{d\hat{\mathbf{t}}}{ds} = \kappa \hat{\mathbf{n}}$
B $\frac{d\hat{\mathbf{n}}}{ds} = -\kappa \hat{\mathbf{t}}$
C Both (A) and (B)
D $\frac{d\hat{\mathbf{t}}}{ds} = \hat{\mathbf{n}}$
Derivation & Analysis:

Frenet-Serret formulas in 2D plane geometry are $\frac{d\hat{\mathbf{t}}}{ds} = \kappa \hat{\mathbf{n}}$ and $\frac{d\hat{\mathbf{n}}}{ds} = -\kappa \hat{\mathbf{t}}$, where $\kappa = \frac{1}{\rho}$.

Pillar 8 Frequently Asked Questions

Resolving along intrinsic coordinates $(\hat{\mathbf{t}}, \hat{\mathbf{n}})$ directly isolates the rate of change of speed from the turning effect of the trajectory, greatly simplifying problems involving constraints, strings, curves, and resistance.

Candidates often confuse speed $v = \dot{s}$ with displacement components, or forget to include the centrifugal/normal acceleration term $\frac{v^2}{\rho}$ when calculating total force or acceleration on curved paths.

Mechanics (Statics and Dynamics combined) accounts for roughly 15-20% of the paper in UP PGT and about 10-15% in UP TGT, with tangential/normal motion and central orbits appearing almost every alternate year.

Memorize standard radii of curvature for parabolas, astroids, and catenaries, and use the formula $a_t = v \frac{dv}{ds}$ directly when speed is given as a function of arc length rather than converting to time $t$.
