8. Vector Calculus

Gauss-divergence theorem

The Gauss-Divergence Theorem (Ostrogradsky's Theorem) bridges volume integrals and surface integrals by equating the flux of a continuously differentiable vector field across a closed surface to the volume integral of its divergence over the enclosed region. It forms a cornerstone of vector calculus and physics, making it a high-yield, scoring topic in competitive examinations like UP PGT/TGT, KVS, and DSSSB.

Pillar 1 Rigorous Definition & Mathematical Statement

Gauss's Divergence Theorem (Ostrogradsky's Theorem)
Let $V$ be a closed, bounded volume in $\mathbb{R}^3$ bounded by a piecewise smooth, orientable closed surface $S$. If $\vec{F}(x, y, z) = F_1\hat{i} + F_2\hat{j} + F_3\hat{k}$ is a vector field whose component functions have continuous first-order partial derivatives throughout $V$, then $$\iint_{S} \vec{F} \cdot \hat{n} \, dS = \iiint_{V} (\nabla \cdot \vec{F}) \, dV$$ where $\hat{n}$ is the outward-pointing unit normal vector to the surface $S$, and $\nabla \cdot \vec{F} = \frac{\partial F_1}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial F_2}{\partial y} + \frac{\partial F_3}{\partial z}$.

📐 Geometric Interpretation

Geometrically, the theorem states that the total net flux of a vector field streaming outward across a closed boundary surface is equal to the accumulated sum of all sources (positive divergence) and sinks (negative divergence) distributed throughout the interior volume.

⚙️ Algebraic & Structural Meaning

Algebraically, it reduces the order of integration from a 2D surface integral to a 3D volume integral (or vice versa), converting complex boundary flux computations into tractable volume divergence operations.

Pillar 3 High-Yield Exam Results & Direct Shortcuts

Standard 15-to-30 second shortcuts frequently tested in UP TGT/PGT and DSSSB:

01

Shortcut 1: Inverse Square Law Flux (Point Source)

$$\iint_{S} \frac{\vec{r}}{r^3} \cdot \hat{n} \, dS = 4\pi \quad (\text{if origin is inside } V)$$

Exam Rule: If the vector field is of inverse-square type $\vec{F} = \frac{\vec{r}}{r^3}$ and the closed surface surrounds the origin, the surface flux evaluates instantly to $4\pi$ (or $0$ if the origin lies outside $V$), bypassing integration entirely.

⚡ PYQ Context: Directly tested in UP PGT and KVS PGT vector calculus sections.
02

Shortcut 2: Constant Divergence Vector Fields

$$\iint_{S} (ax\hat{i} + by\hat{j} + cz\hat{k}) \cdot \hat{n} \, dS = (a+b+c) \times \text{Volume}(V)$$

Exam Rule: When the divergence $\nabla \cdot \vec{F} = a + b + c$ is a constant, factor it out and simply calculate the geometrical volume of the region $V$.

⚡ PYQ Context: Frequently asked with spheres, cylinders, and rectangular parallelepipeds.
03

Shortcut 3: Position Vector Flux across Standard Surfaces

$$\iint_{S} \vec{r} \cdot \hat{n} \, dS = 3 \times \text{Volume}(V)$$

Exam Rule: For $\vec{F} = \vec{r} = x\hat{i} + y\hat{j} + z\hat{k}$, the divergence is $\nabla \cdot \vec{r} = 1 + 1 + 1 = 3$. Thus, the flux is exactly 3 times the volume of the body.

⚡ PYQ Context: Appears in TGT/PGT matching and calculation questions.
04

Shortcut 4: Solenoidal Vector Fields ($

$$\iint_{S} \vec{F} \cdot \hat{n} \, dS = 0 \quad \text{if } \nabla \cdot \vec{F} = 0$$

Exam Rule: If a vector field is solenoidal (divergence-free), the net flux across any closed surface enclosing a source-free region is identically zero.

⚡ PYQ Context: Standard conceptual MCQs in DSSSB and UP PGT.
05

Shortcut 5: Homogeneous Power Fields

$$\iint_{S} (r^n \vec{r}) \cdot \hat{n} \, dS = (n+3) \iiint_{V} r^n \, dV$$

Exam Rule: Use radial symmetry to convert difficult surface flux integrals into single-variable radial volume integrals using spherical polar coordinates.

⚡ PYQ Context: High-level PGT screening questions.
06

Shortcut 6: Rapid Option Elimination via Dimension Scaling

$$V \propto R^3 \implies \text{Flux} \propto R^3$$

Exam Rule: Check the degree of homogeneity in radius $R$. Surface flux scales with the volume dimension ($R^3$) for constant/linear divergence fields, allowing instant elimination of options with incorrect powers of $R$.

⚡ PYQ Context: Time-saving trick for 125-question competitive exams.

Pillar 4 Classic Exam Traps & Counterexamples

Where examiners construct tricky trap options and how to safeguard your marks:

⚠️ EXAM TRAP #01

Trap 1: Ignoring Singularities Inside the Volume

Common Pitfall: Applying $\nabla \cdot \vec{F} = 0$ over a region containing a point where $\vec{F}$ is undefined (e.g., origin for $\frac{\vec{r}}{r^3}$), leading to a false zero flux result.

Counterexample: \vec{F} = \frac{\vec{r}}{r^3} \text{ over sphere } x^2+y^2+z^2 \le R^2

Why it fails: The divergence formula gives $0$ everywhere except at the origin $(0,0,1)$ where it is undefined. The true flux is $4\pi$, not $0$.

🛡️ Exam Defense: Always check if the origin or any coordinate singularity lies inside the integration volume before computing divergence.
⚠️ EXAM TRAP #02

Trap 2: Wrong Orientation of Surface Normal

Common Pitfall: Using inward-pointing normal instead of outward-pointing normal without reversing the sign.

Counterexample: \hat{n}_{\text{inward}} = -\hat{n}_{\text{outward}}

Why it fails: The theorem strictly requires the outward unit normal vector. An inward normal negates the final computed value.

🛡️ Exam Defense: Verify whether the problem specifies 'outward' or 'inward' flux; introduce a negative sign if inward flux is requested.

Pillar 6 Solved Canonical Exam Problems (Dual-Method)

Comparing the formal academic proof against the rapid competitive exam elimination trick:

CANONICAL PROBLEM 01 Dual-Method Breakdown

Evaluate $\iint_{S} (x^3 \hat{i} + y^3 \hat{j} + z^3 \hat{k}) \cdot \hat{n} \, dS$, where $S$ is the surface of the sphere $x^2 + y^2 + z^2 = a^2$.

(A) $\frac{12}{5}\pi a^5$(B) $\frac{4}{5}\pi a^5$(C) $\frac{3}{4}\pi a^5$(D) $4\pi a^3$
✓ Correct Answer: (A)
📚 Method A: Formal Textbook Derivation
Step 1: Compute divergence: $\nabla \cdot \vec{F} = \frac{\partial}{\partial x}(x^3) + \frac{\partial}{\partial y}(y^3) + \frac{\partial}{\partial z}(z^3) = 3(x^2 + y^2 + z^2)$. Step 2: Apply Gauss Divergence Theorem: $\iiint_{V} 3(x^2 + y^2 + z^2) \, dV$. Step 3: Convert to spherical polar coordinates where $x^2+y^2+z^2 = r^2$ and $dV = r^2 \sin\theta \, dr \, d\theta \, d\phi$. Step 4: Integrate from $r=0$ to $a$, $\theta=0$ to $\pi$, $\phi=0$ to $2\pi$ to get $\frac{12}{5}\pi a^5$.
⚡ Method B: 25-Second Exam Shortcut
Use spherical symmetry: volume integral of $r^2$ over sphere equals $3 \int_{0}^{a} r^2 (4\pi r^2) dr = 12\pi \frac{a^5}{5}$.
💡 Key Takeaway: For powers of coordinates over a sphere, spherical polar coordinates convert triple integrals into rapid single-variable polynomial integrations.
CANONICAL PROBLEM 02 Dual-Method Breakdown

If $V$ is the region bounded by $x=0, x=1, y=0, y=1, z=0, z=1$, evaluate $\iint_{S} (2x\hat{i} + 3y\hat{j} + z^2\hat{k}) \cdot \hat{n} \, dS$.

(A) $3$(B) $4$(C) $2$(D) $5$
✓ Correct Answer: (B)
📚 Method A: Formal Textbook Derivation
Step 1: Find divergence $\nabla \cdot \vec{F} = \frac{\partial}{\partial x}(2x) + \frac{\partial}{\partial y}(3y) + \frac{\partial}{\partial z}(z^2) = 2 + 3 + 2z = 5 + 2z$. Step 2: Set up triple integral $\int_{0}^{1} \int_{0}^{1} \int_{0}^{1} (5+2z) \, dx \, dy \, dz$. Step 3: Integrate with respect to $x$ and $y$ (gives 1), leaving $\int_{0}^{1} (5+2z) \, dz = [5z + z^2]_{0}^{1} = 5 + 1 = 6$ ? Wait, let's recompute: $2+3 = 5$, and derivative of $z^2$ is $2z$. Volume is unit cube (Volume = 1). $\iiint (5+2z) dV = 5(1) + 2 \int_0^1 z dz = 5 + 2(1/2) = 5 + 1 = 6$. Let's check options: if correct option is 6, adjust option A/B. Let's make options: (A) 4, (B) 6, (C) 5, (D) 3. Correct is (B).
⚡ Method B: 25-Second Exam Shortcut
For a unit cube, volume is 1. Average value of $2z$ over $[0,1]$ is $1$. Constant sum is $2+3 = 5$. Total = $5 + 1 = 6$.
💡 Key Takeaway: Rectangular cuboids allow decoupling of triple integrals into simple products of single integrals.

Q1
MCQ 1: What does the divergence theorem equate?
A Surface integral to line integral
B Volume integral of divergence to closed surface flux integral
C Line integral to volume integral
D Scalar potential to vector curl
Derivation & Analysis:

Gauss's Divergence Theorem specifically connects a volume integral of the divergence of a vector field to the flux of that vector field across the bounding closed surface.

Q2
MCQ 2: If $\vec{F} = x\hat{i} + y\hat{j} + z\hat{k}$, the divergence $\nabla \cdot \vec{F}$ is:
A 0
B 1
C 2
D 3
Derivation & Analysis:

$\nabla \cdot \vec{F} = \frac{\partial}{\partial x}(x) + \frac{\partial}{\partial y}(y) + \frac{\partial}{\partial z}(z) = 1 + 1 + 1 = 3$.

Q3
MCQ 3: The value of $\iint_{S} (x\hat{i} + y\hat{j} + z\hat{k}) \cdot \hat{n} \, dS$ where $S$ is the unit sphere $x^2+y^2+z^2=1$ is:
A $4\pi$
B $\frac{4}{3}\pi$
C $3$
D $\frac{12}{3}\pi$
Derivation & Analysis:

Divergence is 3. Theorem gives $3 \times \text{Volume of sphere} = 3 \times \left(\frac{4}{3}\pi (1)^3\right) = 4\pi$.

Q4
MCQ 4: If $\vec{F}$ is a solenoidal vector field in a region $V$ bounded by $S$, then:
A $\iint_{S} \vec{F} \cdot \hat{n} \, dS = 0$
B $\iiint_{V} \vec{F} \, dV = 0$
C $\iint_{S} \vec{F} \times \hat{n} \, dS = 0$
D $\nabla \times \vec{F} = 0$
Derivation & Analysis:

A solenoidal field has $\nabla \cdot \vec{F} = 0$. By Gauss theorem, the surface flux integral is the volume integral of $0$, which is $0$.

Q5
MCQ 5: Which of the following conditions is mandatory for applying the Divergence Theorem?
A Vector field must be irrotational
B Partial derivatives of $\vec{F}$ must be continuous in $V$
C Surface must be open and infinite
D Volume must be negative
Derivation & Analysis:

Continuity of first-order partial derivatives throughout the closed volume $V$ is a fundamental hypothesis of Gauss's theorem.

Q6
MCQ 6: For $\vec{F} = y\hat{i} + z\hat{j} + x\hat{k}$, the divergence $\nabla \cdot \vec{F}$ is:
A 1
B 3
C 0
D x + y + z
Derivation & Analysis:

$\nabla \cdot \vec{F} = \frac{\partial}{\partial x}(y) + \frac{\partial}{\partial y}(z) + \frac{\partial}{\partial z}(x) = 0 + 0 + 0 = 0$.

Q7
MCQ 7: If the divergence of a vector field is constant $k$ everywhere in a region of volume $V$, the total outward flux is:
A $k$
B $kv$
C $0$
D $k/v$
Derivation & Analysis:

$\iiint_{V} k \, dV = k \iiint_{V} dV = k \times V$.

Q8
MCQ 8: Assertion (A): Divergence theorem converts surface integrals to volume integrals. Reason (R): It reduces the dimensionality of the domain of integration by one.
A Both (A) and (R) are true and (R) is the correct explanation
B Both (A) and (R) are true but (R) is not the correct explanation
C (A) is true but (R) is false
D (A) is false but (R) is true
Derivation & Analysis:

Divergence theorem converts a 2D surface integral to a 3D volume integral, thereby increasing the dimension of integration from 2 to 3. Hence Reason (R) is false.

Q9
MCQ 9: The flux of $\vec{F} = x^2\hat{i} + y^2\hat{j} + z^2\hat{k}$ across the cube $0 \le x, y, z \le 1$ is:
A 1
B 2
C 3
D 6
Derivation & Analysis:

Divergence is $2x + 2y + 2z$. Integrating over unit cube: $\int_{0}^{1} \int_{0}^{1} \int_{0}^{1} 2(x+y+z) dx dy dz = 2 \left(\frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{2}\right) = 2 \left(\frac{3}{2}\right) = 3$ ? Wait: $\int_0^1 2x dx = 1$. Sum for $x, y, z$ is $1 + 1 + 1 = 3$. Correct option is 3.

Q10
MCQ 10: Gauss's divergence theorem is the 3D analogue of which 1D/2D theorem?
A Rolle's Theorem
B Green's Theorem in the plane
C Taylor's Theorem
D Cauchy's Theorem
Derivation & Analysis:

Green's Theorem (2D) and Gauss's Theorem (3D) are direct higher-dimensional generalizations of the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus.
