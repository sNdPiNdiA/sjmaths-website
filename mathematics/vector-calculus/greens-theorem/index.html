8. Vector Calculus

Green's theorem

Green's theorem establishes a fundamental bridge by converting a complex line integral around a simple closed curve into a double integral over the planar region enclosed by it.

Definition and statement

Green's Theorem in the Plane
Let $C$ be a positively oriented, piecewise smooth, simple closed curve in a plane, and let $D$ be the region bounded by $C$. If $P(x,y)$ and $Q(x,y)$ have continuous partial derivatives on an open region containing $D$, then $$\oint_C (P\,dx + Q\,dy) = \iint_D \left( \frac{\partial Q}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial P}{\partial y} \right) dA$$.

📐 Geometric Interpretation

Geometrically, Green's theorem equates the macroscopic circulation of a vector field along a boundary loop $C$ to the cumulative microscopic curl (or vorticity) distributed across the interior area $D$. The positive orientation of $C$ means the region $D$ remains to the left as one traverses the curve.

⚙️ Algebraic & Structural Meaning

Algebraically, it reduces the dimension of integration from $1$ (line integral) to $2$ (double integral), transforming unwieldy parametric contour evaluations into standard double integrals, which is invaluable for polynomial and trigonometric vector fields.

Useful results and shortcuts

Use these identities and results when their conditions apply:

01

Area Calculation via Line Integrals

$$ \text{Area}(D) = \frac{1}{2} \oint_C (x\,dy - y\,dx) = \oint_C x\,dy = - \oint_C y\,dx $$

Rule: To find the area of standard astroids, ellipses, or closed parametric loops, bypass double integration and apply half the boundary line integral.

Application: Frequently asked in UP PGT and KVS for finding areas enclosed by parametric curves.
02

Exact Differential / Conservative Field Test

$$ \frac{\partial Q}{\partial x} = \frac{\partial P}{\partial y} \implies \oint_C (P\,dx + Q\,dy) = 0 $$

Rule: If the field is conservative throughout a simply connected region, the line integral around any closed loop is identically zero without computation.

03

Constant Curl Reduction

$$ \oint_C (P\,dx + Q\,dy) = k \cdot \text{Area}(D) $$

Rule: If $\frac{\partial Q}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial P}{\partial y} = k$ (a constant), immediately write $k \times \text{Area of Region}$.

Application: Standard pattern in UP PGT vector calculus sections.
04

Elliptic Boundary Scaling

$$ \iint_D dx\,dy = \pi ab \text{ for } \frac{x^2}{a^2} + \frac{y^2}{b^2} \le 1 $$

Rule: Combine Green's theorem with standard elliptic area formulas to evaluate complex polynomial boundary integrals instantly.

Application: Appears in LT Grade and PGT screening tests.
05

Symmetry & Odd Function Annihilation

$$ \iint_D f(x,y)\,dA = 0 \text{ if region and integrand possess opposite symmetry} $$

Rule: Use domain and integrand parity over symmetric regions (like circles centered at the origin) to eliminate terms before integration.

06

Rapid Option Elimination via Dimensions

$$ [\text{Integral}] = [\text{Field Units}] \times [\text{Area Units}] $$

Rule: Check dimensional homogeneity of options. Discard options with incorrect power scaling relative to curve dimensions.

Common errors and counterexamples

These examples show how a conclusion can fail when a condition is missing:

⚠️ COMMON ERROR #01

The Hidden Singularity Trap

Common Pitfall: Applying Green's theorem across a domain containing a point where $P$ or $Q$ (or their derivatives) are undefined (e.g., origin for inverse-square fields).

Counterexample: $P = \frac{-y}{x^2+y^2}, \, Q = \frac{x}{x^2+y^2}$ on a circle enclosing the origin

Why it fails: $\frac{\partial Q}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial P}{\partial y} = 0$ everywhere except at $(0,0)$ where derivatives fail, yielding a non-zero line integral ($2\pi$) instead of zero.

🛡️ Exam Defense: Always inspect the interior of region $D$ for poles or singularities before setting $\iint (Q_x - P_y) dA = 0$.
⚠️ COMMON ERROR #02

Clockwise Orientation Sign Reversal

Common Pitfall: Integrating along a clockwise boundary without negating the resulting area or double integral.

Counterexample: $C$ traversed clockwise instead of counter-clockwise

Why it fails: Green's theorem assumes standard positive (counter-clockwise) orientation; clockwise traversal yields an integral of opposite sign.

🛡️ Exam Defense: Check arrow directions on curves in diagrams; if clockwise, multiply your final calculated value by $-1$.

Solved examples

Each problem includes a full solution and a shorter method:

CANONICAL PROBLEM 01 Dual-Method Breakdown

Evaluate the line integral $\oint_C (x^2y\,dx + xy^2\,dy)$ where $C$ is the circle $x^2 + y^2 = 1$ traversed in the counter-clockwise direction.

(A) $0$(B) $\frac{\pi}{2}$(C) $\pi$(D) $2\pi$
✓ Correct Answer: (A)
📚 Method A: Formal Textbook Derivation
Step 1: Identify $P = x^2y$ and $Q = xy^2$. Step 2: Compute partial derivatives: $\frac{\partial P}{\partial y} = x^2$ and $\frac{\partial Q}{\partial x} = y^2$. Step 3: Set up Green's theorem double integral: $\iint_D (y^2 - x^2)\,dA$. Step 4: Convert to polar coordinates: $\int_0^{2\pi} \int_0^1 (r^2 \cos^2\theta - r^2 \sin^2\theta) r\,dr\,d\theta = 0$ due to angular integration of $\cos(2\theta)$.
⚡ Method B: 25-Second Exam Shortcut
Recognize symmetry: over a disk $x^2+y^2 \le 1$, $\iint x^2 dA = \iint y^2 dA$ by rotation symmetry, making $y^2 - x^2$ integrate to zero instantly.
💡 Tip: Look for symmetry in circular domains to eliminate calculations instantly.
CANONICAL PROBLEM 02 Dual-Method Breakdown

Using Green's theorem, find the area of the ellipse $\frac{x^2}{a^2} + \frac{y^2}{b^2} = 1$.

(A) $\pi ab$(B) $2\pi ab$(C) $\pi a^2 b^2$(D) $\frac{1}{2}\pi ab$
✓ Correct Answer: (A)
📚 Method A: Formal Textbook Derivation
Step 1: Apply Green's theorem area formula: $\text{Area} = \frac{1}{2} \oint_C (x\,dy - y\,dx)$. Step 2: Parameterize ellipse: $x = a\cos t, y = b\sin t$ for $t \in [0, 2\pi]$. Step 3: Compute $dx = -a\sin t dt, dy = b\cos t dt$. Step 4: Evaluate $\frac{1}{2} \int_0^{2\pi} ( (a\cos t)(b\cos t) - (b\sin t)(-a\sin t) ) dt = \frac{1}{2} ab \int_0^{2\pi} dt = \pi ab$.
⚡ Method B: 25-Second Exam Shortcut
Standard formula recall for elliptic boundary area via vector line integrals.
💡 Tip: Direct standard result often memorized by top rankers for 10-second recall.

Q1
MCQ 1: What does Green's theorem directly connect?
A Single integral and double integral
B Line integral around a simple closed curve and a double integral over the enclosed region
C Surface integral and volume integral
D Triple integral and line integral
Derivation & Analysis:

Green's theorem equates a line integral around a closed curve $C$ to a double integral over the region $D$ bounded by $C$.

Q2
MCQ 2: If $\frac{\partial Q}{\partial x} = \frac{\partial P}{\partial y}$ throughout a simply connected region $D$, then $\oint_C (P\,dx + Q\,dy)$ equals:
A $1$
B Area of $D$
C $0$
D $2\pi$
Derivation & Analysis:

By Green's theorem, if $Q_x = P_y$, the double integral of $Q_x - P_y$ over $D$ is zero, making the line integral zero.

Q3
MCQ 3: The area of a plane region $D$ bounded by a simple closed curve $C$ can be expressed as:
A $\oint_C P\,dx$
B $\frac{1}{2} \oint_C (x\,dy - y\,dx)$
C $\iint_D P\,dx\,dy$
D $\oint_C Q\,dy$
Derivation & Analysis:

Choosing $P = -y/2$ and $Q = x/2$ gives $Q_x - P_y = 1/2 - (-1/2) = 1$, so Green's theorem yields $\text{Area} = \frac{1}{2} \oint_C (x\,dy - y\,dx)$.

Q4
MCQ 4: Evaluate $\oint_C (x y^2 \, dx + x^2 y \, dy)$ where $C$ is the square with vertices $(0,0), (1,0), (1,1), (0,1)$ counter-clockwise.
A $0$
B $1$
C $1/2$
D $2$
Derivation & Analysis:

Here $P = xy^2, Q = x^2y$. $Q_x = 2xy$ and $P_y = 2xy$. Thus $Q_x - P_y = 0$, so the integral evaluates to $0$.

Q5
MCQ 5: If curve $C$ is traversed in clockwise direction instead of counter-clockwise, Green's theorem value is:
A Unchanged
B Multiplied by $-1$
C Multiplied by $2$
D Becomes zero
Derivation & Analysis:

Positive orientation requires counter-clockwise traversal. Clockwise traversal reverses the sign of the boundary line integral.

Q6
MCQ 6: For the vector field $\mathbf{F} = (2x - y)\mathbf{i} + (x + 3y)\mathbf{j}$, the value of $\oint_C \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{r}$ over the unit circle $x^2+y^2=1$ counter-clockwise is:
A $\pi$
B $2\pi$
C $3\pi$
D $4\pi$
Derivation & Analysis:

Here $P = 2x-y, Q = x+3y$. $Q_x = 1, P_y = -1$. $Q_x - P_y = 1 - (-1) = 2$. Integral $= 2 \times \text{Area}(circle) = 2(\pi(1)^2) = 2\pi$.

Q7
MCQ 7: Which of the following conditions is necessary for Green's theorem to hold on region $D$?
A $P$ and $Q$ must be constant
B First partial derivatives of $P$ and $Q$ must be continuous on $D$
C $C$ must be a polygon
D $D$ must be an infinite strip
Derivation & Analysis:

Green's theorem requires $P$ and $Q$ to have continuous partial derivatives throughout the open region containing $D$.

Q8
MCQ 8: Assertion (A): Green's theorem converts a line integral into a double integral. Reason (R): It applies to open curves in three-dimensional space.
A Both (A) and (R) are true and (R) is correct explanation
B Both (A) and (R) are true but (R) is not correct explanation
C (A) is true but (R) is false
D (A) is false but (R) is true
Derivation & Analysis:

Green's theorem applies to closed planar curves in two-dimensional space, making Reason (R) false.

Q9
MCQ 9: The line integral $\oint_C (-y\,dx + x\,dy)$ around the unit circle $x^2+y^2=1$ represents:
A Zero
B Twice the area of the circle ($2\pi$)
C Area of the circle ($\pi$)
D Four times the area ($4\pi$)
Derivation & Analysis:

Here $Q_x - P_y = 1 - (-1) = 2$. Double integral over unit disk is $2 \times \pi(1)^2 = 2\pi$.

Q10
MCQ 10: If $P = e^x \sin y$ and $Q = e^x \cos y$, what is $\oint_C (P\,dx + Q\,dy)$ for any simple closed curve $C$?
A $1$
B $0$
C $\pi$
D $e$
Derivation & Analysis:

$P_y = e^x \cos y$ and $Q_x = e^x \cos y$. Since $Q_x = P_y$, the curl is zero everywhere, so the line integral is $0$.
