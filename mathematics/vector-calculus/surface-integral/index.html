8. Vector Calculus

Surface integral

A surface integral extends the concept of definite and double integrals to curved surfaces in three-dimensional space, quantifying scalar or vector fields across a two-dimensional domain. In UP PGT/TGT and DSSSB examinations, vector surface integrals involving flux across coordinate planes and closed surfaces via the Divergence Theorem form a high-yield scoring zone.

Pillar 1 Rigorous Definition & Mathematical Statement

Definition of Surface Integrals (Scalar and Vector Fields)
Let $S$ be a smooth or piecewise smooth oriented surface in $\mathbb{R}^3$ defined by position vector $\mathbf{r}(u,v)$ for $(u,v) \in D$. For a continuous scalar field $\phi(\mathbf{r})$, the surface integral is defined as $$\iint_S \phi \, dS = \iint_D \phi(\mathbf{r}(u,v)) \| \mathbf{r}_u \times \mathbf{r}_v \| \, du \, dv$$. For a continuous vector field $\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{r})$, the surface flux integral is defined as $$\iint_S \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{S} = \iint_S \mathbf{F} \cdot \mathbf{n} \, dS = \iint_D \mathbf{F}(\mathbf{r}(u,v)) \cdot (\mathbf{r}_u \times \mathbf{r}_v) \, du \, dv$$, where $\mathbf{n}$ is the unit outward normal vector.

📐 Geometric Interpretation

A scalar surface integral represents the total mass of a curved shell of varying surface density $\phi$. A vector surface integral (flux) calculates the net volume or quantity of a fluid field passing through the oriented surface per unit time.

⚙️ Algebraic & Structural Meaning

It serves as the natural bridge between double integrals over planar domains and triple integrals over spatial volumes, acting as the foundation for the Divergence (Gauss) Theorem and Stokes' Theorem.

Pillar 3 High-Yield Exam Results & Direct Shortcuts

Standard 15-to-30 second shortcuts frequently tested in UP TGT/PGT and DSSSB:

01

Shortcut 1: Projection onto $xy$-plane for Vector Flux

$$\iint_S \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{S} = \iint_R \left( -P \frac{\partial z}{\partial x} - Q \frac{\partial z}{\partial y} + R \right) dx \, dy$$

Exam Rule: When surface $S$ is given explicitly as $z = g(x,y)$ with upward normal, immediately substitute $dz = g_x dx + g_y dy$ into the coordinate expansion $P dy \wedge dz + Q dz \wedge dx + R dx \wedge dy$.

⚡ PYQ Context: Directly tested in UP PGT for calculating flux of polynomial vector fields across paraboloids and planes.
02

Shortcut 2: Divergence Theorem Conversion for Closed Surfaces

$$\iint_S \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{S} = \iiint_V (\nabla \cdot \mathbf{F}) \, dV$$

Exam Rule: undefined

⚡ PYQ Context: Standard time-saver in KVS/NVS PGT exams where direct evaluation takes 5 minutes versus 30 seconds.
03

Shortcut 3: Symmetry of Fields across Coordinate Planes

$$\iint_S \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{S} = 0 \quad \text{if } \mathbf{F} \text{ is odd w.r.t reflection and } S \text{ is symmetric}$$

Exam Rule: If the surface is symmetric about a plane (e.g., $xy$-plane) and the integrand component perpendicular to that plane is an odd function of $z$, the integral evaluates to zero without calculation.

⚡ PYQ Context: Extremely frequent in DSSSB PGT for eliminating options instantly.
04

Shortcut 4: Sphere Normal Vector Substitution

$$\mathbf{n} = \frac{\mathbf{r}}{a} \implies \iint_S \mathbf{r} \cdot d\mathbf{S} = 3 \times \text{Volume of Sphere} = 4 \pi a^3$$

Exam Rule: For any sphere $x^2 + y^2 + z^2 = a^2$, the position vector dot normal is $\mathbf{r} \cdot \mathbf{n} = a$. Thus $\iint_S \mathbf{r} \cdot d\mathbf{S} = a \times \text{Surface Area} = 4\pi a^3$.

⚡ PYQ Context: Direct question formula tested in UP TGT/PGT matching standard numerical options.
05

Shortcut 5: Cylindrical Polar Transformation for Vertical Cylinders

$$dx \, dy = r \, dr \, d\theta, \quad x = r\cos\theta, \; y = r\in\theta$$

Exam Rule: When integrating over cylindrical surfaces $x^2 + y^2 = a^2$, project onto the $rz$ or polar plane to convert double integrals into standard single-variable integration.

⚡ PYQ Context: Appears in LT Grade exams involving cylindrical flux components.
06

Shortcut 6: Elimination via Divergence Zero (Solenoidal Fields)

$$\nabla \cdot \mathbf{F} = 0 \implies \iint_{S_1} \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{S} = \iint_{S_2} \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{S}$$

Exam Rule: If a vector field is solenoidal ($\nabla \cdot \mathbf{F} = 0$), the surface integral over any open surface depends only on its boundary curve, allowing substitution with an easy flat disk.

⚡ PYQ Context: Advanced PGT conceptual questions.

Pillar 4 Classic Exam Traps & Counterexamples

Where examiners construct tricky trap options and how to safeguard your marks:

⚠️ EXAM TRAP #01

Trap 1: Ignoring Surface Orientation (Sign Error)

Common Pitfall: Failing to check whether the normal vector points inward or outward, leading to a sign error in the final answer.

Counterexample: $-\iint_S \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{S}$ computed with inward normal instead of outward normal.

Why it fails: Surface integrals depend crucially on orientation. Reversing the orientation negates the value of the integral.

🛡️ Exam Defense: Always verify if the problem specifies 'outward drawn normal' or 'upward normal' and adjust the sign or parameterization order accordingly.
⚠️ EXAM TRAP #02

Trap 2: Applying Divergence Theorem to Open Surfaces

Common Pitfall: Applying Gauss's Divergence Theorem to an open surface (like a hemisphere without its base base disk).

Counterexample: $S = \{(x,y,z) : x^2+y^2+z^2=a^2, z \ge 0\}$ (Hemisphere alone)

Why it fails: Divergence theorem requires a closed surface enclosing a volume. For an open surface, one must add the bounding flat surface to form a closed surface before applying the theorem.

🛡️ Exam Defense: Check if $\partial S = \emptyset$. If the surface has a boundary curve, you cannot use the Divergence Theorem directly without closing the surface.

Pillar 6 Solved Canonical Exam Problems (Dual-Method)

Comparing the formal academic proof against the rapid competitive exam elimination trick:

CANONICAL PROBLEM 01 Dual-Method Breakdown

Evaluate the surface integral $\iint_S \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{S}$ where $\mathbf{F} = x\mathbf{i} + y\mathbf{j} + z\mathbf{k}$ and $S$ is the closed surface of the sphere $x^2 + y^2 + z^2 = a^2$.

(A) $\frac{4}{3}\pi a^3$(B) $4\pi a^3$(C) $\frac{3}{4}\pi a^3$(D) $2\pi a^3$
✓ Correct Answer: (B)
📚 Method A: Formal Textbook Derivation
Step 1: Compute divergence: $\nabla \cdot \mathbf{F} = \frac{\partial x}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial y}{\partial y} + \frac{\partial z}{\partial z} = 1 + 1 + 1 = 3$. Step 2: Apply Divergence Theorem: $\iiint_V (3) \, dV = 3 \times \text{Volume of Sphere}$. Step 3: Volume = $\frac{4}{3}\pi a^3$. Step 4: Total flux = $3 \times \frac{4}{3}\pi a^3 = 4\pi a^3$.
⚡ Method B: 25-Second Exam Shortcut
Use Shortcut 4: $\iint_S \mathbf{r} \cdot d\mathbf{S} = 3 \times \text{Volume} = 3 \times (\frac{4}{3}\pi a^3) = 4\pi a^3$.
💡 Key Takeaway: Whenever $\mathbf{F} = \mathbf{r}$ over a closed symmetric surface, the divergence is always 3, making the answer 3 times the volume.
CANONICAL PROBLEM 02 Dual-Method Breakdown

Find the value of $\iint_S \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{S}$ where $\mathbf{F} = yz \mathbf{i} + zx \mathbf{j} + xy \mathbf{k}$ and $S$ is the surface of the sphere $x^2 + y^2 + z^2 = 1$ in the first octant.

(A) $\frac{1}{8}$(B) $\frac{1}{4}$(C) $\frac{1}{3}$(D) $\frac{1}{2}$
✓ Correct Answer: (C)
📚 Method A: Formal Textbook Derivation
Step 1: Calculate divergence $\nabla \cdot \mathbf{F} = \frac{\partial}{\partial x}(yz) + \frac{\partial}{\partial y}(zx) + \frac{\partial}{\partial z}(xy) = 0 + 0 + 0 = 0$. Step 2: Since $\nabla \cdot \mathbf{F} = 0$, if $S$ were closed the integral would be zero. However, $S$ is only the first octant portion plus its three planar base disks. Step 3: By applying the divergence theorem to the closed octant region bounded by $S$ and the three coordinate planes $x=0, y=0, z=0$, the total closed surface integral is zero. Step 4: The flux across the three coordinate planes is zero because the normal vectors are perpendicular to the respective coordinate fields. Thus the flux across the octant spherical surface is $1/3$ when properly integrated over spherical coordinates.
⚡ Method B: 25-Second Exam Shortcut
Recognize that for solenoidal vector fields, flux through curved sections equals flux through standard planar projections evaluated via direct triple or double integrals.
💡 Key Takeaway: Watch out for octant restrictions where closed surface theorems require accounting for bounding planar faces.

Q1
MCQ 1: If $S$ is any closed surface enclosing volume $V$, then $\iint_S (x\mathbf{i} + y\mathbf{j} + z\mathbf{k}) \cdot d\mathbf{S}$ is equal to:
A (A) $V$
B (B) $2V$
C (C) $3V$
D (D) $4V$
Derivation & Analysis:

By the Divergence Theorem, $\iint_S \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{S} = \iiint_V (\nabla \cdot \mathbf{F}) \, dV$. Here $\nabla \cdot \mathbf{F} = 1 + 1 + 1 = 3$. Thus $\iiint_V 3 \, dV = 3V$.

This is in vector notation $\iint_S \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{S}$ where $\mathbf{F} = yz\mathbf{i} + zx\mathbf{j} + xy\mathbf{k}$. By Divergence Theorem, $\nabla \cdot \mathbf{F} = 0 + 0 + 0 = 0$. Q3
MCQ 3: If $\mathbf{F} = z\mathbf{i} + x\mathbf{j} + y\mathbf{k}$, the surface integral $\iint_S \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{S}$ over the plane $x + y + z = 1$ in the first octant is:
A (A) $\frac{1}{2}$
B (B) $\frac{1}{3}$
C (C) $1$
D (D) $\frac{1}{6}$
Derivation & Analysis:

Using projection onto the $xy$-plane where $z = 1 - x - y$, the unit normal vector has components proportional to $(1, 1, 1)$. Dot product with $\mathbf{F}$ yields $\frac{1}{2}$ after integrating over the triangular region in the $xy$-plane.

Q4
MCQ 4: The surface area of the surface $S$ given by vector function $\mathbf{r}(u,v)$ is obtained by setting the scalar integrand $\phi$ equal to:
A (A) $1$
B (B) $0$
C (C) $\mathbf{r}_u \cdot \mathbf{r}_v$
D (D) $\| \mathbf{r}_u \| \| \mathbf{r}_v \$
Derivation & Analysis:

Surface area is defined by the scalar surface integral $\iint_S 1 \, dS = \iint_D \| \mathbf{r}_u \times \mathbf{r}_v \| \, du \, dv$.

Q5
MCQ 5: According to Gauss's Divergence Theorem, a surface integral over a closed surface is converted into which type of integral?
A (A) Single Line Integral
B (B) Double Planar Integral
C (C) Triple Volume Integral
D (D) Contour Integral
Derivation & Analysis:

Gauss's Divergence Theorem relates a surface integral over a closed surface to a triple volume integral over the enclosed region: $\iint_S \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{S} = \iiint_V (\nabla \cdot \mathbf{F}) \, dV$.

Q6
MCQ 6: If $\nabla \cdot \mathbf{F} = 4$ throughout a region $V$ bounded by closed surface $S$, and the volume of $V$ is $5$, then $\iint_S \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{S}$ is:
A (A) $9$
B (B) $20$
C (C) $\frac{4}{5}$
D (D) $1.25$
Derivation & Analysis:

By Divergence Theorem, $\iint_S \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{S} = \iiint_V 4 \, dV = 4 \times \text{Volume} = 4 \times 5 = 20$.

Q7
MCQ 7: Which of the following vector fields is solenoidal?
A (A) $\mathbf{F} = x\mathbf{i} + y\mathbf{j} + z\mathbf{k}$
B (B) $\mathbf{F} = x^2\mathbf{i} + y^2\mathbf{j} + z^2\mathbf{k}$
C (C) $\mathbf{F} = yz\mathbf{i} + zx\mathbf{j} + xy\mathbf{k}$
D (D) $\mathbf{F} = y\mathbf{i} - x\mathbf{j}$
Derivation & Analysis:

A vector field is solenoidal if its divergence is zero. For $\mathbf{F} = y\mathbf{i} - x\mathbf{j}$, $\nabla \cdot \mathbf{F} = \frac{\partial}{\partial x}(y) + \frac{\partial}{\partial y}(-x) = 0 + 0 = 0$.

Q8
MCQ 8: Stokes' Theorem connects a surface integral to which of the following?
A (A) Volume integral of divergence
B (B) Line integral around the boundary curve
C (C) Double integral of curl
D (D) Arc length integral
Derivation & Analysis:

Stokes' Theorem states that $\iint_S (\nabla \times \mathbf{F}) \cdot d\mathbf{S} = \oint_C \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{r}$, connecting a surface integral of curl to a line integral around the boundary curve.

Q9
MCQ 9: The flux of vector field $\mathbf{F} = x^3\mathbf{i} + y^3\mathbf{j} + z^3\mathbf{k}$ across the unit sphere $x^2+y^2+z^2=1$ evaluated via divergence theorem involves volume integral of:
A (A) $x^2 + y^2 + z^2$
B (B) $3(x^2 + y^2 + z^2)$
C (C) $x+y+z$
D (D) $3(x+y+z)$
Derivation & Analysis:

Divergence of $\mathbf{F}$ is $\frac{\partial}{\partial x}(x^3) + \frac{\partial}{\partial y}(y^3) + \frac{\partial}{\partial z}(z^3) = 3x^2 + 3y^2 + 3z^2 = 3(x^2 + y^2 + z^2)$.

Q10
MCQ 10: If the unit normal vector $\mathbf{n}$ to a surface is perpendicular to the vector field $\mathbf{F}$ everywhere on $S$, then the surface flux integral $\iint_S \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{S}$ is:
A (A) $1$
B (B) $-1$
C (C) $0$
D (D) Undefined
Derivation & Analysis:

Since $\mathbf{F} \cdot \mathbf{n} = 0$ everywhere because $\mathbf{F}$ and $\mathbf{n}$ are orthogonal, the integrand is identically zero, making the surface integral zero.

Pillar 8 Frequently Asked Questions

Always use the Divergence Theorem when the surface $S$ is closed and bounds a regular 3D volume $V$, especially when the divergence $\nabla \cdot \mathbf{F}$ simplifies to a constant or simple polynomial. Direct parameterization should only be used for open surfaces (like hemispheres without bases or arbitrary parametric patches) or when $\nabla \cdot \mathbf{F}$ is overly complex.

The primary reasons are sign errors caused by incorrect orientation of the normal vector (inward vs. outward) and failing to include bounding planar surfaces when attempting to apply closed-surface theorems to open regions like octants or hemispherical caps.

Vector Calculus typically contributes 3 to 5 questions in UP PGT Mathematics, with at least 1 or 2 direct questions dedicated to surface integrals, flux calculations, or applications of Gauss's and Stokes' theorems.

Memorize standard flux results for spheres and cylinders (e.g., $\iint_S \mathbf{r} \cdot d\mathbf{S} = 3V$), master quick conversion to cylindrical and spherical polar coordinates, and look for symmetry or solenoidal field properties ($\nabla \cdot \mathbf{F} = 0$) to eliminate lengthy integrations.
