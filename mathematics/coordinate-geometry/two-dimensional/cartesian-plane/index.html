Coordinate Geometry

Cartesian plane

The Cartesian plane establishes a rigorous bijective correspondence between geometric points and ordered pairs of real numbers, $\mathbb{R}^2$. Mastery of distance formulas, section formulas, coordinate transformations, and locus equations forms the foundational bedrock for all analytical geometry in UP TGT/PGT, KVS, and DSSSB examinations.

Pillar 1 Rigorous Definition & Mathematical Statement

Fundamental Distance & Section Formulae in $\mathbb{R}^2$
Let $P(x_1, y_1)$ and $Q(x_2, y_2)$ be two arbitrary points in the Cartesian plane $\mathbb{R}^2$. The Euclidean distance $d(P, Q)$ is given by $d = \sqrt{(x_2 - x_1)^2 + (y_2 - y_1)^2}$. Furthermore, the internal section coordinates dividing segment $PQ$ in ratio $m:n$ are given by $x = \frac{mx_2 + nx_1}{m+n}, y = \frac{my_2 + ny_1}{m+n}$.

📐 Geometric Interpretation

Geometrically, the distance formula represents the application of the Pythagorean theorem on right triangles formed by coordinate projections. The section formula dictates the center of mass or weighted average position of two point masses located at $P$ and $Q$.

⚙️ Algebraic & Structural Meaning

Algebraically, it defines a metric space structure on $\mathbb{R}^2$ satisfying non-negativity, symmetry, identity of indiscernibles, and the triangle inequality. It bridges Euclidean synthetic geometry with modern linear algebra and vector spaces.

Pillar 3 High-Yield Exam Results & Direct Shortcuts

Standard 15-to-30 second shortcuts frequently tested in UP TGT/PGT and DSSSB:

01

Shortcut 1: Distance from Origin & Point Symmetry

$$ d(0, P) = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2}, \quad \text{Reflection across } y=x: (x,y) \to (y,x) $$

Exam Rule: Instantly swap coordinates for reflections along $y=x$, and negate coordinates for origin symmetry $(x,y) \to (-x,-y)$ without full matrix multiplication.

⚡ PYQ Context: Frequently tested in UP TGT/PGT for rapid transformation and locus verification.
02

Shortcut 2: Area of Triangle via Determinant Form

$$ \Delta = \frac{1}{2} \left| x_1(y_2 - y_3) + x_2(y_3 - y_1) + x_3(y_1 - y_2) \right| $$

Exam Rule: Shift one vertex to the origin $(x_1, y_1) \to (0,0)$ to reduce computation to $\frac{1}{2}|x'_2 y'_3 - x'_3 y'_2|$.

⚡ PYQ Context: Standard time-saver in coordinate geometry coordinate calculations.
03

Shortcut 3: Centroid, Circumcenter, Orthocenter Collinearity (Euler Line)

$$ \text{Centroid } G \text{ divides } H (\text{Orthocenter}) \text{ and } O (\text{Circumcenter}) in ratio } 2:1 $$

Exam Rule: If any two of $H, G, O$ are known for a triangle, the third is found instantly using the section formula with ratio $2:1$.

⚡ PYQ Context: Direct result tested in UP PGT examinations.
04

Shortcut 4: Condition of Collinearity for Three Points

$$ (y_2 - y_1)(x_3 - x_2) = (y_3 - y_2)(x_2 - x_1) $$

Exam Rule: Equate slopes $m_{12} = m_{23}$ using cross-multiplication to avoid fractional errors during exam pressure.

⚡ PYQ Context: Direct exam application for checking collinearity of parametric points.
05

Shortcut 5: Length of Perpendicular from Point to Line

$$ p = \frac{|Ax_1 + By_1 + C|}{\sqrt{A^2 + B^2}} $$

Exam Rule: Memorize the numerator as 'substitute point into line equation' and denominator as 'magnitude of normal vector'.

⚡ PYQ Context: Standard question format in TGT/PGT.
06

Shortcut 6: Rapid Elimination via Coordinate Shifting

$$ X = x - h, \quad Y = y - k $$

Exam Rule: Translate origin to eliminate first-degree terms in conics or general equations instantly.

⚡ PYQ Context: Time management trick for 125-question exams.

Pillar 4 Classic Exam Traps & Counterexamples

Where examiners construct tricky trap options and how to safeguard your marks:

⚠️ EXAM TRAP #01

Trap 1: Internal vs. External Division Sign Errors

Common Pitfall: Using positive signs in the section formula for external division or vice versa.

Counterexample: External division ratio $m:n$ uses negative sign: $x = \frac{mx_2 - nx_1}{m - n}$.

Why it fails: Yields coordinates of the point dividing the segment externally rather than internally.

🛡️ Exam Defense: Always check whether the point lies between the two given points (internal) or outside them (external).
⚠️ EXAM TRAP #02

Trap 2: Oblique Axes Assumption Fallacy

Common Pitfall: Applying standard Euclidean distance $\sqrt{\Delta x^2 + \Delta y^2}$ when the coordinate axes intersect at an angle $\omega \neq 90^{\circ}$.

Counterexample: $d^2 = \Delta x^2 + \Delta y^2 + 2\Delta x \Delta y \cos\omega$

Why it fails: Ignoring the angle $\omega$ leads to completely incorrect distance calculations in skewed coordinate systems.

🛡️ Exam Defense: Verify if axes are stated to be rectangular (orthogonal). If unspecified, assume standard orthogonal unless stated otherwise.

Pillar 6 Solved Canonical Exam Problems (Dual-Method)

Comparing the formal academic proof against the rapid competitive exam elimination trick:

CANONICAL PROBLEM 01 Dual-Method Breakdown

If the vertices of a triangle are $(1, 2), (-3, 6)$, and $(5, -2)$, find the area of the triangle.

(A) $0$(B) $4$(C) $8$(D) $12$
✓ Correct Answer: (A)
📚 Method A: Formal Textbook Derivation
Step 1: Apply area formula $\Delta = \frac{1}{2}|x_1(y_2-y_3) + x_2(y_3-y_1) + x_3(y_1-y_2)|$. Step 2: Substitute coordinates: $\frac{1}{2}|1(6 - (-2)) + (-3)(-2 - 2) + 5(2 - 6)| = \frac{1}{2}|8 + 12 - 20| = 0$. Step 3: Conclude points are collinear.
⚡ Method B: 25-Second Exam Shortcut
Check slopes: $m_{12} = \frac{6-2}{-3-1} = -1$, $m_{23} = \frac{-2-6}{5-(-3)} = -1$. Equal slopes imply collinearity, hence Area = 0.
💡 Key Takeaway: Whenever three coordinate points are given, check slope equality before expanding large determinants.
CANONICAL PROBLEM 02 Dual-Method Breakdown

Find the locus of a point whose distance from $(1, 2)$ is twice its distance from $(-2, 3)$.

(A) $3x^2 + 3y^2 + 10x - 20y + 35 = 0$(B) $x^2 + y^2 - 10x + 4y - 5 = 0$(C) $3x^2 + 3y^2 - 18x + 8y + 12 = 0$(D) $2x^2 + 2y^2 + x - y = 0$
✓ Correct Answer: (A)
📚 Method A: Formal Textbook Derivation
Step 1: Let point be $(x,y)$. Step 2: Set condition $\sqrt{(x-1)^2 + (y-2)^2} = 2\sqrt{(x+2)^2 + (y-3)^2}$. Step 3: Square both sides: $(x-1)^2 + (y-2)^2 = 4[(x+2)^2 + (y-3)^2]$. Step 4: Expand and simplify to obtain $3x^2 + 3y^2 + 10x - 20y + 35 = 0$.
⚡ Method B: 25-Second Exam Shortcut
Note the coefficients of $x^2$ and $y^2$ must be equal ($3$), representing a circle (Apollonius circle). Eliminate non-matching options instantly.
💡 Key Takeaway: Recognize Apollonius circle formulations immediately when distance ratio is $k \neq 1$.

Q1
MCQ 1: The distance between the points $(\cos\theta, \sin\theta)$ and $(\sin\theta, -\cos\theta)$ is:
A \text{(A) } 1
B \text{(B) } \sqrt{2}
C \text{(C) } 2
D \text{(D) } \sqrt{3}
Derivation & Analysis:

Distance $d = \sqrt{(\sin\theta - \cos\theta)^2 + (-\cos\theta - \sin\theta)^2} = \sqrt{2(\cos^2\theta + \sin^2\theta)} = \sqrt{2}$.

Q2
MCQ 2: If the points $(k, 2-2k), (-k+1, 2k)$, and $(-4_k, 6-2k)$ are collinear, the number of possible values of $k$ is:
A \text{(A) } 1
B \text{(B) } 2
C \text{(C) } 3
D \text{(D) } 0
Derivation & Analysis:

Set determinant of coordinate matrix to zero or equate slopes. Solving yields quadratic in $k$, giving 2 real values.

Q3
MCQ 3: The ratio in which the segment joining $(2, -3)$ and $(5, 6)$ is divided by the $x$-axis is:
A \text{(A) } 1:2 \text{ internal}
B \text{(B) } 1:2 \text{ external}
C \text{(C) } 2:1 \text{ internal}
D \text{(D) } 2:1 \text{ external}
Derivation & Analysis:

On $x$-axis, $y = 0$. Using section formula for $y$: $0 = \frac{m(6) + n(-3)}{m+n} \implies 6m = 3n \implies \frac{m}{n} = \frac{1}{2}$ internal? Wait, sign check: $y_1 = -3, y_2 = 6$, ratio is $-y_1/y_2 = -(-3)/6 = 1:2$ internally (Wait, opposite signs mean internal division in ratio $|y_1|:|y_2| = 3:6 = 1:2$).

Q4
MCQ 4: If origin is shifted to $(1, 2)$ without rotation, the transformed equation of $x^2 + y^2 - 2x - 4y + 1 = 0$ is:
A \text{(A) } X^2 + Y^2 = 4
B \text{(B) } X^2 + Y^2 = 1
C \text{(C) } X^2 + Y^2 - 4 = 0
D \text{(D) } X^2 + Y^2 = 3
Derivation & Analysis:

Substitute $x = X + 1, y = Y + 2$ into equation: $(X+1)^2 + (Y+2)^2 - 2(X+1) - 4(Y+2) + 1 = 0 \implies X^2 + Y^2 - 4 = 0 \implies X^2 + Y^2 = 4$.

Q5
MCQ 5: The centroid of a triangle with vertices $(2, 3), (-1, 4)$, and $(2, -7)$ is:
A \text{(A) } (1, 0)
B \text{(B) } (1, 1)
C \text{(C) } (0, 0)
D \text{(D) } (3, 0)
Derivation & Analysis:

Centroid $G = \left(\frac{2 - 1 + 2}{3}, \frac{3 + 4 - 7}{3}\right) = \left(\frac{3}{3}, \frac{0}{3}\right) = (1, 0)$.

Q6
MCQ 6: The angle between the coordinate axes in a skewed Cartesian system where distance formula is $d^2 = \Delta x^2 + \Delta y^2 + \sqrt{3}\Delta x \Delta y$ is:
A \text{(A) } 30^\circ
B \text{(B) } 60^\circ
C \text{(C) } 120^\circ
D \text{(D) } 150^\circ
Derivation & Analysis:

Compare with $2\cos\omega = \sqrt{3} \implies \cos\omega = \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2} \implies \omega = 30^\circ$? Wait, formula has $2\Delta x \Delta y \cos\omega = \sqrt{3}\Delta x \Delta y \implies \cos\omega = \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}$, so $\omega = 30^\circ$.

Q7
MCQ 7: If the distance between $(3, a)$ and $(4, 1)$ is $\sqrt{10}$, then the values of $a$ are:
A \text{(A) } 4, -2
B \text{(B) } 2, -4
C \text{(C) } 1, -3
D \text{(D) } 0, 2
Derivation & Analysis:

$(4-3)^2 + (1-a)^2 = 10 \implies 1 + (1-a)^2 = 10 \implies (1-a)^2 = 9 \implies 1-a = \pm 3 \implies a = 4$ or $a = -2$.

Q8
MCQ 8: Assertion (A): Three points with integer coordinates can form an equilateral triangle. Reason (R): The area of a triangle with integer coordinates cannot be irrational.
A \text{(A) Both (A) and (R) are true and (R) is the correct explanation}
B \text{(B) Both (A) and (R) are true but (R) is not the correct explanation}
C \text{(C) (A) is true but (R) is false}
D \text{(D) (A) is false and (R) is true}
Derivation & Analysis:

It is a well-known theorem in number geometry that an equilateral triangle cannot have all three vertices with integer coordinates because its area would be $\frac{\sqrt{3}}{4}s^2$, which is irrational when side squared is rational integer, contradicting the rational area formula for integer coordinate vertices.

Q9
MCQ 9: The orthocenter of the triangle formed by $(0, 0), (3, 0)$, and $(0, 4)$ is:
A \text{(A) } (3, 4)
B \text{(B) } (0, 0)
C \text{(C) } (1.5, 2)
D \text{(D) } (3, 3)
Derivation & Analysis:

This is a right-angled triangle at the origin $(0, 0)$. In any right-angled triangle, the orthocenter coincides with the vertex containing the right angle, which is $(0, 0)$.

Q10
MCQ 10: If the coordinates of the vertices of a triangle are $(0, 0), (3, 4)$, and $(4, 3)$, its circumcenter is:
A \text{(A) } (2, 2)
B \text{(B) } (1.5, 2)
C \text{(C) } (2, 1.5)
D \text{(D) } (3.5, 3.5)
Derivation & Analysis:

Use perpendicular bisectors or circumcenter formula for triangle vertices. Solving yields $(3.5, 3.5)$.
