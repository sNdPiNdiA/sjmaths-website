4. Coordinate Geometry of Two Dimensions

Classification as parabola

The general homogeneous and non-homogeneous second-degree equation in two variables represents a parabola if and only if the discriminant of its second-degree terms vanishes ($\Delta = h^2 - ab = 0$) while the full determinant of the conic does not vanish ($\Delta_0 \neq 0$).

Definition and statement

Discriminant Condition for Parabolic Conics
The general equation of second degree in two variables, given by $$ax^2 + 2hxy + by^2 + 2gx + 2fy + c = 0$$, represents a parabola if and only if the condition $h^2 - ab = 0$ holds and the determinant $\Delta = \begin{vmatrix} a & h & g \\ h & b & f \\ g & f & c \end{vmatrix} \neq 0$.

📐 Geometric Interpretation

Geometrically, the condition $h^2 - ab = 0$ ensures that the pair of straight lines at infinity are coincident, meaning the conic has only one finite center at infinity or is a non-central conic whose eccentricity is exactly equal to unity ($e = 1$).

⚙️ Algebraic & Structural Meaning

Algebraically, the second-degree homogeneous part $ax^2 + 2hxy + by^2$ forms a perfect square when $h^2 - ab = 0$. This implies the principal axes are rotated and shifted, transforming the quadratic form into standard parabolic coordinates.

Conditions and hypotheses

Check each condition and what changes when it is omitted:

Hypothesis / Condition Domain / Interval Necessity What Fails If Condition Dropped
Second-degree term discriminant ($h^2 - ab = 0$) Exact equality Strictly Mandatory If $h^2 - ab < 0$, the conic is an ellipse or circle; if $h^2 - ab > 0$, it is a hyperbola.
Full determinant non-vanishing ($\Delta \neq 0$) Non-zero inequality Strictly Mandatory If $\Delta = 0$, the equation degenerates into a pair of parallel or intersecting straight lines (or a single point line pair).
Coefficients $a, b$ not both zero simultaneously Non-triviality of quadratic form Essential for second-degree nature If $a = b = h = 0$, the equation reduces to a linear equation representing a straight line.

Useful results and shortcuts

Use these identities and results when their conditions apply:

01

Perfect Square Inspection for $h^2 - ab = 0$

$$ax^2 + 2hxy + by^2 = (\sqrt{a}x \pm \sqrt{b}y)^2$$

Rule: If the homogeneous quadratic part can be instantly factorized into the square of a linear expression, $h^2 - ab = 0$ is guaranteed without calculating the determinant.

Application: Use this to quickly eliminate non-parabolic options.
02

Determinant Test for Degeneracy vs Parabola

$$\Delta = abc + 2fgh - af^2 - bg^2 - ch^2$$

Rule: Compute $\Delta$ rapidly using Sarrus' rule. If $\Delta \neq 0$ and $h^2 = ab$, mark parabola immediately.

Application: Standard pattern in DSSSB PGT and KVS Mathematics screening.
03

Axis Rotation Angle for Parabola

$$\tan(2\theta) = \frac{2h}{a - b}$$

Rule: When $a = b$, the angle of rotation is immediately $\theta = \frac{\pi}{4}$ provided $h \neq 0$.

Application: Used in advanced UP PGT questions on axis transformation.
04

Latus Rectum Length via Coefficients

$$L = \frac{2\sqrt{(a-b)^2 + 4h^2}}{a+b} \text{ (when } a+b \neq 0\text)}$$ or derived via shifting.

Rule: Quick formula for latus rectum when standardizing general second-degree equations.

05

Vertex Determination Shortcut

$$\frac{\partial S}{\partial x} = 0 \quad \text{and} \quad \frac{\partial S}{\partial y} = 0$$

Rule: Partially differentiate the conic equation $S(x,y) = 0$ with respect to $x$ and $y$ and solve the linear system to find the pole/center (or use tangent properties for vertex).

06

Rapid Option Elimination via $h^2$ vs $ab$

$$h^2 < ab \implies \text{Ellipse}, \quad h^2 = ab \implies \text{Parabola}, \quad h^2 > ab \implies \text{Hyperbola}$$

Rule: Check $h^2 - ab$ first on all four options; usually 2 options violate this outright.

Common errors and counterexamples

These examples show how a conclusion can fail when a condition is missing:

⚠️ COMMON ERROR #01

Forgetting the Degeneracy Check ($\Delta = 0$)

Common Pitfall: Candidates check $h^2 = ab$ and immediately conclude it is a parabola without verifying whether $\Delta = 0$.

Counterexample: $x^2 + 2xy + y^2 + x + y = 0$

Why it fails: Here $h^2 - ab = 1^2 - (1)(1) = 0$, but $\Delta = 0$ because the equation factorizes into parallel straight lines $(x+y)(x+y+1) = 0$ (or similar degenerate form).

🛡️ Exam Defense: Always compute the 3x3 determinant $\Delta$ before finalizing 'parabola'.
⚠️ COMMON ERROR #02

Misidentifying $2h$ coefficient

Common Pitfall: Taking the coefficient of $xy$ directly as $h$ instead of dividing by $2$.

Counterexample: In $ax^2 + 4xy + by^2$, $2h = 4 \implies h = 2$, so $h^2 = 4$.

Why it fails: Using $h = 4$ leads to $h^2 = 16$, completely ruining the discriminant calculation $h^2 - ab$.

🛡️ Exam Defense: Always extract $h$ by halving the coefficient of the $xy$ term.

Solved examples

Each problem includes a full solution and a shorter method:

CANONICAL PROBLEM 01 Dual-Method Breakdown

Find the value of $\lambda$ for which the equation $x^2 - 4xy + 4y^2 - 4x - 8y + \lambda = 0$ represents a parabola.

(A) $\lambda = 3$(B) $\lambda = -3$(C) Any real number(D) No such real $\lambda$ exists
✓ Correct Answer: (D)
📚 Method A: Formal Textbook Derivation
Here $a = 1, 2h = -4 \implies h = -2, b = 4$. Thus $h^2 - ab = (-2)^2 - (1)(4) = 4 - 4 = 0$. The second-degree terms already form a parabola condition. Now check $\Delta$: $a=1, h=-2, g=-2, b=4, f=-4, c=\lambda$. Compute $\Delta = 1(4\lambda - 16) - (-2)(-2\lambda - 8) - 2(8 - (-8)) = 4\lambda - 16 - 4\lambda - 16 - 32 = -64 \neq 0$ for all $\lambda$. However, for a standard non-degenerate parabola, the quadratic form $(x-2y)^2 - 4(x+2y) + \lambda = 0$ requires consistent constant terms. Wait, let us check if the equation as written can ever form a valid parabola or if $\Delta = 0$ constraint applies to degenerate forms. Actually, for $x^2 - 4xy + 4y^2 - 4x - 8y + \lambda = 0$, $h^2 - ab = 0$ holds identically. The determinant $\Delta = \begin{vmatrix} 1 & -2 & -2 \\ -2 & 4 & -4 \\ -2 & -4 & \lambda \end{vmatrix}$. Expanding: $1(4\lambda - 16) - (-2)(-2\lambda - 8) + (-2)(8 - (-8)) = 4\lambda - 16 - 4\lambda - 16 - 32 = -64 \neq 0$. Since $\Delta \neq 0$ for all $\lambda$, the conic is a non-degenerate parabola for any real $\lambda$. Thus option (C) is correct.
⚡ Method B: 25-Second Exam Shortcut
Observe $h^2 - ab = 0$ is independent of $\lambda$. Check $\Delta$, which evaluates to $-64 \neq 0$ regardless of $\lambda$, confirming a non-degenerate parabola for all real $\lambda$.
💡 Tip: Do not rush to conclude 'no solution' just because $\lambda$ disappears from the determinant expansion of $c$ terms in certain sub-minors; verify every row carefully.
CANONICAL PROBLEM 02 Dual-Method Breakdown

The equation $9x^2 + 24xy + 16y^2 - 20x + 15y - 0 = 0$ represents:

(A) An ellipse(B) A parabola(C) A hyperbola(D) A pair of parallel lines
✓ Correct Answer: (B)
📚 Method A: Formal Textbook Derivation
Identify coefficients: $a = 9$, $2h = 24 \implies h = 12$, $b = 16$. Calculate $h^2 - ab = 12^2 - (9)(16) = 144 - 144 = 0$. Since $h^2 - ab = 0$, the conic is either a parabola or a degenerate pair of parallel lines. Testing the full determinant $\Delta$, we find $\Delta \neq 0$, hence it represents a non-degenerate parabola.
⚡ Method B: 25-Second Exam Shortcut
Notice $9x^2 + 24xy + 16y^2 = (3x + 4y)^2$, which is a perfect square. This immediately confirms $h^2 - ab = 0$, pointing directly to a parabola.
💡 Tip: Recognizing perfect square quadratic forms saves critical seconds in UP PGT exams.

Here $a = 4, 2h = -12 \implies h = -6, b = 9$. Then $h^2 - ab = (-6)^2 - (4)(9) = 36 - 36 = 0$. Also $4x^2 - 12xy + 9y^2 = (2x - 3y)^2$, representing a parabola.
