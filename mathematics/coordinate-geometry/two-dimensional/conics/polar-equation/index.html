Coordinate Geometry

Standard polar equation of conics

The standard polar equation of a conic section with focus at the pole and eccentricity $e$ is expressed as $\frac{l}{r} = 1 + e \cos \theta$. Mastery of this form and its transformations is a vital, high-yielding scoring zone in UP TGT/PGT and DSSSB examinations for rapid problem solving.

Target Exams: UP PGT Mathematics UP TGT Mathematics DSSSB PGT / TGT KVS / NVS PGT LT Grade
📖 Self-Study Mastery Status
○ Mark Topic as Mastered

Pillar 1 Rigorous Definition & Mathematical Statement

Standard Polar Equation of Conics Theorem
Let the focus of a conic be at the pole $O(0,0)$ and the corresponding directrix be perpendicular to the initial line at a distance $d$ from the focus. Then the polar equation of the conic is given by $$\frac{l}{r} = 1 + e \cos(\theta - \alpha)$$ where $l$ is the semi-latus rectum, $e$ is the eccentricity, and $\alpha$ is the angle made by the axis of the conic with the initial line.

📐 Geometric Interpretation

In polar coordinates $(r, \theta)$, the pole represents the focus, $r$ is the focal distance, and $\theta$ is the vectorial angle. The parameter $e$ dictates the shape: $e=0$ (circle), $01$ (hyperbola).

⚙️ Algebraic & Structural Meaning

Converts complicated quadratic Cartesian equations $Ax^2 + Bxy + Cy^2 + Dx + Ey + F = 0$ into a single compact reciprocal radius form $\frac{l}{r} = 1 + e \cos \theta$, greatly simplifying rotation and focal property calculations.

Pillar 2 Hypotheses & Condition Checklist

Every hypothesis is strictly non-negotiable. Inspect the domain type and the exact consequence if a condition fails:

Hypothesis / Condition Domain / Interval Necessity What Fails If Condition Dropped
Focus at the Pole Fixed Origin $(r=0, \theta=0)$ Strictly Mandatory Shifting the focus away from the origin adds extra phase angles or destroys the standard $1 + e \cos \theta$ structure.
Directrix Perpendicular to Initial Line (or at angle $\alpha$) $\theta \in [0, 2\pi)$ Necessary for standard orientation If the directrix is inclined arbitrarily without angle adjustment, the cosine term must be replaced by $\cos(\theta - \alpha)$.
Non-zero Semi-latus Rectum ($l > 0$) $l \in (0, \infty)$ Strictly Mandatory If $l = 0$, the conic degenerates into a point or ray at the pole.

Pillar 3 High-Yield Exam Results & Direct Shortcuts

Standard 15-to-30 second shortcuts frequently tested in UP TGT/PGT and DSSSB:

01

Shortcut 1: Semi-latus Rectum from Perihelion and Aphelion

$$l = \frac{2 r_{\min} r_{\max}}{r_{\min} + r_{\max}} = \frac{2ab^2}{a^2} \text{ (for ellipse)}$$

Exam Rule: When closest distance $r_1$ and farthest distance $r_2$ from the focus are given, the semi-latus rectum $l$ is the harmonic mean of these distances.

⚡ PYQ Context: Frequently asked in UP PGT to find equation of ellipse/hyperbola directly from apsidal distances.
02

Shortcut 2: Reciprocal Polar Sum Property

$$ \frac{1}{r_1} + \frac{1}{r_2} = \frac{2}{l} $$

Exam Rule: For any focal chord inclined at angle $\theta$ to the axis, the sum of the reciprocals of the segments of any focal chord is constant and equals $\frac{2}{l}$.

⚡ PYQ Context: Standard recurring formula tested in DSSSB and UP TGT vector/coordinate sections.
03

Shortcut 3: Direct Identification of Eccentricity from Equation

$$ \frac{5}{r} = 2 + 3 \cos \theta \implies \frac{5/2}{r} = 1 + \frac{3}{2}\cos \theta \implies e = \frac{3}{2} $$

Exam Rule: Always divide the constant numerator term to make the constant term on the right side equal to $1$. The coefficient of $\cos \theta$ then directly gives $e$.

⚡ PYQ Context: Direct 10-second identification questions in KVS/NVS PGT exams.
04

Shortcut 4: Tangent at Pole / Asymptotes Condition

$$ 1 + e \cos \theta_0 = 0 \implies \cos \theta_0 = -\frac{1}{e} $$

Exam Rule: Real tangents from the pole or real asymptotes exist only if $e \ge 1$. For an ellipse ($e < 1$), $\cos \theta = -1/e$ has no real solution, confirming no real tangents from the focus.

⚡ PYQ Context: Tested in conceptual assertions in PGT examinations.
05

Shortcut 5: Polar Equation with Directrix to the Left vs Right

$$ \frac{l}{r} = 1 \pm e \cos \theta \quad \text{and} \quad \frac{l}{r} = 1 \pm e \sin \theta $$

Exam Rule: Use $+ e \cos \theta$ when directrix is to the right ($x = d$), $- e \cos \theta$ when to the left ($x = -d$). Use $\sin \theta$ when directrix is horizontal.

⚡ PYQ Context: Crucial for identifying correct options matching coordinate shifts.
06

Shortcut 6: Rapid Elimination via Asymptote Angle

$$ \theta = \pm \arccos\left(-\frac{1}{e}\right) $$

Exam Rule: Eliminate options for hyperbolas where asymptotic directions fail to satisfy $\sec \theta = -e$.

⚡ PYQ Context: Saves up to 45 seconds on tough hyperbola polar problems.

Pillar 4 Classic Exam Traps & Counterexamples

Where examiners construct tricky trap options and how to safeguard your marks:

⚠️ EXAM TRAP #01

Trap 1: The Right-Hand Side Unity Fallacy

Common Pitfall: Given $\frac{2}{r} = 3 + 4\cos\theta$, candidates incorrectly assume $e = 4$.

Counterexample: \frac{2}{3r} = 1 + \frac{4}{3}\cos\theta \implies e = \frac{4}{3} > 1

Why it fails: The standard form requires the constant term on the RHS to be strictly $1$. Failing to normalize leads to completely wrong conic classification.

🛡️ Exam Defense: Always divide through by the constant term on the RHS before reading off $e$ or $l$.
⚠️ EXAM TRAP #02

Trap 2: Sign Confusion for Directrix Position

Common Pitfall: Assuming $\frac{l}{r} = 1 - e\cos\theta$ represents a directrix on the positive x-axis.

Counterexample: r = \frac{l}{1 - e\cos\theta}

Why it fails: A negative sign corresponds to a directrix located to the left of the focus ($x = -d$).

🛡️ Exam Defense: Remember: Minus sign means directrix on the negative side; plus sign means positive side.

Pillar 6 Solved Canonical Exam Problems (Dual-Method)

Comparing the formal academic proof against the rapid competitive exam elimination trick:

CANONICAL PROBLEM 01 Dual-Method Breakdown

Find the eccentricity of the conic represented by the polar equation $\frac{10}{r} = 3 + 5 \cos \theta$.

(A) $\frac{3}{5}$(B) $\frac{5}{3}$(C) $\frac{4}{3}$(D) $\frac{3}{4}$
✓ Correct Answer: (B)
📚 Method A: Formal Textbook Derivation
Step 1: Rewrite the equation in standard form by dividing by 3: $\frac{10/3}{r} = 1 + \frac{5}{3} \cos \theta$. Step 2: Compare with $\frac{l}{r} = 1 + e \cos \theta$. Step 3: Identify $e = \frac{5}{3}$. Since $e > 1$, it represents a hyperbola.
⚡ Method B: 25-Second Exam Shortcut
Directly take the ratio of the coefficient of $\cos \theta$ to the constant term on the RHS: $e = \frac{5}{3}$.
💡 Key Takeaway: Always check if the RHS constant is 1 before reading off coefficients.
CANONICAL PROBLEM 02 Dual-Method Breakdown

If $SPS'$ is a focal chord of the conic $\frac{l}{r} = 1 + e \cos \theta$ such that $SP = r_1$ and $SP' = r_2$, what is the value of $\frac{1}{r_1} + \frac{1}{r_2}$?

(A) $\frac{1}{l}$(B) $\frac{2}{l}$(C) $\frac{e}{l}$(D) $\frac{2e}{l}$
✓ Correct Answer: (B)
📚 Method A: Formal Textbook Derivation
Step 1: Let the angle of $SP$ be $\theta_1 = \alpha$. Then $SP'$ has angle $\theta_2 = \alpha + \pi$. Step 2: Write $\frac{l}{r_1} = 1 + e \cos \alpha$ and $\frac{l}{r_2} = 1 + e \cos(\alpha + \pi) = 1 - e \cos \alpha$. Step 3: Add the two equations to get $\frac{l}{r_1} + \frac{l}{r_2} = 2$, yielding $\frac{1}{r_1} + \frac{1}{r_2} = \frac{2}{l}$.
⚡ Method B: 25-Second Exam Shortcut
Standard focal chord reciprocal sum property: always equals $\frac{2}{l}$ regardless of inclination angle $\alpha$.
💡 Key Takeaway: Memorize this as a direct result for quick marking in UP PGT.

Q2
The eccentricity of the conic $\frac{6}{r} = 2 + 2 \cos \theta$ is:
A 1
B 2
C 1/2
D 3
Derivation & Analysis:

Dividing by 2 gives $\frac{3}{r} = 1 + \cos \theta$. Thus $e = 1$, representing a parabola.

Q3
If $r = \frac{p}{1 - \cos \theta}$ is a polar equation, its Cartesian equivalent is:
A y^2 = 2px + p^2
B y^2 = 2px - p^2
C x^2 = 2py
D y^2 = -2px
Derivation & Analysis:

Substitute $r = \sqrt{x^2+y^2}$ and $r \cos \theta = x$ into $r(1 - \cos \theta) = p$ to get $\sqrt{x^2+y^2} - x = p \implies x^2+y^2 = (p+x)^2 = p^2 + 2px + x^2 \implies y^2 = 2px + p^2$.

Q4
The semi-latus rectum of the conic $\frac{5}{r} = 3 + 4 \cos \theta$ is:
A 5
B 5/3
C 3/5
D 4/3
Derivation & Analysis:

Standard form is $\frac{l/3}{r} = 1 + \frac{4}{3}\cos\theta$. Comparing $\frac{l}{3} = \frac{5}{3}$ gives $l = \frac{5}{3}$? Wait, standard form $\frac{l}{r} = 1 + e \cos\theta$ means from $\frac{5}{r} = 3(1 + \frac{4}{3}\cos\theta) \implies \frac{5/3}{r} = 1 + \frac{4}{3}\cos\theta$, so $l = \frac{5}{3}$. Let's verify: if numerator is $l$, dividing equation by 3 gives $\frac{5/3}{r}$, so $l = 5/3$. Wait, original equation $\frac{5}{r} = 3 + 4\cos\theta$, when $r$ is latus rectum $\theta=\pi/2 \implies r = 5/3$. Correct.

Q5
The polar equation of a conic with focus at pole and directrix $x = p$ is:

B \frac{l}{r} = 1 - e \cos \theta
C \frac{l}{r} = 1 + e \sin \theta
D \frac{l}{r} = 1 - e \sin \theta
Derivation & Analysis:

When the directrix is to the right ($x = p > 0$), the standard polar equation takes the positive sign: $\frac{l}{r} = 1 + e \cos \theta$.

Q6
If the eccentricity $e > 1$, the polar equation $\frac{l}{r} = 1 + e \cos \theta$ represents:
A Ellipse

By definition, $e > 1$ corresponds to a hyperbola.

Q7

A \frac{1}{l}
B \frac{2}{l}

Derivation & Analysis:

By the standard focal chord property for conics, $\frac{1}{r_1} + \frac{1}{r_2} = \frac{2}{l}$.

Q8
What is the nature of the conic given by $\frac{3}{r} = 4 + 2 \cos \theta$?

B Hyperbola with $e = 2$

D Ellipse with $e = 2/3$
Derivation & Analysis:

Dividing by 4 gives $\frac{3/4}{r} = 1 + \frac{1}{2} \cos \theta$. Here $e = 1/2 < 1$, which represents an ellipse.

Q9
The polar equation of the directrix corresponding to the focus at the pole for $\frac{l}{r} = 1 + e \cos \theta$ is:
A r \cos \theta = \frac{l}{e}
B r \cos \theta = \frac{e}{l}
C r = \frac{l}{e}
D r \sin \theta = \frac{l}{e}
Derivation & Analysis:

The directrix is given in polar coordinates by $r \cos \theta = \frac{l}{e}$, which corresponds to the Cartesian line $x = \frac{l}{e}$.

Q10
If $r = a(1 + \cos \theta)$ represents a parabola, its semi-latus rectum is:
A a

Rewriting $r = a(1 + \cos \theta)$ as $\frac{a}{r} = \frac{1}{1 + \cos \theta}$ or transforming to standard form $\frac{2a}{r} = 1 + \cos \theta$ shows that the semi-latus rectum $l = 2a$.

Pillar 8 Frequently Asked Questions

Polar equations place the focus directly at the origin (pole), which elegantly simplifies equations involving orbital mechanics, focal properties, and central forces without cumbersome coordinate shifts.

Normalize the RHS constant to $1$ by division. The coefficient of the cosine (or sine) term is precisely the eccentricity $e$. If $e < 1$ it's an ellipse, if $e = 1$ it's a parabola, and if $e > 1$ it's a hyperbola.

Coordinate geometry typically contributes 12-15% of the total mathematics paper, with polar equations of conics appearing at least 1-2 times directly as property or eccentricity identification questions.

Always utilize the invariant reciprocal sum formula $\frac{1}{r_1} + \frac{1}{r_2} = \frac{2}{l}$, which bypasses trigonometric angle expansions entirely.

If the eccentricity $e > 1$, the polar equation $\frac{l}{r} = 1 + e \cos \theta$ represents:
A Ellipse
B Parabola
C Hyperbola
D Circle
Derivation & Analysis:

By definition, $e > 1$ corresponds to a hyperbola.

Q7
The sum of the reciprocals of the segments of any focal chord of a conic of semi-latus rectum $l$ is:
A \frac{1}{l}
B \frac{2}{l}
C \frac{4}{l}
D \frac{l}{2}
Derivation & Analysis:

By the standard focal chord property for conics, $\frac{1}{r_1} + \frac{1}{r_2} = \frac{2}{l}$.

Q8
What is the nature of the conic given by $\frac{3}{r} = 4 + 2 \cos \theta$?
A Ellipse with $e = 1/2$
B Hyperbola with $e = 2$
C Parabola with $e = 1$
D Ellipse with $e = 2/3$
Derivation & Analysis:

Dividing by 4 gives $\frac{3/4}{r} = 1 + \frac{1}{2} \cos \theta$. Here $e = 1/2 < 1$, which represents an ellipse.

Q9
The polar equation of the directrix corresponding to the focus at the pole for $\frac{l}{r} = 1 + e \cos \theta$ is:
A r \cos \theta = \frac{l}{e}
B r \cos \theta = \frac{e}{l}
C r = \frac{l}{e}
D r \sin \theta = \frac{l}{e}
Derivation & Analysis:

The directrix is given in polar coordinates by $r \cos \theta = \frac{l}{e}$, which corresponds to the Cartesian line $x = \frac{l}{e}$.

Q10
If $r = a(1 + \cos \theta)$ represents a parabola, its semi-latus rectum is:
A a
B 2a
C a/2
D 4a
Derivation & Analysis:

Rewriting $r = a(1 + \cos \theta)$ as $\frac{a}{r} = \frac{1}{1 + \cos \theta}$ or transforming to standard form $\frac{2a}{r} = 1 + \cos \theta$ shows that the semi-latus rectum $l = 2a$.

Normalize the RHS constant to $1$ by division. The coefficient of the cosine (or sine) term is precisely the eccentricity $e$. If $e < 1$ it's an ellipse, if $e = 1$ it's a parabola, and if $e > 1$ it's a hyperbola.
