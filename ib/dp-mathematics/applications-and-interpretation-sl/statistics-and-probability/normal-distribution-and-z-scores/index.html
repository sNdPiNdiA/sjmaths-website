Normal Distribution & $z$-Scores

The continuous Gaussian bell curve: $X \sim N(\mu, \sigma^2)$, standard normal scores $z = \frac{x-\mu}{\sigma}$, GDC commands (normCDF and invNorm), and determining unknown parameters $\mu$ and $\sigma$.

AI SL 4.10 Continuous Distribution GDC Distribution Tools ~6 Study Hours
4.10A

Properties of the Normal Distribution $X \sim N(\mu, \sigma^2)$

A continuous random variable $X$ follows a normal distribution with mean $\mu$ and variance $\sigma^2$ (standard deviation $\sigma$):

  • Symmetry: Symmetrical bell shape centered at the mean, median, and mode: $x = \mu$.
  • Total Area: The total area under the probability density curve equals exactly $1$ ($100\%$).
  • Points of Inflexion: Located at $x = \mu - \sigma$ and $x = \mu + \sigma$.
  • Continuity: For any single point $a$, $P(X = a) = 0$. Hence $P(X \le a) = P(X < a)$.
$$\text{Empirical Rule: } P(\mu - \sigma < X < \mu + \sigma) \approx 68.3\%$$ $$P(\mu - 2\sigma < X < \mu + 2\sigma) \approx 95.4\%$$ $$P(\mu - 3\sigma < X < \mu + 3\sigma) \approx 99.7\%$$
4.10B

Standard Normal Distribution & $z$-Scores

The standard normal distribution $Z$ has mean $0$ and variance $1$: $Z \sim N(0, 1)$. Any normal variable $X$ is standardized using:

$$z = \frac{x - \mu}{\sigma}$$

The $z$-score measures how many standard deviations an observation lies above (positive) or below (negative) the mean.

4.10C

GDC Workflows: Finding Probabilities & Inverse Normal

In IB Math AI SL, calculations are conducted directly via calculator distribution menus:

1. Probability from bounds: Use normCDF(lower, upper, μ, σ)

  • For $P(X < b)$: lower $= -1\text{E}99$, upper $= b$.
  • For $P(X > a)$: lower $= a$, upper $= 1\text{E}99$.

2. Boundary value from probability (percentile): Use invNorm(area, μ, σ)

  • Input area is always the cumulative area to the left (unless your GDC allows specifying RIGHT or CENTER tail).
4.10D

Determining Unknown $\mu$ and $\sigma$

When the parameters $\mu$ or $\sigma$ are unknown, standard normal $z$-scores must be used:

  1. Find the corresponding $z$-value using invNorm(probability, 0, 1).
  2. Set up the linear equation: $z = \frac{x - \mu}{\sigma} \iff x = \mu + z\sigma$.
  3. If both $\mu$ and $\sigma$ are unknown, two equations from two given probabilities are formed and solved simultaneously.

Check a normal model and condition on selection

  • Inspect symmetry, outliers and a roughly bell-shaped distribution. A normal model has unbounded tails, so consider whether predictions below0or beyond physical limits are negligible. A sample mean alone does not establish normality.
  • For b>a, $P(X>b\mid X>a)=P(X>b)/P(X>a)$. The denominator represents the selected group, not the entire population.
  • Calculator workflow: enter standard deviation, not variance; sketch the requested tail before selecting CDF or inverse CDF. Recheck the boundary by putting it back into the CDF.
Exercise 1 [3 marks]

A random variable $X$ is normally distributed with mean $\mu = 50$ and standard deviation $\sigma = 8$. Find $P(42 < X < 62)$.

Solution:

Using GDC command $\text{normCDF}(42, 62, 50, 8)$: [M1]

$P(42 < X < 62) = 0.774537...$ [A1]

To 3 significant figures: $0.775$ (or $77.5\%$). [A1]

Exercise 2 [3 marks]

The weights of packages are modeled by $W \sim N(3.5, 0.4^2)$ kg. Find the probability that a randomly chosen package weighs more than $4.1\text{ kg}$.

Solution:

$P(W > 4.1) = \text{normCDF}(4.1, 1\text{E}99, 3.5, 0.4)$ [M1]

$= 0.066807...$ [A1]

To 3 sig figs: $0.0668$ (or $6.68\%$). [A1]

Exercise 3 [4 marks]

Exam scores follow $S \sim N(68, 12^2)$. The top $10\%$ of students are awarded a grade 7 certificate. Find the minimum score required to receive a grade 7.

Solution:

Let $k$ be the threshold score. $P(S > k) = 0.10 \implies P(S < k) = 0.90$. [M1]

Using GDC: $k = \text{invNorm}(0.90, 68, 12)$ [M1]

$k = 83.378...$ [A1]

Minimum score is $83.4$ (or integer score 84). [A1]

Exercise 4 [3 marks]

Convert an observed score of $x = 118$ from a distribution with $\mu = 100$ and $\sigma = 15$ to a standardized $z$-score, and interpret its meaning.

Solution:

$z = \frac{x - \mu}{\sigma} = \frac{118 - 100}{15} = \frac{18}{15} = 1.20$ [M1][A1]

Interpretation: The observation lies $1.2$ standard deviations above the population mean. [R1]

Exercise 5 [5 marks]

A variable $X \sim N(\mu, 5^2)$ has $P(X < 32) = 0.12$. Find the value of the population mean $\mu$.

Solution:

Find the standard normal $z$-value: $P(Z < z) = 0.12$ [M1]

$z = \text{invNorm}(0.12, 0, 1) = -1.174986...$ [A1]

Apply the transformation: $z = \frac{32 - \mu}{5}$ [M1]

$-1.174986 \times 5 = 32 - \mu \implies -5.8749 = 32 - \mu$ [M1]

$\mu = 32 + 5.8749 = 37.87... \approx 37.9$ [A1]

Exercise 6 [6 marks]

A machine fills soda cans. The volume of liquid $V$ follows $V \sim N(\mu, \sigma^2)$. If $P(V < 325) = 0.05$ and $P(V > 335) = 0.10$, calculate $\mu$ and $\sigma$.

Solution:

For $P(V < 325) = 0.05$: $z_1 = \text{invNorm}(0.05, 0, 1) = -1.64485$ [A1]

Equation 1: $\frac{325 - \mu}{\sigma} = -1.64485 \implies 325 = \mu - 1.64485\sigma$ [M1]

For $P(V > 335) = 0.10 \implies P(V < 335) = 0.90$: $z_2 = \text{invNorm}(0.90, 0, 1) = 1.28155$ [A1]

Equation 2: $\frac{335 - \mu}{\sigma} = 1.28155 \implies 335 = \mu + 1.28155\sigma$ [M1]

Subtract Eq 1 from Eq 2: $10 = (1.28155 + 1.64485)\sigma = 2.9264\sigma \implies \sigma = \frac{10}{2.9264} \approx 3.42$ [A1]

Substitute $\sigma$: $\mu = 335 - 1.28155(3.417) \approx 330.6 \approx 331\text{ ml}$ [A1]

Exercise 7 [3 marks]

Explain why $P(X \le 70) = P(X < 70)$ for a normal random variable $X$.

Solution:

The normal distribution is a continuous distribution. [R1]

The probability of any exact single point is zero: $P(X = 70) = 0$. [A1]

Therefore, $P(X \le 70) = P(X < 70) + P(X = 70) = P(X < 70)$. [R1]

Exercise 8 [4 marks]

In a production run of 500 light bulbs whose lifespans are modeled by $T \sim N(1200, 150^2)$ hours, estimate the expected number of bulbs that will last fewer than 1000 hours.

Solution:

$P(T < 1000) = \text{normCDF}(-1\text{E}99, 1000, 1200, 150)$ [M1]

$= 0.091211...$ [A1]

Expected count $= 500 \times 0.091211 = 45.605...$ [M1]

Approximately $46$ light bulbs. [A1]

Exercise 9 [3 marks]

Sketch or describe the effect on the normal curve when the standard deviation $\sigma$ is doubled while the mean $\mu$ remains constant.

Solution:

The curve remains centered at the same mean value $x = \mu$. [A1]

The curve becomes wider and more spread out (greater dispersion). [A1]

The peak height at $\mu$ decreases so that the total area remains exactly 1. [R1]

Exercise 10 [4 marks]

A symmetrical interval around the mean contains $80\%$ of the population for $X \sim N(200, 25^2)$. Find the boundaries of this interval $[200 - d, 200 + d]$.

Solution:

If $80\%$ lies in the center, each outer tail has $\frac{1 - 0.80}{2} = 0.10$ probability. [M1]

Lower boundary $= \text{invNorm}(0.10, 200, 25) = 167.96... \approx 168$ [A1]

Upper boundary $= \text{invNorm}(0.90, 200, 25) = 232.03... \approx 232$ [A1]

Interval: $[168, 232]$ (or $d \approx 32.0$). [A1]

Core Formulas (Topic 4.10)

$z$-Score Standardization $$z = \frac{x - \mu}{\sigma} \iff x = \mu + z\sigma$$
GDC Probability Function $$P(a < X < b) = \text{normCDF}(a, b, \mu, \sigma)$$
GDC Inverse Normal $$k = \text{invNorm}(p, \mu, \sigma) \quad \text{where } P(X < k) = p$$
Total Area & Symmetry $$P(X < \mu) = P(X > \mu) = 0.5$$

Top 5 IB Exam Traps to Avoid

  • Trap 1: Confusing Variance with Standard Deviation: When written as $N(50, 16)$, $\sigma^2 = 16$, which means $\sigma = 4$! Do not enter 16 into the GDC standard deviation prompt.
  • Trap 2: Feeding upper tail percentage into invNorm: For "top $15\%$", the cumulative area to the left is $1 - 0.15 = 0.85$. Entering $0.15$ finds the bottom $15\%$.
  • Trap 3: Forgetting the sign of $z$: Values below the mean have negative $z$-scores. Omitting the minus sign when setting up simultaneous equations causes major errors.
  • Trap 4: Rounding intermediate $z$-scores too early: Store full calculator accuracy (e.g. $z = -1.28155...$) to avoid 3-sig-fig rounding errors in $\mu$ and $\sigma$.
  • Trap 5: Using pdf instead of cdf: Never use normPDF for probabilities! In continuous distributions, probability is area, which requires normCDF.
