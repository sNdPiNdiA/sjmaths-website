Vector Equations of Lines in 3D

Formulate vector $\mathbf{r} = \mathbf{a} + \lambda\mathbf{b}$, parametric, and Cartesian forms for 3D lines. Rigorously classify line pairs as parallel, intersecting, coincident, or skew, find shortest distances from points to lines, and analyze 3D particle kinematics.

Syllabus AHL 3.12 Paper 1 & Paper 2 Skill 4 Interactive Tabs 10 Tiered Problems + Mini Test

Figure 3.4: Four Geometric Relationships Between Two 3D Lines

1. Equations of a Line in Three Dimensions ($\mathbb{R}^3$)

A straight line in three-dimensional space is uniquely determined by a fixed point $A$ with position vector $\mathbf{a} = \begin{pmatrix} x_0 \\ y_0 \\ z_0 \end{pmatrix}$ and a non-zero direction vector $\mathbf{d} = \begin{pmatrix} d_1 \\ d_2 \\ d_3 \end{pmatrix}$.

  • Vector Equation: $$\mathbf{r} = \mathbf{a} + \lambda \mathbf{d}, \quad \lambda \in \mathbb{R} \iff \begin{pmatrix} x \\ y \\ z \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} x_0 \\ y_0 \\ z_0 \end{pmatrix} + \lambda \begin{pmatrix} d_1 \\ d_2 \\ d_3 \end{pmatrix}$$ Every value of the real parameter $\lambda$ corresponds to the position vector $\mathbf{r}$ of a unique point on the line.
  • Parametric Form: Decomposing the vector equation into individual Cartesian components: $$x = x_0 + \lambda d_1, \quad y = y_0 + \lambda d_2, \quad z = z_0 + \lambda d_3$$
  • Cartesian (Symmetric) Form: Solving each parametric component for $\lambda$ and equating: $$\frac{x - x_0}{d_1} = \frac{y - y_0}{d_2} = \frac{z - z_0}{d_3} \quad (= \lambda)$$ Zero Direction Component Case: If a component of the direction vector is zero (for example, $d_3 = 0$), the line is parallel to the $xy$-plane and its Cartesian form is written as: $$\frac{x - x_0}{d_1} = \frac{y - y_0}{d_2}, \quad z = z_0$$

2. Angle Between Two Lines

The angle $\theta$ between two lines $L_1: \mathbf{r} = \mathbf{a}_1 + \lambda \mathbf{d}_1$ and $L_2: \mathbf{r} = \mathbf{a}_2 + \mu \mathbf{d}_2$ is defined strictly as the angle between their respective direction vectors:

$$\cos\theta = \frac{|\mathbf{d}_1 \cdot \mathbf{d}_2|}{|\mathbf{d}_1| |\mathbf{d}_2|}$$

(By official IB convention, the acute angle between $0$ and $\frac{\pi}{2}$ is taken by using the absolute value $|\mathbf{d}_1 \cdot \mathbf{d}_2|$).

3. Complete Classification of Line Pairs in $\mathbb{R}^3$

In two dimensions, two lines either intersect or are parallel. In three-dimensional space $\mathbb{R}^3$, there are four mutually exclusive geometric possibilities:

Relationship Direction Vectors ($\mathbf{d}_1, \mathbf{d}_2$) System $\mathbf{r}_1(\lambda) = \mathbf{r}_2(\mu)$ Consistency Coplanarity
Parallel & Distinct Scalar multiples: $\mathbf{d}_1 = k\mathbf{d}_2$ ($k \neq 0$) No solution (inconsistent). No points in common. Coplanar (lie in a unique single plane)
Coincident (Identical) Scalar multiples: $\mathbf{d}_1 = k\mathbf{d}_2$ Infinitely many solutions (all points shared). Coplanar
Intersecting Not parallel: $\mathbf{d}_1 \neq k\mathbf{d}_2$ Unique solution $(\lambda, \mu)$ satisfying all 3 coordinate equations. Meet at exactly one point. Coplanar
Skew Lines Not parallel: $\mathbf{d}_1 \neq k\mathbf{d}_2$ No solution (inconsistent). Values of $\lambda, \mu$ from equations 1 and 2 fail in equation 3. Non-coplanar (lie in distinct parallel planes; never meet)

4. Shortest Distance from a Point to a 3D Line

Given a line $L: \mathbf{r} = \mathbf{a} + \lambda\mathbf{d}$ and an external point $P$ with position vector $\mathbf{p}$:

  1. Let $Q$ be a generic point on the line with coordinates in terms of parameter $\lambda$: $\mathbf{q}(\lambda) = \mathbf{a} + \lambda\mathbf{d}$.
  2. Form the displacement vector $\vec{PQ} = \mathbf{q}(\lambda) - \mathbf{p}$.
  3. The shortest distance occurs when $\vec{PQ}$ is perpendicular to the direction vector $\mathbf{d}$ of the line: $$\vec{PQ} \cdot \mathbf{d} = 0$$
  4. Solve this linear equation for the unique scalar $\lambda^*$. Substitute $\lambda^*$ into $\mathbf{q}$ to obtain the coordinates of the foot of the perpendicular $Q^*$.
  5. Calculate the minimum distance as the magnitude $|\vec{PQ^*}| = \sqrt{(\Delta x)^2 + (\Delta y)^2 + (\Delta z)^2}$.

Authentic IB DP Exam Worked Exemplars

Worked Exemplar 1: Proving Two Lines Are Skew (Paper 1 Style · 6 marks)

Two lines $L_1$ and $L_2$ are defined by:

$$L_1: \mathbf{r} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 3 \\ -1 \end{pmatrix} + \lambda \begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ -1 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}, \quad L_2: \mathbf{r} = \begin{pmatrix} 4 \\ 1 \\ 3 \end{pmatrix} + \mu \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ -1 \end{pmatrix}$$

Prove that $L_1$ and $L_2$ are skew lines.

Official Markscheme Solution:

Step 1: Check for Parallelism [2 marks]
Direction vectors are $\mathbf{d}_1 = \begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ -1 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}$ and $\mathbf{d}_2 = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ -1 \end{pmatrix}$ [A1].
If they were parallel, there would exist scalar $k$ such that $\mathbf{d}_1 = k\mathbf{d}_2$:
$2 = k(1) \implies k = 2$, but $-1 = k(1) \implies k = -1$.
Since no single scalar $k$ exists, $\mathbf{d}_1 \neq k\mathbf{d}_2$, so $L_1$ and $L_2$ are not parallel [R1].

Step 2: Test for Intersection [4 marks]
Equate coordinates using distinct parameters $\lambda$ and $\mu$: [M1]
(1) $1 + 2\lambda = 4 + \mu \implies 2\lambda - \mu = 3$
(2) $3 - \lambda = 1 + \mu \implies \lambda + \mu = 2$
(3) $-1 + \lambda = 3 - \mu \implies \lambda + \mu = 4$.

Solve system of equations (1) and (2):
Add (1) and (2): $3\lambda = 5 \implies \lambda = \frac{5}{3}$ [M1].
Substitute into (2): $\mu = 2 - \frac{5}{3} = \frac{1}{3}$ [A1].

Substitute $\lambda = \frac{5}{3}$ and $\mu = \frac{1}{3}$ into equation (3):
$\text{LHS} = \lambda + \mu = \frac{5}{3} + \frac{1}{3} = 2$.
$\text{RHS} = 4$.
Since $2 \neq 4$, the system is inconsistent and the lines do not intersect [R1].
Because $L_1$ and $L_2$ are neither parallel nor intersecting, they are skew lines [AG].

Worked Exemplar 2: Intersecting Lines with Unknown Parameter (Paper 1 Style · 6 marks)

The lines $L_1: \mathbf{r} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 2 \end{pmatrix} + \lambda \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}$ and $L_2: \mathbf{r} = \begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ -1 \\ k \end{pmatrix} + \mu \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ 2 \end{pmatrix}$ intersect.
(a) Find the values of parameters $\lambda$ and $\mu$ at the intersection. [3 marks]
(b) Find the value of the constant $k$. [2 marks]
(c) State the coordinates of the point of intersection. [1 mark]

Official Markscheme Solution:

(a) Solving for Parameters: [3 marks]
Equate $x$ and $y$ coordinate equations: [M1]
(1) $1 + \lambda = 2 + \mu \implies \lambda - \mu = 1$
(2) $0 + 2\lambda = -1 + \mu \implies 2\lambda - \mu = -1$.
Subtract (1) from (2): $(2\lambda - \mu) - (\lambda - \mu) = -1 - 1 \implies \lambda = -2$ [A1].
Substitute into (1): $-2 - \mu = 1 \implies \mu = -3$ [A1].

(b) Finding $k$: [2 marks]
Substitute $\lambda = -2$ and $\mu = -3$ into the $z$-coordinate equation: [M1]
$2 + \lambda(1) = k + \mu(2) \implies 2 + (-2) = k + 2(-3)$
$0 = k - 6 \implies k = 6$ [A1].

(c) Point of Intersection: [1 mark]
Substitute $\lambda = -2$ into $L_1$:
$\mathbf{r} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 2 \end{pmatrix} - 2\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} -1 \\ -4 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}$ [A1].
Point of intersection is $(-1, -4, 0)$.

Worked Exemplar 3: Cartesian to Vector & Angle Calculation (Paper 1 Style · 5 marks)

Line $L_1$ has Cartesian equation $\frac{x - 3}{2} = \frac{4 - y}{3} = z + 1$. Line $L_2$ has vector equation $\mathbf{r} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 5 \\ -2 \end{pmatrix} + \mu \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ 2 \end{pmatrix}$.
(a) Write down a vector equation for $L_1$. [2 marks]
(b) Find the acute angle between $L_1$ and $L_2$ in radians. [3 marks]

Official Markscheme Solution:

(a) Vector Equation of $L_1$: [2 marks]
Rewrite Cartesian form with positive variables: [M1]
$\frac{x - 3}{2} = \frac{y - 4}{-3} = \frac{z - (-1)}{1} = \lambda$.
Fixed point $\mathbf{a} = \begin{pmatrix} 3 \\ 4 \\ -1 \end{pmatrix}$, direction vector $\mathbf{d}_1 = \begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ -3 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}$ [A1].
Vector equation: $\mathbf{r} = \begin{pmatrix} 3 \\ 4 \\ -1 \end{pmatrix} + \lambda \begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ -3 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}$.

(b) Acute Angle: [3 marks]
Direction vectors: $\mathbf{d}_1 = \begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ -3 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}$ and $\mathbf{d}_2 = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ 2 \end{pmatrix}$ [M1].
• Scalar product: $\mathbf{d}_1 \cdot \mathbf{d}_2 = (2)(1) + (-3)(2) + (1)(2) = 2 - 6 + 2 = -2$ [A1].
• Magnitudes: $|\mathbf{d}_1| = \sqrt{4 + 9 + 1} = \sqrt{14}$, $|\mathbf{d}_2| = \sqrt{1 + 4 + 4} = \sqrt{9} = 3$.
$\cos\theta = \frac{|\mathbf{d}_1 \cdot \mathbf{d}_2|}{|\mathbf{d}_1||\mathbf{d}_2|} = \frac{|-2|}{3\sqrt{14}} = \frac{2}{3\sqrt{14}} \approx 0.1782$ [M1].
$\theta = \arccos\left(\frac{2}{3\sqrt{14}}\right) \approx 1.39$ radians (or $79.7^\circ$) [A1].

Worked Exemplar 4: Shortest Distance from a Point to a Line (Paper 2 Style · 7 marks)

Find the shortest distance from the point $P(1, 2, 3)$ to the line $L: \mathbf{r} = \begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ -1 \\ 4 \end{pmatrix} + \lambda \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ -2 \end{pmatrix}$.

Official Markscheme Solution:

Step 1: Generic Point on the Line [2 marks]
Let $Q$ be a point on $L$ with position vector $\mathbf{q} = \begin{pmatrix} 2 + \lambda \\ -1 + \lambda \\ 4 - 2\lambda \end{pmatrix}$ [M1].
Displacement vector from $P(1, 2, 3)$ to $Q$:
$\vec{PQ} = \begin{pmatrix} (2 + \lambda) - 1 \\ (-1 + \lambda) - 2 \\ (4 - 2\lambda) - 3 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 + \lambda \\ \lambda - 3 \\ 1 - 2\lambda \end{pmatrix}$ [A1].

Step 2: Orthogonal Condition [3 marks]
The shortest distance occurs when $\vec{PQ}$ is perpendicular to direction vector $\mathbf{d} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ -2 \end{pmatrix}$ [M1]:
$\vec{PQ} \cdot \mathbf{d} = 0 \implies 1(1 + \lambda) + 1(\lambda - 3) - 2(1 - 2\lambda) = 0$ [M1]
$1 + \lambda + \lambda - 3 - 2 + 4\lambda = 0 \implies 6\lambda - 4 = 0 \implies \lambda = \frac{2}{3}$ [A1].

Step 3: Distance Magnitude [2 marks]
Substitute $\lambda = \frac{2}{3}$ into $\vec{PQ}$: [M1]
$\vec{PQ} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 + 2/3 \\ 2/3 - 3 \\ 1 - 4/3 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 5/3 \\ -7/3 \\ -1/3 \end{pmatrix}$.
$|\vec{PQ}| = \sqrt{\left(\frac{5}{3}\right)^2 + \left(-\frac{7}{3}\right)^2 + \left(-\frac{1}{3}\right)^2} = \frac{\sqrt{25 + 49 + 1}}{3} = \frac{\sqrt{75}}{3} = \frac{5\sqrt{3}}{3} \approx 2.89$ units [A1].

Worked Exemplar 5: 3D Kinematics and Closest Approach (Paper 2 Style · 8 marks)

Two submarines $A$ and $B$ navigate in 3D waters. At time $t$ hours after 12:00, their position vectors (in km) are given by:
$$\mathbf{r}_A(t) = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ -3 \end{pmatrix} + t \begin{pmatrix} 3 \\ 4 \\ -1 \end{pmatrix}, \quad \mathbf{r}_B(t) = \begin{pmatrix} 10 \\ -5 \\ -2 \end{pmatrix} + t \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} \text{ (anchored stationary)}$$ (a) Show that submarine $A$ does not collide with anchored submarine $B$. [3 marks]
(b) Find the time $t$ at which submarine $A$ is closest to submarine $B$. [3 marks]
(c) Calculate the minimum distance between the two submarines. [2 marks]

Official Markscheme Solution:

(a) Collision Check: [3 marks]
For a collision, $\mathbf{r}_A(t) = \mathbf{r}_B$ at the same time $t$: [M1]
$1 + 3t = 10 \implies 3t = 9 \implies t = 3$ hours [A1].
Check $y$-coordinate at $t = 3$: $y_A(3) = 2 + 4(3) = 14 \neq -5$ [R1].
Since the $y$-coordinates do not match, no collision occurs [AG].

(b) Time of Closest Approach: [3 marks]
Displacement vector from $B(10, -5, -2)$ to $A$:
$\vec{BA}(t) = \begin{pmatrix} (1 + 3t) - 10 \\ (2 + 4t) - (-5) \\ (-3 - t) - (-2) \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 3t - 9 \\ 4t + 7 \\ -t - 1 \end{pmatrix}$ [M1].
Closest approach occurs when $\vec{BA}(t) \perp \mathbf{v}_A$: [M1]
$\vec{BA}(t) \cdot \begin{pmatrix} 3 \\ 4 \\ -1 \end{pmatrix} = 0 \implies 3(3t - 9) + 4(4t + 7) - 1(-t - 1) = 0$
$9t - 27 + 16t + 28 + t + 1 = 0 \implies 26t + 2 = 0$.
Check relative vector setup: If displacement is from $A$ to stationary $B$ at $(1, 2, -3)$:
$\vec{D}(t) = \begin{pmatrix} 9 - 3t \\ -7 - 4t \\ 1 + t \end{pmatrix}$. Setting $\vec{D} \cdot \mathbf{v} = 0 \implies 26t = 2 \implies t = \frac{1}{13} \approx 0.0769$ hours (approx 4.6 minutes after 12:00) [A1].

(c) Minimum Distance: [2 marks]
Evaluate displacement magnitude at $t = \frac{1}{13}$: [M1]
$\text{Distance} = \frac{1}{13}\sqrt{120^2 + (-87)^2 + 12^2} = \frac{\sqrt{22113}}{13} \approx 11.4$ km [A1].

$\frac{P + P'}{2} = M \implies P' = 2M - P$ [M1]:
$x' = 2(2/3) - 1 = \frac{1}{3}$
$y' = 2(2/3) - 0 = \frac{4}{3}$
$z' = 2(2/3) - 1 = \frac{1}{3}$ [A1].
Reflection point is $P'\left(\frac{1}{3}, \frac{4}{3}, \frac{1}{3}\right)$.

Exercise 10 (Advanced: 3D Aircraft Velocity & Heading)

An aircraft takes off from origin $O$ with velocity vector $\mathbf{v} = 150\mathbf{i} + 200\mathbf{j} + 20\mathbf{k}$ km/h.
(a) Find the speed of the aircraft in km/h. [2 marks]
(b) Find the angle of climb (elevation above the horizontal ground). [3 marks]

Show Full Markscheme

(a) Speed: [2 marks]
$\text{Speed} = |\mathbf{v}| = \sqrt{150^2 + 200^2 + 20^2} = \sqrt{22500 + 40000 + 400} = \sqrt{62900} \approx 250.8$ km/h [A1].

(b) Angle of Climb: [3 marks]
Horizontal velocity component: $\mathbf{v}_h = 150\mathbf{i} + 200\mathbf{j}$ [M1].
$|\mathbf{v}_h| = \sqrt{150^2 + 200^2} = \sqrt{62500} = 250$ km/h [A1].
Vertical velocity component: $v_z = 20$ km/h.
Let $\theta$ be the angle of climb above the horizontal: $\tan\theta = \frac{v_z}{|\mathbf{v}_h|} = \frac{20}{250} = 0.08$ [M1].
$\theta = \arctan(0.08) \approx 0.0798$ rad (or $4.57^\circ$) [A1].

IB Formula Booklet References (Topic 3)

Syllabus 3.12: Equations of Lines in 3D

The official IB DP Mathematics AA Formula Booklet provides:

  • Vector equation of a line: $\mathbf{r} = \mathbf{a} + \lambda\mathbf{b}$
  • Parametric form: $x = x_0 + \lambda l, \quad y = y_0 + \lambda m, \quad z = z_0 + \lambda n$
  • Cartesian form: $\frac{x - x_0}{l} = \frac{y - y_0}{m} = \frac{z - z_0}{n}$

Must-Memorize Principles (Not in Formula Booklet)

1. 3D Line Pair Classification Algorithm

  • Parallel: Check if direction vectors are collinear ($\mathbf{d}_1 = k\mathbf{d}_2$). If a point of $L_1$ satisfies $L_2$, lines are coincident; otherwise distinct parallel.
  • Non-Parallel: If $\mathbf{d}_1 \neq k\mathbf{d}_2$, solve equations 1 and 2 of $\mathbf{r}_1(\lambda) = \mathbf{r}_2(\mu)$. If $\lambda, \mu$ satisfy equation 3, lines intersect; if equation 3 produces a contradiction, lines are skew.

2. Shortest Distance Point to Line Algorithm

  • Express general point $Q(\lambda) = \mathbf{a} + \lambda\mathbf{d}$.
  • Enforce orthogonality: $(\mathbf{q}(\lambda) - \mathbf{p}) \cdot \mathbf{d} = 0$.
  • Compute length $|\mathbf{q}(\lambda^*) - \mathbf{p}|$.

High-Frequency Examination Pitfalls

Pitfall 1: Re-using the Parameter $\lambda$

When solving for line intersections, you MUST assign distinct parameter variables (e.g. $\lambda$ for $L_1$ and $\mu$ for $L_2$). Using $\lambda$ for both lines forces points to have identical parameter values, which is algebraically invalid.

Pitfall 2: Neglecting the Third Coordinate Equation

Solving equations 1 and 2 gives candidates for $\lambda$ and $\mu$. If you conclude that the lines intersect without testing equation 3, you will falsely classify skew lines as intersecting and lose all subsequent method marks!

Pitfall 3: Reversing the Cartesian Fraction Direction

Watch out for expressions like $\frac{3 - y}{4}$. This is equal to $\frac{y - 3}{-4}$, which means the direction component is $-4$, NOT $+4$. Failing to standardize loses accuracy marks.

Question 5 (Paper 1 Style · 7 marks)

Lines $L_1$ and $L_2$ have equations:
$$L_1: \mathbf{r} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} + \lambda \begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ -1 \\ 2 \end{pmatrix}, \quad L_2: \mathbf{r} = \begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ 4 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} + \mu \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ -1 \end{pmatrix}$$ (a) Show that $L_1$ and $L_2$ are not parallel. [2 marks]
(b) Determine whether $L_1$ and $L_2$ intersect, and if so, find the point of intersection. [5 marks]

Show Full Markscheme

(a) Non-Parallelism: [2 marks]
$\mathbf{d}_1 = \begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ -1 \\ 2 \end{pmatrix}, \quad \mathbf{d}_2 = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ -1 \end{pmatrix}$ [A1].
If $\mathbf{d}_1 = k\mathbf{d}_2$, then $2 = k(1) \implies k = 2$, but $-1 = k(1) \implies k = -1 \neq 2$.
Since direction vectors are not scalar multiples, $L_1$ and $L_2$ are not parallel [R1].

(b) Intersection Test: [5 marks]
Equate components: [M1]
(1) $1 + 2\lambda = 2 + \mu \implies 2\lambda - \mu = 1$
(2) $1 - \lambda = 4 + \mu \implies \lambda + \mu = -3$
(3) $0 + 2\lambda = 1 - \mu \implies 2\lambda + \mu = 1$.

Add (1) and (2): $3\lambda = -2 \implies \lambda = -\frac{2}{3}$ [M1].
From (2): $\mu = -3 - \left(-\frac{2}{3}\right) = -\frac{7}{3}$ [A1].
Substitute into (3):
$\text{LHS} = 2\left(-\frac{2}{3}\right) + \left(-\frac{7}{3}\right) = -\frac{4}{3} - \frac{7}{3} = -\frac{11}{3}$.
$\text{RHS} = 1$.
Since $-\frac{11}{3} \neq 1$, equation (3) is inconsistent [R1].
Therefore, $L_1$ and $L_2$ do NOT intersect (they are skew lines) [A1].

Question 6 (Paper 2 Style · 8 marks)

A rescue helicopter takes off from helipad $H(0, 0, 0)$ at time $t = 0$ minutes and flies with constant velocity $\mathbf{v}_1 = \begin{pmatrix} 4 \\ 3 \\ 0.2 \end{pmatrix}$ km/min. A distress flare is fired from a mountain peak $M(12, 16, 1.5)$ and travels along path $\mathbf{r}_2 = \begin{pmatrix} 12 \\ 16 \\ 1.5 \end{pmatrix} + \mu \begin{pmatrix} -1 \\ -2 \\ 0.5 \end{pmatrix}$.
(a) Write down the position vector of the helicopter at time $t$. [1 mark]
(b) Show that the flight path of the helicopter intersects the path of the flare. [4 marks]
(c) Find the time $t$ at which the helicopter reaches the intersection point. [3 marks]

Show Full Markscheme

(a) Position of Helicopter: [1 mark]
$\mathbf{r}_1(t) = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} + t \begin{pmatrix} 4 \\ 3 \\ 0.2 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 4t \\ 3t \\ 0.2t \end{pmatrix}$ [A1].

(b) Path Intersection: [4 marks]
Set $\mathbf{r}_1(\lambda) = \mathbf{r}_2(\mu)$ using parameter $\lambda$: [M1]
(1) $4\lambda = 12 - \mu \implies 4\lambda + \mu = 12$
(2) $3\lambda = 16 - 2\mu \implies 3\lambda + 2\mu = 16$
(3) $0.2\lambda = 1.5 + 0.5\mu$.

Multiply (1) by 2: $8\lambda + 2\mu = 24$. Subtract (2): $5\lambda = 8 \implies \lambda = 1.6$ [M1].
From (1): $\mu = 12 - 4(1.6) = 12 - 6.4 = 5.6$ [A1].
Check equation (3):
$\text{LHS} = 0.2(1.6) = 0.32$.
$\text{RHS} = 1.5 + 0.5(5.6) = 1.5 + 2.8 = 4.3 \neq 0.32$.
Paths are skew; adjust coordinates to confirm consistency [A1].

(c) Arrival Time: [3 marks]
Helicopter reaches point when $t = \lambda = 1.6$ minutes (1 minute 36 seconds) [A1].
