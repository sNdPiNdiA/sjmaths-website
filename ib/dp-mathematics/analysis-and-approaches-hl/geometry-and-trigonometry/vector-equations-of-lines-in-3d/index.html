Vector Equations of Lines in 3D

Write vector $\mathbf{r} = \mathbf{a} + \lambda\mathbf{b}$ and Cartesian equations for 3D lines. Classify line pairs as parallel, intersecting, coincident, or skew, and find their shortest distance.

Syllabus AHL 3.12 Paper 1 & Paper 2 Skill 4 Interactive Tabs 10 Tiered Problems + Mini Test

Figure 3.4: Four Geometric Relationships Between Two 3D Lines

1. Equations of a Line in Three Dimensions

A straight line in $\mathbb{R}^3$ passing through a fixed point with position vector $\mathbf{a}$ and parallel to non-zero direction vector $\mathbf{b}$ has three standard representations:

  • Vector Equation: $$\mathbf{r} = \mathbf{a} + \lambda \mathbf{b}, \quad \lambda \in \mathbb{R}$$ $$\begin{pmatrix} x \\ y \\ z \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} x_0 \\ y_0 \\ z_0 \end{pmatrix} + \lambda \begin{pmatrix} d_1 \\ d_2 \\ d_3 \end{pmatrix}$$
  • Parametric Equations: $$x = x_0 + \lambda d_1, \quad y = y_0 + \lambda d_2, \quad z = z_0 + \lambda d_3$$
  • Cartesian (Symmetric) Form: Eliminating parameter $\lambda$: $$\frac{x - x_0}{d_1} = \frac{y - y_0}{d_2} = \frac{z - z_0}{d_3} \quad (= \lambda)$$

2. Angle Between Two Lines

The angle $\theta$ between two lines $L_1: \mathbf{r} = \mathbf{a}_1 + \lambda \mathbf{d}_1$ and $L_2: \mathbf{r} = \mathbf{a}_2 + \mu \mathbf{d}_2$ is defined as the angle between their direction vectors:

$$\cos\theta = \frac{|\mathbf{d}_1 \cdot \mathbf{d}_2|}{|\mathbf{d}_1| |\mathbf{d}_2|}$$

(By convention, the acute angle is taken using the absolute value $|\mathbf{d}_1\cdot\mathbf{d}_2|$).

3. Classification of Lines in $\mathbb{R}^3$

Unlike 2D geometry where lines either intersect or are parallel, two lines in 3D have four possible relationships:

Relationship Direction Vectors ($\mathbf{d}_1, \mathbf{d}_2$) System $\mathbf{r}_1(\lambda) = \mathbf{r}_2(\mu)$
Parallel & Distinct Scalar multiples: $\mathbf{d}_1 = k\mathbf{d}_2$ Inconsistent (no solutions). Lines never meet.
Coincident Scalar multiples: $\mathbf{d}_1 = k\mathbf{d}_2$ Consistent for all points. Same line.
Intersecting Not parallel: $\mathbf{d}_1 \neq k\mathbf{d}_2$ Unique solution $(\lambda, \mu)$ satisfies all 3 coordinate equations. Meet at 1 point.
Skew Not parallel: $\mathbf{d}_1 \neq k\mathbf{d}_2$ Inconsistent (values of $\lambda, \mu$ from equations 1 and 2 fail in equation 3). Lie in different parallel planes.

4. Worked Examples

Worked Example 1: Finding Intersection Point of Two 3D Lines

Determine whether lines $L_1$ and $L_2$ intersect, and if so, find the point of intersection:

$$L_1: \mathbf{r} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 3 \\ -1 \end{pmatrix} + \lambda \begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ -1 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}, \quad L_2: \mathbf{r} = \begin{pmatrix} 4 \\ 1 \\ 3 \end{pmatrix} + \mu \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ -1 \end{pmatrix}$$

Solution:

Set parametric coordinates equal:

$$\begin{cases} 1 + 2\lambda = 4 + \mu & (1) \\ 3 - \lambda = 1 + \mu & (2) \\ -1 + \lambda = 3 - \mu & (3) \end{cases}$$

From (1): $2\lambda - \mu = 3$.
From (2): $-\lambda - \mu = -2 \implies \lambda + \mu = 2$.
Add the two equations: $3\lambda = 5 \implies \lambda = \frac{5}{3}$. Wait, let's recheck equation 2: $3 - \lambda = 1 + \mu \implies \lambda + \mu = 2$.
Let's test an integer point: if $\lambda = 1$: $L_1 = (3, 2, 0)$. Then for $L_2$: $4 + \mu = 3 \implies \mu = -1$.
$y = 1 + (-1) = 0 \neq 2$.
Solving systematically: $\lambda = 5/3 \implies \mu = 2 - 5/3 = 1/3$.
Now check Equation (3):
LHS: $-1 + \lambda = -1 + 5/3 = 2/3$.
RHS: $3 - \mu = 3 - 1/3 = 8/3$.
Since $2/3 \neq 8/3$, Equation (3) is NOT satisfied!
Since direction vectors $(2, -1, 1)$ and $(1, 1, -1)$ are not scalar multiples, these lines are skew.

Worked Example 2: Intersecting Lines with Parameter

Lines $L_1: \mathbf{r} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 2 \end{pmatrix} + \lambda \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}$ and $L_2: \mathbf{r} = \begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ -1 \\ k \end{pmatrix} + \mu \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ 2 \end{pmatrix}$ intersect. Find $k$ and the intersection point.

Solution:

Equate $x$ and $y$ coordinates:

$$1 + \lambda = 2 + \mu \implies \lambda - \mu = 1 \quad (1)$$ $$0 + 2\lambda = -1 + \mu \implies 2\lambda - \mu = -1 \quad (2)$$

Subtract (1) from (2): $(2\lambda - \mu) - (\lambda - \mu) = -1 - 1 \implies \lambda = -2$.
Substitute into (1): $-2 - \mu = 1 \implies \mu = -3$.
Now equate $z$ coordinate with $\lambda = -2, \mu = -3$:

$$2 + \lambda(1) = k + \mu(2) \implies 2 + (-2) = k + (-3)(2) \implies 0 = k - 6 \implies k = 6$$

Point of intersection: $\mathbf{r} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 2 \end{pmatrix} - 2\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} -1 \\ -4 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}$.

Worked Example 3: Shortest Distance from a Point to a Line

Find the shortest distance from the point $P(1, 2, 3)$ to the line $L: \mathbf{r} = \begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ -1 \\ 4 \end{pmatrix} + \lambda \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ -2 \end{pmatrix}$.

Solution:

A general point on the line is $Q(2 + \lambda, -1 + \lambda, 4 - 2\lambda)$.
Vector $\vec{PQ} = \begin{pmatrix} (2 + \lambda) - 1 \\ (-1 + \lambda) - 2 \\ (4 - 2\lambda) - 3 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 + \lambda \\ \lambda - 3 \\ 1 - 2\lambda \end{pmatrix}$.
The distance is minimized when $\vec{PQ}$ is perpendicular to the direction vector $\mathbf{d} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ -2 \end{pmatrix}$:

$$\vec{PQ} \cdot \mathbf{d} = 0 \implies 1(1 + \lambda) + 1(\lambda - 3) - 2(1 - 2\lambda) = 0$$ $$1 + \lambda + \lambda - 3 - 2 + 4\lambda = 0 \implies 6\lambda - 4 = 0 \implies \lambda = \frac{2}{3}$$

Substitute $\lambda = \frac{2}{3}$ into $\vec{PQ}$:

$$\vec{PQ} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 + 2/3 \\ 2/3 - 3 \\ 1 - 4/3 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 5/3 \\ -7/3 \\ -1/3 \end{pmatrix}$$ $$\text{Distance } |\vec{PQ}| = \sqrt{\left(\frac{5}{3}\right)^2 + \left(-\frac{7}{3}\right)^2 + \left(-\frac{1}{3}\right)^2} = \frac{\sqrt{25 + 49 + 1}}{3} = \frac{\sqrt{75}}{3} = \frac{5\sqrt{3}}{3} \approx 2.89$$

Practice Exercises (10 Tiered Questions)

Exercise 1 (Foundational: Writing Vector Line Equation)

Write down a vector equation of the line passing through points $A(3, -1, 4)$ and $B(5, 2, 1)$.

Show Full Solution

Direction vector $\mathbf{d} = \vec{AB} = \begin{pmatrix} 5 - 3 \\ 2 - (-1) \\ 1 - 4 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ 3 \\ -3 \end{pmatrix}$. [M1]
Vector equation: $\mathbf{r} = \begin{pmatrix} 3 \\ -1 \\ 4 \end{pmatrix} + \lambda \begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ 3 \\ -3 \end{pmatrix}$, $\lambda \in \mathbb{R}$. [A1]

Exercise 2 (Foundational: Converting to Cartesian Form)

Convert the vector line $\mathbf{r} = \begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ 0 \\ -5 \end{pmatrix} + \lambda \begin{pmatrix} 4 \\ 3 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}$ into Cartesian form.

Show Full Solution

$x = 2 + 4\lambda \implies \lambda = \frac{x - 2}{4}$. [M1]
$y = 0 + 3\lambda \implies \lambda = \frac{y}{3}$.
$z = -5 + \lambda \implies \lambda = z + 5$.
Cartesian form: $\frac{x - 2}{4} = \frac{y}{3} = z + 5$. [A1]

Exercise 3 (Foundational: Angle Between Two Lines)

Find the acute angle between $L_1: \mathbf{r} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} + \lambda \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}$ and $L_2: \mathbf{r} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \\ 4 \end{pmatrix} + \mu \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}$.

Show Full Solution

$\mathbf{d}_1 = (1, 0, 1)$, $\mathbf{d}_2 = (0, 1, 1)$. [M1]
$\mathbf{d}_1\cdot\mathbf{d}_2 = 1(0) + 0(1) + 1(1) = 1$.
$|\mathbf{d}_1| = \sqrt{2}$, $|\mathbf{d}_2| = \sqrt{2}$.
$\cos\theta = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}\sqrt{2}} = \frac{1}{2} \implies \theta = \frac{\pi}{3} = 60^\circ$. [A1]

Exercise 4 (Intermediate: Parallel Lines Distinction)

Show that lines $L_1: \mathbf{r} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ 3 \end{pmatrix} + \lambda \begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ -4 \\ 6 \end{pmatrix}$ and $L_2: \mathbf{r} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 4 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} + \mu \begin{pmatrix} -1 \\ 2 \\ -3 \end{pmatrix}$ are parallel and distinct.

Show Full Solution

Direction vectors: $\mathbf{d}_1 = (2, -4, 6) = -2(-1, 2, -3) = -2\mathbf{d}_2$. Since $\mathbf{d}_1$ is a scalar multiple of $\mathbf{d}_2$, the lines are parallel. [M1]
Check if point $(1, 2, 3)$ lies on $L_2$:
$1 = -\mu \implies \mu = -1$.
$y = 4 + 2(-1) = 2$ (matches).
$z = 0 - 3(-1) = 3$ (matches!).
Wait, $(1, 2, 3)$ satisfies $L_2$! Therefore the lines are coincident (identical)! [A1]

Exercise 5 (Intermediate: Determining Intersecting Lines)

Find the point of intersection of $L_1: \frac{x-1}{2} = \frac{y+2}{3} = z-1$ and $L_2: \frac{x+1}{1} = \frac{y-3}{-2} = \frac{z-4}{1}$.

Show Full Solution

In parametric form:
$L_1: (1 + 2\lambda, -2 + 3\lambda, 1 + \lambda)$.
$L_2: (-1 + \mu, 3 - 2\mu, 4 + \mu)$. [M1]
Equate $x$ and $z$:
$1 + 2\lambda = -1 + \mu \implies 2\lambda - \mu = -2$.
$1 + \lambda = 4 + \mu \implies \lambda - \mu = 3$.
Subtract: $\lambda = -5 \implies \mu = -5 - 3 = -8$. [M1]
Test $y$ coordinate:
$L_1: -2 + 3(-5) = -17$.
$L_2: 3 - 2(-8) = 19$.
Since $-17 \neq 19$, the equations are inconsistent. The lines do not intersect (they are skew). [A1]

Exercise 6 (Intermediate: Closest Approach of Two Particles)

Two drones fly in straight lines with position vectors $\mathbf{r}_1(t) = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} + t\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}$ and $\mathbf{r}_2(t) = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} + t\begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}$ where $t$ is time. Find the time $t$ when the drones are closest to each other.

Show Full Solution

Relative displacement: $\mathbf{d}(t) = \mathbf{r}_1 - \mathbf{r}_2 = \begin{pmatrix} 1 + t \\ 2 - t - 1 \\ t - (1+t) \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 + t \\ 1 - t \\ -1 \end{pmatrix}$. [M1]
Squared distance: $S(t) = (1 + t)^2 + (1 - t)^2 + (-1)^2 = (1 + 2t + t^2) + (1 - 2t + t^2) + 1 = 2t^2 + 3$. [M1]
To minimize $2t^2 + 3$, set $\frac{dS}{dt} = 4t = 0 \implies t = 0$. [A1]
The drones are closest at $t = 0$, with minimum distance $\sqrt{3}$. [A1]

Exercise 7 (Exam Standard: Skew Lines Proof)

Prove that lines $L_1: \mathbf{r} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} + \lambda \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ 3 \end{pmatrix}$ and $L_2: \mathbf{r} = \begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} + \mu \begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}$ are skew.

Show Full Solution

Step 1: Check direction vectors: $\mathbf{d}_1 = (1, 2, 3)$, $\mathbf{d}_2 = (2, 1, 1)$. Since $1/2 \neq 2/1 \neq 3/1$, $\mathbf{d}_1 \neq k\mathbf{d}_2$. Lines are not parallel. [M1]

Step 2: Test for intersection:
$1 + \lambda = 2 + 2\mu \implies \lambda - 2\mu = 1$ (1)
$1 + 2\lambda = \mu \implies 2\lambda - \mu = -1$ (2)
From (1): $\lambda = 1 + 2\mu$. In (2): $2(1 + 2\mu) - \mu = -1 \implies 2 + 3\mu = -1 \implies 3\mu = -3 \implies \mu = -1$.
Then $\lambda = 1 + 2(-1) = -1$. [M1]
Check $z$-coordinate: $L_1: 1 + 3(-1) = -2$. $L_2: 1 + 1(-1) = 0$.
Since $-2 \neq 0$, the system is inconsistent. [R1]
Non-parallel and non-intersecting $\implies$ the lines are skew. [A1]

Exercise 8 (Advanced: Shortest Distance Between Two Skew Lines)

Find the shortest distance between skew lines $L_1: \mathbf{r} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} + \lambda \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}$ and $L_2: \mathbf{r} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} + \mu \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}$.

Show Full Solution

The common perpendicular vector $\mathbf{n}$ is orthogonal to both direction vectors: [M1]
$\mathbf{n} = \mathbf{d}_1 \times \mathbf{d}_2 = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} \times \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} -1 \\ 1 \\ -1 \end{pmatrix}$. [A1]
Unit normal: $\hat{\mathbf{n}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1+1+1}}(-1, 1, -1) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}(-1, 1, -1)$.
Vector connecting points on the lines: $\mathbf{a}_1 - \mathbf{a}_2 = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} - \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ -1 \end{pmatrix}$. [M1]
Shortest distance $= |(\mathbf{a}_1 - \mathbf{a}_2) \cdot \hat{\mathbf{n}}| = \left|\frac{1(-1) + 0(1) + (-1)(-1)}{\sqrt{3}}\right| = \left|\frac{-1 + 1}{\sqrt{3}}\right| = 0$?
Wait, if distance is 0, do they intersect? Check: $(1, \lambda, \lambda) = (\mu, \mu, 1) \implies \mu = 1, \lambda = 1$. Both points equal $(1, 1, 1)$! They intersect at $(1, 1, 1)$! Distance is 0. [A1]

Exercise 9 (Advanced: Reflection of a Point in a 3D Line)

Find the coordinates of the reflection of point $P(2, 1, 4)$ in the line $L: \mathbf{r} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} + \lambda \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}$.

Show Full Solution

Let $F(1+\lambda, 1, 1+\lambda)$ be the foot of the perpendicular from $P$ to $L$. [M1]
$\vec{PF} = \begin{pmatrix} 1+\lambda-2 \\ 1-1 \\ 1+\lambda-4 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} \lambda-1 \\ 0 \\ \lambda-3 \end{pmatrix}$.
$\vec{PF}\cdot\mathbf{d} = 0 \implies 1(\lambda - 1) + 0(0) + 1(\lambda - 3) = 0 \implies 2\lambda - 4 = 0 \implies \lambda = 2$. [M1]
Foot of perpendicular $F = (1+2, 1, 1+2) = (3, 1, 3)$. [A1]
Let $P'(x', y', z')$ be the reflection. Then $F$ is the midpoint of $PP'$:
$F = \frac{P + P'}{2} \implies P' = 2F - P = 2(3, 1, 3) - (2, 1, 4) = (6-2, 2-1, 6-4) = (4, 1, 2)$. [A1]

Exercise 10 (Advanced: Line as Intersection of Two Planes)

Express the line of intersection of planes $x + y + z = 3$ and $2x - y + z = 1$ in vector form $\mathbf{r} = \mathbf{a} + \lambda\mathbf{b}$.

Show Full Solution

Add the two equations: $3x + 2z = 4 \implies x = \frac{4 - 2z}{3}$. [M1]
Let $z = 3\lambda$. Then $x = \frac{4 - 6\lambda}{3} = \frac{4}{3} - 2\lambda$.
Substitute into plane 1: $y = 3 - x - z = 3 - (4/3 - 2\lambda) - 3\lambda = \frac{5}{3} - \lambda$. [M1]
Vector equation: $\mathbf{r} = \begin{pmatrix} 4/3 \\ 5/3 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} + \lambda \begin{pmatrix} -2 \\ -1 \\ 3 \end{pmatrix}$, $\lambda \in \mathbb{R}$. [A1]

Must-Memorize Principles (Not in Booklet)

1. 3D Line Classification

  • Parallel: Direction vectors are scalar multiples ($\mathbf{d}_1 = k\mathbf{d}_2$).
  • Intersecting: Non-parallel and the system $\mathbf{r}_1(\lambda) = \mathbf{r}_2(\mu)$ has a unique consistent solution.
  • Skew: Non-parallel and no common point (inconsistent system).

Examination Pitfalls & Examiner Tips

Different Parameters for Different Lines

When finding the intersection of two lines, ALWAYS use different parameters (e.g. $\lambda$ for $L_1$ and $\mu$ for $L_2$). Using $\lambda$ for both lines will cause an immediate catastrophic algebraic failure.

Checking the Third Equation

Solving equations 1 and 2 gives candidate values for $\lambda$ and $\mu$. You MUST substitute them into equation 3 to check consistency. If equation 3 fails, the lines are skew, NOT intersecting!

Section B: Examination-Style Free Response Questions

Question 5 (Paper 1 Style · 6 marks)

Two lines are defined by $L_1: \mathbf{r} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ 3 \end{pmatrix} + \lambda \begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \\ -1 \end{pmatrix}$ and $L_2: \mathbf{r} = \begin{pmatrix} -1 \\ 1 \\ 4 \end{pmatrix} + \mu \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 3 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}$.
(a) Show that $L_1$ and $L_2$ are not parallel. [1 mark]
(b) Determine whether $L_1$ and $L_2$ intersect or are skew. [5 marks]

Show Full Markscheme

(a) Non-parallel test: [1 mark]
Direction vectors are $\mathbf{d}_1 = (2, 1, -1)$ and $\mathbf{d}_2 = (1, 3, 1)$.
Since $\frac{2}{1} \neq \frac{1}{3}$, $\mathbf{d}_1$ is not a scalar multiple of $\mathbf{d}_2$, so the lines are not parallel [A1].

(b) Intersection or skew: [5 marks]
Set coordinates equal: [M1]
(1) $1 + 2\lambda = -1 + \mu \implies 2\lambda - \mu = -2$
(2) $2 + \lambda = 1 + 3\mu \implies \lambda - 3\mu = -1$
(3) $3 - \lambda = 4 + \mu \implies \lambda + \mu = -1$
From (1) and (3): add them: $3\lambda = -3 \implies \lambda = -1$ [M1].
From (3): $-1 + \mu = -1 \implies \mu = 0$ [A1].
Check (2): $\lambda - 3\mu = -1 - 3(0) = -1$ (matches equation 2!) [R1].
Since a consistent solution $(\lambda = -1, \mu = 0)$ satisfies all three equations, the lines intersect at the point $(-1, 1, 4)$ [A1].

Question 6 (Paper 2 Style · 8 marks)

A submarine travels along a straight line $L: \mathbf{r} = \begin{pmatrix} 10 \\ -5 \\ -2 \end{pmatrix} + t\begin{pmatrix} 3 \\ 4 \\ -1 \end{pmatrix}$ where $t$ is time in hours and coordinates are in kilometres.
A stationary sonar station is located at $S(1, 2, -3)$.
(a) Find the time $t$ when the submarine is closest to the sonar station. [4 marks]
(b) Calculate the minimum distance between the submarine and the station. [4 marks]

Show Full Markscheme

(a) Closest approach time: [4 marks]
Submarine position $P(10 + 3t, -5 + 4t, -2 - t)$ [M1].
Vector $\vec{SP} = \begin{pmatrix} (10+3t)-1 \\ (-5+4t)-2 \\ (-2-t)-(-3) \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 9 + 3t \\ -7 + 4t \\ 1 - t \end{pmatrix}$ [A1].
At closest approach, $\vec{SP} \perp \mathbf{d}$: [M1]
$\vec{SP}\cdot\begin{pmatrix} 3 \\ 4 \\ -1 \end{pmatrix} = 0 \implies 3(9 + 3t) + 4(-7 + 4t) - 1(1 - t) = 0$
$27 + 9t - 28 + 16t - 1 + t = 0 \implies 26t - 2 = 0 \implies 26t = 2 \implies t = \frac{1}{13} \approx 0.0769$ hours [A1].

(b) Minimum distance: [4 marks]
At $t = 1/13$:
$\vec{SP} = \begin{pmatrix} 9 + 3/13 \\ -7 + 4/13 \\ 1 - 1/13 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 120/13 \\ -87/13 \\ 12/13 \end{pmatrix}$ [M1].
Distance $= \frac{1}{13}\sqrt{120^2 + (-87)^2 + 12^2} = \frac{1}{13}\sqrt{14400 + 7569 + 144} = \frac{1}{13}\sqrt{22113} \approx \frac{148.7}{13} \approx 11.4$ km [A1].
