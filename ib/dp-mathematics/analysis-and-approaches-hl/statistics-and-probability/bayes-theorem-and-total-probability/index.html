Bayes’ Theorem and Total Probability

Reverse conditional probabilities with two or three source events, tables and probability trees.

AA HLNotes · worked examples · independent practice

Partition the sample space

  • Events A₁, …, Aₖ form a partition if they are disjoint and exhaustive. Then $P(B)=\sum_iP(A_i)P(B\mid A_i)$. Each product is a joint probability.
  • For $P(B)>0$, $P(A_i\mid B)=P(A_i)P(B\mid A_i)/P(B)$. The denominator includes every path ending in B, not only the path of interest.
  • Prior probability describes sources before observing B; posterior probability describes the revised source probability after B. $P(B\mid A)$ and $P(A\mid B)$ are usually different.

Worked three-source example

  • Factories A, B, and C supply 50%, 30%, and 20% of the items; their defect rates are 1%, 2%, and 5%. The joint defect probabilities are 0.005, 0.006, and 0.010.
  • The total defect probability is 0.021. Given a defect, $P(C\mid D)=0.010/0.021=10/21\approx0.47619$. The largest defect rate is not the posterior probability.
  • For a nondefect, use the complement rates 0.99, 0.98, and 0.95. $P(A\mid D^c)=0.495/0.979\approx0.50562$.

Base rates and diagnostic interpretation

  • Disease prevalence is 1%, sensitivity is 95%, and the false-positive rate is 5%. $P(+)=0.01(0.95)+0.99(0.05)=0.059$. $P(D\mid+)=0.0095/0.059=19/118\approx16.1\%$.
  • A positive result is not the same as certainty of disease. A low base rate can make most positives false. Use a 10,000-person frequency table to check: 95 true positives and 495 false positives.
  • Repeated-test multiplication requires conditional independence given the disease status. Reusing the single-test posterior without a valid dependence model is incorrect.

Inverse probability and modelling

  • When a mixture fraction is unknown, total probability can form an equation for it. Check that the resulting fraction is in $[0,1]$.
  • State the partition, label every tree branch, calculate joint paths, sum the evidence paths, and divide. An evidence probability of zero makes the conditional probability undefined.

Independent practice

Attempt each question before opening its solution.

Exercise 1

Using the factory data above, find $P(B\mid\text{defect})$.

$0.006/0.021=2/7\approx0.286$. The denominator is the total defect probability.

Exercise 2

Using the same data, find $P(C\mid\text{nondefect})$.

0.20(0.95)/0.979=190/979≈0.194076. Conditional proportions still sum to 1 across factories.

Exercise 3

Two machines produce in proportions 40% and 60%, with failure rates of 3% and 1%. Given a failure, find the probability that it came from the first machine.

The joint failure probabilities are 0.012 and 0.006; the posterior probability is $0.012/0.018=2/3$.

Exercise 4

A test has 90% sensitivity, a 10% false-positive rate, and 20% prevalence. Find $P(\text{disease}\mid\text{positive})$.

$0.18/(0.18+0.08)=9/13\approx0.692308$. This reverses the condition; it is not 0.9.

Exercise 5

Bags A and B are chosen with probabilities $p$ and $1-p$. Their red probabilities are 0.8 and 0.2; the overall red probability is 0.5. Find $p$.

$0.8p+0.2(1-p)=0.5$ implies $0.6p=0.3$, so $p=0.5$. This is a valid mixture fraction.

Exercise 6

For the 1% prevalence test above, compute the posterior probability after two positive results, assuming conditional independence.

The joint probability of two positives for a diseased person is $0.01(0.95)^2=0.009025$; for a healthy person it is $0.99(0.05)^2=0.002475$. The posterior probability is $0.009025/0.0115=361/460\approx0.784783$. Explain the independence assumption.

Exercise 7

Sources A, B, and C have prior probabilities 0.2, 0.3, and 0.5 and evidence likelihoods 0.4, 0.2, and 0.1. Find all posterior probabilities.

The joint terms are 0.08, 0.06, and 0.05; their total is 0.19. The posterior probabilities are 8/19, 6/19, and 5/19, which sum to 1.

Total probability and Bayes’ theorem

  • If $A_1,\ldots,A_k$ form a disjoint, exhaustive partition, then $P(B)=\sum_{i=1}^{k}P(A_i)P(B\mid A_i)$.
  • For $P(B)>0$, Bayes’ theorem gives $P(A_j\mid B)=\frac{P(A_j)P(B\mid A_j)}{P(B)}$.
  • Each path has joint probability $P(A_i\cap B)=P(A_i)P(B\mid A_i)$. Add all paths that end in the observed evidence to find the denominator.
  • For evidence $E_1,E_2$ conditionally independent given $D$, $P(E_1\cap E_2\mid D)=P(E_1\mid D)P(E_2\mid D)$. State and check the independence assumption before multiplying.
  • Conditional probability given an event of probability zero is undefined. A posterior probability is not the same as a likelihood or a prior.

  • Record variable definitions, domain restrictions and units.
