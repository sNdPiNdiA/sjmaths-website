Complex Numbers: Cartesian, Polar & Euler Forms

Represent complex numbers in Cartesian form $z = a + bi$, polar form $r\text{ cis}\theta$, and Euler form $r e^{i\theta}$. Use conjugates, Argand diagrams, products, and quotients.

Syllabus AHL 1.12 Paper 1 & Paper 2 Skill 4 Interactive Tabs 10 Tiered Problems + Mini Test

Figure 1.2: The Argand Plane, Modulus-Argument & Complex Conjugate

A complex number $z = a + bi$ plotted as a position vector $(a, b)$ with modulus $r = \sqrt{a^2+b^2}$ and principal argument $\theta \in (-\pi, \pi]$. Its complex conjugate $z^* = a - bi$ is the reflection across the real axis with argument $-\theta$.

1.12

Cartesian Form & Complex Conjugate Properties

  • Cartesian Definition: Any complex number $z \in \mathbb{C}$ is written as $z = a + bi$ where $a, b \in \mathbb{R}$ and $i = \sqrt{-1}$ ($i^2 = -1$). $$\text{Re}(z) = a, \quad \text{Im}(z) = b$$ Note: The imaginary part $\text{Im}(z)$ is the real number $b$, NOT $bi$.
  • Complex Conjugate: If $z = a + bi$, its complex conjugate is $z^* = a - bi$ (often denoted $\bar{z}$).
  • Key Conjugate Algebraic Identities:
    • $z + z^* = (a + bi) + (a - bi) = 2a = 2\text{Re}(z) \in \mathbb{R}$
    • $z - z^* = (a + bi) - (a - bi) = 2bi = 2i\text{Im}(z)$
    • $z z^* = (a + bi)(a - bi) = a^2 - (bi)^2 = a^2 + b^2 = |z|^2 \in \mathbb{R}^+$
  • Division Protocol: To simplify a quotient $\frac{w}{z}$, multiply numerator and denominator by the conjugate $z^*$ to realize the denominator: $$\frac{w}{z} = \frac{w \cdot z^*}{z \cdot z^*} = \frac{w z^*}{|z|^2}$$

Example 1

Cartesian Operations & Conjugate Realization

Given $z_1 = 3 + 4i$ and $z_2 = 1 - 2i$, express in the form $x + yi$ ($x, y \in \mathbb{R}$):
(a) $z_1 z_2$
(b) $\frac{z_1}{z_2}$

  1. Part (a): Direct FOIL expansion: $$z_1 z_2 = (3 + 4i)(1 - 2i) = 3 - 6i + 4i - 8i^2$$ Since $i^2 = -1$, $-8i^2 = +8$: $$z_1 z_2 = (3 + 8) + (-6 + 4)i = 11 - 2i$$
  2. Part (b): Multiply by conjugate $z_2^* = 1 + 2i$: $$\frac{3 + 4i}{1 - 2i} = \frac{(3 + 4i)(1 + 2i)}{(1 - 2i)(1 + 2i)} = \frac{3 + 6i + 4i + 8i^2}{1^2 + (-2)^2}$$ $$= \frac{3 - 8 + 10i}{1 + 4} = \frac{-5 + 10i}{5} = -1 + 2i$$

Answers: (a) $11 - 2i$; (b) $-1 + 2i$

1.12

Modulus, Principal Argument & Polar Form

  • Modulus ($r$ or $|z|$): The Euclidean distance from the origin in the Argand plane: $$r = |z| = \sqrt{a^2 + b^2} \ge 0$$
  • The Principal Argument ($\theta = \text{Arg}(z)$): The angle measured counterclockwise from the positive real axis. Under the IB syllabus, the principal argument must strictly satisfy: $$-\pi < \theta \le \pi \quad (\text{or } -180^\circ < \theta \le 180^\circ)$$
  • Determining Argument by Quadrant: Let $\alpha = \arctan\left(\left|\frac{b}{a}\right|\right)$ be the acute reference angle:
    • Quadrant 1 ($a > 0, b > 0$): $\theta = \alpha$
    • Quadrant 2 ($a < 0, b > 0$): $\theta = \pi - \alpha$
    • Quadrant 3 ($a < 0, b < 0$): $\theta = -(\pi - \alpha) = -\pi + \alpha$
    • Quadrant 4 ($a > 0, b < 0$): $\theta = -\alpha$
  • Polar (Modulus-Argument) Form: $$z = r(\cos\theta + i\sin\theta) \equiv r\text{ cis}\theta$$

Example 2

Conversion to Polar Form in Quadrant 2 & 3

Convert $z = -\sqrt{3} - i$ to polar modulus-argument form with principal argument $-\pi < \theta \le \pi$.

  1. Calculate modulus $r$: $$r = \sqrt{(-\sqrt{3})^2 + (-1)^2} = \sqrt{3 + 1} = \sqrt{4} = 2$$
  2. Locate quadrant & reference angle: $a = -\sqrt{3} < 0$ and $b = -1 < 0 \implies \text{Quadrant 3}$. Reference angle: $$\alpha = \arctan\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}\right) = \frac{\pi}{6}$$
  3. Find principal argument $\theta$: In Quadrant 3: $$\theta = -\left(\pi - \frac{\pi}{6}\right) = -\frac{5\pi}{6}$$
  4. Assemble polar form: $$z = 2\left(\cos\left(-\frac{5\pi}{6}\right) + i\sin\left(-\frac{5\pi}{6}\right)\right) = 2\text{ cis}\left(-\frac{5\pi}{6}\right)$$

Answer: $z = 2\left(\cos\left(-\frac{5\pi}{6}\right) + i\sin\left(-\frac{5\pi}{6}\right)\right)$

1.12

Euler's Exponential Form & Geometric Operations

  • Euler's Identity: For any real angle $\theta \in \mathbb{R}$: $$e^{i\theta} = \cos\theta + i\sin\theta$$ Setting $\theta = \pi$ yields Euler's celebrated formula: $e^{i\pi} + 1 = 0$.
  • Euler's Form of a Complex Number: $$z = r e^{i\theta}$$
  • Multiplication in Exponential Form (Scale & Rotate): $$z_1 z_2 = (r_1 e^{i\theta_1})(r_2 e^{i\theta_2}) = (r_1 r_2)e^{i(\theta_1 + \theta_2)}$$ Rule: Multiply moduli ($|z_1 z_2| = |z_1||z_2|$); add arguments ($\arg(z_1 z_2) = \arg(z_1) + \arg(z_2)$).
  • Division in Exponential Form: $$\frac{z_1}{z_2} = \frac{r_1 e^{i\theta_1}}{r_2 e^{i\theta_2}} = \left(\frac{r_1}{r_2}\right)e^{i(\theta_1 - \theta_2)}$$ Rule: Divide moduli ($|\frac{z_1}{z_2}| = \frac{|z_1|}{|z_2|}$); subtract arguments ($\arg(\frac{z_1}{z_2}) = \arg(z_1) - \arg(z_2)$).

Example 3

Multiplication with Argument Normalization

Let $z_1 = 4e^{i\frac{2\pi}{3}}$ and $z_2 = 2e^{i\frac{3\pi}{4}}$. Find $z_1 z_2$ in Euler's form with principal argument $-\pi < \theta \le \pi$.

  1. Multiply moduli: $$r = r_1 r_2 = 4 \times 2 = 8$$
  2. Sum arguments: $$\theta_{\text{raw}} = \frac{2\pi}{3} + \frac{3\pi}{4} = \frac{8\pi + 9\pi}{12} = \frac{17\pi}{12}$$
  3. Normalize into principal range $(-\pi, \pi]$: Since $\frac{17\pi}{12} > \pi$, subtract $2\pi$: $$\theta = \frac{17\pi}{12} - 2\pi = \frac{17\pi - 24\pi}{12} = -\frac{7\pi}{12}$$ Notice $-\pi < -\frac{7\pi}{12} \le \pi$.

Answer: $z_1 z_2 = 8e^{-i\frac{7\pi}{12}}$

Tier 1: Cartesian Operations & Modulus-Argument Fluency (Questions 1–4)
Exercise 1 Paper 1 Cartesian Division

Express $\frac{5 - i}{2 + 3i}$ in the form $a + bi$, where $a, b \in \mathbb{R}$.

View Complete Solution
  1. Multiply numerator and denominator by conjugate of denominator ($2 - 3i$): $$\frac{5 - i}{2 + 3i} \times \frac{2 - 3i}{2 - 3i} = \frac{10 - 15i - 2i + 3i^2}{2^2 + 3^2}$$
  2. Simplify $3i^2 = -3$: $$\frac{10 - 3 - 17i}{4 + 9} = \frac{7 - 17i}{13} = \frac{7}{13} - \frac{17}{13}i$$

Final Answer: $\frac{7}{13} - \frac{17}{13}i$

Exercise 2 Paper 1 Quadratic with Complex Roots

Solve the equation $z^2 - 4z + 13 = 0$, giving your answers in the form $a \pm bi$.

View Complete Solution
  1. Apply quadratic formula: $$z = \frac{-(-4) \pm \sqrt{(-4)^2 - 4(1)(13)}}{2(1)} = \frac{4 \pm \sqrt{16 - 52}}{2}$$
  2. Evaluate discriminant: $$\sqrt{-36} = \sqrt{36} \cdot \sqrt{-1} = 6i$$
  3. Divide by 2: $$z = \frac{4 \pm 6i}{2} = 2 \pm 3i$$

Final Answer: $z = 2 + 3i$ or $z = 2 - 3i$

Exercise 3 Paper 1 Polar Conversion in Quadrant 4

Find the modulus and principal argument of $z = 1 - i\sqrt{3}$, and express $z$ in polar form $r\text{ cis}\theta$.

View Complete Solution
  1. Modulus: $$r = \sqrt{1^2 + (-\sqrt{3})^2} = \sqrt{1 + 3} = \sqrt{4} = 2$$
  2. Argument: $a = 1 > 0$ and $b = -\sqrt{3} < 0 \implies \text{Quadrant 4}$. $$\theta = -\arctan\left(\frac{\sqrt{3}}{1}\right) = -\frac{\pi}{3}$$
  3. Polar form: $$z = 2\left(\cos\left(-\frac{\pi}{3}\right) + i\sin\left(-\frac{\pi}{3}\right)\right) = 2\text{ cis}\left(-\frac{\pi}{3}\right)$$

Final Answer: $r = 2$, $\theta = -\frac{\pi}{3}$; $z = 2\text{ cis}\left(-\frac{\pi}{3}\right)$

Exercise 4 Paper 1 Euler Form Quotient

Given $w_1 = 6e^{i\frac{\pi}{4}}$ and $w_2 = 3e^{i\frac{5\pi}{6}}$, find $\frac{w_1}{w_2}$ in Euler form $r e^{i\theta}$ where $-\pi < \theta \le \pi$.

View Complete Solution
  1. Divide moduli: $r = \frac{6}{3} = 2$.
  2. Subtract arguments: $$\theta = \frac{\pi}{4} - \frac{5\pi}{6} = \frac{3\pi - 10\pi}{12} = -\frac{7\pi}{12}$$
  3. Check range: $-\pi < -\frac{7\pi}{12} \le \pi$, so no normalization needed.

Final Answer: $\frac{w_1}{w_2} = 2e^{-i\frac{7\pi}{12}}$

Tier 2: Exam Modeling & Geometric Loci (Questions 5–8)
Exercise 5 Paper 1 Simultaneous Complex Equations

Find the complex number $z = x + iy$ that satisfies the equation: $$2z + 3z^* = 15 - 4i$$

View Complete Solution
  1. Substitute $z = x + iy$ and $z^* = x - iy$: $$2(x + iy) + 3(x - iy) = 15 - 4i$$ $$(2x + 3x) + (2y - 3y)i = 15 - 4i$$ $$5x - iy = 15 - 4i$$
  2. Equate real and imaginary parts: $$\text{Real: } 5x = 15 \implies x = 3$$ $$\text{Imaginary: } -y = -4 \implies y = 4$$
  3. Assemble $z$: $z = 3 + 4i$.

Final Answer: $z = 3 + 4i$

Exercise 6 Paper 1 Perpendicular Bisector Locus

The locus of points $z$ in the complex plane satisfies $|z - 2| = |z - 4i|$.
Find the Cartesian equation of this locus in terms of $x$ and $y$, where $z = x + iy$.

View Complete Solution
  1. Express both moduli algebraically: $$|(x - 2) + iy|^2 = |x + i(y - 4)|^2$$ $$(x - 2)^2 + y^2 = x^2 + (y - 4)^2$$
  2. Expand both sides: $$x^2 - 4x + 4 + y^2 = x^2 + y^2 - 8y + 16$$
  3. Cancel $x^2 + y^2$ and rearrange: $$-4x + 4 = -8y + 16 \implies 8y = 4x + 12 \implies y = \frac{1}{2}x + \frac{3}{2}$$
  4. Geometric insight: This is the perpendicular bisector of the segment connecting $(2, 0)$ and $(0, 4)$.

Final Answer: $y = \frac{1}{2}x + \frac{3}{2}$ (or $x - 2y + 3 = 0$)

Exercise 7 Paper 1 Circle Locus

Describe geometrically the locus in the complex plane given by $|z - (3 - 2i)| = 5$, and write down its Cartesian equation.

View Complete Solution
  1. The equation $|z - z_0| = r$ represents a circle with center $z_0$ and radius $r$.
  2. Center: $z_0 = 3 - 2i$, corresponding to coordinates $(3, -2)$.
  3. Radius: $r = 5$.
  4. Cartesian equation: $$(x - 3)^2 + (y + 2)^2 = 5^2 = 25$$

Final Answer: Circle with center $(3, -2)$ and radius $5$; $(x - 3)^2 + (y + 2)^2 = 25$

Exercise 8 Paper 1 Purely Imaginary Condition

Given $z = x + iy$, find the condition relating $x$ and $y$ such that $\frac{z - 1}{z + 1}$ is purely imaginary.

View Complete Solution
  1. Substitute $z = x + iy$: $$\frac{(x - 1) + iy}{(x + 1) + iy}$$
  2. Multiply numerator and denominator by conjugate of denominator $[(x + 1) - iy]$: $$= \frac{[(x - 1) + iy][(x + 1) - iy]}{(x + 1)^2 + y^2}$$
  3. Expand numerator real part: $$\text{Re}(\text{Numerator}) = (x - 1)(x + 1) - i^2 y^2 = (x^2 - 1) + y^2 = x^2 + y^2 - 1$$
  4. For the quotient to be purely imaginary, its real part must equal $0$: $$x^2 + y^2 - 1 = 0 \implies x^2 + y^2 = 1 \quad (\text{with } z \neq -1)$$

Final Answer: $x^2 + y^2 = 1$ with $(x, y) \neq (-1, 0)$ (unit circle minus the point $-1$)

Tier 3: Complex Trigonometry & Synthesis (Questions 9–10)
Exercise 9 Paper 1 Euler Trigonometric Identity

Using Euler's relations $e^{i\theta} = \cos\theta + i\sin\theta$ and $e^{-i\theta} = \cos\theta - i\sin\theta$:
(a) Show that $\cos\theta = \frac{e^{i\theta} + e^{-i\theta}}{2}$ and $\sin\theta = \frac{e^{i\theta} - e^{-i\theta}}{2i}$.
(b) Hence prove that $\cos^2\theta = \frac{1 + \cos(2\theta)}{2}$.

View Complete Solution
  1. Part (a): Adding: $e^{i\theta} + e^{-i\theta} = 2\cos\theta \implies \cos\theta = \frac{e^{i\theta} + e^{-i\theta}}{2}$. Subtracting: $e^{i\theta} - e^{-i\theta} = 2i\sin\theta \implies \sin\theta = \frac{e^{i\theta} - e^{-i\theta}}{2i}$.
  2. Part (b): $$\cos^2\theta = \left(\frac{e^{i\theta} + e^{-i\theta}}{2}\right)^2 = \frac{e^{2i\theta} + 2e^0 + e^{-2i\theta}}{4}$$ $$= \frac{(e^{i(2\theta)} + e^{-i(2\theta)}) + 2}{4} = \frac{2\cos(2\theta) + 2}{4} = \frac{1 + \cos(2\theta)}{2}$$

Final Answer: Identities verified algebraically.

Exercise 10 Paper 1 Complex Geometric Triangle

Points $A, B, C$ in the Argand plane represent complex numbers $z_1, z_2, z_3$ respectively.
Given that $\frac{z_3 - z_1}{z_2 - z_1} = e^{i\frac{\pi}{3}}$, describe the geometric shape of $\triangle ABC$.

View Complete Solution
  1. Inspect modulus: $$\left|\frac{z_3 - z_1}{z_2 - z_1}\right| = |e^{i\frac{\pi}{3}}| = 1 \implies |z_3 - z_1| = |z_2 - z_1|$$ This means the length of side $AC$ equals the length of side $AB$.
  2. Inspect argument: $$\arg\left(\frac{z_3 - z_1}{z_2 - z_1}\right) = \arg(z_3 - z_1) - \arg(z_2 - z_1) = \frac{\pi}{3} = 60^\circ$$ The angle $\angle BAC$ at vertex $A$ is $60^\circ$.
  3. Conclusion: An isosceles triangle with vertex angle $60^\circ$ is an equilateral triangle.

Final Answer: $\triangle ABC$ is an equilateral triangle.

Formula Booklet AHL 1.12

Complex Number Formats

  • Cartesian Form: $z = a + bi$ ($i^2 = -1$)
  • Modulus: $|z| = r = \sqrt{a^2 + b^2}$
  • Argument: $\theta = \arg(z)$, with $-\pi < \theta \le \pi$
  • Polar Form: $z = r(\cos\theta + i\sin\theta) = r\text{ cis}\theta$
  • Euler's Form: $z = r e^{i\theta}$
Must Memorize

Algebraic & Geometric Laws

  • Conjugate Product: $z z^* = |z|^2 = a^2 + b^2$
  • Product Law: $|z_1 z_2| = |z_1||z_2|$ and $\arg(z_1 z_2) = \arg(z_1) + \arg(z_2)$
  • Quotient Law: $|\frac{z_1}{z_2}| = \frac{|z_1|}{|z_2|}$ and $\arg(\frac{z_1}{z_2}) = \arg(z_1) - \arg(z_2)$
  • Euler Trig Inversions: $$\cos\theta = \frac{e^{i\theta} + e^{-i\theta}}{2}, \quad \sin\theta = \frac{e^{i\theta} - e^{-i\theta}}{2i}$$

Critical IB Examination Pitfalls in Complex Numbers

  • Failing the Principal Argument Range $(-\pi, \pi]$: Writing an argument as $\frac{4\pi}{3}$ or $240^\circ$ loses accuracy marks. You must subtract $2\pi$ to write $-\frac{2\pi}{3}$.
  • Misidentifying $\text{Im}(z)$ as $bi$: $\text{Im}(3 + 4i) = 4$, NOT $4i$. Writing $4i$ on exam papers forfeits the mark.
  • Quadrant Confusion with $\arctan$: The calculator $\arctan\left(\frac{b}{a}\right)$ only outputs values in $\left(-\frac{\pi}{2}, \frac{\pi}{2}\right)$. For $z = -1 - i$, $\frac{-1}{-1} = 1$, but the angle is $-\frac{3\pi}{4}$, NOT $\frac{\pi}{4}$. Always plot the coordinates!
  • Forgetting Conjugate in Denominator: Trying to divide complex numbers without multiplying by the conjugate of the denominator leads to algebraic dead-ends.

Section A: Diagnostic Check

4 core concept check questions. Immediate feedback and scoring.

Score: 0 / 4

Question 1

What is the principal argument of $z = -2 + 2i$?

Correct! $z$ lies in Quadrant 2 ($a < 0, b > 0$), so $\theta = \pi - \frac{\pi}{4} = \frac{3\pi}{4}$.

Question 2

If $z = 4e^{i\frac{5\pi}{6}}$, what is its complex conjugate $z^*$ expressed in Euler's form with principal argument?

Correct! The conjugate of $r e^{i\theta}$ is $r e^{-i\theta}$, so $4e^{-i\frac{5\pi}{6}}$.

Question 3

What is the value of $(1 + i)^4$?

Correct! $(1+i)^2 = 1 + 2i - 1 = 2i$, so $[(1+i)^2]^2 = (2i)^2 = 4i^2 = -4$.

Question 4

In the Argand plane, the equation $|z - 3i| = 2$ represents which geometric shape?

Correct! $|z - z_0| = r$ is a circle centered at $z_0 = 0 + 3i$ (coordinates $(0, 3)$) with radius $r = 2$.

Section B: Examination-Style Free Response Questions

Question 5 (Paper 1 Style · 6 marks)

Let $z = \frac{1 + i\sqrt{3}}{1 - i}$.
(a) Find the modulus and principal argument of $1 + i\sqrt{3}$ and $1 - i$. [3 marks]
(b) Hence express $z$ in polar form $r\text{ cis}\theta$. [2 marks]
(c) Find the smallest positive integer $n$ such that $z^n$ is a real number. [1 mark]

Show Full Markscheme

(a) Modulus & Argument: [3 marks]
• For $1 + i\sqrt{3}$: $r_1 = \sqrt{1 + 3} = 2$ [A1], $\theta_1 = \arctan(\sqrt{3}) = \frac{\pi}{3}$ [A1].
• For $1 - i$: $r_2 = \sqrt{1 + 1} = \sqrt{2}$, $\theta_2 = -\frac{\pi}{4}$ [A1].

(b) Polar Quotient: [2 marks]
$r = \frac{r_1}{r_2} = \frac{2}{\sqrt{2}} = \sqrt{2}$ [A1].
$\theta = \theta_1 - \theta_2 = \frac{\pi}{3} - \left(-\frac{\pi}{4}\right) = \frac{\pi}{3} + \frac{\pi}{4} = \frac{7\pi}{12}$ [A1].
$z = \sqrt{2}\text{ cis}\left(\frac{7\pi}{12}\right)$.

(c) Real condition: [1 mark]
$z^n$ is real when $\arg(z^n) = n\left(\frac{7\pi}{12}\right) = k\pi$ for $k \in \mathbb{Z}$.
$\frac{7n}{12} = k \implies 7n = 12k$. Since $7$ and $12$ are coprime, smallest positive $n = 12$. [A1]

Question 6 (Paper 2 Style · 7 marks)

A complex number $w$ satisfies the equation $w^3 = 8i$.
(a) Express $8i$ in polar form $r e^{i\theta}$, where $-\pi < \theta \le \pi$. [1 mark]
(b) Find the three distinct roots of $w^3 = 8i$, giving your answers in Euler's form $r e^{i\theta}$. [4 marks]
(c) Plot the three roots on an Argand diagram sketch and describe their geometric configuration. [2 marks]

Show Full Markscheme

(a) Polar form of $8i$: [1 mark]
$8i = 8e^{i\frac{\pi}{2}}$ [A1].

(b) Finding the 3 roots: [4 marks]
Let $w = R e^{i\phi}$. Then $w^3 = R^3 e^{i3\phi} = 8e^{i(\frac{\pi}{2} + 2k\pi)}$ for $k = 0, 1, 2$ [M1].
$R = 8^{1/3} = 2$ [A1].
$\phi = \frac{\frac{\pi}{2} + 2k\pi}{3} = \frac{\pi}{6} + \frac{2k\pi}{3}$ [M1]:
• $k = 0$: $w_1 = 2e^{i\frac{\pi}{6}}$ [A1]
• $k = 1$: $w_2 = 2e^{i(\frac{\pi}{6} + \frac{4\pi}{6})} = 2e^{i\frac{5\pi}{6}}$ [A1]
• $k = 2$: $\frac{\pi}{6} + \frac{4\pi}{3} = \frac{9\pi}{6} = \frac{3\pi}{2}$. Normalize to $(-\pi, \pi]$: $\frac{3\pi}{2} - 2\pi = -\frac{\pi}{2}$.
$w_3 = 2e^{-i\frac{\pi}{2}}$ [A1]

(c) Geometric configuration: [2 marks]
The three roots lie on a circle centered at the origin with radius $R = 2$ [A1].
They are spaced by angular increments of $\frac{2\pi}{3} = 120^\circ$, forming the vertices of an equilateral triangle [R1].
