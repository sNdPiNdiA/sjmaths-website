De Moivre’s Theorem & Roots of Unity

Study De Moivre’s theorem $[r\text{ cis}\theta]^n = r^n\text{ cis}(n\theta)$, deriving multi-angle trigonometric identities, finding all $n$ complex roots of equations $z^n = w$, and exploring the geometric symmetry of roots of unity.

Figure 1.3: The 6th Roots of Unity ($z^6 = 1$) Inscribed in the Unit Circle

The six roots of $z^6 = 1$ are equally spaced by $\Delta\theta = \frac{2\pi}{6} = \frac{\pi}{3}$ radians around the unit circle, forming a regular hexagon. Their vector sum is identically zero: $\sum_{k=0}^5 z_k = 0$.

1.13

De Moivre’s Theorem for Integer Powers

  • The Theorem: For any real angle $\theta \in \mathbb{R}$ and any integer $n \in \mathbb{Z}$: $$[r(\cos\theta + i\sin\theta)]^n = r^n(\cos(n\theta) + i\sin(n\theta)) = r^n e^{in\theta}$$
  • Proof for $n \in \mathbb{Z}^+$ by Induction:
    • Base case ($n=1$): $[r(\cos\theta + i\sin\theta)]^1 = r^1(\cos(1\theta) + i\sin(1\theta))$, holds trivially.
    • Hypothesis ($n=k$): Assume $[r\text{ cis}\theta]^k = r^k\text{ cis}(k\theta)$.
    • Inductive step ($n=k+1$): $$[r\text{ cis}\theta]^{k+1} = [r\text{ cis}\theta]^k \cdot [r\text{ cis}\theta] = r^k\text{ cis}(k\theta) \cdot r\text{ cis}\theta = r^{k+1}\text{ cis}(k\theta + \theta) = r^{k+1}\text{ cis}((k+1)\theta)$$
    • Conclusion: Holds for all $n \in \mathbb{Z}^+$ by mathematical induction.
  • Negative Integer Powers: $$z^{-n} = \frac{1}{z^n} = \frac{1}{r^n e^{in\theta}} = r^{-n}e^{-in\theta} = r^{-n}[\cos(-n\theta) + i\sin(-n\theta)] = r^{-n}[\cos(n\theta) - i\sin(n\theta)]$$

Example 1

High-Power Evaluation Using De Moivre’s Theorem

Evaluate $(-\sqrt{3} + i)^{12}$ without calculator expansion, giving your answer as an exact real number.

  1. Convert $z = -\sqrt{3} + i$ to polar form: $$r = \sqrt{(-\sqrt{3})^2 + 1^2} = \sqrt{3 + 1} = 2$$ Quadrant 2: $\theta = \pi - \arctan\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}\right) = \pi - \frac{\pi}{6} = \frac{5\pi}{6}$. $$z = 2\left(\cos\left(\frac{5\pi}{6}\right) + i\sin\left(\frac{5\pi}{6}\right)\right)$$
  2. Apply De Moivre's Theorem with $n = 12$: $$z^{12} = 2^{12}\left(\cos\left(12 \times \frac{5\pi}{6}\right) + i\sin\left(12 \times \frac{5\pi}{6}\right)\right)$$ $$12 \times \frac{5\pi}{6} = 2 \times 5\pi = 10\pi$$
  3. Evaluate trigonometric values: $$\cos(10\pi) = 1, \quad \sin(10\pi) = 0$$ $$z^{12} = 2^{12}(1 + 0i) = 4096$$

Answer: $(-\sqrt{3} + i)^{12} = 4096$

1.13

Trigonometric Identities via De Moivre & Binomial Expansion

  • Expressing Multiple Angles $\cos(n\theta)$ and $\sin(n\theta)$ in Powers of $\cos\theta$ and $\sin\theta$: Expand $(\cos\theta + i\sin\theta)^n$ using the Binomial Theorem, and equate with $\cos(n\theta) + i\sin(n\theta)$ from De Moivre: $$\cos(n\theta) = \text{Re}\left[(\cos\theta + i\sin\theta)^n\right], \quad \sin(n\theta) = \text{Im}\left[(\cos\theta + i\sin\theta)^n\right]$$
  • Expressing Powers $\cos^n\theta$ and $\sin^n\theta$ as Linear Combinations of Multiple Angles: Let $z = e^{i\theta} = \cos\theta + i\sin\theta$. Then $\frac{1}{z} = e^{-i\theta} = \cos\theta - i\sin\theta$. $$z^k + \frac{1}{z^k} = 2\cos(k\theta), \quad z^k - \frac{1}{z^k} = 2i\sin(k\theta)$$ Expand $(2\cos\theta)^n = \left(z + \frac{1}{z}\right)^n$ and $(2i\sin\theta)^n = \left(z - \frac{1}{z}\right)^n$ binomially, grouping terms with exponents $k$ and $-k$.

Example 2

Deriving $\cos(3\theta)$ via Binomial & De Moivre

Express $\cos(3\theta)$ in terms of powers of $\cos\theta$.

  1. Equate De Moivre and Binomial expansion for $n=3$: $$\cos(3\theta) + i\sin(3\theta) = (\cos\theta + i\sin\theta)^3$$
  2. Expand using Binomial Theorem ($\binom{3}{0}=1, \binom{3}{1}=3, \binom{3}{2}=3, \binom{3}{3}=1$): $$= \cos^3\theta + 3\cos^2\theta(i\sin\theta) + 3\cos\theta(i\sin\theta)^2 + (i\sin\theta)^3$$ Substitute $i^2 = -1$ and $i^3 = -i$: $$= \cos^3\theta + 3i\cos^2\theta\sin\theta - 3\cos\theta\sin^2\theta - i\sin^3\theta$$ $$= (\cos^3\theta - 3\cos\theta\sin^2\theta) + i(3\cos^2\theta\sin\theta - \sin^3\theta)$$
  3. Equate the Real Parts: $$\cos(3\theta) = \cos^3\theta - 3\cos\theta\sin^2\theta$$ Substitute $\sin^2\theta = 1 - \cos^2\theta$: $$\cos(3\theta) = \cos^3\theta - 3\cos\theta(1 - \cos^2\theta) = \cos^3\theta - 3\cos\theta + 3\cos^3\theta = 4\cos^3\theta - 3\cos\theta$$

Answer: $\cos(3\theta) = 4\cos^3\theta - 3\cos\theta$

1.13

$n$th Roots of Complex Numbers & Roots of Unity

  • Solving $z^n = w$: Express $w = R e^{i(\theta + 2k\pi)}$ where $k \in \mathbb{Z}$. By the Fundamental Theorem of Algebra, there are exactly $n$ distinct complex roots: $$z_k = R^{1/n} \exp\left(i \frac{\theta + 2k\pi}{n}\right) \quad \text{for } k = 0, 1, 2, \dots, n-1$$
  • The $n$th Roots of Unity ($z^n = 1$): Since $1 = 1 e^{i(0 + 2k\pi)}$, the roots are: $$\omega_k = \exp\left(i \frac{2k\pi}{n}\right) = \left(\exp\left(i\frac{2\pi}{n}\right)\right)^k = \omega^k \quad \text{where } \omega = e^{i\frac{2\pi}{n}}$$ The set of roots is $\{1, \omega, \omega^2, \omega^3, \dots, \omega^{n-1}\}$.
  • Fundamental Sum and Product Properties: $$\sum_{k=0}^{n-1} \omega^k = 1 + \omega + \omega^2 + \dots + \omega^{n-1} = \frac{1 - \omega^n}{1 - \omega} = \frac{1 - 1}{1 - \omega} = 0$$ $$\prod_{k=0}^{n-1} \omega_k = (-1)^{n-1}$$

Example 3

Cube Roots of Unity & Factorization

Find the three cube roots of unity ($z^3 = 1$), and show that $(1 - \omega)(1 - \omega^2) = 3$, where $\omega = e^{i\frac{2\pi}{3}}$.

  1. Solve $z^3 = 1 = e^{i 2k\pi}$: $$z_k = e^{i\frac{2k\pi}{3}} \quad \text{for } k = 0, 1, 2$$ • $k = 0$: $z_0 = 1$
    • $k = 1$: $\omega = e^{i\frac{2\pi}{3}} = \cos\left(\frac{2\pi}{3}\right) + i\sin\left(\frac{2\pi}{3}\right) = -\frac{1}{2} + i\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}$
    • $k = 2$: $\omega^2 = e^{i\frac{4\pi}{3}} = e^{-i\frac{2\pi}{3}} = -\frac{1}{2} - i\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2} = \omega^*$
  2. Expand product $(1 - \omega)(1 - \omega^2)$: $$(1 - \omega)(1 - \omega^2) = 1 - \omega^2 - \omega + \omega^3 = 1 - (\omega + \omega^2) + 1$$ Since $1 + \omega + \omega^2 = 0 \implies \omega + \omega^2 = -1$, and $\omega^3 = 1$: $$= 1 - (-1) + 1 = 1 + 1 + 1 = 3$$

Proven: $(1 - \omega)(1 - \omega^2) = 3$

Using De Moivre’s theorem, express $(\sqrt{3} + i)^6$ in the form $a + bi$, where $a, b \in \mathbb{R}$.

View Complete Solution
  1. Find polar form of $\sqrt{3} + i$: $$r = \sqrt{3 + 1} = 2, \quad \theta = \arctan\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}\right) = \frac{\pi}{6}$$ $$z = 2\text{ cis}\left(\frac{\pi}{6}\right)$$

Exercise 2 Paper 1 Fractional Power of Unit Complex

Evaluate $\left(\cos\left(\frac{\pi}{5}\right) + i\sin\left(\frac{\pi}{5}\right)\right)^{10}$.

View Complete Solution
  1. By De Moivre’s theorem: $$\left(\cos\left(\frac{\pi}{5}\right) + i\sin\left(\frac{\pi}{5}\right)\right)^{10} = \cos\left(10 \times \frac{\pi}{5}\right) + i\sin\left(10 \times \frac{\pi}{5}\right)$$
  2. Simplify angle: $$10 \times \frac{\pi}{5} = 2\pi$$
  3. Evaluate trig values: $$\cos(2\pi) + i\sin(2\pi) = 1 + 0i = 1$$

Final Answer: $1$

Exercise 3 Paper 1 4th Roots of a Negative Real

Find all four solutions to the equation $z^4 = -16$, expressing each root in the form $r e^{i\theta}$ where $-\pi < \theta \le \pi$.

View Complete Solution
  1. Express $-16$ in polar form: $$-16 = 16e^{i(\pi + 2k\pi)} \quad \text{for } k = 0, 1, 2, 3$$
  2. Take the 4th root: $$r = 16^{1/4} = 2, \quad \theta = \frac{\pi + 2k\pi}{4} = \frac{\pi}{4} + \frac{k\pi}{2}$$
  3. Generate the 4 roots:
    • $k = 0$: $z_0 = 2e^{i\frac{\pi}{4}}$
    • $k = 1$: $z_1 = 2e^{i\frac{3\pi}{4}}$
    • $k = 2$: $z_2 = 2e^{i\frac{5\pi}{4}} = 2e^{-i\frac{3\pi}{4}}$
    • $k = 3$: $z_3 = 2e^{i\frac{7\pi}{4}} = 2e^{-i\frac{\pi}{4}}$

Final Answer: $2e^{i\frac{\pi}{4}}, 2e^{i\frac{3\pi}{4}}, 2e^{-i\frac{3\pi}{4}}, 2e^{-i\frac{\pi}{4}}$

Exercise 4 Paper 1 Cube Roots of Unity Quadratic Relation

Let $\omega$ be a non-real root of $z^3 = 1$. Simplify the expression $(2 + 5\omega + 2\omega^2)^3$.

View Complete Solution
  1. Use the identity $1 + \omega + \omega^2 = 0 \implies 2 + 2\omega + 2\omega^2 = 0$.
  2. Decompose $5\omega = 2\omega + 3\omega$: $$2 + 5\omega + 2\omega^2 = (2 + 2\omega + 2\omega^2) + 3\omega = 0 + 3\omega = 3\omega$$
  3. Cube the result: $$(3\omega)^3 = 3^3 \cdot \omega^3 = 27 \cdot 1 = 27$$

Final Answer: $27$

Tier 2: Trigonometric Identities & Root Geometry (Questions 5–8)
Exercise 5 Paper 1 Derivation of $\sin(3\theta)$

Using De Moivre’s theorem, prove that $\sin(3\theta) = 3\sin\theta - 4\sin^3\theta$.

View Complete Solution
  1. From De Moivre: $\cos(3\theta) + i\sin(3\theta) = (\cos\theta + i\sin\theta)^3$.
  2. Binomial expansion imaginary part: $$\text{Im}[(\cos\theta + i\sin\theta)^3] = \binom{3}{1}\cos^2\theta(i\sin\theta) + \binom{3}{3}(i\sin\theta)^3$$ $$= 3\cos^2\theta\sin\theta - \sin^3\theta$$
  3. Substitute $\cos^2\theta = 1 - \sin^2\theta$: $$\sin(3\theta) = 3(1 - \sin^2\theta)\sin\theta - \sin^3\theta = 3\sin\theta - 3\sin^3\theta - \sin^3\theta = 3\sin\theta - 4\sin^3\theta$$

Final Answer: Identity proven algebraically.

Exercise 6 Paper 1 Powers to Multiple Angles Integration

Show that $\cos^4\theta = \frac{1}{8}(\cos(4\theta) + 4\cos(2\theta) + 3)$, and hence find $\int_0^{\pi/2} \cos^4\theta\,d\theta$.

View Complete Solution
  1. Let $z = e^{i\theta}$, so $2\cos\theta = z + \frac{1}{z}$. $$(2\cos\theta)^4 = 16\cos^4\theta = \left(z + \frac{1}{z}\right)^4$$ $$= z^4 + 4z^2 + 6 + \frac{4}{z^2} + \frac{1}{z^4} = \left(z^4 + \frac{1}{z^4}\right) + 4\left(z^2 + \frac{1}{z^2}\right) + 6$$ $$= 2\cos(4\theta) + 4(2\cos(2\theta)) + 6 = 2\cos(4\theta) + 8\cos(2\theta) + 6$$
  2. Divide by 16: $$\cos^4\theta = \frac{1}{8}\cos(4\theta) + \frac{1}{2}\cos(2\theta) + \frac{3}{8} = \frac{1}{8}(\cos(4\theta) + 4\cos(2\theta) + 3)$$
  3. Integrate: $$\int_0^{\pi/2} \cos^4\theta\,d\theta = \left[\frac{1}{32}\sin(4\theta) + \frac{1}{4}\sin(2\theta) + \frac{3}{8}\theta\right]_0^{\pi/2} = 0 + 0 + \frac{3}{8}\left(\frac{\pi}{2}\right) = \frac{3\pi}{16}$$

Final Answer: $\frac{3\pi}{16}$

Exercise 7 Paper 1 Area of Polygon Formed by Roots

The roots of the equation $z^8 = 256$ form the vertices of a regular octagon in the Argand plane.
Find the exact area of this octagon.

View Complete Solution
  1. Find radius $R$: $$R = 256^{1/8} = (2^8)^{1/8} = 2$$
  2. The regular octagon is composed of $8$ congruent isosceles triangles meeting at the origin, each with central angle $\theta = \frac{2\pi}{8} = \frac{\pi}{4}$.
  3. Area of one triangle: $$A_1 = \frac{1}{2} R^2 \sin\left(\frac{\pi}{4}\right) = \frac{1}{2}(2^2)\left(\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}\right) = 2 \times \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2} = \sqrt{2}$$
  4. Total area: $$\text{Area} = 8 \times A_1 = 8\sqrt{2}$$

Final Answer: $8\sqrt{2}$

Exercise 8 Paper 1 Roots of Unity Polynomial Factorization

Given that $\omega = e^{i\frac{2\pi}{5}}$ is a fifth root of unity, show that: $$1 + \omega + \omega^2 + \omega^3 + \omega^4 = 0$$ Hence prove that $\cos\left(\frac{2\pi}{5}\right) + \cos\left(\frac{4\pi}{5}\right) = -\frac{1}{2}$.

View Complete Solution
  1. $\omega^5 = 1 \implies \omega^5 - 1 = 0 \implies (\omega - 1)(1 + \omega + \omega^2 + \omega^3 + \omega^4) = 0$. Since $\omega \neq 1$, $1 + \omega + \omega^2 + \omega^3 + \omega^4 = 0$.
  2. Take real parts of the sum: $$\text{Re}(1 + \omega + \omega^2 + \omega^3 + \omega^4) = 1 + \cos\left(\frac{2\pi}{5}\right) + \cos\left(\frac{4\pi}{5}\right) + \cos\left(\frac{6\pi}{5}\right) + \cos\left(\frac{8\pi}{5}\right) = 0$$
  3. Using cosine symmetry $\cos(2\pi - \theta) = \cos\theta$: $$\cos\left(\frac{8\pi}{5}\right) = \cos\left(\frac{2\pi}{5}\right), \quad \cos\left(\frac{6\pi}{5}\right) = \cos\left(\frac{4\pi}{5}\right)$$ $$1 + 2\cos\left(\frac{2\pi}{5}\right) + 2\cos\left(\frac{4\pi}{5}\right) = 0$$ $$2\left[\cos\left(\frac{2\pi}{5}\right) + \cos\left(\frac{4\pi}{5}\right)\right] = -1 \implies \cos\left(\frac{2\pi}{5}\right) + \cos\left(\frac{4\pi}{5}\right) = -\frac{1}{2}$$

Final Answer: Proven: $-\frac{1}{2}$

Exercise 9 Paper 3 Extended Roots of Unity Product

Let $\omega_k = e^{i\frac{2k\pi}{n}}$ for $k = 0, 1, \dots, n-1$ be the $n$th roots of unity.
(a) Explain why $z^n - 1 = (z - 1)(z - \omega)(z - \omega^2)\dots(z - \omega^{n-1})$.
(b) By dividing both sides by $(z - 1)$ and taking the limit as $z \to 1$, evaluate the product: $$(1 - \omega)(1 - \omega^2)(1 - \omega^3)\dots(1 - \omega^{n-1})$$

View Complete Solution
  1. Part (a): By the Fundamental Theorem of Algebra, any degree $n$ polynomial with roots $r_k$ and leading coefficient $1$ factors as $\prod (z - r_k)$. Since the roots of $z^n - 1 = 0$ are precisely $\omega^k$, the factorization holds.
  2. Part (b): $$\frac{z^n - 1}{z - 1} = z^{n-1} + z^{n-2} + \dots + z + 1 = (z - \omega)(z - \omega^2)\dots(z - \omega^{n-1})$$ Substitute $z = 1$: $$1^{n-1} + 1^{n-2} + \dots + 1 + 1 = \underbrace{1 + 1 + \dots + 1}_{n \text{ terms}} = n$$ Therefore: $(1 - \omega)(1 - \omega^2)\dots(1 - \omega^{n-1}) = n$.

Final Answer: $(1 - \omega)(1 - \omega^2)\dots(1 - \omega^{n-1}) = n$

Exercise 10 Paper 1 General Exponential Series Sum

Prove that for $\theta \neq 2k\pi$: $$\sum_{r=0}^n \cos(r\theta) = \frac{\sin\left(\frac{(n+1)\theta}{2}\right)\cos\left(\frac{n\theta}{2}\right)}{\sin\left(\frac{\theta}{2}\right)}$$

View Complete Solution
  1. Consider the geometric series $S = \sum_{r=0}^n e^{ir\theta} = 1 + z + z^2 + \dots + z^n$, where $z = e^{i\theta}$. $$S = \frac{1 - z^{n+1}}{1 - z} = \frac{1 - e^{i(n+1)\theta}}{1 - e^{i\theta}}$$
  2. Factor out half-angles from numerator and denominator: $$1 - e^{i\phi} = e^{i\phi/2}(e^{-i\phi/2} - e^{i\phi/2}) = e^{i\phi/2}(-2i\sin(\phi/2))$$ $$S = \frac{e^{i(n+1)\theta/2}\left(-2i\sin\left(\frac{(n+1)\theta}{2}\right)\right)}{e^{i\theta/2}\left(-2i\sin\left(\frac{\theta}{2}\right)\right)} = e^{in\theta/2} \cdot \frac{\sin\left(\frac{(n+1)\theta}{2}\right)}{\sin\left(\frac{\theta}{2}\right)}$$
  3. Take the real part: $\text{Re}(e^{in\theta/2}) = \cos\left(\frac{n\theta}{2}\right)$: $$\sum_{r=0}^n \cos(r\theta) = \frac{\cos\left(\frac{n\theta}{2}\right)\sin\left(\frac{(n+1)\theta}{2}\right)}{\sin\left(\frac{\theta}{2}\right)}$$

Final Answer: Formula proven via geometric sum.

Formula Booklet AHL 1.13

De Moivre’s Theorem

  • Integer & Rational Powers: $$[r(\cos\theta + i\sin\theta)]^n = r^n(\cos(n\theta) + i\sin(n\theta)) = r^n e^{in\theta}$$
  • Roots of a Complex Number: For $z^n = r e^{i\theta}$: $$z_k = r^{1/n} \exp\left(i \frac{\theta + 2k\pi}{n}\right), \quad k = 0, 1, \dots, n-1$$
Must Memorize

Roots of Unity & Trig Substitutions

  • Roots of Unity: $\omega_k = e^{i\frac{2k\pi}{n}}$, with $1 + \omega + \dots + \omega^{n-1} = 0$
  • Trigonometric Groupings: $$z + \frac{1}{z} = 2\cos\theta, \quad z - \frac{1}{z} = 2i\sin\theta$$ $$z^k + \frac{1}{z^k} = 2\cos(k\theta), \quad z^k - \frac{1}{z^k} = 2i\sin(k\theta)$$

Critical IB Examination Pitfalls in De Moivre’s Theorem

  • Forgetting to Distribute the Exponent to the Modulus: Writing $[2\text{ cis}\theta]^4 = 2\text{ cis}(4\theta)$ instead of $2^4\text{ cis}(4\theta) = 16\text{ cis}(4\theta)$ is a recurring error.
  • Missing the $+2k\pi$ in Finding Roots: Calculating only the principal root ($k=0$) loses marks. An equation $z^n = w$ ALWAYS has exactly $n$ distinct complex roots.
  • Incorrect Powers of $i$ in Binomial Expansions: Remember: $i^2 = -1, i^3 = -i, i^4 = 1$. A sign slip invalidates the final identity.
  • Neglecting Polygon Geometric Symmetry: All roots of $z^n = w$ share identical modulus $r^{1/n}$ and are separated by uniform angles of $\frac{2\pi}{n}$.

(b) Hence express $\sin^3\theta$ in the form $A\sin(3\theta) + B\sin\theta$, where $A, B \in \mathbb{Q}$. [4 marks]

Show Full Markscheme

$z^n = \cos(n\theta) + i\sin(n\theta)$, $z^{-n} = \cos(-n\theta) + i\sin(-n\theta) = \cos(n\theta) - i\sin(n\theta)$ [M1].
Sum $= 2\cos(n\theta)$ [A1]; Difference $= 2i\sin(n\theta)$ [A1].

$(2i\sin\theta)^3 = -8i\sin^3\theta = (z - z^{-1})^3$ [M1]

Question 6 (Paper 2 Style · 8 marks)

Consider the complex equation $(z + 1)^4 = z^4$.
(a) Show that any solution $z$ must satisfy $\left|\frac{z+1}{z}\right| = 1$, and deduce that all solutions have $\text{Re}(z) = -\frac{1}{2}$. [3 marks]
(b) By writing $\frac{z+1}{z} = e^{i\theta}$, find all three solutions to the equation. [5 marks]

Show Full Markscheme

(a) Modulus & Real part deduction: [3 marks]
$|(z+1)^4| = |z^4| \implies |z+1|^4 = |z|^4 \implies |z+1| = |z|$, so $\left|\frac{z+1}{z}\right| = 1$ [M1].
$|z - (-1)| = |z - 0|$ represents points equidistant from $0$ and $-1$ in the Argand plane [M1].
The perpendicular bisector of the segment from $-1$ to $0$ is the vertical line $x = -\frac{1}{2}$, so $\text{Re}(z) = -\frac{1}{2}$ [A1].

(b) Finding the 3 solutions: [5 marks]
$\left(\frac{z+1}{z}\right)^4 = 1 \implies \frac{z+1}{z} = e^{i\frac{2k\pi}{4}} = e^{i\frac{k\pi}{2}}$ for $k = 1, 2, 3$ ($k=0$ gives $\frac{z+1}{z}=1 \implies 1=0$, impossible) [M1].
$1 + \frac{1}{z} = e^{i\frac{k\pi}{2}} \implies \frac{1}{z} = e^{i\frac{k\pi}{2}} - 1 \implies z = \frac{1}{e^{i\frac{k\pi}{2}} - 1}$ [M1]:
• $k = 1$: $e^{i\pi/2} = i \implies z_1 = \frac{1}{i - 1} = \frac{-1 - i}{2} = -\frac{1}{2} - \frac{1}{2}i$ [A1]
• $k = 2$: $e^{i\pi} = -1 \implies z_2 = \frac{1}{-1 - 1} = -\frac{1}{2}$ [A1]
• $k = 3$: $e^{i3\pi/2} = -i \implies z_3 = \frac{1}{-i - 1} = \frac{-1 + i}{2} = -\frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{2}i$ [A1].
All three solutions have $\text{Re}(z) = -\frac{1}{2}$.
