Counting, Permutations and Combinations

Count ordered arrangements and unordered selections, with repetition, restrictions and probability applications.

AA HLNotes · worked examples · independent practice

Counting principles

  • Addition rule: add mutually exclusive choices. Product rule: multiply successive choice counts. Check whether choices remain constant after earlier decisions.
  • For n distinct objects, $n!=n(n-1)\cdots1$, with $0!=1$. Ordered r-selections without replacement: $P(n,r)=n!/(n-r)!$. Unordered selections: $\binom nr=n!/[r!(n-r)!]$.
  • Worked comparison: choose captain and deputy from 8 people: 8 × 7 = 56; choose a two-person committee: $\binom82=28$. Order doubles the first count.

Repetition and indistinguishable objects

  • A length-r code from n symbols with repetition has nʳ possibilities. If repetition is forbidden, use falling products. Leading-zero restrictions must be handled at the first position.
  • Permuting n objects with repeats n₁,…,nₖ gives $n!/(n_1!\cdots n_k!)$. Worked word LEVEL has 5 letters with two Ls and two Es: 5!/(2! 2!) = 30 distinct arrangements.
  • Circular arrangements of n distinct objects have (n−1)! when rotations are identical and reflections are distinct. Fix one object’s position before arranging the rest.

Restrictions and complementary counting

  • For adjacent objects treat the group as one block, then multiply by its internal arrangements. For separated objects arrange the others first and choose gaps.
  • Worked: five people in a row, A and B together: 4! × 2 = 48. Not together: 5! − 48 = 72. These cases partition all arrangements.
  • At least one success is often total minus none. For three people chosen from four seniors and five juniors: at least one senior $\binom93-\binom53=74$.

Counting for probability and binomial coefficients

  • With equally likely unordered samples use favourable selections/total selections. Exactly two reds when drawing 3 from 5 red and 4 blue is $\binom52\binom41/\binom93=10/21$.
  • In $(a+b)^n$, choosing r of the n factors to supply b explains the coefficient $\binom nr$. Do not apply equally likely counting to unequal-probability outcomes.
  • Check that $0\le r\le n$ and whether replacement is allowed. Enumerating small cases is a useful check; it is not a substitute for a justified count.

Independent practice

Attempt each question before opening its solution.

Exercise 1

A four-digit code uses digits 0–9 with repetition. How many codes? How many four-digit numbers?

Codes: 10⁴ = 10,000. Numbers require a nonzero first digit: 9 × 10³ = 9,000.

Exercise 2

How many ordered podiums from 7 competitors?

7×6×5=210; the three roles differ and nobody repeats.

Exercise 3

How many four-person committees from 10 people?

$\binom{10}4=210$. The same numerical result as a different problem does not make the methods interchangeable.

Exercise 4

Count distinct arrangements of BANANA.

$6!/(3!2!)=60$: three As and two Ns.

Exercise 5

Six distinct people sit around a circular table. Count arrangements with rotations identical, reflections distinct.

Fix one person: $5!=120$. If labelled seats are distinct instead, use $6!=720$.

Exercise 6

Four boys and three girls form a row with no two girls adjacent. Count arrangements.

Arrange the boys first: $4!$. Choose 3 of the 5 gaps, then arrange the girls: $4!\binom{5}{3}3!=1440$.

Exercise 7

Draw 4 cards from a 52-card deck without replacement. Find the probability of exactly 2 aces.

$\binom42\binom{48}2/\binom{52}4$. Choosing positions or ordering the entire hand is unnecessary.

Exercise 8

Choose 3 people from 5 seniors and 4 juniors, with at least 1 of each. Count the committees.

$\binom93-\binom53-\binom43=84-10-4=70$, Alternatively, $5\times6+10\times4=70$.

Counting formulas

  • Addition rule: add counts for mutually exclusive cases. Product rule: multiply the counts for successive choices.
  • Factorial: $n!=n(n-1)\cdots1$, with $0!=1$.
  • Ordered selections without replacement: $P(n,r)=\frac{n!}{(n-r)!}$, for $0\le r\le n$.
  • Unordered selections: $\binom{n}{r}=\frac{n!}{r!(n-r)!}$.
  • Arrangements with repeated objects: $\frac{n!}{n_1!\cdots n_k!}$, where $n_1+\cdots+n_k=n$.
  • Length-$r$ codes with repetition: $n^r$. Circular arrangements up to rotation: $(n-1)!$.
  • At least one: count all outcomes, then subtract the cases with none.

Method and answer checklist

  • Choose the method from the assumptions, not from a keyword.
  • Record variable definitions, domain restrictions and units.
  • Keep exact or unrounded intermediate values; check each result against the original question.
  • Use each formula with its stated conditions. Distinguish an algebraic result from a justified contextual conclusion.
