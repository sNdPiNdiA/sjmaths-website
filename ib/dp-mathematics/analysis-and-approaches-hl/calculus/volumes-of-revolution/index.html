Volumes of Revolution (x and y Axes)

Calculate volumes of solids generated by revolving regions $2\pi$ radians about coordinate axes: $V = \pi \int [f(x)]^2 dx$ around the $x$-axis, $V = \pi \int [g(y)]^2 dy$ around the $y$-axis, and the washer method $V = \pi \int (f^2 - g^2) dx$.

Syllabus AHL 5.11 & 5.14 Paper 1 & Paper 2 Skill 4 Interactive Tabs 10 Tiered Problems + Mini Test

Figure 5.5: Cylindrical Disk Slicing for Volume of Revolution about the x-axis

1. Revolution About the x-axis (Topic 5.11)

When a plane region bounded by $y = f(x)$, the $x$-axis, and vertical lines $x = a$ and $x = b$ is rotated through $360^\circ$ ($2\pi$ radians) about the $x$-axis, slicing perpendicular to the axis of rotation produces thin cylindrical disks with radius $r = f(x)$ and thickness $dx$:

$$dV = \pi r^2 \, dx = \pi [f(x)]^2 \, dx$$

Integrating all circular cross-sections from $x = a$ to $x = b$ gives:

$$V_x = \pi \int_a^b [f(x)]^2 \, dx = \pi \int_a^b y^2 \, dx$$

2. Revolution About the y-axis (Topic 5.14 HL)

When a region bounded by $x = g(y)$, the $y$-axis, and horizontal lines $y = c$ and $y = d$ is rotated $360^\circ$ about the $y$-axis, the circular disks have radius $r = g(y) = x$ and thickness $dy$:

$$V_y = \pi \int_c^d [g(y)]^2 \, dy = \pi \int_c^d x^2 \, dy$$
Examiner Tip for y-axis Rotations: Always rearrange the equation to make $x$ the subject in terms of $y$, and remember that the limits of integration must be $y$-coordinates ($y = c$ to $y = d$).

3. Washer Method: Region Between Two Curves

When the rotated region is bounded between an outer curve $y_{\text{outer}} = f(x)$ and an inner curve $y_{\text{inner}} = g(x)$:

$$V = \pi \int_a^b \left( [f(x)]^2 - [g(x)]^2 \right) dx$$
Fatal Examination Error: Never calculate $[f(x) - g(x)]^2$! The area of a washer ring is $\pi R^2 - \pi r^2 = \pi (R^2 - r^2)$, which is the difference of squares, not the square of the difference!

Practice Exercises (10 Tiered Questions)

Core Algorithmic Exercise 1

Find the volume of the solid generated when the curve $y = \sqrt{x}$ from $x = 0$ to $x = 4$ is revolved $360^\circ$ about the $x$-axis.

View Full Algebraic Solution

Step 1: Formula for rotation about the $x$-axis:

$$V = \pi \int_0^4 y^2\,dx$$

Step 2: Substitute $y^2 = (\sqrt{x})^2 = x$:

$$V = \pi \int_0^4 x\,dx = \pi \left[ \frac{1}{2}x^2 \right]_0^4 = \pi \left( \frac{16}{2} - 0 \right) = 8\pi$$
Core Algorithmic Exercise 2

Find the volume of the solid generated by revolving the region bounded by $y = e^x$, the lines $x = 0$, $x = 2$, and the $x$-axis about the $x$-axis.

View Full Algebraic Solution

Step 1: Set up the integral:

$$V = \pi \int_0^2 (e^x)^2\,dx = \pi \int_0^2 e^{2x}\,dx$$

Step 2: Evaluate:

$$V = \pi \left[ \frac{1}{2}e^{2x} \right]_0^2 = \frac{\pi}{2}(e^4 - e^0) = \frac{\pi}{2}(e^4 - 1)$$
Core Algorithmic Exercise 3

The region enclosed by $y = x^2$, the $y$-axis, and the line $y = 4$ is revolved $360^\circ$ about the $y$-axis. Calculate the volume.

View Full Algebraic Solution

Step 1: Express $x$ in terms of $y$:

Since $y = x^2$ in the first quadrant, $x^2 = y$.

Step 2: Set up the integral with respect to $y$:

$$V = \pi \int_0^4 x^2\,dy = \pi \int_0^4 y\,dy$$

Step 3: Compute the volume:

$$V = \pi \left[ \frac{1}{2}y^2 \right]_0^4 = \pi \left( \frac{16}{2} \right) = 8\pi$$
Standard Exam Exercise 4

Find the volume formed when the region bounded by $y = \sin x$ from $x = 0$ to $x = \pi$ and the $x$-axis is rotated $360^\circ$ about the $x$-axis.

View Full Algebraic Solution

Step 1: Set up the integral:

$$V = \pi \int_0^\pi \sin^2 x\,dx$$

Step 2: Use the double angle identity $\sin^2 x = \frac{1 - \cos 2x}{2}$:

$$V = \pi \int_0^\pi \frac{1 - \cos 2x}{2}\,dx = \frac{\pi}{2} \left[ x - \frac{1}{2}\sin 2x \right]_0^\pi$$

Step 3: Evaluate limits:

$$V = \frac{\pi}{2} \left( \pi - \frac{1}{2}\sin(2\pi) - (0 - 0) \right) = \frac{\pi}{2}(\pi - 0) = \frac{\pi^2}{2}$$
Standard Exam Exercise 5

The region between $y = x$ and $y = x^2$ is revolved $360^\circ$ about the $x$-axis. Find the volume of the resulting solid.

View Full Algebraic Solution

Step 1: Find points of intersection:

$$x = x^2 \implies x(x - 1) = 0 \implies x = 0 \text{ and } x = 1$$

On $[0, 1]$, $x \ge x^2$, so $y_{\text{outer}} = x$ and $y_{\text{inner}} = x^2$.

Step 2: Apply the washer method:

$$V = \pi \int_0^1 \left( [x]^2 - [x^2]^2 \right) dx = \pi \int_0^1 (x^2 - x^4)\,dx$$

Step 3: Integrate:

$$V = \pi \left[ \frac{1}{3}x^3 - \frac{1}{5}x^5 \right]_0^1 = \pi \left( \frac{1}{3} - \frac{1}{5} \right) = \pi \left( \frac{2}{15} \right) = \frac{2\pi}{15}$$
Standard Exam Exercise 6

Find the volume generated when the curve $y = \ln x$, from $y = 0$ to $y = 2$, is rotated $360^\circ$ about the $y$-axis.

View Full Algebraic Solution

Step 1: Express $x$ as a function of $y$:

$$y = \ln x \implies x = e^y$$

Step 2: Set up the integral with respect to $y$:

$$V = \pi \int_0^2 x^2\,dy = \pi \int_0^2 (e^y)^2\,dy = \pi \int_0^2 e^{2y}\,dy$$

Step 3: Evaluate:

$$V = \pi \left[ \frac{1}{2}e^{2y} \right]_0^2 = \frac{\pi}{2}(e^4 - 1)$$
Extended Problem Exercise 7

The region enclosed by $y = \frac{1}{\sqrt{x^2 + 1}}$, the $x$-axis, $x = 0$, and $x = 1$ is rotated $360^\circ$ about the $x$-axis. Find the volume.

View Full Algebraic Solution

Step 1: Set up the volume integral:

$$V = \pi \int_0^1 y^2\,dx = \pi \int_0^1 \frac{1}{x^2 + 1}\,dx$$

Step 2: Integrate using standard arctan derivative:

$$V = \pi [\arctan x]_0^1 = \pi (\arctan(1) - \arctan(0)) = \pi \left( \frac{\pi}{4} - 0 \right) = \frac{\pi^2}{4}$$
Extended Problem Exercise 8

Find the volume generated by rotating the region enclosed by $y = x^2$ and $y = 4$ about the line $y = 4$.

View Full Algebraic Solution

Step 1: Understand the shifted axis of rotation:

The line of rotation is the horizontal line $y = 4$. The distance from a point on the curve $(x, x^2)$ to the line $y = 4$ is the radius:

$$r(x) = 4 - x^2$$

Step 2: Determine limits:

Intersection points are $x^2 = 4 \implies x = -2$ to $x = 2$.

Step 3: Set up and evaluate integral:

$$V = \pi \int_{-2}^2 (4 - x^2)^2\,dx = 2\pi \int_0^2 (16 - 8x^2 + x^4)\,dx$$ $$V = 2\pi \left[ 16x - \frac{8}{3}x^3 + \frac{1}{5}x^5 \right]_0^2 = 2\pi \left( 32 - \frac{64}{3} + \frac{32}{5} \right)$$

Finding common denominator $15$:

$$32 - \frac{64}{3} + \frac{32}{5} = \frac{480 - 320 + 96}{15} = \frac{256}{15}$$ $$V = 2\pi \times \frac{256}{15} = \frac{512\pi}{15}$$
HL Distinction Exercise 9

A glass goblet is designed by revolving the curve $y = 2 + \sqrt{x}$ from $x = 0$ to $x = 9$ about the $x$-axis, where all coordinates are in centimetres.
(a) Find the total volume of the solid in $\text{cm}^3$.
(b) If water is poured into the goblet at a constant rate of $10\text{ cm}^3/\text{s}$, find the rate of change of the water level $h$ when $h = 4\text{ cm}$.

View Full Algebraic Solution

(a) Volume of the solid:

$$V = \pi \int_0^9 (2 + \sqrt{x})^2\,dx = \pi \int_0^9 (4 + 4\sqrt{x} + x)\,dx$$ $$V = \pi \left[ 4x + 4\left(\frac{2}{3}x^{3/2}\right) + \frac{1}{2}x^2 \right]_0^9 = \pi \left[ 4(9) + \frac{8}{3}(27) + \frac{81}{2} \right]$$ $$V = \pi \left[ 36 + 72 + 40.5 \right] = 148.5\pi\text{ cm}^3 = \frac{297\pi}{2}\text{ cm}^3$$

(b) Related rates for depth $h$:

The volume as a function of depth $h$ is $V(h) = \pi \int_0^h (2 + \sqrt{x})^2 dx$.

By the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus: $\frac{dV}{dh} = \pi (2 + \sqrt{h})^2$.

Using the chain rule: $\frac{dV}{dt} = \frac{dV}{dh} \cdot \frac{dh}{dt}$.

At $h = 4$: $\frac{dV}{dh} = \pi (2 + \sqrt{4})^2 = \pi (2 + 2)^2 = 16\pi$.

$$10 = 16\pi \cdot \frac{dh}{dt} \implies \frac{dh}{dt} = \frac{10}{16\pi} = \frac{5}{8\pi}\text{ cm/s} \approx 0.199\text{ cm/s}$$
HL Distinction Exercise 10

A circle of radius $r$ centered at $(R, 0)$ where $R > r$ has equation $(x - R)^2 + y^2 = r^2$. Rotating this circle $360^\circ$ about the $y$-axis generates a solid ring called a torus. Prove using calculus that the volume of the torus is $V = 2\pi^2 R r^2$.

View Full Algebraic Solution

Step 1: Express curves as $x$ in terms of $y$:

$$(x - R)^2 = r^2 - y^2 \implies x = R \pm \sqrt{r^2 - y^2}$$

Outer radius: $x_2 = R + \sqrt{r^2 - y^2}$

Inner radius: $x_1 = R - \sqrt{r^2 - y^2}$

The limits in $y$ are from $-r$ to $+r$.

Step 2: Set up washer method for rotation about $y$-axis:

$$V = \pi \int_{-r}^r (x_2^2 - x_1^2)\,dy$$

Notice the difference of squares factorization:

$$x_2^2 - x_1^2 = (x_2 - x_1)(x_2 + x_1)$$ $$x_2 - x_1 = 2\sqrt{r^2 - y^2}, \quad x_2 + x_1 = 2R$$ $$(x_2^2 - x_1^2) = (2\sqrt{r^2 - y^2})(2R) = 4R\sqrt{r^2 - y^2}$$

Step 3: Integrate:

$$V = \pi \int_{-r}^r 4R\sqrt{r^2 - y^2}\,dy = 4\pi R \int_{-r}^r \sqrt{r^2 - y^2}\,dy$$

Notice that $\int_{-r}^r \sqrt{r^2 - y^2} dy$ is geometrically the area of a semicircle of radius $r$, which is $\frac{1}{2}\pi r^2$:

$$V = 4\pi R \left( \frac{1}{2}\pi r^2 \right) = 2\pi^2 R r^2$$

This matches the famous Pappus Centroid Theorem result ($V = 2\pi R \cdot \pi r^2$).

Common Errors & Exam Tips

Pitfall: Forgetting $\pi$ in Volume Integrals

Because students are accustomed to finding 2D area $\int y dx$, they frequently omit the leading multiplier $\pi$ when finding volumes of revolution. Always check the $\pi$ symbol first!

Pitfall: Limits in y-axis Rotation

When revolving about the $y$-axis, the integration variable is $y$. You must convert given $x$-limits to corresponding $y$-limits using $y = f(x)$ before setting up $\int_c^d x^2 dy$.

Section B: Examination-Style Free Response Questions

Examination Paper 1/2 Question 5 (FRQ) · Dual Axis Comparison

Let $R$ be the region enclosed by $y = e^x$, the line $x = 1$, and both coordinate axes.
(a) Find the volume $V_x$ when $R$ is revolved $360^\circ$ about the $x$-axis. [3 marks]
(b) Find the volume $V_y$ when $R$ is revolved $360^\circ$ about the $y$-axis. [4 marks]

View Official IB Markscheme

(a) Volume around $x$-axis:

$$V_x = \pi \int_0^1 (e^x)^2 dx = \pi \int_0^1 e^{2x} dx \quad \text{[M1]}$$ $$V_x = \pi \left[ \frac{1}{2}e^{2x} \right]_0^1 = \frac{\pi}{2}(e^2 - 1) \quad \text{[A1][A1]}$$

(b) Volume around $y$-axis:

The region spans $y$ from $0$ to $e$. From $y=0$ to $y=1$, it is a cylinder of radius $x=1$. From $y=1$ to $y=e$, the curve bounds it with outer radius $1$ and inner radius $x = \ln y$ [M1]:

$$V_y = \pi \int_0^1 (1)^2 dy + \pi \int_1^e \left( 1^2 - (\ln y)^2 \right) dy \quad \text{[M1]}$$ $$V_1 = \pi(1) = \pi$$

Using $\int (\ln y)^2 dy = y(\ln y)^2 - 2y\ln y + 2y$ [M1]:

$$\int_1^e (\ln y)^2 dy = [e(1) - 2e(1) + 2e] - [0 - 0 + 2] = e - 2$$ $$\pi \int_1^e (1 - (\ln y)^2) dy = \pi [ (e - 1) - (e - 2) ] = \pi(1) = \pi$$ $$V_y = \pi + \pi = 2\pi \quad \text{[A1]}$$
Examination Paper 1/2 Question 6 (FRQ) · Architectural Dome Model

An architectural dome has the shape of a solid formed by rotating $y = 6 - \frac{x^2}{6}$ about the $y$-axis, where $x, y$ are measured in metres and $y \ge 0$.
(a) Find the maximum height and the ground radius of the dome. [2 marks]
(b) Calculate the enclosed volume of the dome in $\text{m}^3$. [4 marks]

View Official IB Markscheme

(a) Dimensions:

At $x=0$, maximum height is $y = 6\text{ m}$ [A1].

At ground level $y=0$: $6 - \frac{x^2}{6} = 0 \implies x^2 = 36 \implies x = 6\text{ m}$ ground radius [A1].

(b) Volume calculation:

Express $x^2$ in terms of $y$:

$$\frac{x^2}{6} = 6 - y \implies x^2 = 6(6 - y) = 36 - 6y \quad \text{[M1]}$$

Integrate with respect to $y$ from $y = 0$ to $y = 6$ [M1]:

$$V = \pi \int_0^6 x^2 dy = \pi \int_0^6 (36 - 6y) dy \quad \text{[A1]}$$ $$V = \pi \left[ 36y - 3y^2 \right]_0^6 = \pi [ 36(6) - 3(36) ] = \pi [ 216 - 108 ] = 108\pi\text{ m}^3 \quad \text{[A1]}$$ $$\approx 339.3\text{ m}^3$$
