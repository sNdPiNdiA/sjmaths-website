Discrete Random Variables and Binomial Distribution

A discrete random variable assigns probabilities to countable outcomes. A binomial distribution is appropriate only when its repeated-trial assumptions hold.

Learn the ideas

Key ideas and methods

  • Probabilities in a discrete distribution sum to 1. The expectation is E(X)=ΣxP(X=x), a long-run average.
  • A binomial model has a fixed number n of independent trials, two outcomes per trial, and constant success probability p.
  • For X~B(n,p), P(X=k)=ⁿCₖpᵏ(1−p)ⁿ⁻ᵏ; the mean is np and variance is np(1−p).

Follow the reasoning

Worked example

This original example shows the method step by step; it is not copied from an IB examination paper.

Worked example 1

Find an exact binomial probability

Question. For X~B(4,0.25), find P(X=2) and the expected value.

  1. P(X=2)=⁴C₂(0.25)²(0.75)².
  2. This is 6×0.0625×0.5625=0.2109375.
  3. E(X)=np=4(0.25)=1.

Answer: P(X=2)=0.2109375; E(X)=1.

Try it yourself

Practice with worked answers

  1. For X~B(6,0.5), find E(X).

    Show worked answer

    E(X)=np=6(0.5)=3.

  2. State why a binomial model may fail for sampling without replacement from a very small group.

    Show worked answer

    Trials are dependent and the success probability changes after each selection.

Avoid these slips

Common mistakes

  • Using nCk with k outside 0≤k≤n.
  • Forgetting the failure factor (1−p)ⁿ⁻ᵏ.
  • Using a binomial model without checking independence and constant p.
