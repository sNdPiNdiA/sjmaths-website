Probability and Conditional Events

Structure complex event spaces using Venn and tree diagrams, calculate conditional probabilities, apply the addition rule, and rigorously test event independence.

Syllabus SL 4.5 & 4.6 Paper 1 & Paper 2 Topic 4: Statistics & Probability

SL 4.5 · Fundamentals

1. Probability Axioms and the Addition Rule

Probability measures the likelihood of an event $A$ occurring within a sample space $U$. Probabilities satisfy $0 \le P(A) \le 1$ with $P(U) = 1$ and $P(\emptyset) = 0$.

  • Complementary Events: $P(A') = 1 - P(A)$.
  • The General Addition Rule (Combined Events): $$P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \cap B)$$ Subtracting the intersection $P(A \cap B)$ prevents double-counting outcomes that belong to both events.
  • Mutually Exclusive Events: Two events cannot occur simultaneously ($A \cap B = \emptyset \implies P(A \cap B) = 0$): $$P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B)$$

Worked Example 1.1 · Applying the Addition Rule

Given $P(A) = 0.6$, $P(B) = 0.5$, and $P(A \cup B) = 0.85$, determine: (a) $P(A \cap B)$, (b) $P(A \cap B')$, and (c) whether $A$ and $B$ are mutually exclusive.

  1. (a) Rearrange the addition rule: $$P(A \cap B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \cup B) = 0.6 + 0.5 - 0.85 = 0.25$$
  2. (b) $P(A \cap B')$ represents outcomes in $A$ but not in $B$: $$P(A \cap B') = P(A) - P(A \cap B) = 0.6 - 0.25 = 0.35$$
  3. (c) Since $P(A \cap B) = 0.25 \neq 0$, the events can occur simultaneously; therefore, $A$ and $B$ are not mutually exclusive.

Answer: (a) $0.25$; (b) $0.35$; (c) Not mutually exclusive because $P(A \cap B) = 0.25 \neq 0$.

SL 4.6 · Conditional Logic

2. Conditional Probability and Reduced Sample Spaces

Conditional probability $P(A|B)$ is the probability that event $A$ occurs given that event $B$ has already occurred. It restricts the universal set directly to event $B$.

  • Conditional Probability Formula: $$P(A|B) = \frac{P(A \cap B)}{P(B)}, \quad \text{provided } P(B) > 0$$ Similarly, $P(B|A) = \frac{P(A \cap B)}{P(A)}$.
  • Multiplication Rule: $$P(A \cap B) = P(B) \times P(A|B) = P(A) \times P(B|A)$$

Worked Example 1.2 · Contingency Table Conditional Query

A survey of 100 students records their preference for studying online or in person:

OnlineIn PersonTotal
Grade 11203050
Grade 12351550
Total5545100

A student is selected at random. Calculate the probability that: (a) the student prefers Online given they are in Grade 12, (b) the student is in Grade 12 given they prefer Online.

  1. (a) Restricted to Grade 12 (denominator $= 50$): $$P(\text{Online}|\text{Grade 12}) = \frac{35}{50} = 0.70$$
  2. (b) Restricted to Online (denominator $= 55$): $$P(\text{Grade 12}|\text{Online}) = \frac{35}{55} = \frac{7}{11} \approx 0.636$$

Answer: (a) $0.70$; (b) $\frac{7}{11} \approx 0.636$.

SL 4.6 · Independence

3. Independent Events and Probability Diagrams

Two events $A$ and $B$ are independent if the occurrence of one does not affect the probability of the other.

  • Formal Mathematical Tests for Independence (Any ONE suffices): $$P(A \cap B) = P(A) \times P(B)$$ $$P(A|B) = P(A) \quad \text{or} \quad P(B|A) = P(B)$$
  • Warning: Never assume independence just because two events sound unrelated. In IB exams, you must rigorously demonstrate that $P(A \cap B) = P(A)P(B)$ numerically!
  • Tree Diagrams: Used for sequential multi-stage experiments. Multiply along branches to find joint probabilities ($P(\text{path}) = P(B_1) \times P(B_2|B_1)$); add vertical terminal branch products to find combined event probabilities.

Topic 4.5 & 4.6 Practice Exercises

10 tiered problems covering addition rules, conditional probability $P(A|B)$, independence tests, Venn diagrams, and tree diagrams.

Tier 1 · Question 1 (Core Fluency)

Given $P(A) = 0.7$, $P(B) = 0.4$, and $P(A \cap B) = 0.28$.
(a) Calculate $P(A \cup B)$.
(b) Determine whether events $A$ and $B$ are independent.

Show Step-by-Step Solution

Part (a): Addition rule:

$$P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \cap B) = 0.7 + 0.4 - 0.28 = 0.82$$

Part (b): Test for independence:

$$P(A) \times P(B) = 0.7 \times 0.4 = 0.28$$

Since $P(A \cap B) = P(A) \times P(B) = 0.28$, events $A$ and $B$ are independent.

Final Answer: (a) $0.82$; (b) Yes, independent because $P(A \cap B) = P(A)P(B) = 0.28$.

Tier 1 · Question 2 (Core Fluency)

Events $C$ and $D$ satisfy $P(C) = 0.5$ and $P(C \cap D) = 0.15$. Calculate $P(D|C)$.

Show Step-by-Step Solution

Step 1: Apply conditional probability formula:

$$P(D|C) = \frac{P(C \cap D)}{P(C)}$$

Step 2: Substitute values:

$$P(D|C) = \frac{0.15}{0.50} = 0.30$$

Final Answer: $0.30$

Tier 1 · Question 3 (Core Fluency)

A bag contains 5 red marbles and 3 blue marbles. Two marbles are drawn at random without replacement. Calculate the probability that both marbles are red.

Show Step-by-Step Solution

Step 1: First draw: Total marbles $= 8$. $P(R_1) = \frac{5}{8}$.

Step 2: Second draw (without replacement): Remaining $= 7$, red remaining $= 4$. $P(R_2|R_1) = \frac{4}{7}$.

Step 3: Multiplication rule:

$$P(R_1 \cap R_2) = \frac{5}{8} \times \frac{4}{7} = \frac{20}{56} = \frac{5}{14} \approx 0.357$$

Final Answer: $\frac{5}{14}$

Tier 1 · Question 4 (Core Fluency)

Events $E$ and $F$ are mutually exclusive with $P(E) = 0.35$ and $P(F) = 0.45$. Find $P(E \cap F)$ and $P(E \cup F)$.

Show Step-by-Step Solution

Part (a): By definition of mutually exclusive events, $P(E \cap F) = 0$.

Part (b): For mutually exclusive events:

$$P(E \cup F) = P(E) + P(F) = 0.35 + 0.45 = 0.80$$

Final Answer: $P(E \cap F) = 0$; $P(E \cup F) = 0.80$.

Tier 2 · Question 5 (Exam Modeling)

In a Venn diagram of two events $A$ and $B$, $P(A \cap B') = 0.3$, $P(A \cap B) = x$, $P(A' \cap B) = 0.2$, and $P(A' \cap B') = 0.25$.
(a) Find the value of $x$.
(b) Calculate $P(A|B)$.
(c) State whether $A$ and $B$ are independent, justifying your answer.

Show Step-by-Step Solution

Part (a): Total probability axiom:
All four disjoint regions in the universal set must sum to 1: $$0.3 + x + 0.2 + 0.25 = 1 \implies x + 0.75 = 1 \implies x = 0.25$$

Part (b): Calculate $P(A|B)$:
$P(B) = P(A \cap B) + P(A' \cap B) = 0.25 + 0.2 = 0.45$. $$P(A|B) = \frac{P(A \cap B)}{P(B)} = \frac{0.25}{0.45} = \frac{5}{9} \approx 0.556$$

Part (c): Independence test:
$P(A) = P(A \cap B') + P(A \cap B) = 0.3 + 0.25 = 0.55$.
Compare $P(A|B) = \frac{5}{9} \approx 0.5556$ with $P(A) = 0.5500$.
Alternatively: $P(A) \times P(B) = 0.55 \times 0.45 = 0.2475 \neq 0.25 = P(A \cap B)$.
Since $P(A \cap B) \neq P(A)P(B)$, events $A$ and $B$ are not independent.

Final Answer: (a) $x = 0.25$; (b) $\frac{5}{9}$; (c) Not independent because $P(A \cap B) = 0.25 \neq 0.2475 = P(A)P(B)$.

Tier 2 · Question 6 (Exam Modeling)

A weather forecast states that the probability of rain on Saturday is $0.6$. If it rains on Saturday, the probability of rain on Sunday is $0.7$. If it does not rain on Saturday, the probability of rain on Sunday is $0.3$.
(a) Draw or represent the probabilities using a tree diagram.
(b) Find the probability that it rains on at least one of the two days.
(c) Given that it rains on Sunday, find the probability that it rained on Saturday.

Show Step-by-Step Solution

Part (a) Branches:
• Saturday Rain ($R_1$): $0.6$
    → Sunday Rain ($R_2|R_1$): $0.7 \implies P(R_1 \cap R_2) = 0.6 \times 0.7 = 0.42$
    → Sunday Dry ($R_2'|R_1$): $0.3 \implies P(R_1 \cap R_2') = 0.6 \times 0.3 = 0.18$
• Saturday Dry ($R_1'$): $0.4$
    → Sunday Rain ($R_2|R_1'$): $0.3 \implies P(R_1' \cap R_2) = 0.4 \times 0.3 = 0.12$
    → Sunday Dry ($R_2'|R_1'$): $0.7 \implies P(R_1' \cap R_2') = 0.4 \times 0.7 = 0.28$

Part (b): Rains on at least one day:
$$P(\text{At least one}) = 1 - P(\text{Dry both}) = 1 - 0.28 = 0.72$$

Part (c): Conditional $P(R_1|R_2)$:
Total probability of rain on Sunday: $$P(R_2) = P(R_1 \cap R_2) + P(R_1' \cap R_2) = 0.42 + 0.12 = 0.54$$ $$P(R_1|R_2) = \frac{P(R_1 \cap R_2)}{P(R_2)} = \frac{0.42}{0.54} = \frac{7}{9} \approx 0.778$$

Final Answer: (b) $0.72$; (c) $\frac{7}{9} \approx 0.778$.

Tier 2 · Question 7 (Exam Modeling)

Two independent events $A$ and $B$ have $P(A) = 2x$, $P(B) = x + 0.1$, and $P(A \cap B) = 0.12$.
(a) Form a quadratic equation in terms of $x$ and show that it simplifies to $2x^2 + 0.2x - 0.12 = 0$.
(b) Find the valid value of $x$.
(c) Hence, calculate $P(A \cup B)$.

Show Step-by-Step Solution

Part (a): Set up equation:
Since $A$ and $B$ are independent, $P(A \cap B) = P(A) \times P(B)$: $$2x(x + 0.1) = 0.12 \implies 2x^2 + 0.2x - 0.12 = 0 \quad \blacksquare$$

Part (b): Solve the quadratic:
Multiply by 50 to clear decimals: $100x^2 + 10x - 6 = 0 \implies 50x^2 + 5x - 3 = 0$.
Factor or use quadratic formula: $$x = \frac{-0.2 \pm \sqrt{0.2^2 - 4(2)(-0.12)}}{2(2)} = \frac{-0.2 \pm \sqrt{0.04 + 0.96}}{4} = \frac{-0.2 \pm \sqrt{1.0}}{4} = \frac{-0.2 \pm 1}{4}$$ Positive root: $x = \frac{0.8}{4} = 0.2$. (Negative root $x = -0.3$ is rejected because probability cannot be negative).

Part (c): Calculate $P(A \cup B)$:
$P(A) = 2(0.2) = 0.4$, $P(B) = 0.2 + 0.1 = 0.3$. $$P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \cap B) = 0.4 + 0.3 - 0.12 = 0.58$$

Final Answer: (b) $x = 0.2$; (c) $P(A \cup B) = 0.58$.

Tier 2 · Question 8 (Exam Modeling)

Can two non-empty events with non-zero probabilities be simultaneously mutually exclusive and independent? Justify your answer using mathematical definitions.

Show Step-by-Step Solution

Mathematical Proof:
Let $P(A) > 0$ and $P(B) > 0$.
1. If $A$ and $B$ are mutually exclusive, then $P(A \cap B) = 0$.
2. If $A$ and $B$ are independent, then $P(A \cap B) = P(A) \times P(B)$.
3. For both conditions to hold simultaneously: $$P(A) \times P(B) = 0$$ This requires either $P(A) = 0$ or $P(B) = 0$, which contradicts the premise that both events have non-zero probabilities.
Conclusion: Two non-impossible events can NEVER be both mutually exclusive and independent.

Final Answer: No; if mutually exclusive, $P(A \cap B) = 0$, but if independent with non-zero probabilities, $P(A \cap B) = P(A)P(B) > 0$.

Tier 3 · Question 9 (Synthesis)

A medical test is designed to screen for a rare disease that affects $1\%$ of a population ($P(D) = 0.01$).
• If an individual has the disease, the test is positive with probability $0.98$ (true positive rate).
• If an individual does not have the disease, the test is positive with probability $0.05$ (false positive rate).
(a) Calculate the probability that a randomly chosen person tests positive.
(b) Given that an individual receives a positive test result, calculate the probability that they actually have the disease.
(c) Comment on why this result might be counter-intuitive to patients.

Show Step-by-Step Solution

Part (a): Total probability of positive test ($T^+$):
$P(D) = 0.01 \implies P(D') = 0.99$.
$$P(T^+|D) = 0.98 \implies P(D \cap T^+) = 0.01 \times 0.98 = 0.0098$$ $$P(T^+|D') = 0.05 \implies P(D' \cap T^+) = 0.99 \times 0.05 = 0.0495$$ $$P(T^+) = 0.0098 + 0.0495 = 0.0593$$

Part (b): Reverse conditional probability $P(D|T^+)$:
$$P(D|T^+) = \frac{P(D \cap T^+)}{P(T^+)} = \frac{0.0098}{0.0593} = \frac{98}{593} \approx 0.16526 \approx 16.5\%$$

Part (c): Reflection on counter-intuition:
Even though the test is $98\%$ sensitive, an individual with a positive test has only about a $16.5\%$ chance of actually having the disease. Because the disease is so rare ($1\%$), the absolute number of false positives ($0.0495$) significantly outnumbers the true positives ($0.0098$).

Final Answer: (a) $0.0593$; (b) $\frac{98}{593} \approx 0.165$ ($16.5\%$); (c) The false positive pool dwarfs the true positive pool due to the low disease base rate.

Tier 3 · Question 10 (Synthesis)

Box 1 contains 4 green balls and 2 yellow balls. Box 2 contains 3 green balls and 5 yellow balls. A fair six-sided die is rolled. If the score is 1 or 2, a ball is selected from Box 1. Otherwise, a ball is selected from Box 2.
(a) Find the probability that the ball drawn is green.
(b) Given that the ball drawn is green, find the probability that it was selected from Box 1.
(c) The ball drawn is green and is not replaced. A second ball is drawn from the same box. Find the probability that this second ball is also green.

Show Step-by-Step Solution

Part (a): Probability of green ball:
Die roll determines box:
$P(B_1) = P(\{1, 2\}) = \frac{2}{6} = \frac{1}{3}$.
$P(B_2) = P(\{3, 4, 5, 6\}) = \frac{4}{6} = \frac{2}{3}$.
In Box 1: $P(G|B_1) = \frac{4}{6} = \frac{2}{3}$.
In Box 2: $P(G|B_2) = \frac{3}{8}$.
$$P(G) = P(B_1 \cap G) + P(B_2 \cap G) = \left(\frac{1}{3} \times \frac{2}{3}\right) + \left(\frac{2}{3} \times \frac{3}{8}\right) = \frac{2}{9} + \frac{6}{24} = \frac{2}{9} + \frac{1}{4} = \frac{8 + 9}{36} = \frac{17}{36} \approx 0.472$$

Part (b): Conditional $P(B_1|G)$:
$$P(B_1|G) = \frac{P(B_1 \cap G)}{P(G)} = \frac{2/9}{17/36} = \frac{2}{9} \times \frac{36}{17} = \frac{8}{17} \approx 0.471$$

Part (c): Second green ball from same box:
Using conditional probabilities given which box was selected:
If from Box 1: Remaining balls $= 3$ green, $2$ yellow (total 5) $\implies P(G_2|B_1 \cap G_1) = \frac{3}{5}$.
If from Box 2: Remaining balls $= 2$ green, $5$ yellow (total 7) $\implies P(G_2|B_2 \cap G_1) = \frac{2}{7}$.
Using Bayes' theorem probabilities of the box given $G_1$ (from (b), $P(B_1|G_1) = \frac{8}{17}$ and $P(B_2|G_1) = \frac{9}{17}$):
$$P(G_2|G_1) = \left(\frac{8}{17} \times \frac{3}{5}\right) + \left(\frac{9}{17} \times \frac{2}{7}\right) = \frac{24}{85} + \frac{18}{119} = \frac{168 + 90}{595} = \frac{258}{595} \approx 0.4336 \approx 0.434$$

Final Answer: (a) $\frac{17}{36}$; (b) $\frac{8}{17}$; (c) $\frac{258}{595} \approx 0.434$.

IB Formula Booklet References (Topic 4)

Combined Events (Addition Rule):

$$P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \cap B)$$

Complementary Events:

$$P(A') = 1 - P(A)$$

Mutually Exclusive Events:

$$P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B)$$

Conditional Probability:

$$P(A|B) = \frac{P(A \cap B)}{P(B)}$$

Independent Events:

$$P(A \cap B) = P(A)P(B)$$

Must-Memorize Set Identities

De Morgan's Laws:

$$P((A \cup B)') = P(A' \cap B'), \quad P((A \cap B)') = P(A' \cup B')$$

Disjoint Partitions:

$$P(A) = P(A \cap B) + P(A \cap B'), \quad P(B) = P(A \cap B) + P(A' \cap B)$$

Critical IB Examination Pitfalls

  • Confusing Mutually Exclusive with Independent: Mutually exclusive means they cannot happen together ($P(A \cap B) = 0$). Independent means one doesn't affect the other ($P(A \cap B) = P(A)P(B)$). They are mutually contradictory for events with non-zero probability!
  • Reversing $P(A|B)$ and $P(B|A)$: The given condition is always in the denominator: $P(A|B) = \frac{P(A \cap B)}{P(B)}$. Reversing the condition changes the entire numerical fraction.
  • Without Replacement Reductions: When sampling items sequentially without replacement, both the numerator AND denominator of the second probability must be decremented.
  • Assuming Independence Without Proof: Never write "since the events are independent, $P(A \cap B) = P(A)P(B)$" unless the question explicitly declares it. If asked to determine whether they are independent, compute $P(A \cap B)$ and $P(A)P(B)$ separately and compare.

Question 5 (6 marks)

A group of 80 students is surveyed about studying Chemistry ($C$) and Biology ($B$).
• 45 study Chemistry
• 40 study Biology
• 15 study neither subject

(a) Find the number of students who study both subjects. [2 marks]
(b) A student is chosen at random. Find the probability that they study Chemistry given that they study Biology. [2 marks]
(c) Determine whether studying Chemistry and studying Biology are independent events for this group. [2 marks]

Show Full Markscheme

(a) Intersection:
Students studying at least one subject $= 80 - 15 = 65$. [M1]
$n(C \cap B) = n(C) + n(B) - n(C \cup B) = 45 + 40 - 65 = 20$. [A1]

(b) Conditional probability:
$P(C|B) = \frac{n(C \cap B)}{n(B)} = \frac{20}{40} = 0.5$. [M1 A1]

(c) Independence test:
$P(C) = \frac{45}{80} = 0.5625$.
Since $P(C|B) = 0.50 \neq 0.5625 = P(C)$, the events are not independent. [M1 R1]

Question 6 (7 marks)

Two ordinary six-sided dice are rolled. Event $A$ is "the sum of the scores is 7" and Event $B$ is "at least one die shows a 6".
(a) List the outcomes in event $A$ and calculate $P(A)$. [2 marks]
(b) Calculate $P(B)$ and $P(A \cap B)$. [2 marks]
(c) Show whether events $A$ and $B$ are independent. [3 marks]

Show Full Markscheme

(a) Event A:
Total sample space $= 6 \times 6 = 36$.
$A = \{(1, 6), (2, 5), (3, 4), (4, 3), (5, 2), (6, 1)\}$. [A1]
$P(A) = \frac{6}{36} = \frac{1}{6}$. [A1]

(b) Event B and intersection:
$B$ contains all pairs with at least one 6: $(6, 1), (6, 2), \dots, (6, 6)$ ($6$ pairs) plus $(1, 6), (2, 6), \dots, (5, 6)$ ($5$ pairs) $\implies n(B) = 11$.
$P(B) = \frac{11}{36}$. [A1]
$A \cap B = \{(1, 6), (6, 1)\} \implies n(A \cap B) = 2$.
$P(A \cap B) = \frac{2}{36} = \frac{1}{18}$. [A1]

(c) Independence verification:
Compute $P(A) \times P(B) = \frac{1}{6} \times \frac{11}{36} = \frac{11}{216}$. [M1]
Compare with $P(A \cap B) = \frac{1}{18} = \frac{12}{216}$.
Since $\frac{12}{216} \neq \frac{11}{216}$, $P(A \cap B) \neq P(A)P(B)$. [M1]
Therefore, events $A$ and $B$ are not independent. [R1]
