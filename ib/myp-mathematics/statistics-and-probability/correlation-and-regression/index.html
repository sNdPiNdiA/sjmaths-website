Correlation & Linear Regression

Model bivariate relationships and forecast trends. Analyze scatter plot patterns, quantify strength and direction with Pearson's correlation coefficient ($r$), anchor lines of best fit through the centroid $(\bar{x}, \bar{y})$, evaluate interpolation versus extrapolation, and distinguish correlation from causation.

1

Bivariate Data & Scatter Plot Interpretation

Bivariate data involves pairs of numerical observations $(x, y)$ measured on the same individual or subject to determine if a relationship exists.

Variable Roles:
  • Independent (Explanatory) Variable ($x$): Plotted on the horizontal axis. Represents the controlling or predictor factor.
  • Dependent (Response) Variable ($y$): Plotted on the vertical axis. Represents the outcome being measured.
Three Dimensions of Scatter Plot Interpretation:
  1. Direction:
    • Positive Correlation: As $x$ increases, $y$ tends to increase (slopes upward from bottom-left to top-right).
    • Negative Correlation: As $x$ increases, $y$ tends to decrease (slopes downward from top-left to bottom-right).
    • Zero Correlation: Random cloud of points with no discernable linear trend.
  2. Form: Linear (points cluster along a straight line) vs. Non-linear (points cluster along an exponential, quadratic, or cyclical curve).
  3. Strength: Strong (points tightly clustered close to the trend line), Moderate, or Weak (points loosely scattered around the trend).
2

Pearson's Product-Moment Correlation Coefficient ($r$)

Pearson's $r$ provides a rigorous numerical metric measuring the strength and direction of linear association between two quantitative variables.

The Scale of Pearson's $r$ ($-1 \le r \le 1$):
  • $r = +1.0$: Perfect positive linear correlation.
  • $+0.7 \le r < +1.0$: Strong positive linear correlation.
  • $+0.3 \le r < +0.7$: Moderate positive linear correlation.
  • $0 < r < +0.3$: Weak positive linear correlation.
  • $r = 0$: No linear correlation (data points are randomly scattered or related non-linearly).
  • $-0.3 < r < 0$: Weak negative linear correlation.
  • $-0.7 < r \le -0.3$: Moderate negative linear correlation.
  • $-1.0 < r \le -0.7$: Strong negative linear correlation.
  • $r = -1.0$: Perfect negative linear correlation.

Properties of $r$: $r$ is a dimensionless number with no units. Multiplying or adding constants to either variable does not alter $r$. $r$ detects only linear relationships; a strong curved parabolic relationship will yield an $r \approx 0$!

3

The Centroid Mean Point $(\bar{x}, \bar{y})$ & Line of Best Fit

A line of best fit models the linear trend, enabling estimations of $y$ from values of $x$.

Centroid Rule:
Every valid line of best fit MUST pass through the mean point (centroid) of the dataset: $$(\bar{x}, \bar{y}) = \left(\frac{\sum x}{n}, \; \frac{\sum y}{n}\right)$$
Linear Regression Equation ($y$ on $x$): $$y = mx + c \quad \text{or} \quad y = ax + b$$ where: $$m = r \left(\frac{s_y}{s_x}\right)$$ $$\text{y-intercept } c = \bar{y} - m\bar{x}$$

Prediction Reliability & Correlation vs. Causation

When utilizing regression models to forecast outcomes in real-world contexts (Criterion D), mathematicians must evaluate two fundamental risks.

1. Interpolation vs. Extrapolation:
  • Interpolation: Predicting values of $y$ for an $x$-value that lies within the range of the collected data ($x_{\min} \le x \le x_{\max}$).
    Reliability: Generally reliable and valid, especially when $|r|$ is strong.
  • Extrapolation: Predicting values of $y$ for an $x$-value that lies outside the range of collected data ($x < x_{\min}$ or $x > x_{\max}$).
    Reliability: Highly unreliable! We have no evidence that the linear relationship continues beyond the observed data range (e.g. human height vs age does not continue into adulthood).
2. Correlation Does NOT Imply Causation:
A strong correlation ($r \approx 1$) between variable $X$ and variable $Y$ does NOT prove that $X$ causes $Y$.
  • Lurking (Confounding) Variables: A third unmeasured variable drives both (e.g. sales of ice cream and rates of sunburn correlate strongly because warm sunny weather drives both).
  • Spurious Correlation: Purely coincidental mathematical association without real causal link.

Worked Example 1: Line of Best Fit via Mean Point

A physics student measures the force $x$ (in Newtons) applied to a spring and the resulting extension $y$ (in mm). The summary statistics for 8 data points are: $$\sum x = 40, \quad \sum y = 96, \quad \sum x^2 = 240, \quad \sum y^2 = 1200, \quad \sum xy = 530$$
a) Calculate the coordinates of the mean point $(\bar{x}, \bar{y})$.
b) If the gradient of the line of best fit is $m = 2.15$, find the equation of the regression line.
c) Estimate the extension of the spring under a force of $6\text{ N}$ and state whether this is interpolation or extrapolation.

Step-by-step Solution:

Part a: Mean point coordinates:
$\bar{x} = \frac{\sum x}{n} = \frac{40}{8} = 5\text{ N}$.
$\bar{y} = \frac{\sum y}{n} = \frac{96}{8} = 12\text{ mm}$.
Mean point: $(\bar{x}, \bar{y}) = (5, 12)$. [A1]

Part b: Equation of regression line:
The line passes through $(5, 12)$ with slope $m = 2.15$: [M1]
$y - \bar{y} = m(x - \bar{x}) \implies y - 12 = 2.15(x - 5)$
$y - 12 = 2.15x - 10.75 \implies y = 2.15x + 1.25$. [A1]

Part c: Prediction for $x = 6\text{ N}$:
$y = 2.15(6) + 1.25 = 12.9 + 1.25 = 14.15\text{ mm}$. [A1]
Since forces ranged from $1$ to $8\text{ N}$, $x = 6\text{ N}$ lies comfortably inside the data range. Therefore, this prediction is an interpolation and is considered reliable. [Criterion C & D: Communication & Context]

Worked Example 2: Extrapolation Critique & Causation Analysis

A biologist models tree trunk diameter $d$ (in cm) as a function of age $t$ (in years) for trees aged between $5$ and $25$ years. The regression equation is $d = 1.4t + 8.2$, with $r = 0.94$.
a) Interpret the meaning of the gradient $1.4$ in the context of the problem.
b) Predict the trunk diameter of a 60-year-old tree and evaluate the validity of this prediction.
c) Explain why strong correlation ($r = 0.94$) does not mean age is the sole cause of trunk diameter.

Step-by-step Solution:

Part a: Contextual interpretation of gradient:
The gradient $1.4$ indicates that for each additional year of growth, the tree trunk diameter increases by an average of $1.4\text{ cm}$. [A1: Criterion C]

Part b: Prediction for $t = 60$:
$d = 1.4(60) + 8.2 = 84 + 8.2 = 92.2\text{ cm}$. [A1]
Validity evaluation: This prediction is an extrapolation because $t = 60$ lies far beyond the observed upper limit ($25$ years). Biological growth typically decelerates as trees mature (following a logistic curve rather than linear growth). Therefore, this linear extrapolation is unreliable and likely overestimates true trunk diameter. [R1: Criterion D]

Part c: Correlation vs. Causation:
While age is directly related to growth, other confounding factors such as soil nutrient levels, annual rainfall, sunlight availability, and pest infestation also strongly influence growth rate. A strong correlation reflects association, but does not imply that age operates in isolation. [R1: Criterion D]

Tier 1: Standard Exercise 2

For a dataset of 10 student study hours ($x$) and exam scores ($y$), $\sum x = 80$ and $\sum y = 650$.
a) Calculate the coordinates of the centroid mean point $(\bar{x}, \bar{y})$.
b) Explain why any valid line of best fit must pass through this point.

View Solution & Markscheme

Method & Marks:

a) $\bar{x} = \frac{80}{10} = 8\text{ hours}$. $\bar{y} = \frac{650}{10} = 65\text{ marks}$.
Centroid: $(\bar{x}, \bar{y}) = (8, 65)$. [A1]
b) The centroid represents the center of mass of the bivariate distribution. In least-squares regression, minimizing the sum of squared vertical residuals guarantees that the regression line passes through $(\bar{x}, \bar{y})$. [R1]

Tier 2: Intermediate Exercise 4

A line of best fit passes through the mean point $(14, 52)$ and has a gradient of $-1.8$.
a) Write the equation of the line of best fit in form $y = mx + c$.
b) Estimate the value of $y$ when $x = 20$.

View Solution & Markscheme

Method & Marks:

a) Using $y - \bar{y} = m(x - \bar{x})$: [M1]
$y - 52 = -1.8(x - 14) \implies y - 52 = -1.8x + 25.2$
$y = -1.8x + 77.2$. [A1]

Tier 2: Intermediate Exercise 5

A scientist measures temperature $T$ ($^\circ$C) and gas pressure $P$ (kPa) for temperatures between $20^\circ\text{C}$ and $80^\circ\text{C}$. The regression line is $P = 0.35T + 95$.
a) Predict the pressure at $50^\circ\text{C}$ and classify this as interpolation or extrapolation.
b) Predict the pressure at $180^\circ\text{C}$ and evaluate the reliability of this estimate.

View Solution & Markscheme

Method & Marks:

a) $P = 0.35(50) + 95 = 17.5 + 95 = 112.5\text{ kPa}$. [A1]
This is interpolation because $50^\circ\text{C}$ lies inside the experimental range $[20, 80]$, making it reliable. [A1]
b) $P = 0.35(180) + 95 = 63 + 95 = 158\text{ kPa}$.
This is extrapolation because $180^\circ\text{C}$ is far outside the range. The gas container may rupture or the gas may undergo phase change at high temperature, making this prediction highly unreliable. [R1: Criterion D]

Tier 2: Intermediate Exercise 6

The heights ($x$ cm) and foot lengths ($y$ cm) of 6 adults are recorded:
$(160, 24), (165, 25), (170, 26), (175, 27), (180, 28), (190, 30)$.
a) Calculate $\bar{x}$ and $\bar{y}$.
b) Find the gradient of the line connecting $(160, 24)$ and $(190, 30)$.
c) Show that the centroid $(\bar{x}, \bar{y})$ lies exactly on this line.

View Solution & Markscheme

Method & Marks:

a) $\bar{x} = \frac{160+165+170+175+180+190}{6} = \frac{1040}{6} = 173.33\text{ cm}$.
$\bar{y} = \frac{24+25+26+27+28+30}{6} = \frac{160}{6} = 26.67\text{ cm}$. [A1]
b) Gradient $m = \frac{30 - 24}{190 - 160} = \frac{6}{30} = 0.2$. [A1]
Equation through $(160, 24)$: $y - 24 = 0.2(x - 160) \implies y = 0.2x - 8$.
c) Test centroid: $0.2(173.33) - 8 = 34.67 - 8 = 26.67 = \bar{y}$. $\blacksquare$ [R1]

Tier 2: Intermediate Exercise 7

A study finds a strong correlation ($r = 0.91$) between the number of firefighters sent to a fire ($x$) and the financial damage caused by the fire ($y$). The city mayor concludes: "To reduce fire damage, we must send fewer firefighters."
Identify the major flaw in the mayor's logic and name the confounding variable involved.

View Solution & Markscheme

Method & Marks:

The mayor committed the fallacy of assuming correlation implies causation. Firefighters do not cause fire damage. [R1: Criterion C]
The confounding (lurking) variable is the severity/size of the fire. Larger, more severe fires naturally result in greater financial damage AND require dispatching more firefighters. Reducing firefighters would actually increase damage. [R1: Criterion D Evaluation]

Tier 3: Extended Exercise 8

A dataset has standard deviations $s_x = 4$ and $s_y = 6$. The Pearson correlation coefficient is $r = 0.85$, and the mean point is $(10, 25)$.
a) Use the regression gradient formula $m = r\left(\frac{s_y}{s_x}\right)$ to calculate the exact slope of the line of best fit of $y$ on $x$.
b) Determine the complete linear regression equation.
c) Explain why the line of best fit of $x$ on $y$ has a different slope from the line of $y$ on $x$.

View Solution & Markscheme

Method & Marks:

a) $m = r\left(\frac{s_y}{s_x}\right) = 0.85\left(\frac{6}{4}\right) = 0.85 \times 1.5 = 1.275$. [M1][A1]
b) $y - 25 = 1.275(x - 10) \implies y - 25 = 1.275x - 12.75$
$y = 1.275x + 12.25$. [A1]
c) The regression of $y$ on $x$ minimizes vertical residuals (errors in $y$), whereas the regression of $x$ on $y$ minimizes horizontal residuals (errors in $x$, with slope $m' = r\frac{s_x}{s_y} = 0.85\times \frac{4}{6} \approx 0.567$). The two lines only coincide when correlation is perfect ($r = \pm 1$). [R1: Rigorous conceptual insight]

Tier 3: Extended Exercise 9

Consider the points: $(-2, 4), (-1, 1), (0, 0), (1, 1), (2, 4)$.
a) Show that the relationship between $x$ and $y$ is deterministic and exact: $y = x^2$.
b) Calculate $\sum x$, $\sum y$, and $\sum xy$.
c) Show that Pearson's correlation coefficient $r = 0$, and explain why this demonstrates that $r$ detects only linear relationships.

View Solution & Markscheme

Method & Marks:

a) For every point, $(-2)^2 = 4, (-1)^2 = 1, 0^2 = 0, 1^2 = 1, 2^2 = 4 \implies y = x^2$. [A1]
b) $\sum x = -2 - 1 + 0 + 1 + 2 = 0 \implies \bar{x} = 0$.
$\sum y = 4 + 1 + 0 + 1 + 4 = 10 \implies \bar{y} = 2$.
$\sum xy = (-2)(4) + (-1)(1) + (0)(0) + (1)(1) + (2)(4) = -8 - 1 + 0 + 1 + 8 = 0$. [M1][A1]
c) Covariance numerator is $\sum (x - \bar{x})(y - \bar{y}) = \sum xy - n\bar{x}\bar{y} = 0 - 0 = 0 \implies r = 0$.
Even though there is a perfect quadratic relationship ($y = x^2$), $r = 0$ because Pearson's $r$ measures strictly linear association. Symmetrical curvature cancels out positive and negative slopes, proving that $r = 0$ does not imply no relationship! [R1: Criterion C Communication]

Tier 3: Extended Exercise 10

Criterion B & D Modelling Investigation: An environmental agency tracks greenhouse gas emissions $x$ (in megatons) and mean summer temperature anomaly $y$ ($^\circ$C) over a 30-year period ($x$ ranges from $320$ to $420\text{ Mt}$). The regression model is $y = 0.015x - 4.2$, with $r = 0.88$.
a) Interpret the slope $0.015$ in terms of temperature increase per $100\text{ Mt}$ increase in emissions.
b) A climate model projects temperature anomalies if emissions reach $700\text{ Mt}$. Calculate the predicted temperature and critically evaluate two mathematical limitations of using this linear model for this prediction.

View Solution & Markscheme

Method & Marks:

Part a: An increase of $100\text{ Mt}$ corresponds to a temperature increase of $100 \times 0.015 = 1.5^\circ\text{C}$. [A1]
Part b: Prediction for $x = 700\text{ Mt}$:
$y = 0.015(700) - 4.2 = 10.5 - 4.2 = +6.3^\circ\text{C}$. [A1]
Critical evaluation of limitations:
1. Severe Extrapolation: $700\text{ Mt}$ is far beyond the observed range $[320, 420]$. Linear extrapolation assumes unchecked straight-line behavior without physical boundaries.
2. Non-linear Feedback Loops: Climate systems possess complex non-linear tipping points (e.g. melting permafrost, ocean carbon saturation, albedo loss) where temperature responses deviate sharply from simple linear regression. [R1: Criterion D Contextual Reflection]

Correlation & Linear Regression Reference Sheet

Pearson's $r$ Range

$$-1 \le r \le 1$$

Measure of linear correlation.

Centroid Mean Point

$$(\bar{x}, \bar{y}) = \left(\frac{\sum x}{n}, \; \frac{\sum y}{n}\right)$$

Every line of best fit passes through here.

Regression Line ($y$ on $x$)

$$y - \bar{y} = m(x - \bar{x})$$

Where $m = r\left(\frac{s_y}{s_x}\right)$.

$y$-Intercept

$$c = \bar{y} - m\bar{x}$$

From point-slope form.

Interpolation

$$x_{\min} \le x \le x_{\max}$$

Reliable within data range.

Extrapolation

$$x < x_{\min} \text{ or } x > x_{\max}$$

Unreliable outside data range.

Top 5 Common MYP Correlation Pitfalls

  • Drawing a Line of Best Fit That Misses $(\bar{x}, \bar{y})$: In MYP exams, any hand-drawn line of best fit that does not pass through the plotted centroid $(\bar{x}, \bar{y})$ loses marks automatically!
  • Assuming Correlation Proves Causation: A high correlation coefficient $r$ only shows that two variables move together. It does NOT prove that $X$ causes $Y$. Always consider lurking/confounding variables.
  • Treating Extrapolations as Reliable Facts: Predicting outside the observed data range assumes the same linear relationship continues indefinitely, which is often biologically or physically impossible.
  • Assuming $r = 0$ Means No Relationship: $r = 0$ means there is no linear relationship. A perfect curved parabola ($y = x^2$) or circle can yield $r = 0$. Always inspect the scatter plot visually!
  • Switching $x$ and $y$ in Regression: The regression line of $y$ on $x$ is used to predict $y$ from $x$. It is NOT the same line as the regression of $x$ on $y$.

Self-Grading Mini Test

5 quiz questions on scatter plots, Pearson's r, centroid lines of best fit, and regression predictions.

Question 1 of 5 Pearson's r Interpretation

Which value of Pearson's correlation coefficient indicates the strongest linear relationship?

Explanation: Strength depends on the absolute magnitude $|r|$. $|-0.92| = 0.92$, which is greater than $0.85$ and $0.75$. The negative sign merely indicates the direction.

Question 2 of 5 Centroid Point Rule

Which point must every linear regression line of best fit pass through?

Explanation: By mathematical derivation of least squares, the line of best fit always passes through the mean point $(\bar{x}, \bar{y})$.

Question 3 of 5 Interpolation vs Extrapolation

In a study with observed $x$-values ranging from $10$ to $50$, predicting $y$ for $x = 65$ is an example of:

Explanation: Because $x = 65$ lies outside the observed interval $[10, 50]$, it is extrapolation and is considered unreliable.

Question 4 of 5 Correlation and Causation

If two variables have a correlation of $r = 0.95$, what does this mathematically prove?

Explanation: A high value of $r$ proves strong linear association, but does NOT prove causation or rule out lurking confounding variables.

Question 5 of 5 Regression Prediction

The regression equation is $y = 3.2x - 4.5$. What is the predicted value of $y$ when $x = 5$?

Explanation: $y = 3.2(5) - 4.5 = 16.0 - 4.5 = 11.5$.
