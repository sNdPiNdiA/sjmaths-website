1.1

The Real Number Hierarchy: $\mathbb{N} \subset \mathbb{Z} \subset \mathbb{Q} \subset \mathbb{R}$

  • Natural Numbers ($\mathbb{N}$): The counting numbers $\mathbb{N} = \{0, 1, 2, 3, 4, \dots\}$ (by IB MYP/DP convention, $0 \in \mathbb{N}$; the strictly positive set is written $\mathbb{Z}^+ = \{1, 2, 3, \dots\}$).
  • Integers ($\mathbb{Z}$): Whole numbers including positive, negative, and zero: $\mathbb{Z} = \{\dots, -3, -2, -1, 0, 1, 2, 3, \dots\}$.
  • Rational Numbers ($\mathbb{Q}$): Any number that can be expressed as a ratio of two integers in the form: $$\mathbb{Q} = \left\{ \frac{p}{q} \;\middle|\; p, q \in \mathbb{Z},\, q \ne 0 \right\}$$ Rational numbers produce either terminating decimals (e.g., $\frac{3}{8} = 0.375$) or recurring decimals (e.g., $\frac{5}{11} = 0.\overline{45}$).
  • Irrational Numbers ($\mathbb{Q}'$ or $\mathbb{R} \setminus \mathbb{Q}$): Numbers that cannot be expressed as $\frac{p}{q}$. Their decimal expansions are non-terminating and non-repeating (e.g., $\sqrt{2}, \sqrt{3}, \pi, e, \sqrt{7}$).
  • Real Numbers ($\mathbb{R}$): The union of all rational and irrational numbers ($\mathbb{R} = \mathbb{Q} \cup \mathbb{Q}'$). Every real number corresponds to a unique point on the continuous real number line.
  • Nested Subset Inclusion: $$\mathbb{N} \subset \mathbb{Z} \subset \mathbb{Q} \subset \mathbb{R}$$

Figure 1.1: Strict Venn hierarchy of numerical sets: $\mathbb{N} \subset \mathbb{Z} \subset \mathbb{Q} \subset \mathbb{R}$ with disjoint irrational set $\mathbb{Q}'$.

Example 1 · Criterion A

Classifying Numbers into the Smallest Appropriate Set

Determine the most specific (smallest) number set ($\mathbb{N}, \mathbb{Z}, \mathbb{Q}, \mathbb{Q}'$) for each of the following:
(a) $\sqrt{49}$   (b) $-\frac{36}{4}$   (c) $3.14159$   (d) $\sqrt{18}$   (e) $0.\overline{81}$

  1. (a) $\sqrt{49} = 7$: Since $7$ is a non-negative whole counting number, its most specific set is the Natural numbers ($\mathbb{N}$).
  2. (b) $-\frac{36}{4} = -9$: Since $-9$ is a negative whole number, it is not in $\mathbb{N}$, but belongs to the Integers ($\mathbb{Z}$).
  3. (c) $3.14159$: This is a terminating decimal ($=\frac{314159}{100000}$), hence a ratio of integers. Its smallest set is the Rational numbers ($\mathbb{Q}$) (Note: it is an approximation of $\pi$, but as written it is terminating).
  4. (d) $\sqrt{18} = \sqrt{9 \times 2} = 3\sqrt{2}$: Since $2$ is not a perfect square, $\sqrt{2}$ cannot be expressed as $\frac{p}{q}$. Therefore, $\sqrt{18}$ is an Irrational number ($\mathbb{Q}'$).
  5. (e) $0.\overline{81}$: A repeating decimal with period 2. All periodic decimals are rational: $0.\overline{81} = \frac{81}{99} = \frac{9}{11}$. Smallest set: Rational numbers ($\mathbb{Q}$).

Summary: (a) $\mathbb{N}$; (b) $\mathbb{Z}$; (c) $\mathbb{Q}$; (d) $\mathbb{Q}'$; (e) $\mathbb{Q}$.

1.2

Converting Recurring Decimals to Exact Fractions $\frac{p}{q}$

  • Why Fractions? Floating-point decimals lose precision in computations. In MYP Criterion A and DP AA examinations, exact values in terms of $\frac{p}{q}$ are required.
  • Pure Recurring Decimals: Where the repeating period begins immediately after the decimal point.
    • Multiply by $10^k$, where $k$ is the period length (number of digits in repeating cycle).
    • Subtract the original equation $x$ to eliminate the infinite recurring tail. $$\text{If } x = 0.\overline{a_1 a_2 \dots a_k}, \quad 10^k x - x = a_1 a_2 \dots a_k \implies x = \frac{a_1 a_2 \dots a_k}{10^k - 1}$$
  • Mixed Recurring Decimals: Where a non-repeating prefix precedes the periodic cycle (e.g., $0.5\overline{24}$).
    • Step 1: Multiply by $10^m$ (where $m$ is the number of non-repeating digits) to align the repeat at the decimal boundary.
    • Step 2: Multiply by $10^{m+k}$ to shift one full repeating period.
    • Step 3: Subtract Equation 1 from Equation 2 and simplify to lowest terms.
  • Terminating Decimals Condition: A fraction $\frac{p}{q}$ in irreducible form terminates if and only if the prime factorization of the denominator $q$ contains only $2$ and/or $5$: $$q = 2^a \cdot 5^b \quad (a, b \in \mathbb{N})$$

Example 2 · Criterion A & C

Converting Pure and Mixed Recurring Decimals

Express each decimal as an irreducible fraction $\frac{p}{q}$ where $p, q \in \mathbb{Z}$:
(a) $0.\overline{54}$   (b) $2.1\overline{36}$

  1. (a) Pure repeating ($k = 2$ digits): $$\text{Let } x = 0.545454\dots \quad \text{--- (1)}$$ Multiply by $10^2 = 100$: $$100x = 54.545454\dots \quad \text{--- (2)}$$ Subtract (1) from (2): $$100x - x = 54.5454\dots - 0.5454\dots \implies 99x = 54$$ $$x = \frac{54}{99} = \frac{54 \div 9}{99 \div 9} = \frac{6}{11}$$
  2. (b) Mixed repeating ($1$ non-repeating digit, period $k = 2$): $$\text{Let } y = 2.1363636\dots$$ Multiply by $10^1 = 10$ to shift the non-repeating digit: $$10y = 21.363636\dots \quad \text{--- (1)}$$ Multiply by $10^3 = 1000$ to shift one full repeat cycle: $$1000y = 2136.363636\dots \quad \text{--- (2)}$$ Subtract (1) from (2): $$1000y - 10y = 2136.36\dots - 21.36\dots \implies 990y = 2115$$ $$y = \frac{2115}{990}$$ Divide numerator and denominator by $\gcd(2115, 990) = 45$: $$y = \frac{2115 \div 45}{990 \div 45} = \frac{47}{22} = 2\frac{3}{22}$$

Answers: (a) $\frac{6}{11}$; (b) $\frac{47}{22}$.

1.3

Proof of Irrationality by Contradiction (Extended eAssessment)

  • Logic of Proof by Contradiction (Criterion C):
    1. Assume the negation of the proposition to be proved (e.g., assume $\sqrt{2} \in \mathbb{Q}$).
    2. Deduce logical consequences through rigorous algebraic steps.
    3. Reach an impossible statement or violation of initial definitions (a contradiction).
    4. Conclude that the initial assumption must be false; thus the proposition is true.
  • Classic Euclid Proof for $\sqrt{2}$ (Key Theorem):
    Assume $\sqrt{2} = \frac{p}{q}$, where $p, q \in \mathbb{Z}^+$ and $\gcd(p, q) = 1$ (coprime, i.e., in simplest form). $$\sqrt{2} = \frac{p}{q} \implies 2 = \frac{p^2}{q^2} \implies p^2 = 2q^2$$ Since $p^2$ is a multiple of $2$, $p^2$ is even, which implies $p$ is even.
    Let $p = 2k$ for some integer $k$. Substitute into $p^2 = 2q^2$: $$(2k)^2 = 2q^2 \implies 4k^2 = 2q^2 \implies q^2 = 2k^2$$ This implies $q^2$ is even, so $q$ must also be even.
    The Contradiction: Both $p$ and $q$ are even, so they share a common factor of $2$. This contradicts the assumption that $\gcd(p, q) = 1$. Hence, $\sqrt{2}$ cannot be rational; $\sqrt{2} \in \mathbb{Q}'$.
  • Operations with Rationals and Irrationals:
    • $\text{Rational} \pm \text{Irrational} = \text{Irrational}$ (e.g., $3 + \sqrt{5} \in \mathbb{Q}'$).
    • $\text{Non-zero Rational} \times \text{Irrational} = \text{Irrational}$ (e.g., $\frac{2}{3}\pi \in \mathbb{Q}'$).
    • $\text{Irrational} \times \text{Irrational}$ can be rational or irrational (e.g., $\sqrt{2} \times \sqrt{8} = \sqrt{16} = 4 \in \mathbb{Q}$, but $\sqrt{2} \times \sqrt{3} = \sqrt{6} \in \mathbb{Q}'$).
1.4

Interval Notation, Inequalities & Number Line Graphs

  • Interval Notation vs Set-Builder:
    • Open Interval $(a, b)$: $\{x \in \mathbb{R} \mid a < x < b\}$ — endpoints excluded (open circles on number line).
    • Closed Interval $[a, b]$: $\{x \in \mathbb{R} \mid a \le x \le b\}$ — endpoints included (solid dots on number line).
    • Half-open $[a, b)$: $\{x \in \mathbb{R} \mid a \le x < b\}$ — $a$ included, $b$ excluded.
    • Unbounded Intervals: $(-\infty, b] \iff x \le b$; $(a, \infty) \iff x > a$; $(-\infty, \infty) \iff x \in \mathbb{R}$. Note that $\pm \infty$ always take round parentheses $($ and $)$.
  • Absolute Value as Distance on $\mathbb{R}$: $$|x| = \begin{cases} x, & x \ge 0 \\ -x, & x < 0 \end{cases}$$ Geometrically, $|x - c|$ represents the distance between $x$ and $c$ on the real number line.
    • $|x - c| \le d \iff c - d \le x \le c + d \iff x \in [c - d, c + d]$ (closed neighborhood).
    • $|x - c| > d \iff x < c - d \text{ or } x > c + d \iff x \in (-\infty, c - d) \cup (c + d, \infty)$.

Example 3 · Criterion A & D

Interval Notation and Tolerance Neighborhoods

An precision engineering ball bearing is manufactured with a nominal radius of $15\text{ mm}$ and an allowable error tolerance of $\pm 0.04\text{ mm}$.
(a) Write an absolute value inequality for the acceptable radius $r$.
(b) Express the acceptable range of $r$ in interval notation.
(c) State whether a measured ball bearing with $r = 15.038\text{ mm}$ is accepted.

  1. (a) Absolute value inequality: The distance between the actual radius $r$ and $15\text{ mm}$ must not exceed $0.04\text{ mm}$: $$|r - 15| \le 0.04$$
  2. (b) Interval notation: $$-0.04 \le r - 15 \le 0.04 \implies 15 - 0.04 \le r \le 15 + 0.04$$ $$14.96 \le r \le 15.04 \implies r \in [14.96, 15.04]\text{ mm}$$
  3. (c) Verification: Since $14.96 \le 15.038 \le 15.04$, the ball bearing satisfies the tolerance constraint and is accepted.

Answers: (a) $|r - 15| \le 0.04$; (b) $[14.96, 15.04]$; (c) Accepted.

1.5

Prime Factorization, HCF & LCM Applications

  • Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic: Every integer $n > 1$ can be uniquely factored as a product of prime powers: $$n = p_1^{a_1} \cdot p_2^{a_2} \cdots p_k^{a_k}$$
  • Finding HCF and LCM via Index Form: Let $A = 2^3 \cdot 3^2 \cdot 5^1$ and $B = 2^2 \cdot 3^3 \cdot 7^1$:
    • $\text{HCF}(A, B)$ = product of common prime factors with lowest exponents: $$\text{HCF} = 2^{\min(3,2)} \cdot 3^{\min(2,3)} = 2^2 \cdot 3^2 = 4 \cdot 9 = 36$$
    • $\text{LCM}(A, B)$ = product of all prime factors with highest exponents: $$\text{LCM} = 2^{\max(3,2)} \cdot 3^{\max(2,3)} \cdot 5^1 \cdot 7^1 = 2^3 \cdot 3^3 \cdot 5 \cdot 7 = 8 \cdot 27 \cdot 35 = 7560$$
  • Fundamental Product Identity: $$\text{HCF}(A, B) \times \text{LCM}(A, B) = A \times B$$

Criterion C Question 7 (Extended Proof · 5 marks)

Prove by contradiction that $\sqrt{3}$ is an irrational number. Present a complete, logically structured mathematical argument.

Show Full Markscheme

Step 1: Assumption for contradiction [1 mark]
Assume $\sqrt{3}$ is rational. Then $\sqrt{3} = \frac{a}{b}$ where $a, b \in \mathbb{Z}^+$, $b \ne 0$, and $\gcd(a, b) = 1$ (irreducible form). [M1]

Step 2: Square both sides [1 mark]
$3 = \frac{a^2}{b^2} \implies a^2 = 3b^2$. [A1]

Step 3: Deduce divisibility of $a$ [1 mark]
Since $a^2$ is a multiple of $3$, $3$ divides $a^2$. Because $3$ is prime, $3$ must divide $a$.
Let $a = 3k$ for some integer $k$. [R1]

Step 4: Deduce divisibility of $b$ [1 mark]
Substitute $a = 3k$: $(3k)^2 = 3b^2 \implies 9k^2 = 3b^2 \implies b^2 = 3k^2$.
Thus $3$ divides $b^2$, which implies $3$ divides $b$. [R1]

Step 5: Identify contradiction & conclusion [1 mark]
Both $a$ and $b$ are divisible by $3$, contradicting the condition that $\gcd(a, b) = 1$.
Therefore, the initial assumption is false, and $\sqrt{3} \in \mathbb{Q}'$. $\blacksquare$ [R1]

Criterion C Question 8 (Extended · 4 marks)

Let $r$ be a non-zero rational number and $x$ be an irrational number.
Prove by contradiction that their product $P = r \cdot x$ is an irrational number.

Show Full Markscheme

Step 1: Assumption [1 mark]
Assume for contradiction that $P = r \cdot x$ is rational, so $P \in \mathbb{Q}$. [M1]

Step 2: Express in rational form [1 mark]
Since $r \in \mathbb{Q}$ and $r \ne 0$, we can write $r = \frac{a}{b}$ and $P = \frac{c}{d}$ where $a, b, c, d \in \mathbb{Z}$ and $a, b, d \ne 0$. [A1]

Step 3: Solve for $x$ [1 mark]
$$x = \frac{P}{r} = \frac{\frac{c}{d}}{\frac{a}{b}} = \frac{bc}{ad}$$ Since the integers are closed under multiplication, $bc \in \mathbb{Z}$ and $ad \in \mathbb{Z}$ with $ad \ne 0$.
Thus $x \in \mathbb{Q}$. [M1]

Step 4: Contradiction [1 mark]
This contradicts the premise that $x$ is irrational ($x \in \mathbb{Q}'$).
Hence, $r \cdot x$ must be irrational. $\blacksquare$ [R1]

$45 = 3^2 \times 5^1$

Number Systems Quick Reference

Number Set Classifications

Natural Numbers: $$\mathbb{N} = \{0, 1, 2, 3, \dots\}$$
Integers: $$\mathbb{Z} = \{\dots, -2, -1, 0, 1, 2, \dots\}$$
Rational Numbers: $$\mathbb{Q} = \left\{\frac{p}{q} \;\middle|\; p, q \in \mathbb{Z}, q \ne 0\right\}$$
Subset Relation: $$\mathbb{N} \subset \mathbb{Z} \subset \mathbb{Q} \subset \mathbb{R}$$

Decimal Conversions & Conditions

Terminating Condition: $$\text{In } \frac{p}{q}, \quad q = 2^a \cdot 5^b \quad (a, b \in \mathbb{N})$$
Pure Repeating (period $k$): $$x = \frac{\text{repeat cycle}}{10^k - 1} = \frac{\text{cycle}}{\underbrace{99\dots9}_{k \text{ nines}}}$$
Example: $$0.\overline{27} = \frac{27}{99} = \frac{3}{11}$$

Intervals & Absolute Values

Closed vs Open: $$[a, b] \iff a \le x \le b, \quad (a, b) \iff a < x < b$$
Distance from Center: $$|x - c| \le d \iff c - d \le x \le c + d$$
Triangle Inequality: $$|a + b| \le |a| + |b|$$

HCF & LCM Identities

Product Property: $$\text{HCF}(a, b) \times \text{LCM}(a, b) = a \times b$$
Prime Power Indices: $$\text{HCF} = \prod p_i^{\min(a_i, b_i)}, \quad \text{LCM} = \prod p_i^{\max(a_i, b_i)}$$

Top 5 MYP Examination Pitfalls

1. Forgetting to Simplify Before Checking Denominators

When testing if $\frac{21}{60}$ terminates, looking at $60 = 2^2 \times 3 \times 5$ leads students to falsely claim it recurs because of factor $3$. Always simplify first: $\frac{21}{60} = \frac{7}{20}$; $20 = 2^2 \times 5$, so it terminates!

2. Confusing $\pi$ and $\frac{22}{7}$

$\frac{22}{7}$ is a rational approximation ($0.\overline{142857}$), while $\pi$ is strictly irrational ($3.14159265\dots$ never recurs or terminates). Never classify $\pi$ as rational.

3. Infinity with Square Brackets

Writing $[0, \infty]$ or $(-\infty, 4]$ is a strict mathematical error. Since infinity is a concept of unbound growth and not a reachable real number, $\pm \infty$ must always take round brackets: $[0, \infty)$ and $(-\infty, 4]$.

4. Mixed Recurring Algebraic Multipliers

For $x = 0.1\overline{6}$, students often compute $10x - x = 9x = 1.5$, leaving a decimal in the fraction $\frac{1.5}{9}$. Better practice: use $100x = 16.6\dots$ and $10x = 1.6\dots$, giving $90x = 15 \implies x = \frac{15}{90} = \frac{1}{6}$.

What is the exact fraction representation of the recurring decimal $0.\overline{135}$ in lowest terms?

Explanation: The period length is $3$ digits, so denominator has three $9$s: $$x = \frac{135}{999}$$ Divide numerator and denominator by $27$: $$\frac{135 \div 27}{999 \div 27} = \frac{5}{37}$$ Hence, option B is correct.
Question 3 of 5 Criterion A 1 Mark

Which inequality corresponds exactly to the interval notation $(-4, 7]$?

Explanation: Round bracket $($ excludes the left endpoint ($-4 < x$), while square bracket $]$ includes the right endpoint ($x \le 7$). Therefore, $(-4, 7] \iff -4 < x \le 7$.
Question 4 of 5 Criterion A 1 Mark

If the HCF of two numbers is $18$ and their product is $19440$, what is their LCM?

Explanation: Using the fundamental identity: $$\text{HCF} \times \text{LCM} = A \times B$$ $$18 \times \text{LCM} = 19440 \implies \text{LCM} = \frac{19440}{18} = 1080$$
Question 5 of 5 Criterion D 1 Mark

A sensor measures a temperature reading $T$ that satisfies $|T - 22| \le 3$. Which interval contains all acceptable temperature readings?

Explanation: $|T - 22| \le 3 \iff -3 \le T - 22 \le 3 \iff 22 - 3 \le T \le 22 + 3 \iff 19 \le T \le 25$. In interval notation, this closed interval is written $[19, 25]$.
