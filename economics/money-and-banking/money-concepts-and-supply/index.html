Money and Banking

Money: Meaning, Functions, Demand, Supply, and Value of Money

These authoritative study notes cover the foundational principles of monetary economics, encompassing the evolution, functions, demand theories, supply determinants, and the inverse value of money. Designed specifically for competitive examinations like UP PGT Economics, UP TGT Social Science, UGC-NET, and State Assistant Professor posts.

🏛️ Key Economists & Landmark Publications

Sir William Petty
1662, 'Treatise of Taxes and Contributions'

Pioneered early monetary velocity concepts and the political-economic analysis of circulating medium.

Irving Fisher
1911, 'The Purchasing Power of Money'

Formulated the traditional Quantity Theory of Money via the iconic Equation of Exchange: MV = PT.

John Maynard Keynes
1936, 'The General Theory of Employment, Interest and Money'

Introduced the Liquidity Preference Theory, establishing Transactionary, Precautionary, and Speculative motives for money demand.

Milton Friedman
1956, 'Studies in the Quantity Theory of Money'

Revived modern Monetarism with the Restatement of the Quantity Theory, emphasizing permanent income and portfolio choice.

William J. Baumol & James Tobin
1952 & 1956, 'Transactions Demand for Cash'

Applied inventory-theoretic approaches (Inventory Square Root Rule) to transaction demand for money.

⚖️ Fundamental Assumptions & Hypotheses

  • Exogeneity of Money Supply: Central bank completely controls the nominal money supply independently of interest rates (classical baseline assumption).
  • Constant Velocity in the Short Run: Institutional arrangements governing payments remain stable, making velocity ($V$) relatively constant.
  • Full Employment of Resources: In classical models, output ($Y$) is fixed at the natural rate of unemployment and full employment capacity.
  • Rational Economic Agents: Individuals maximize utility and firms maximize profit, allocating assets efficiently between money and alternative interest-bearing bonds.
  • Dichotomy of the Monetary Economy: Nominal variables (price level, nominal GDP) are determined by money supply, while real variables are determined by real factors (classical neutrality).
  • Stable Demand Function for Money: The demand for real money balances is a stable function of a small number of key macroeconomic variables.

1. Meaning and Evolutionary Functions of Money

Money is anything that is generally accepted as a medium of exchange, a standard of deferred payment, a unit of account, and a store of value. It evolved to overcome the severe double coincidence of wants defect inherent in the barter economy. Functions are broadly classified into Primary (medium of exchange, measure of value), Secondary (standard of deferred payment, store of value, transfer of value), and Contingent (distribution of national income, basis of credit, maximization of collective utility).

Key Exam Takeaways:
  • Medium of exchange and unit of account are the two defining, indispensable functions of money.
  • Store of value function distinguishes money from mere transient exchange mediums, though inflation erodes this function.
  • Barter requires simultaneous mutual wants, creating massive transaction costs that money eliminates.

2. Theories of Demand for Money: Classical, Keynesian, and Monetarist

Demand for money analyzes why agents hold liquid cash rather than earning assets. The Classical Cash-Transactions (Fisher) approach focuses on exchange mechanics ($M_d = PT/V$). The Cambridge Cash-Balances approach ($M_d = kPY$) emphasizes income proportion held for convenience. Keynes identified three motives: Transactionary and Precautionary (dependent on income level, $L_1 = k(Y)$) and Speculative (inverse function of the market rate of interest, $L_2 = r(i)$). Friedman's modern quantity theory treats money as a capital asset yielding utility/services, demanded as a function of permanent income and relative yields of bonds, equities, and physical goods.

Key Exam Takeaways:
  • Cambridge 'k' is the reciprocal of Fisher's velocity of circulation ($k = 1/V$).
  • Keynesian speculative demand generates the Liquidity Trap at extremely low interest rates where money demand becomes perfectly elastic.
  • Friedman includes human wealth, non-human wealth, and expected inflation rates in his robust money demand function.

3. Supply of Money: Determinants, Monetary Aggregates, and Money Multiplier

Money supply ($M^s$) is the total stock of monetary media available in an economy at a specific time. It is determined by the monetary base (High-Powered Money, $H = C + R$) supplied by the central authority, combined with the public's currency-deposit ratio ($c = C/D$) and commercial banks' reserve-deposit ratio ($r = R/D$). The money multiplier process links high-powered money to broad money aggregates via $M = m cdot H$, where the multiplier $m = (1 + c) / (c + r)$. Central banks measure money using tiered aggregates like $M_1$ (currency + demand deposits), $M_2$ ($M_1$ + savings/time deposits), up to broad measures incorporating institutional liquidity.

Key Exam Takeaways:
  • High-powered money ($H$) is managed directly by the Central Bank and serves as the foundation for deposit expansion.
  • The value of the money multiplier varies inversely with both the currency-deposit ratio and the reserve-deposit ratio.
  • RBI historical aggregates include $M_1, M_2, M_3, M_4$, with $M_3$ being the most widely used broad money measure.

4. Value of Money and the Quantity Theory

The value of money refers to its purchasing power—the quantity of goods and services that a unit of money can purchase ($1/P$). It varies inversely with the general price level. Irving Fisher's Equation of Exchange ($MV + M'V' = PT$) establishes that, given $V, V', $ and $T$ as constant in the short run, the price level varies directly and proportionately with the quantity of money in circulation. Critics like Keynes and Wicksell pointed out that the equation is an identity, ignores interest rate transmission mechanisms, and assumes static conditions.

Key Exam Takeaways:
  • Value of money = $1/P$; if price level doubles, purchasing power is halved.
  • Fisher's transaction equation assumes full employment and output independence from money supply.
  • Cash balance equation (Marshall-Pigou) modifies transaction approaches by focusing on proportion of income held as cash.

📐 Mathematical Formulations & Equilibrium Conditions

Fisher's Equation of Exchange
MV = PT

M = Money Supply, V = Velocity of Circulation, P = Price Level, T = Volume of Transactions. Proves direct proportionality between money supply and price level under constant V and T.

Cambridge Cash Balance Equation
Md = kPY

M_d is demand for money, k is proportion of nominal income (PY) held in cash balances, P is price level, Y is real income.

Standard Money Multiplier Formulation
M = 1 + cc + r H

M is total money supply, c is currency-deposit ratio ($C/D$), r is reserve-deposit ratio ($R/D$), H is high-powered money ($C + R$). Multiplier $m = \frac{1 + c}{c + r}$.

Baumol-Tobin Inventory Square Root Rule
Md = \sqrt{bY2i}

b represents brokerage cost per cash withdrawal, Y is annual real income, i is nominal interest rate. Demonstrates economies of scale in cash management.

Friedman's Demand for Money Function
M/P = f(Yp, rb - rm, re - rm, 1PdPdt)

Real cash balances depend on permanent income ($Y_p$), opportunity cost yields on bonds ($r_b$) and equities ($r_e$) relative to money return ($r_m$), and expected inflation.

🧠 Exam Tips, Tricks & Mnemonics (10-Second Shortcuts)

💡 Fisher Velocity Reciprocal Trick V = 1/k
Remember that Cambridge 'k' is mathematically the inverse of Fisher's velocity of circulation V. If velocity increases, k falls.

Exam Application: Instantly solve matching and calculation questions relating Fisher and Cambridge equations on exam day.

💡 Baumol-Tobin Exponent Rule SQUARE-ROOT (Power of 0.5)
Income elasticity of money demand in Baumol-Tobin inventory model is 0.5, and interest elasticity is -0.5.

Exam Application: Directly pick '0.5' when asked about the income elasticity of transaction demand in inventory models.

💡 Money Multiplier Direction Check CR-DOWN, MULT-UP
Both currency ratio ($c$) and reserve ratio ($r$) sit in the denominator of the money multiplier formula. Therefore, any rise in $c$ or $r$ causes the money multiplier to fall.

Exam Application: Instantly solve directional questions on RBI reserve ratio hikes and banking multiplier contractions.

⚠️ Economic Paradoxes & Boundary Exceptions

Exception / Paradox The Liquidity Trap

Conditions: Occurs when nominal interest rates fall to extremely low levels, creating expectations that bond prices will fall and interest rates will rise.

Diagrammatic Behavior: The money demand curve becomes completely horizontal (infinite interest elasticity), rendering monetary policy expansion ineffective in reducing interest rates further.

Exception / Paradox Paradox of Thrift in Monetary Context

Conditions: When an entire community attempts to increase savings by holding more cash or reducing consumption during economic slumps.

Diagrammatic Behavior: Leads to a contraction in aggregate demand and national income via the multiplier, ultimately leaving total savings unchanged or lower.

Exception / Paradox Wicksellian Cumulative Process

Conditions: When the market rate of interest diverges persistently from the natural rate of interest due to bank credit creation.

Diagrammatic Behavior: Triggers cumulative, self-reinforcing inflation (if market rate < natural rate) or deflation (if market rate > natural rate) without self-correcting tendencies.

⚡ Must-Remember Economic Identities & Laws

  • Fisher's Equation of Exchange: MV + M'V' = PT
  • Cambridge Cash Balance Equation: M = kPY
  • Money Multiplier Formula: m = 1 + cc + r where c = C/D and r = R/D
  • Total Money Supply Identity: M = m × H
  • Keynesian Speculative Demand for Money: Ms = f(r), inversely related to the interest rate
  • Friedman's Money Demand Function: MP = f(Yp, rb - 1pdpdt, re - 1pdpdt, u)
  • Value of Money Identity: Vm = 1P

🚫 Common Analytical Confusions (Contrast Table)

Narrow Money (M1) vs Broad Money (M3) VS M1 includes only liquid transactional assets (Currency + Demand Deposits), whereas M3 includes M1 plus less liquid time deposits with banks.

M1 measures medium of exchange functions strictly, while M3 measures total monetary resources including store of value components.

High-Powered money is the monetary base issued directly by the monetary authority ($C + R$), whereas Total Money Supply is the total stock of money held by the public created via fractional reserve banking. VS High-Powered Money ($H$) vs Total Money Supply ($M$)

$H$ serves as the root foundation, while $M$ is a multiple of $H$ determined by the money multiplier.

Fisher's $V$ vs Cambridge $k$ VS Fisher's velocity ($V$) measures the turnover rate of money in transactions, whereas Cambridge $k$ measures the fraction of nominal income individuals desire to hold as cash.

They are inverse concepts: $k = 1/V$ under steady-state assumptions.

Liquidity Trap vs Normal Monetary Policy VS In a liquidity trap, the LM curve is horizontal and interest rate elasticity of money demand is infinite; in normal states, LM is upward sloping.

Monetary expansion fails to lower interest rates or boost output inside a liquidity trap.

📚 Complete Concept-by-Concept Glossary

Every fundamental term, curve, and mechanism covered in this chapter.

Barter System: An economy of direct exchange of goods and services for goods and services without the medium of money, constrained by the double coincidence of wants.
Fiat Money: Money that is not backed by a physical commodity like gold or silver, but declared as legal tender by government decree.
M1 Money Supply: The narrow measure of money supply comprising currency with the public plus demand deposits with commercial banks plus other deposits with the central bank.
M3 Money Supply: The broad measure of money supply (aggregate monetary resources) comprising M1 plus time deposits with commercial banks.
High Powered Money (H): Base money or monetary base ($H = C + R$), consisting of currency held by the public and reserves of commercial banks held with the central bank.
Money Multiplier ($m$): The ratio of total money supply to high-powered money ($m = M/H$), indicating how many times the money base expands into the total money supply.
Liquidity Preference: Keynes's theory that individuals prefer to hold liquid assets (cash) over non-liquid assets, driven by transactions, precautionary, and speculative motives.
Liquidity Trap: A situation where nominal interest rates are so low and the demand for money becomes perfectly elastic, rendering monetary policy completely ineffective.
Cambridge Cash Balance Approach: Alfred Marshall and A.C. Pigou's formulation of the demand for money focusing on cash balances held as a proportion of nominal income ($M^d = kPY$).
Quantity Theory of Money (Fisher's Equation): The classical identity $MV = PT$, stating that the total money supply multiplied by its velocity equals the price level multiplied by the volume of transactions.
Friedman's Modern Quantity Theory: A reformulation treating money as an asset and a demand function based on permanent income, bond yields, equity returns, and expected inflation.
Currency-Deposit Ratio ($c$): The ratio of currency held by the public to their total bank deposits ($c = C/D$), acting as a major leakage parameter reducing the money multiplier.
Reserve-Deposit Ratio ($r$): The ratio of total bank reserves to total deposits ($r = R/D$), encompassing both required and excess reserves held by commercial banks.
Value of Money: The purchasing power of money, defined mathematically as the reciprocal of the general price level ($1/P$).

Question 1 of 18

Which of the following functions of money specifically overcomes the 'lack of double coincidence of wants' in a barter economy?

Explanation: Medium of exchange is the primary function of money that eliminates the cumbersome requirement of double coincidence of wants by acting as an intermediary in all transactions.

Question 2 of 18

In Irving Fisher's Quantity Theory of Money equation ($MV = PT$), what does $V$ represent?

Explanation: In Fisher's equation, $V$ stands for the velocity of circulation of money, representing the average number of times a unit of money changes hands in purchasing final goods and services over a given period.

Question 3 of 18

According to the Cambridge cash-balance approach, the demand for money is expressed as:

Explanation: The Cambridge approach formulated by Marshall, Pigou, and Keynes represents money demand as $M = kPY$, where $k$ is the proportion of nominal income ($PY$) that people wish to hold in cash.

Question 4 of 18

Keynes postulated three motives for holding money. Which of the following is NOT one of them?

Explanation: Keynes identified transactions, precautionary, and speculative motives for liquidity preference. Profit-maximization is a firm objective, not a liquidity preference motive.

Question 5 of 18

What happens to the money multiplier ($m$) when the currency-deposit ratio ($c$) increases, assuming the reserve-deposit ratio remains constant?

Explanation: The money multiplier formula is $m = (1+c)/(c+r)$. An increase in $c$ means more cash is drained from the banking system, reducing the multiplier's value.

Question 6 of 18

High-powered money ($H$) is defined as:

Explanation: High-powered money ($H = C + R$) consists of currency held by the non-bank public plus the cash reserves of commercial banks.

Question 7 of 18

A situation where the interest rate is extremely low and the public holds any amount of money supplied without buying bonds is known as:

Explanation: A liquidity trap occurs when the interest rate hits a floor and money demand becomes perfectly elastic; bond prices are expected to fall, so everyone prefers cash.

Question 8 of 18

Milton Friedman's reformulation of the quantity theory of money is primarily a theory of:

Explanation: Friedman explicitly stated that his restatement of the quantity theory of money is a theory of the demand for money, not a theory of output, employment, or price level per se.

Question 9 of 18

In the context of the supply of money, what does M3 represent in India?

Explanation: In India (RBI definitions), M1 and M2 are narrow money measures, while M3 (Currency with public + Demand deposits + Time deposits with banks) is the broad money measure.

Question 10 of 18

If the velocity of money ($V$) is constant, the volume of transactions ($T$) is constant, and the money supply ($M$) doubles according to Fisher's equation, what will happen to the price level ($P$)?

Explanation: Fisher's equation is $MV = PT$. If $V$ and $T$ are constant, $P$ is directly proportional to $M$. If $M$ doubles, $P$ must double.

Question 11 of 18

Which of the following represents the correct formula for the value of money?

Explanation: The value of money refers to its purchasing power, which varies inversely with the price level ($P$). Thus, value of money = $1/P$.

Question 12 of 18

According to Keynes, the speculative demand for money is an inverse function of:

Explanation: Speculative demand for money is inversely related to the market rate of interest. Higher interest rates mean higher opportunity cost of holding cash, lowering speculative cash holdings.

Question 13 of 18

Which economist is associated with the phrase 'Inflation is always and everywhere a monetary phenomenon'?

Explanation: Milton Friedman famously asserted that inflation is always and everywhere a monetary phenomenon in the sense that it is and can be produced only by a more rapid increase in the quantity of money than in output.

Question 14 of 18

The reserve-deposit ratio ($r$) of commercial banks is determined by:

Explanation: The reserve-deposit ratio includes required reserves (mandated by central bank rules like CRR/SLR) plus excess reserves held voluntarily by banks for liquidity management.

Question 15 of 18

In Friedman's demand for money function, what replaces current income used in Keynesian formulations?

Explanation: Friedman introduced 'permanent income'—the expected average long-term income over a multi-year horizon—as the primary scale variable in his money demand function.

Question 16 of 18

If the money supply increases from 500 to 600 and the price level rises from 100 to 130, what happens to the real value of money?

Explanation: The real value of money is $1/P$. As the price level increases from 100 to 130, the purchasing power (value) of a unit of money drops from $1/100$ to $1/130$.

Question 17 of 18

Which of the following is considered secondary or derivative function of money?

Explanation: Primary functions are medium of exchange and measure of value. Standard of deferred payments, store of value, and transfer of value are secondary/derivative functions.

Question 18 of 18

What is the primary implication of the Classical Quantity Theory of Money regarding output?

Explanation: Classicals believed in money neutrality and full employment, meaning changes in money supply ($M$) affect only the price level ($P$), leaving real output ($Y$ or $T$) unchanged.

Rationale: At very low interest rates, bond prices are exceptionally high. Investors expect interest rates to rise and bond prices to fall, leading to massive capital losses on bonds. Hence, everyone prefers holding cash, creating a liquidity trap.

Rationale: When the central bank sells government securities in open market operations, commercial bank reserves or public currency held decrease, contracting High-Powered Money ($H$).
