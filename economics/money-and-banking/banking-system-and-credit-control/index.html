Money and Banking

Banking System: Commercial Banking, Credit Creation, Central Bank Functions, and Credit Control

This study note provides an exhaustive analysis of the banking mechanism, spanning commercial bank operations, the credit multiplier process, central banking institutional functions, and quantitative/qualitative credit control instruments. It is explicitly tailored for competitive examinations such as UP PGT Economics, UP TGT Social Science, UGC-NET, and State Assistant Professor tests.

🏛️ Key Economists & Landmark Publications

Adam Smith
1776, 'The Wealth of Nations'

Laid foundational doctrines on the nature of capital, banking liquidity, and the role of paper currency vs. metallic currency.

Walter Bagehot
1873, 'Lombard Street: A Description of the Money Market'

Formulated the classic central banking principle of the 'Lender of Last Resort', advising central banks to lend freely against good collateral at high penalty rates during panics.

John Maynard Keynes
1930, 'A Treatise on Money'

Expanded monetary theory by analyzing bank deposits, savings-investment equilibria, and the mechanics of credit expansion through the banking system.

Milton Friedman & Anna Schwartz
1963, 'A Monetary History of the United States, 1867-1960'

Demonstrated empirically the critical role of the money supply and Federal Reserve policy errors in precipitating and worsening economic depressions.

Chester A. Phillips
1920, 'Bank Credit'

Developed the modern rigorous algebraic formulation of the single bank vs. systemic multiple credit creation multiplier.

⚖️ Fundamental Assumptions & Hypotheses

  • Constant Cash Drain Ratio (c): Public behavior regarding the proportion of cash withdrawn from deposits remains fixed.
  • Fixed Excess Reserve Ratio (e): Commercial banks maintain a constant ratio of excess reserves relative to total deposits.
  • Single-Currency Closed Banking System: Absence of international capital leakages, international currency substitution, or foreign trade multipliers distorting domestic credit creation.
  • Homogeneous Reserve Requirements: Legal reserve ratios set by the central bank apply uniformly across all qualifying commercial bank deposit liabilities.
  • Absence of Structural Rationing Friction: Borrowers are fully creditworthy and loan demand is infinitely elastic at the prevailing market interest rate during the credit expansion phase.
  • Exogenous Central Bank Policy Base: High-powered money or the monetary base ($H$) is completely controlled by the monetary authority.

1. Commercial Banking: Core Operations and Liabilities-Assets Management

Commercial banks are financial intermediaries operating on the fractional reserve principle. Their balance sheets consist of liabilities (demand deposits, time deposits, borrowings) and assets (cash reserves, investments in government securities, and commercial loans). Profit maximization depends on asset portfolio management balancing liquidity, safety, and profitability.

Key Exam Takeaways:
  • Demand deposits constitute primary liabilities and form the basis of the active money supply.
  • The golden rule of commercial banking balances liquidity against profitability.
  • Primary deposits are cash injections by customers, whereas derivative deposits arise out of loans granted by the bank.

2. Credit Creation Mechanism: Single Bank vs. Multiple Expansion

While an individual commercial bank can only lend out its excess reserves (minus the required reserve ratio), the banking system as a whole multiplies initial cash reserves via the credit multiplier process. As loans are disbursed and redeposited recursively across the banking network, total deposit expansion reaches a multiple of the initial primary deposit.

Key Exam Takeaways:
  • The credit multiplier formula is given by $K = 1 / r$, where $r$ is the legal reserve ratio.
  • A single bank can create credit equal to its excess reserves, whereas the banking system expands credit by $1/r$ times the initial deposit.
  • Leakages in credit creation occur due to cash drains, excess reserves held by banks, and inactive time deposits.

3. Central Bank Functions: Traditional, Developmental, and Supervisory Roles

The central bank (e.g., Reserve Bank of India) stands at the apex of the monetary hierarchy. Traditional functions include issuing currency, acting as banker to the government, serving as banker's bank, managing foreign exchange reserves, and acting as lender of last resort. Modern central banks also shoulder developmental and promotional roles in emerging economies.

Key Exam Takeaways:
  • Monopoly of note issue ensures uniformity in legal tender and elasticity of currency supply.
  • As 'Lender of Last Resort', the central bank prevents systemic banking panics by discounting eligible paper during liquidity squeezes.
  • Clearinghouse operations streamline inter-bank settlement of checks and electronic transfers.

4. Quantitative and Qualitative Instruments of Credit Control

Central banks regulate money supply and credit volume using quantitative (general) and qualitative (selective) instruments. Quantitative tools influence the aggregate volume of credit (Bank Rate, Open Market Operations, Cash Reserve Ratio, Statutory Liquidity Ratio). Qualitative tools direct credit toward specific sectors (Margin Requirements, Moral Suasion, Direct Action, Rationing of Credit).

Key Exam Takeaways:
  • CRR and SLR are statutory liquidity constraints; an increase in either contracts credit creation capacity.
  • Repo Rate is the rate at which the central bank lends short-term funds to commercial banks against government securities.
  • Selective Credit Controls (SCC) are particularly effective in curbing speculative hoarding of essential commodities during inflation.

📐 Mathematical Formulations & Equilibrium Conditions

Simple Credit Multiplier Formula
M = 1r × D0

Measures total money or deposit creation ($M$) generated from an initial primary cash deposit ($D_0$) given a legal reserve ratio ($r$).

Money Multiplier (High-Powered Money Framework)
m = 1 + c(r + e + c)

Links the broad money supply ($M$) to high-powered money ($H$), where $c$ is currency-deposit ratio, $r$ is reserve ratio, and $e$ is excess reserve ratio.

Bank Net Interest Margin (NIM)
NIM = Interest Earned - Interest ExpendedTotal Earning Assets

Measures the profitability of a commercial bank's intermediation operations between lending and borrowing rates.

Statutory Liquidity Ratio (SLR) Requirement
SLR Assets ≥ SLR Rate × Net Demand and Time Liabilities (NDTL)

Ensures that commercial banks maintain a specified fraction of their NDTL in liquid assets such as gold, approved government securities, or cash.

🧠 Exam Tips, Tricks & Mnemonics (10-Second Shortcuts)

💡 Mnemonic for Quantitative Credit Control Tools B-O-C-S-R (Bankers Often Control Supply Rigorously)
Remember the five primary quantitative tools: Bank Rate, Open Market Operations, Cash Reserve Ratio, Statutory Liquidity Ratio, and Repo/Reverse Repo Rates.

Exam Application: Instantly eliminate incorrect options in matching questions asking to identify quantitative vs. qualitative central bank instruments.

💡 Credit Multiplier Calculation Trick Multiplication is Inverse of Reserve Ratio
If the reserve ratio is given as a percentage (e.g., 10%), convert to decimal (0.10) and take its reciprocal ($1/0.10 = 10$). Multiply this by the primary deposit change.

Exam Application: Saves critical seconds in numerical PGT/NET questions regarding total credit creation.

💡 Directional Impact of Monetary Policy DEAR Money = DECREASE Credit, CHEAP Money = INCREASE Credit
Dear (tight) money policy raises rates and reserve ratios to combat inflation; Cheap (expansionary) money policy cuts rates to fight recession.

Exam Application: Quickly verify statements regarding central bank policy actions during inflationary vs. deflationary gaps.

⚠️ Economic Paradoxes & Boundary Exceptions

Exception / Paradox Paradox of Thrift in Banking

Conditions: When households attempt to increase their net savings simultaneously during a downturn, aggregate demand collapses, leading to lower aggregate income and paradoxically lower total actual savings.

Diagrammatic Behavior: Downward shift in the aggregate consumption/savings schedule resulting in a lower Keynesian equilibrium income level.

Exception / Paradox Liquidity Trap

Conditions: When the nominal interest rate falls to near zero and public expectations anticipate bond prices to fall (interest rates to rise), the demand for money becomes perfectly elastic.

Diagrammatic Behavior: The LM curve or the money demand schedule becomes horizontal at a minimum interest rate floor, rendering monetary policy completely ineffective.

Exception / Paradox Credit Rationing Under Asymmetric Information

Conditions: Banks refuse to raise interest rates to clear excess demand for loans because higher rates attract riskier borrowers (adverse selection) and induce moral hazard.

Diagrammatic Behavior: Loan supply curve bends backward or truncates at an optimal interest rate, creating persistent credit rationing.

⚡ Must-Remember Economic Identities & Laws

  • Credit Multiplier Formula: k = 1LRR = 1CRR + SLR
  • Total Credit Creation Formula: Total Credit = Initial Deposit × 1LRR
  • Total Deposit Expansion Identity: Δ D = 1LRR × Δ A, where Δ D is change in total deposits and Δ A is initial cash injection.
  • Net Demand and Time Liabilities (NDTL) Equation: NDTL = Demand Deposits + Time Deposits + Other Interbank Adjustments
  • Equation of Exchange (Quantity Theory linkage): M · V = P · T, where M is money supply managed via Central Bank credit controls.
  • Money Multiplier linking Monetary Base to Money Supply: M = m · MB, where m = 1 + cc + r (c is currency-deposit ratio, r is reserve-deposit ratio).

🚫 Common Analytical Confusions (Contrast Table)

Bank Rate vs Repo Rate VS Short-Term vs Long-Term Central Bank Lending

Bank Rate is the discount rate applied to long-term accommodations or rediscounting of bills without collateral, whereas Repo Rate is a short-term collateralized lending rate used extensively in modern liquidity management.

Quantitative vs Qualitative Credit Control VS General vs Selective Credit Regulation

Quantitative instruments (CRR, SLR, Repo, OMO) alter the total volume and overall cost of money supply across the entire economy, whereas qualitative instruments (Margin Requirements, Moral Suasion, Rationing) regulate the allocation and direction of credit to specific sectors.

Primary Deposit vs Derivative Deposit VS Initial Cash vs Multiplied Credit

Primary deposits represent actual cash brought in by the public that increases the monetary base, while derivative deposits are created through bookkeeping entries when banks disburse loans.

CRR vs SLR VS Cash with Central Bank vs Liquid Assets with Banks

CRR requires banks to keep cash reserves strictly deposited with the Central Bank (earning zero or negligible interest), whereas SLR requires maintaining liquid assets in the form of approved securities or gold within the bank's own vaults or accounts, earning yields.

📚 Complete Concept-by-Concept Glossary

Every fundamental term, curve, and mechanism covered in this chapter.

Commercial Bank: A financial intermediary that accepts deposits from the public and extends loans to households and firms, operating primarily for profit and creating credit through fractional reserve banking.
Credit Creation: The process by which commercial banks multiply initial primary deposits into a much larger volume of total demand deposits via successive loans and repayments throughout the banking system.
Legal Reserve Ratio (LRR): The minimum fraction of total deposits that commercial banks are legally mandated to keep as liquid reserves, consisting of the Cash Reserve Ratio (CRR) and Statutory Liquidity Ratio (SLR).
Cash Reserve Ratio (CRR): The specific percentage of total demand and time liabilities (NDTL) that commercial banks must maintain in cash with the Central Bank.
Statutory Liquidity Ratio (SLR): The minimum percentage of NDTL that commercial banks must maintain in liquid assets such as approved government securities, gold, or cash directly with themselves.
Credit Multiplier: The reciprocal of the Legal Reserve Ratio (1/LRR), indicating the maximum multiple by which initial primary deposits expand into total credit within the banking system.
Central Bank: The apex monetary institution of a country responsible for formulating and implementing monetary policy, regulating the financial system, issuing currency, and acting as lender of last resort.
Bank Rate: The standard rate at which the Central Bank is prepared to buy or rediscount bills of exchange or lend long-term funds to commercial banks without collateral.
Repo Rate: The short-term interest rate at which the Central Bank lends money to commercial banks against eligible government securities, typically under repurchase agreements.
Reverse Repo Rate: The rate at which the Central Bank borrows funds from commercial banks or absorbs excess liquidity from the system by parking government securities.
Open Market Operations (OMO): The outright sale or purchase of government securities by the Central Bank in the open market to contract or expand money supply in the economy.
Margin Requirement: The difference between the market value of the security offered for a loan and the maximum amount of loan granted against it by a commercial bank.
Moral Suasion: A qualitative method of credit control where the Central Bank uses informal persuasion, pressure, or requests to guide commercial banks to align their lending practices with national monetary policy.
Lender of Last Resort: The crucial function of the Central Bank where it provides emergency financial assistance and liquidity support to solvent commercial banks facing acute temporary runs or liquidity crises.

Question 1 of 18

If the Legal Reserve Ratio (LRR) is 20%, what will be the total credit creation in the banking system given an initial primary cash deposit of $1,000?

Explanation: The credit multiplier is calculated as $k = \frac{1}{LRR} = \frac{1}{0.20} = 5$. Total credit creation equals Initial Deposit $\times$ Multiplier = $1,000 \times 5 = $5,000.

Question 2 of 18

Which of the following components constitutes the Legal Reserve Ratio (LRR) mandated for commercial banks?

Explanation: The Legal Reserve Ratio (LRR) is composed of the Cash Reserve Ratio (CRR), which is kept with the Central Bank, and the Statutory Liquidity Ratio (SLR), which is maintained by the bank itself.

Question 3 of 18

In the context of Central Bank monetary policy, an increase in the Repo Rate will result in:

Explanation: An increase in the Repo Rate makes borrowing from the Central Bank expensive for commercial banks. Consequently, banks raise their lending rates to the public, contracting credit and money supply.

Question 4 of 18

Open Market Operations (OMO) conducted by a Central Bank involve:

Explanation: Open Market Operations refer to the direct sale or purchase of government securities by the Central Bank to regulate liquidity and money supply in the economy.

Question 5 of 18

What is the primary effect of raising the Margin Requirement on loans by the Central Bank?

Explanation: Raising the margin requirement increases the gap between the loan value and collateral value, reducing the amount a borrower can secure, thereby curbing inflationary credit expansion.

Question 6 of 18

Which of the following is NOT a traditional function of a Central Bank?

Explanation: Central banks do not deal with the general public for retail deposit acceptance. Commercial banks accept public deposits, while central banks manage monetary policy and act as bankers to commercial banks and governments.

Question 7 of 18

If the credit multiplier in an economy is 4, what is the prevailing Legal Reserve Ratio (LRR)?

Explanation: The credit multiplier $k = \frac{1}{LRR}$. Given $k = 4$, solving for LRR gives $LRR = \frac{1}{4} = 0.25$ or 25%.

Question 8 of 18

Which qualitative instrument of credit control involves direct instructions or rationing of credit to specific sectors?

Explanation: Credit Rationing is a selective/qualitative tool where the Central Bank fixes maximum limits for loans and advances or rations credit for specific commercial sectors.

Question 9 of 18

The process of credit creation by commercial banks assumes that:

Explanation: The standard textbook multiple expansion model assumes that all loan proceeds are redeposited into the banking system and transactions occur entirely through bank accounts (banking habits).

Question 10 of 18

When a Central Bank purchases government securities from the open market, it leads to:

Explanation: Purchasing securities injects cash into the financial system, increasing commercial banks' reserves and empowering them to create more credit, thus expanding the money supply.

Question 11 of 18

The rate at which the Central Bank absorbs liquidity from commercial banks by selling government securities under an agreement to repurchase them later is known as:

Explanation: The Reverse Repo Rate is the interest rate at which commercial banks park their excess short-term funds with the Central Bank.

Question 12 of 18

Assertion (A): Central banks act as the lender of last resort during financial panics. Reason (R): They provide liquidity to solvent commercial banks facing temporary deposit runs to prevent systemic collapse.

Explanation: The lender of last resort function exists precisely because central banks step in to provide emergency liquidity to prevent bank runs from translating into catastrophic systemic banking failures.

Question 13 of 18

Which of the following groups comprises exclusively quantitative instruments of credit control?

Explanation: Bank Rate, Open Market Operations, and CRR (along with SLR and Repo) are quantitative instruments because they regulate the overall aggregate volume of credit.

Question 14 of 18

If a Central Bank wants to combat severe inflation, which combination of policy measures should it adopt?

Explanation: To curb inflation, the Central Bank must contract money supply by raising reserve ratios (CRR), selling government securities (OMO), and increasing lending rates (Repo/Bank Rate).

Question 15 of 18

What is the economic significance of the 'Monetary Base' (High-Powered Money)?

Explanation: High-powered money ($H = C + R$) serves as the foundational base upon which the entire superstructure of commercial bank credit creation rests.

Question 16 of 18

Which institution regulates the banking system in India under the Banking Regulation Act of 1949?

Explanation: The Reserve Bank of India (RBI) serves as the central regulatory authority for commercial banking operations in India under statutory powers derived from the RBI Act 1934 and Banking Regulation Act 1949.

Question 17 of 18

If initial deposits are $500 and the LRR is 10%, what is the amount of derivative (secondary) deposits created in the system?

Explanation: Total deposits = $\frac{500}{0.10} = $5,000. Derivative deposits = Total Deposits - Initial Deposits = $5,000 - $500 = $4,500.

Question 18 of 18

Which of the following statements about Statutory Liquidity Ratio (SLR) is correct?

Explanation: SLR is maintained in liquid assets such as government securities, which earn interest for commercial banks, unlike CRR balances kept with the Central Bank.

Test Item 3 of 10

Which of the following correctly describes the operation of the 'Repo Rate' mechanism?

Rationale: A repo (repurchase agreement) involves commercial banks selling government securities to the central bank with a simultaneous agreement to buy them back at a predetermined future date and rate.

Test Item 4 of 10

During an economic recession, which monetary policy combination is most appropriate for a Central Bank?

Rationale: To stimulate economic activity during a recession, the Central Bank adopts an expansionary monetary policy by lowering reserve requirements, purchasing securities in OMO, and reducing policy rates.

What is the primary distinction between the Bank Rate and the Repo Rate in contemporary central banking?

Rationale: Bank Rate traditionally governs long-term lending and rediscounting of bills without collateral, whereas Repo Rate is the primary short-term policy rate backed by collateralized repurchase agreements.

Test Item 6 of 10

Rationale: Imposing credit ceilings or quotas on specific sectors (like stock market speculation) is a classic example of selective or qualitative credit control known as credit rationing.
