Growth and Development

Theories of Economic Growth: Harrod-Domar, Arthur Lewis, and Rostow Stages

This study note provides an exhaustive theoretical examination of the Harrod-Domar model, Arthur Lewis's dual-sector model, and W.W. Rostow's stages of economic growth. Essential for UP PGT Economics, TGT Social Science, UGC-NET, and State Assistant Professor examinations, it integrates foundational definitions, mathematical formulations, assumptions, and critical exam-oriented mnemonics.

🏛️ Key Economists & Landmark Publications

Sir Roy F. Harrod
1939, 'An Essay in Dynamic Theory', Economic Journal

Introduced the dynamic counterpart to Keynesian static equilibrium, formulating the warranted rate of growth and the unstable knife-edge equilibrium.

Evsey D. Domar
1946, 'Expansion and Employment', American Economic Review

Independently derived growth conditions emphasizing the dual character of investment: creating income via multiplier and increasing productive capacity via accelerator.

W. Arthur Lewis
1954, 'Economic Development with Unlimited Supplies of Labour', The Manchester School

Developed the classic dual-sector model of economic development characterized by a traditional subsistence agricultural sector and a modern capitalist industrial sector.

Walt Whitman Rostow
1960, 'The Stages of Economic Growth: A Non-Communist Manifesto'

Proposed a historical-linear theory mapping every economy's trajectory across five distinct sequential stages from traditional society to high mass-consumption.

⚖️ Fundamental Assumptions & Hypotheses

  • Fixed Proportions Production Function: Capital-output ratio (v) and labor-output ratio are assumed constant, ruling out factor substitution in the short run (Harrod-Domar).
  • Closed Economy: Absence of international trade and foreign capital flows, simplifying the models to domestic saving-investment interactions.
  • Constant Marginal Propensity to Save (s): Savings are assumed to be a fixed, constant proportion of aggregate national income (Harrod-Domar).
  • Unlimited Labor Supply: In the Lewis model, the traditional agricultural sector has zero or negligible marginal product of labor, providing an infinitely elastic supply of labor at a subsistence wage.
  • Capital Accumulation as Engine: Growth in output is fundamentally driven by net capital formation and reinvestment of capitalist profits (Lewis model and Harrod-Domar).
  • Linear-Staged Progression: Rostow assumes all developing economies pass through the same identical linear sequence of historical growth milestones.

1. The Harrod-Domar Model: Growth Mechanics and Equilibrium Rates

The Harrod-Domar model integrates Keynesian income determination with capital accumulation. It defines three distinct growth rates: Actual growth rate (g), Warranted growth rate (g_w), and Natural growth rate (g_n). The warranted growth rate keeps the capital market in continuous equilibrium (s = v * g_w). The natural growth rate is determined by population growth and technological progress. Harrod's 'knife-edge' proposition demonstrates that any divergence between g and g_w leads to cumulative and unstable departures (secular stagnation or runaway inflation), as capital-output ratios are rigid.

Key Exam Takeaways:
  • Warranted growth rate formula: g_w = s / v, where s is saving rate and v is capital-output ratio.
  • Knife-edge instability problem arises due to the fixity of capital-output and savings ratios.
  • Domar's contribution focuses on the dual capacity of investment: generating aggregate demand (multiplier) and aggregate supply (productive capacity).

2. Arthur Lewis Dual-Sector Model: Unlimited Labor Supplies and Capital Accumulation

W. Arthur Lewis formulated a two-sector model comprising a backward, overpopulated subsistence agricultural sector with zero marginal productivity of labor, and a dynamic capitalist industrial sector. Capitalists extract surplus value because wages in the modern sector are set at a slight premium over institutional subsistence agricultural wages. Reinvestment of capitalist profits expands the modern sector, absorbing surplus rural labor. The 'turning point' occurs when the surplus labor is completely exhausted, and the labor supply curve shifts from perfectly elastic to upward sloping.

Key Exam Takeaways:
  • Subsistence wage in the capitalist sector is determined by average productivity in agriculture plus a margin.
  • Capitalist surplus is reinvested, shifting the labor demand curve rightward and causing continuous capital accumulation.
  • The 'Lewis Turning Point' marks the exhaustion of the unlimited labor supply and the beginning of rising real wages.

3. W.W. Rostow Stages of Economic Growth: Historical Take-Off

Rostow presented a historical theory of development viewing growth as a sequence of five stages: (1) Traditional Society, characterized by pre-Newtonian science and primitive production; (2) Preconditions for Take-off, marked by the rise of modern science, centralized states, and social overhead capital; (3) The Take-off, the crucial watershed decade where net investment rises from 5% to over 10% of national income, overcoming structural blockages; (4) Drive to Maturity, where economic growth becomes self-sustained and technologically diversified; and (5) Age of High Mass-Consumption, where attention shifts to welfare and durable consumer goods.

Key Exam Takeaways:
  • The 'Take-off' stage requires a surge in net investment to over 10% of GNP alongside the emergence of leading manufacturing sectors.
  • Rostow explicitly termed his work 'A Non-Communist Manifesto' as a historical alternative to Marxist historical materialism.
  • Criticized heavily for determinism, teleology, and ignoring international capitalist exploitation (dependency theory).

4. Comparative Policy Implications and Limitations of Early Growth Models

The Harrod-Domar framework justified state-led capital imports, foreign aid, and heavy industrial planning to bridge domestic savings gaps (Two-Gap Model of Chenery and Strout). The Lewis model underpinned import-substituting industrialization policies by prioritizing urban-industrial capital formation over rural agriculture. Both models were heavily criticized post-1970s for neglecting human capital, institutional frameworks, income inequality, and environmental sustainability, paving the way for Endogenous Growth Theory (Romer-Lucas).

Key Exam Takeaways:
  • Harrod-Domar directly inspired foreign aid programs and foreign exchange/savings gap models.
  • Lewis model assumes profits are entirely reinvested and wages are consumed, ignoring worker savings.
  • Both models lack endogenous technological change, treating tech progress as an exogenous residual.

📐 Mathematical Formulations & Equilibrium Conditions

Domar's Equilibrium Condition for Capacity Growth
Δ YY = s · σ

Rate of growth of national income (ΔY/Y) equals the marginal propensity to save (s) multiplied by the social productivity of capital (σ = ΔY/I).

Harrod's Warranted Growth Rate
gw = sCr

The rate of output growth that maintains full capacity utilization of capital, where s is savings ratio and C_r is desired capital-output ratio.

Harrod's Natural Growth Rate
gn = n + λ

The maximum sustainable growth rate limited by workforce growth rate (n) plus rate of labor-augmenting technological progress (λ).

Harrod's Knife-Edge Condition
g ≠ gw ⇒ g \gtrless gw leads to cumulative divergence

Demonstrates instability: if actual growth exceeds warranted growth (g > g_w), capital is deficient, triggering chronic boom/hyper-inflation; if g < g_w, secular depression occurs.

🧠 Exam Tips, Tricks & Mnemonics (10-Second Shortcuts)

💡 Harrod's Growth Rate Mnemonic A-W-N (Actual, Warranted, Natural)
Remember the tri-partite growth rates: g (Actual), g_w (Warranted = s/v), g_n (Natural = n + λ).

Exam Application: Quickly identify questions testing dynamic equilibrium conditions where g = g_w = g_n defines the golden path.

💡 Rostow's 5 Stages Memory Trick T-P-T-D-H
Traditional -> Preconditions -> Take-off -> Drive to maturity -> High mass-consumption.

Exam Application: Instantly answer sequencing and matching questions in UP PGT and UGC-NET exams by recalling the acronym T-P-T-D-H.

💡 Lewis Model Core Shortcut AG-IND-SURP
Agriculture has surplus labor (MP=0), Industry pays subsistence + premium, profits drive accumulation.

Exam Application: Fast-track questions identifying the source of capitalist surplus and the definition of the Lewis turning point.

💡 Domar Dual Nature Trick Demand = Multiplier, Supply = Sigma (Capacity)
Investment creates income on demand side, but builds capital stock on supply side.

Exam Application: Solve assertion-reasoning questions regarding why investment expansion can paradoxically cause excess capacity if output doesn't match.

⚠️ Economic Paradoxes & Boundary Exceptions

Exception / Paradox Harrod's Knife-Edge Instability Paradox

Conditions: Rigid fixed-coefficient production functions prevent automatic price adjustments in factor markets from restoring equilibrium.

Diagrammatic Behavior: A single unstable equilibrium point where any minimal disturbance pushes the actual growth path permanently away from the warranted growth path.

Exception / Paradox Lewis Zero Marginal Product Paradox

Conditions: Surplus agricultural labor has zero marginal product, meaning rural output remains completely unchanged even when workers migrate to cities.

Diagrammatic Behavior: Horizontal total agricultural output curve relative to labor withdrawal up to the critical threshold point.

Exception / Paradox Rostow's Teleological Historical Trap

Conditions: Assumes all countries follow an identical, unilinear trajectory, failing to account for colonial extraction, unequal exchange, and institutional path dependence.

Diagrammatic Behavior: A rigid step-function ladder of development where regression or arrested development is theoretically unexplained.

⚡ Must-Remember Economic Identities & Laws

  • Harrod-Domar Fundamental Equation: G × v = s, where G is growth rate (Δ Y/Y), v is capital-output ratio (K/Y), and s is saving propensity (S/Y).
  • Warranted Growth Formula: Gw = s / v, where s is the average/marginal propensity to save and v is the desired capital-output ratio.
  • Harrod Stability Condition: When G > Gw, induced investment exceeds planned saving, leading to cumulative inflationary spirals; when G < Gw, secular depression ensues.
  • Full Employment Equilibrium Condition in Harrod Model: G = Gw = Gn, combining dynamic equilibrium with full employment of labor.
  • Lewis Capitalist Sector Equilibrium: Real wage is determined by institutional subsistence earnings in agriculture plus a constant percentage premium to induce migration.
  • Rostow Take-Off Investment Threshold: Net domestic capital formation must rise from under 5% to 10% or more of national income.

🚫 Common Analytical Confusions (Contrast Table)

Warranted Growth Rate ($G_w$) VS Natural Growth Rate ($G_n$)

$G_w$ is determined by the saving rate and capital-output ratio ensuring equilibrium in the goods market (entrepreneurial equilibrium), whereas $G_n$ is determined by population growth and technological progress ensuring full employment of the labor force.

Harrod-Domar Model VS Solow-Swan Neo-Classical Model

Harrod-Domar assumes a fixed capital-output ratio and fixed proportions (Leontief production function) leading to instability, whereas Solow-Swan incorporates variable factor proportions and smooth substitution between labor and capital, eliminating the knife-edge instability.

Lewis Turning Point VS Ranis-Fei Critical Sub-phase

The Lewis turning point specifically marks the exhaustion of the unlimited labor supply where the horizontal labor supply curve starts to slope upward. Ranis-Fei further subdivides this by analyzing the commercialization point in agriculture when agricultural surplus matches industrial labor demand.

Traditional Society (Rostow) VS Preconditions for Take-off (Rostow)

Traditional society is bound by pre-Newtonian science with low productivity ceilings and ceiling on output per head. Preconditions represent the transitional phase where modern science is applied to agriculture and transport, building social overhead capital.

📚 Complete Concept-by-Concept Glossary

Every fundamental term, curve, and mechanism covered in this chapter.

Harrod-Domar Model: A Keynesian-based dynamic model of economic growth emphasizing that the rate of economic growth depends directly on the saving rate and inversely on the capital-output ratio.
Warranted Growth Rate ($G_w$): The growth rate of output at which the economy's planned saving equals planned investment, keeping entrepreneurs satisfied with their level of capital accumulation.
Natural Growth Rate ($G_n$): The maximum rate of growth that an economy can sustain, determined by the rate of growth of the labor force and technological progress, ensuring full employment.
Actual Growth Rate ($G$): The actual percentage change in real GDP realized by an economy in a given period, which can diverge from both the warranted and natural growth rates.
Knife-Edge Equilibrium: Harrod's proposition that economic equilibrium between $G$, $G_w$, and $G_n$ is unstable; any slight departure leads to cumulative inflation or secular depression.
Capital-Output Ratio ($v$): The ratio of total capital stock to total output ($K/Y$), or its incremental counterpart ($ICOR = Delta K/Delta Y$), representing the productivity of capital.
Arthur Lewis Dual-Sector Model: A development economics model dividing the economy into a traditional subsistence agricultural sector (unlimited surplus labor) and a modern capitalist sector.
Unlimited Supply of Labor: A condition in the Lewis model where labor is available in virtually limitless quantities at a constant real wage slightly above subsistence level due to zero or negligible marginal productivity in agriculture.
Capitalist Surplus: The excess of total output over wages in the modern capitalist sector, which is entirely reinvested to expand capital stock and absorb surplus labor.
Lewis Turning Point: The critical juncture in economic development when the surplus labor from the traditional agricultural sector is exhausted, causing real wages in the capitalist sector to start rising.
Rostow Stages of Economic Growth: A historical, linear model outlining five stages through which all countries must pass as they transition from traditional societies to high mass consumption.
The Take-Off Stage: The critical transitional stage in Rostow's model where old blocks and resistances to growth are overcome, net investment rises to over 10% of GDP, and self-sustained growth is established.
Drive to Maturity: Rostow's fourth stage where the economy demonstrates the capacity to move beyond the original industries that powered take-off, applying modern technology to the whole economic spectrum.
Age of High Mass Consumption: The final stage of Rostow's model where western societies shift toward durable consumer goods and services, and the state devotes increased resources to social welfare.

Question 1 of 18

In the Harrod-Domar growth model, what does the condition $G > G_w$ imply for the economy?

Explanation: When actual growth ($G$) exceeds the warranted growth rate ($G_w$), actual output exceeds planned demand. This creates a shortage of capital goods, inducing higher investment that outstrips saving, leading to cumulative upward price movements and inflation.

Question 2 of 18

Which of the following best describes the 'knife-edge' property of the Harrod model?

Explanation: Harrod's model assumes fixed coefficients ($v$ is constant) and adaptive/static expectations. Without price flexibility to equilibrate factor markets, any divergence between $G$ and $G_w$ triggers cumulative expansion or contraction, making equilibrium as precarious as walking on a knife-edge.

Question 3 of 18

In Domar's formulation of the growth model, what dual role does investment play?

Explanation: Domar emphasized that investment acts on both sides of the ledger: through the multiplier it creates aggregate demand and income, and through capital accumulation it expands the economy's productive capacity.

Question 4 of 18

According to Arthur Lewis, what is the primary source of capital accumulation in the capitalist sector?

Explanation: In the Lewis model, capitalists do not consume their entire profits; they reinvest the capitalist surplus (profits minus consumption) back into capital stock, driving economic expansion.

Question 5 of 18

What happens to the real wage in the modern capitalist sector during the unlimited labor supply phase of the Lewis model?

Explanation: As long as surplus labor exists in agriculture with zero marginal productivity, the capitalist sector can draw unlimited workers at a constant real wage determined by subsistence earnings plus a migration premium.

Question 6 of 18

The 'Lewis Turning Point' is reached when:

Explanation: The Lewis turning point marks the depletion of disguised unemployment in agriculture. Beyond this point, further industrial labor absorption requires higher wages to draw workers away from productive farming, causing the labor supply curve to slope upward.

Question 7 of 18

Which of the following is NOT one of W.W. Rostow’s stages of economic growth?

Explanation: Rostow’s five stages are: (1) Traditional Society, (2) Preconditions for Take-off, (3) Take-off, (4) Drive to Maturity, and (5) Age of High Mass Consumption. 'Bureaucratic centralism' is not part of his sequence.

Question 8 of 18

What quantitative criterion did Rostow assign to define the 'Take-Off' stage?

Explanation: Rostow defined the take-off period by a sharp rise in the rate of productive investment from under 5% to over 10% of net national product (or national income), alongside the rapid growth of one or more leading manufacturing sectors.

Question 9 of 18

A major limitation of the Harrod-Domar model in developing country policy formulation is:

Explanation: The Harrod-Domar model assumes fixed proportions between capital and output ($v$ is constant) and a fixed saving rate ($s$). In reality, factor substitution is possible, and saving rates respond to income levels and distribution.

Question 10 of 18

In the Lewis model, what assumption is made regarding the marginal productivity of labor in the traditional agricultural sector during the initial phase?

Explanation: Lewis assumed that traditional agriculture features disguised unemployment where the marginal product of labor is zero or close to zero, meaning workers can be withdrawn without reducing total agricultural output.

Question 11 of 18

Assertion (A): In the Harrod-Domar framework, economic growth is directly proportional to the saving rate and inversely proportional to the capital-output ratio. Reason (R): The fundamental equation $G = s/v$ dictates that higher savings and lower capital-output ratios accelerate output growth.

Explanation: From $G imes v = s$, rearranging gives $G = s/v$. Thus, growth $G$ varies directly with $s$ and inversely with $v$. Both assertion and reason are correct and correctly linked.

Question 12 of 18

Which economist criticized the Lewis model for ignoring the possibility that real wages in the modern sector might rise before the turning point due to trade union pressure?

Explanation: Development economists like Hla Myint, as well as subsequent elaborations by Ranis and Fei, noted that institutional pressures, trade unions, and higher urban living costs often drive up modern sector wages prematurely, before surplus labor is exhausted.

Question 13 of 18

According to Rostow, the emergence of 'leading sectors' is most characteristic of which stage?

Explanation: The take-off stage is driven by the rapid expansion of one or more manufacturing sectors (leading sectors) whose direct and indirect effects stimulate overall economic dynamism.

Question 14 of 18

If the saving rate $s = 0.20$ and the incremental capital-output ratio $v = 4$, what is the warranted growth rate ($G_w$) according to the Harrod model?

Explanation: Using the formula $G_w = s / v$, we get $G_w = 0.20 / 4 = 0.05$ or $5%$.

Question 15 of 18

Which of the following models is best classified as a 'stages of growth' historical approach rather than a formal mathematical growth model?

Explanation: Rostow’s model is a historical, descriptive framework classifying economic evolution into sequential stages, unlike Harrod-Domar and Solow-Swan which are formal mathematical models.

Question 16 of 18

In the context of Arthur Lewis's dual-sector model, 'capitalist' refers to:

Explanation: Lewis used 'capitalist sector' to designate that part of the economy which uses reproducible capital and employs labor for profit, which can encompass private enterprises, cooperatives, or state-owned industrial undertakings.

Question 17 of 18

What is the primary policy implication derived from the Harrod-Domar model for developing nations seeking rapid growth?

Explanation: Since $G = s/v$, and $v$ is relatively fixed in the short-to-medium run, the key policy lever for developing countries is to raise the domestic saving rate ($s$) or secure foreign aid/investment to bridge the savings gap.

Question 18 of 18

Which phenomenon did Arthur Lewis use to explain why terms of trade historically tended to turn against primary-producing developing nations?

Explanation: Lewis argued that the existence of unlimited labor supplies in developing countries prevents wages from rising, transferring productivity gains in export agriculture to lower prices for developed countries, thereby worsening developing nations' terms of trade.

Previous Year Pattern • Q5

[UGC NET Economics] Given a saving rate $s = 0.15$ and capital-output ratio $v = 3$, what is the warranted rate of growth in the Harrod model?

A. 3%
B. 5%
C. 15%
D. 45%
Solution: Step-by-step solution: 1. The formula for the warranted growth rate in the Harrod model is $G_w = s / v$. 2. Substituting the given values: $s = 0.15$ and $v = 3$. 3. $G_w = 0.15 / 3 = 0.05$. 4. Expressed as a percentage, $0.05 imes 100% = 5%$. Therefore, option 2 is correct.

Test Item 1 of 10

In the Harrod-Domar framework, what condition must hold simultaneously for the economy to achieve steady-state growth with full employment?

Rationale: Steady-state equilibrium with full employment requires that the actual growth rate ($G$), the warranted growth rate ($G_w$), and the natural growth rate ($G_n$) all be equal ($G = G_w = G_n$). If $G_w eq G_n$, the economy experiences either chronic unemployment or secular inflation.

Test Item 2 of 10

Which of the following best characterizes the transition mechanism in the Arthur Lewis model?

Rationale: The core mechanism of the Lewis model is capital accumulation in the capitalist sector funded by reinvested profits. This increases labor demand, pulling surplus labor out of subsistence agriculture where marginal product is zero.

Test Item 3 of 10

If an economy's capital stock is $K = 500$ and total output is $Y = 100$, what is the average capital-output ratio ($v$)?

Rationale: The capital-output ratio $v$ is calculated as $K / Y$. Given $K = 500$ and $Y = 100$, $v = 500 / 100 = 5.0$.

Test Item 4 of 10

According to W.W. Rostow, which stage is marked by the dominance of complex technology, international economic competitiveness, and roughly 10-20% of national income regularly reinvested?

Rationale: In the 'Drive to Maturity' (Stage 4), the economy demonstrates technological maturity across a wide front, investing 10-20% of its national income regularly to produce a wide range of goods efficiently.

Test Item 5 of 10

Why is the Harrod-Domar model often categorized as 'instability growth theory'?

Rationale: Harrod's knife-edge theorem proves that without price-wage flexibility or variable factor substitution, any deviation of actual growth from the warranted growth rate sets off cumulative divergence, making steady growth highly unstable.

Test Item 6 of 10

In the context of the dual-sector model, what happens to the average product of labor in agriculture as surplus workers are withdrawn during the unlimited labor phase?

Rationale: Since surplus labor has a zero marginal product, withdrawing workers leaves total agricultural output constant while reducing the denominator (total agricultural workers). Consequently, the average product of labor in agriculture rises.

Test Item 7 of 10

Consider the Harrod equation $G imes v = s$. If the saving rate $s$ increases from 0.10 to 0.15, while $v$ remains constant at 3, what is the new warranted growth rate ($G_w$)?

Rationale: Using $G_w = s / v$, with the new saving rate $s = 0.15$ and $v = 3$, $G_w = 0.15 / 3 = 0.05$ or $5.00%$.

Test Item 8 of 10

Which of the following criticisms is most frequently leveled against Rostow's stages of growth model?

Rationale: Rostow's model is heavily criticized for being unilinear and Eurocentric, implying all countries must follow the exact historical trajectory of Western nations while ignoring global imperialism, unequal trade, and institutional diversity.

Test Item 9 of 10

In Domar's model, the condition for steady economic growth requires that the relative rate of growth of net investment equals:

Rationale: Domar's capacity equation requires that $Delta Y / Y = \alpha \beta$, where $\alpha$ is the marginal propensity to save (or propensity to save out of national income) and $\beta$ is the productivity of capital ($Delta Y / I$). Thus, investment must grow at a rate equal to the product of the saving propensity and capital productivity.

Test Item 10 of 10

What primary institutional change distinguishes Rostow's 'Preconditions for Take-Off' stage from a 'Traditional Society'?

Rationale: The preconditions stage involves building social overhead capital (transport, ports), establishing financial institutions, and transforming agriculture through modern techniques, moving away from the static science ceiling of traditional society.
