Growth and Development

Circulation-Causation Theory

The Circulation-Causation Theory, pioneered by Gunnar Myrdal, explains regional inequalities and underdevelopment through the mechanism of cumulative causation and circular interdependence. It asserts that market forces unhindered by institutional intervention tend to increase, rather than decrease, economic disparities between regions.

🏛️ Key Economists & Landmark Publications

Gunnar Myrdal
1957, 'Economic Theory and Under-developed Regions'

Introduced the Principle of Circular and Cumulative Causation to explain international and regional economic inequalities.

Nicholas Kaldor
1970, 'The Case for Regional Policies'

Applied circular causation models to manufacturing growth and increasing returns, developing export-led regional growth models.

Albert O. Hirschman
1958, 'The Strategy of Economic Development'

Formulated the concepts of 'Polarization Effects' (backwash) and 'Trickling-Down Effects' as parallel mechanisms to Myrdal's backwash and spread effects.

Allyn Young
1928, 'Increasing Returns and Economic Progress'

Provided the foundational insight that the size of the market is determined by the volume of production, feeding circular economic expansion.

⚖️ Fundamental Assumptions & Hypotheses

  • Rejection of general equilibrium stability: Economic systems are inherently unstable and prone to cumulative divergence rather than automatic self-correction.
  • Interdependence of social and economic factors: Non-economic variables (institutions, education, health) are treated as endogenous rather than exogenous constants.
  • Presence of increasing returns to scale: Manufacturing and advanced sectors experience internal and external economies of scale, driving productivity growth.
  • Geographical agglomeration: Capital, skilled labor, and entrepreneurial talent naturally migrate toward prosperous growth poles, creating regional imbalances.
  • Ineffectiveness of classical price mechanisms: Traditional price adjustments are inadequate to overcome structural rigidities and institutional bottlenecks in developing regions.

1. Principle of Circular and Cumulative Causation (CCC)

Myrdal's core thesis posits that an initial change in any given variable triggers a series of secondary and tertiary changes that reinforce the initial change. In a depressed region, a negative shock causes capital flight and brain drain, which lowers local demand, reduces tax revenues, degrades infrastructure, and further depresses the economic base in an unending downward spiral.

Key Exam Takeaways:
  • CCC rejects the classical tendency toward equilibrium.
  • Change in one factor causes a circular chain reaction amplifying the initial stimulus.
  • Both virtuous (growth) and vicious (underdevelopment) circles operate through CCC.

2. Backwash Effects vs. Spread Effects

Backwash effects represent all the adverse centrifugal movements of capital, labor, and goods from backward to advanced regions (e.g., migration of young professionals and drain of savings). Spread effects are the centripetal, beneficial impulses from the center to the periphery (e.g., demand for raw materials and technological spillovers). In developing nations, backwash effects overwhelmingly dominate spread effects.

Key Exam Takeaways:
  • Backwash effects = Negative externalities pulling resources to growth poles.
  • Spread effects = Positive diffusion of growth from center to periphery.
  • Myrdal argued that free markets lead to net negative impact on periphery regions.

3. Regional Inequalities and National Integration

Unchecked market forces widen regional disparities within a nation. Rich regions accumulate agglomeration economies while poor regions stagnate. Myrdal argued that state intervention through progressive fiscal policies, regional subsidies, and public investment is essential to counteract market-induced spatial polarization and achieve national economic integration.

Key Exam Takeaways:
  • Market forces inherently generate spatial inequality.
  • State intervention is mandatory to break the vicious circle of underdevelopment.
  • National integration requires deliberate spatial redistribution policies.

4. Synthesis with Hirschman's Polarization and Trickle-Down Effects

Hirschman's 'Polarization Effects' are synonymous with Myrdal's 'Backwash Effects,' and his 'Trickle-Down Effects' correspond to 'Spread Effects.' Hirschman emphasized that unbalanced growth is a prerequisite for development, where growth poles inevitably emerge first before trickling down, whereas Myrdal stressed immediate state corrective action to halt cumulative divergence.

Key Exam Takeaways:
  • Myrdal's Backwash = Hirschman's Polarization Effect.
  • Myrdal's Spread = Hirschman's Trickle-Down Effect.
  • Hirschman viewed polarization as a temporary necessary phase; Myrdal viewed it as a chronic pathology.

📐 Mathematical Formulations & Equilibrium Conditions

Cumulative Growth Feedback Equation
Yt = f(It, St, Et)   where   Δ It = α Yt-1 - β Rt

Demonstrates how current income ($Y_t$) depends on past investments ($I_t$), technological spillovers ($S_t$), and external shocks ($E_t$), with regional divergence captured by dynamic feedback loops.

Kaldor's Cumulative Causation (Verdoorn's Law)
pq = λ + β (py)   where   β > 0

Relates productivity growth to output growth. High output growth leads to economies of scale, lower unit costs, increased competitiveness, and further export growth.

Net Regional Impact Function
NRI = \sum (Si) - \sum (Bj)

Represents net regional impact as the aggregate of Spread Effects ($S_i$) minus Backwash Effects ($B_j$). If $NRI < 0$, the region undergoes cumulative impoverishment.

Regional Income Divergence Rate
d(Y1 - Y2)dt = \gamma (Y1 - Y2)   with   \gamma > 0

Models the divergence of per capita incomes between rich region ($Y_1$) and poor region ($Y_2$) over time under free market assumptions.

⚠️ Economic Paradoxes & Boundary Exceptions

Exception / Paradox The Regional Divergence Paradox

Conditions: Neoclassical trade and factor price equalization theories predict convergence of regional incomes through free trade and factor mobility.

Diagrammatic Behavior: Empirically, income gaps widen over time (diverging paths), violating neoclassical convergence curves.

Exception / Paradox Growth Pole Exhaustion

Conditions: Agglomeration economies in a core region eventually turn into diseconomies (congestion, pollution, high land rents).

Diagrammatic Behavior: An inverted U-shape curve where concentration initially boosts growth but eventually triggers decentralization and spread effects.

Exception / Paradox Perverse Migration Paradox

Conditions: Skilled and educated labor migrates out of poor regions to rich regions, aggravating the human capital deficit of the sending region.

Diagrammatic Behavior: Net human capital transfer flows uphill toward wealthy centers, widening the technological gradient.

⚡ Must-Remember Economic Identities & Laws

  • Myrdal's Law of Circular Interdependence: Any social or economic factor is not in equilibrium but is functionally dependent on multiple other changing variables.
  • The Cumulative Causation Inequality: Net Regional Divergence occurs if Magnitude of Backwash Effects (B) > Magnitude of Spread Effects (S).
  • Hirschman's Unbalanced Growth Condition: Development must be intentionally lynchpinned to strategic sectors/regions to maximize linkage effects (Backward and Forward Linkages).
  • The Spatial Multiplier Identity: Delta Y = ks Delta Ic, where ks represents the regional open-economy multiplier incorporating leakages through interregional trade.
  • Perkins-Myrdal Divergence Theorem: Free market mechanisms in developing countries amplify dualism by systematically favoring core over peripheral regions.
  • The Interregional Factor Migration Rule: Labor and capital flow from low-wage periphery to high-wage core, worsening the marginal productivity gap between regions.

🚫 Common Analytical Confusions (Contrast Table)

Myrdal's Backwash Effects VS Hirschman's Polarization Effects

While conceptually identical in describing negative impacts on the periphery, Myrdal emphasizes socio-institutional factors and cumulative migration drain, whereas Hirschman emphasizes market-driven investment concentration and structural bottlenecks.

Spread Effects VS Trickle-Down Effects

Myrdal termed them 'Spread Effects' focusing on trade and infrastructural spillovers, whereas Hirschman coined 'Trickle-Down Effects' focusing on intersectoral purchases and entrepreneurial migration.

Circular and Cumulative Causation VS Neoclassical General Equilibrium

CCC rejects neoclassical self-correcting equilibrium, asserting that market forces are inherently destabilizing and produce cumulative divergence rather than convergence.

Backward Linkages VS Backwash Effects

Backward linkages are positive intersectoral demand stimulants for upstream input suppliers, whereas backwash effects are negative spatial drains of resources from periphery to core.

📚 Complete Concept-by-Concept Glossary

Every fundamental term, curve, and mechanism covered in this chapter.

Circular and Cumulative Causation (CCC): Gunnar Myrdal's core principle stating that a change in one economic factor sets off a chain of circular, mutually reinforcing effects that magnify the initial change rather than bringing the system back to equilibrium.
Backwash Effects: A.O. Hirschman and Gunnar Myrdal's term for adverse centrifugal forces whereby economic growth in a core region drains capital, skilled labor, and dynamic industries away from peripheral regions.
Spread Effects: Centripetal forces of expansion where growth in a core region stimulates economic activity in peripheral regions through increased demand for raw materials and agricultural goods.
Polarization Effects: Albert Hirschman's designation for the agglomeration and cumulative forces that concentrate investments, innovative industries, and human capital in prosperous growing regions at the expense of lagging regions.
Trickle-Down Effects: Hirschman's term for the beneficial interregional transmission of growth from the core to the periphery, synonymous with Myrdal's spread effects.
Regional Inequality: The persistent divergence in per capita income, industrial density, infrastructure quality, and living standards between advanced core regions and backward peripheral zones.
National Integration: The political, economic, and social unification of diverse regions within a nation, often hindered by unmitigated circular causation that deepens regional polarization.
Agglomeration Economies: Cost savings resulting from urbanization and the spatial clustering of firms, specialized labor pools, and auxiliary service providers in core metropolitan regions.
Cumulative Mechanism: The self-sustaining dynamic where an initial economic shock (positive or negative) generates subsequent endogenous adjustments that accelerate the original trajectory.
Myrdal's Hypothesis: The proposition that market forces, left to themselves, tend to increase rather than decrease regional inequalities between developed and underdeveloped regions.
Hirschman's Synthesis: The analytical framework combining polarization and trickle-down effects to explain why unbalanced growth is an inevitable prerequisite for sustained developmental takeoff.
Centrifugal Forces: Market forces that pull economic activities outward from the periphery toward the core, reinforcing regional disparities.
Centripetal Forces: Forces that tend to spread economic development outward from core growth poles into surrounding peripheral territories.
Export Base Theory Link: The conceptual tie-in where regional growth is driven by external demand, which triggers Myrdal's circular multiplier process within the localized economy.

Who propounded the Principle of Circular and Cumulative Causation (CCC)?

Question 2 of 18

According to Gunnar Myrdal, what is the primary consequence of unrestrained market forces in developing economies?

Explanation: Myrdal argued that market forces tend to increase rather than decrease regional inequalities, as backwash effects typically outweigh spread effects in developing nations.

Question 3 of 18

Which of the following best defines 'Backwash Effects' in spatial economics?

Explanation: Backwash effects refer to adverse centrifugal forces that suck resources, human capital, and investments out of backward regions into booming core centers.

Question 4 of 18

Albert Hirschman's terminology for Myrdal's 'Spread Effects' is:

Explanation: Hirschman used the term 'Trickle-Down Effects' to describe the positive transmission of growth from advanced regions to lagging regions, corresponding to Myrdal's spread effects.

Question 5 of 18

In Hirschman's analytical framework, what term corresponds to Gunnar Myrdal's 'Backwash Effects'?

Explanation: Hirschman's 'Polarization Effects' capture the same phenomenon as Myrdal's 'Backwash Effects'—the negative impact of core growth on peripheral regions.

Question 6 of 18

What is the ultimate outcome if Spread Effects outweigh Backwash Effects in a regional economy?

Explanation: When spread effects dominate over backwash effects, growth from the core successfully diffuses outward, leading to regional convergence and integration.

Question 7 of 18

Which school of economic thought does the Circulation-Causation Theory primarily challenge?

Explanation: CCC directly challenges the neoclassical assumption that free markets automatically generate spatial equilibrium and factor price equalization across regions.

Question 8 of 18

According to Myrdal, why do cumulative processes persist once initiated?

Explanation: The essence of circular and cumulative causation is that initial impulses trigger secondary adjustments that amplify the primary shock in a continuous feedback loop.

Question 9 of 18

How does migration typically operate under Myrdal's backwash effects?

Explanation: Backwash effects involve selective out-migration of dynamic human capital from peripheral regions to core agglomerations, impoverishing the periphery's demographic profile.

Question 10 of 18

Hirschman argued that in the early stages of economic development, policy should deliberately encourage:

Explanation: Hirschman advocated unbalanced growth, arguing that investments should be concentrated in growth poles to trigger powerful polarization and subsequent trickle-down effects.

Question 11 of 18

Which of the following is a classic example of agglomeration economies driving circular causation?

Explanation: Agglomeration economies occur when firms cluster in urban centers, gaining cost advantages that attract even more businesses and workers in a cumulative cycle.

Question 12 of 18

In the context of regional inequalities, national integration is threatened when:

Explanation: When backwash effects perpetually dominate, severe regional polarization occurs, creating political friction and hindering true national economic integration.

Question 13 of 18

What role do public policy and state intervention play in Myrdal's theory of cumulative causation?

Explanation: Myrdal advocated for deliberate state planning and welfare policies to break vicious circles of cumulative causation and stimulate spread effects.

Question 14 of 18

Which concept describes the mechanism where an increase in demand in the core region stimulates raw material production in the periphery?

Explanation: Spread effects include increased demand from the prosperous core for agricultural goods and raw materials produced in peripheral areas.

Question 15 of 18

Assertion (A): Unrestricted market forces naturally reduce regional disparities over time. Reason (R): Backwash effects are invariably weaker than spread effects in developing countries.

Explanation: Both statements are false. Unrestricted market forces increase regional disparities because backwash effects are typically stronger than spread effects in developing countries.

Question 16 of 18

Which development economist integrated Myrdal's regional concepts with the theory of development poles and intersectoral linkages?

Explanation: Albert O. Hirschman synthesized regional growth dynamics by linking polarization/trickle-down effects with backward and forward linkage mechanisms.

Question 17 of 18

What is the 'vicious circle of poverty' closely related to in Gunnar Myrdal's framework?

Explanation: The vicious circle of poverty is a manifestation of circular and cumulative causation where low income leads to low savings, low investment, low productivity, and back to low income.

Question 18 of 18

Which of the following best characterizes the long-run spatial equilibrium predicted by Circulation-Causation Theory without state intervention?

Explanation: Without corrective state intervention, the theory predicts chronic uneven development characterized by a wealthy core and an impoverished, neglected periphery.
