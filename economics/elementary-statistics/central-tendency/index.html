Elementary Statistics

Measures of Central Tendency: Mean, Median, and Mode

Measures of central tendency represent foundational statistical techniques in economic analysis used to summarize large datasets of socioeconomic variables like income, wage rates, and price indices. Mastery of the Arithmetic Mean, Median, and Mode is critical for empirical economic research as well as high-stakes competitive examinations such as UP PGT, TGT, and UGC-NET.

🏛️ Key Economists & Landmark Publications

Sir William Petty
1690, Political Arithmetic

Pioneered the quantitative method in economics, relying on numerical averages to analyze political and economic power.

Adolphe Quetelet
1835, A Treatise on Man and the Development of His Faculties

Introduced the concept of 'l'homme moyen' (the average man), applying statistical means to social and economic phenomena.

Karl Pearson
1895, Skewness of Variate Homogeneity

Established rigorous mathematical foundations for measuring frequency distributions, including the exact empirical relationship between Mean, Median, and Mode in moderately asymmetrical distributions.

Francis Ysidro Edgeworth
1883, Mathematical Psychics

Applied advanced statistical averages and probability distributions to economic utility and exchange.

⚖️ Fundamental Assumptions & Hypotheses

  • Homogeneity of Data: The dataset represents observations drawn from a single, well-defined economic population.
  • Cardinal Measurability (for Mean): The underlying economic variable must possess at least interval-level properties to permit arithmetic summation.
  • Ordinal Rankability (for Median): Observations must be capable of strict sequential ordering from lowest to highest magnitude.
  • Frequency Continuity: The underlying distribution of economic indicators (such as wage rates) is assumed to follow a continuous or discrete frequency distribution without catastrophic data gaps.
  • Representativeness: The chosen central metric adequately summarizes the underlying data without severe distortion from extreme outliers.

1. Arithmetic Mean: Properties, Mathematical Rigor, and Economic Applications

The Arithmetic Mean is the most commonly used measure of central tendency, calculated by summing all individual economic observations and dividing by the total number of observations. In economics, it is widely used to calculate per capita income, average cost functions, and average productivity. Its chief limitation is extreme sensitivity to outliers (extreme values), making it less robust in skewed income distributions.

Key Exam Takeaways:
  • The sum of deviations of all observations from their arithmetic mean is always equal to zero: \sum (X - \bar{X}) = 0.
  • The sum of squared deviations of observations from the arithmetic mean is a minimum, less than the sum of squared deviations from any other point.
  • The combined mean of two or more series can be calculated directly using weighted arithmetic averages.

2. Median: Positional Advantage, Quartiles, and Robustness to Extreme Inequality

The Median is the positional middle value of a dataset when arranged in ascending or descending order. Because it is a positional average rather than a mathematical summation, it is completely unaffected by extreme values or outliers. In labor economics and development economics, the median income or median wage is preferred over the mean when analyzing highly skewed distributions, such as national income distribution.

Key Exam Takeaways:
  • The sum of absolute deviations taken from the median is the absolute minimum, less than from any other value.
  • It splits the frequency distribution exactly into two equal halves (50% above, 50% below).
  • It can be graphically determined using the intersection point of 'Less than' and 'More than' cumulative frequency curves (Ogive curves).

3. Mode: The Most Frequent Economic State and Business Forecasting

The Mode represents the value that occurs most frequently in a dataset. In business economics and market research, the mode is invaluable for determining modal modal-size preferences, such as the most frequently demanded shirt size, most popular automobile color, or modal wage bracket in a factory. It can be unimodal, bimodal, or multimodal.

Key Exam Takeaways:
  • Mode cannot be calculated mathematically if data lacks a distinct peak frequency.
  • It can be located graphically from a histogram using geometric cross-lines.
  • In moderately asymmetrical distributions, Karl Pearson's empirical relation holds: Mean - Mode = 3(Mean - Median).

4. Relationship Between Mean, Median, Mode, and Distributional Skewness

The relative positions of the mean, median, and mode provide instant diagnostic information regarding the symmetry or skewness of an economic dataset. In a perfectly symmetrical bell-shaped distribution, Mean = Median = Mode. In positively skewed distributions (such as personal income), Mean > Median > Mode. In negatively skewed distributions, Mode > Median > Mean.

Key Exam Takeaways:
  • Positively skewed income distribution order: Mode < Median < Mean.
  • Negatively skewed distribution order: Mean < Median < Mode.
  • Pearson's Skewness Coefficient (Sk) is calculated as \frac{\text{Mean} - \text{Mode}}{\text{Standard Deviation}} or \frac{3(\text{Mean} - \text{Median})}{\text{Standard Deviation}}.

📐 Mathematical Formulations & Equilibrium Conditions

Simple Arithmetic Mean
\bar{X} = \sum XiN = X1 + X2 + \dots + XNN

Measures the overall average per unit of observation; highly sensitive to extreme values.

Median for Grouped Data
Median = L + N2 - cff × h

Locates the exact middle value in grouped frequency distributions where L is lower limit of median class, cf is cumulative frequency preceding median class, f is frequency of median class, and h is class interval width.

Mode for Grouped Data
Mode = L + f1 - f02f1 - f0 - f2 × h

Identifies peak concentration where f1 is modal class frequency, f0 is preceding frequency, f2 is succeeding frequency, and h is class width.

Karl Pearson's Empirical Relationship
Mode = 3(Median) - 2(Mean)

Allows quick calculation of the third measure when any two measures are known in a moderately asymmetrical distribution.

⚠️ Economic Paradoxes & Boundary Exceptions

Exception / Paradox Simpson's Paradox in Average Aggregation

Conditions: A trend or relationship observed in different groups of data can reverse when these groups are combined into a single aggregated mean.

Diagrammatic Behavior: Disaggregated subgroup regression lines or averages slope in one direction, while the aggregated overall mean slope or weighted average moves in the opposite direction.

Exception / Paradox The Fallacy of the Average Man

Conditions: Using the arithmetic mean to design standardized economic or physical goods when the underlying distribution is heavily skewed or multimodal.

Diagrammatic Behavior: A frequency curve with multiple peaks (bimodal/multimodal) where the arithmetic mean falls in a trough with zero actual representation in the population.

⚡ Must-Remember Economic Identities & Laws

  • Sum of Deviations Property: Sum of (Xi - Mean) = 0 for all i from 1 to n.
  • Least Squares Criterion: Sum of (Xi - A)2 is minimized when A = Mean.
  • Combined Arithmetic Mean: Mean12 = (N1 Mean1 + N2 Mean2) / (N1 + N2).
  • Karl Pearson's Empirical Formula: Mode = 3(Median) - 2(Mean).
  • Bowley's Coefficient of Skewness: Sk = (Q3 + Q1 - 2*Median) / (Q3 - Q1).
  • Pythagorean Inequality of Means: Arithmetic Mean >= Geometric Mean >= Harmonic Mean (AM >= GM >= HM).
  • Combined Geometric Mean formula: GM12 = anti-log [ (N1 log GM1 + N2 log GM2) / (N1 + N2) ].

🚫 Common Analytical Confusions (Contrast Table)

Arithmetic Mean VS Median

Arithmetic mean utilizes all numerical values and is heavily distorted by outliers; median is a positional value determined solely by ranking and is completely robust to extreme income/wealth inequality.

Geometric Mean VS Harmonic Mean

Geometric mean multiplies relative changes and is ideal for compound growth rates; harmonic mean utilizes reciprocals and is specifically designed for rates, ratios, and time-per-unit metrics.

Mode VS Median

Mode is the single most frequent observation (useful for modal inventory sizing); median divides the distribution into two equal numerical halves regardless of frequency clustering.

Positive Skewness VS Negative Skewness

In positive skewness (right-skewed income distribution), Mean > Median > Mode; in negative skewness, Mode > Median > Mean.

Mathematical Average VS Positional Average

Mathematical averages (Mean, GM, HM) change if any single value changes; positional averages (Median, Quartiles) may remain completely unchanged if values shift as long as their relative positional rank stays the same.

📚 Complete Concept-by-Concept Glossary

Every fundamental term, curve, and mechanism covered in this chapter.

Arithmetic Mean: The sum of all individual observations divided by the total number of observations; highly sensitive to extreme values due to its mathematical foundation.
Geometric Mean: The nth root of the product of n observations; exceptionally suited for averaging growth rates, ratios, and index numbers.
Harmonic Mean: The reciprocal of the arithmetic mean of the reciprocals of observations; optimal for averaging speeds, rates, and productivity per unit of time.
Median: The middle value of an ordered dataset that divides the distribution into two equal halves; highly robust to extreme outliers and income inequality.
Mode: The value that occurs with the highest frequency in a dataset; represents the most typical or popular economic state used in business forecasting.
Empirical Relation (Karl Pearson): The fundamental mathematical link among central tendencies in moderately skewed distributions: Mean - Mode = 3(Mean - Median).
Positional Average: Measures of central tendency like the median, quartiles, deciles, and percentiles whose location depends on the ordered position of data rather than algebraic summation.
Mathematical Average: Measures like arithmetic, geometric, and harmonic means that incorporate every single numerical value in their computation.
Deciles: Partition values that divide an ordered dataset into ten equal parts, denoted as D1 through D9.
Percentiles: Partition values that split an ordered dataset into one hundred equal segments, widely used in income distribution and standardized test scoring.
Bimodal Distribution: A frequency distribution possessing two distinct peaks or modes, indicating structural heterogeneity within the population.
Distributional Skewness: The degree of asymmetry exhibited by a probability or frequency distribution around its central axis.
Zero Sum Property of Deviations: A fundamental algebraic property stating that the algebraic sum of deviations of all observations taken from their arithmetic mean is always zero.
Minimum Sum of Squares Property: The theorem establishing that the sum of squared deviations of observations from the arithmetic mean is less than the sum of squared deviations from any other arbitrary point.
Cumulative Frequency Distribution: A tabular aggregation of frequencies up to a given upper or lower class boundary, essential for locating the median and quartiles.

Question 1 of 19

Which measure of central tendency is most severely affected by extreme outliers in a right-skewed distribution such as personal income?

Explanation: The arithmetic mean incorporates every exact numerical value, making it disproportionately pulled toward extreme high values in right-skewed distributions.

Question 2 of 19

If the arithmetic mean of a dataset is 50 and the median is 48, what is the approximate value of the mode using Karl Pearson's empirical relation?

Explanation: Using Mode = 3(Median) - 2(Mean), we get Mode = 3(48) - 2(50) = 144 - 100 = 44.

Question 3 of 19

Which of the following inequalities always holds true for any set of positive, non-identical numbers?

Explanation: By mathematical property, the Arithmetic Mean (AM) is greater than or equal to the Geometric Mean (GM), which in turn is greater than or equal to the Harmonic Mean (HM).

Question 4 of 19

What is the algebraic sum of deviations of a set of N observations taken from their arithmetic mean?

Explanation: A defining mathematical property of the arithmetic mean is that the sum of deviations of all observations from their mean is identically zero.

Question 5 of 19

When averaging rates of speed over equal distances, which measure of central tendency is mathematically appropriate?

Explanation: Harmonic mean is theoretically superior when averaging rates, speeds, or time taken per unit of constant distance.

Question 6 of 19

In a symmetrical frequency distribution, which of the following relationships is strictly true?

Explanation: In a perfectly symmetrical bell-shaped distribution, all three measures of central tendency coincide completely at the exact center.

Question 7 of 19

Which of the following statistics is classified strictly as a positional average?

Explanation: The median is a positional average because its value is determined by the ranking position of observations rather than algebraic summation.

Question 8 of 19

If each observation in a dataset is multiplied by a constant k (where k > 0), what happens to the arithmetic mean?

Explanation: The arithmetic mean is a linear operator; if every value is multiplied by k, the mean is also multiplied by k.

Question 9 of 19

What is the median of the following discrete dataset: 12, 7, 15, 22, 9, 3, 19?

Explanation: Arranging in ascending order: 3, 7, 9, 12, 15, 19, 22. The middle (4th) term is 12.

Question 10 of 19

Which statistical measure is most useful for analyzing compound growth rates of GDP over multiple decades?

Explanation: Geometric mean correctly accounts for multiplicative compounding over successive periods.

Question 11 of 19

In a positively skewed distribution, what is the sequence of the central tendencies from lowest to highest?

Explanation: In positive (right) skewness, the tail extends to the right, dragging the mean upward: Mode is lowest, Median is middle, and Mean is highest.

Question 12 of 19

If a constant c is added to every observation in a dataset, how does the median change?

Explanation: Adding a constant to all observations shifts the entire distribution, causing the median (and all other position values) to increase by exactly c.

Question 13 of 19

Which of the following is NOT a mathematical property of the arithmetic mean?

Explanation: The arithmetic mean is highly sensitive to extreme outliers, making option B incorrect.

Question 14 of 19

For an open-ended frequency distribution table, which measure of central tendency cannot be accurately calculated without assumptions?

Explanation: Open-ended first or last classes lack exact lower or upper limits, making it impossible to compute class midpoints required for the arithmetic mean.

Question 15 of 19

What is the mode of the dataset: 4, 5, 2, 5, 6, 7, 5, 8, 2, 5?

Explanation: The number 5 appears 4 times, which is more frequently than any other number in the dataset.

Question 16 of 19

If the coefficient of skewness (Pearson's) is zero, what can be inferred about the mean, median, and mode?

Explanation: A skewness coefficient of zero indicates perfect symmetry, where Mean, Median, and Mode are identical.

Question 17 of 19

Which partition value divides the distribution into four equal parts?

Explanation: Quartiles (Q1, Q2, Q3) divide an ordered dataset into four equal quarters.

Question 18 of 19

What happens to the harmonic mean if any single observation in the dataset is zero?

Explanation: Since harmonic mean involves the reciprocals of observations (1/X), a zero value results in 1/0, making the harmonic mean undefined.

Question 19 of 19

Assertion (A): The median is preferred over the arithmetic mean when measuring average household income in an economy with severe wealth inequality. Reason (R): The median is a positional average that is unaffected by extreme upper-tail income values.

Explanation: Severe income inequality creates extreme upper outliers. Because the median ignores exact magnitudes of extremes and focuses solely on rank order, it provides a superior measure of central tendency.

In a moderately asymmetrical distribution, the values of arithmetic mean and mode are 86 and 80 respectively. What is the value of the median?

A. 82
B. 84
C. 88
D. 90
Solution: Using Pearson's empirical formula: Mean - Mode = 3(Mean - Median). Substituting the values: 86 - 80 = 3(86 - Median) => 6 = 258 - 3(Median) => 3(Median) = 252 => Median = 84.

D. Rs. 350
Solution: Combined Mean = (N1Mean1 + N2Mean2) / (N1 + N2) = (50400 + 100250) / (50 + 100) = (20000 + 25000) / 150 = 45000 / 150 = Rs. 300.

Which of the following measures is independent of both change of origin and change of scale?

A. Arithmetic Mean
B. Standard Deviation
C. Coefficient of Variation
D. None of the central tendency measures
Solution: Measures of central tendency (like Mean, Median, Mode) are affected by change of origin and change of scale. Only dimensionless relative dispersion measures like the Coefficient of Variation are independent of both.
Previous Year Pattern • Q4

If the sum of squares of deviations of 10 observations taken from the value 5 is 250, and the sum of deviations is -20, what is the arithmetic mean of the observations?

A. 3
B. 5
C. 7
D. 8
Solution: Let assumed mean A = 5. Sum of deviations Sigma(X_i - 5) = -20. Mean = A + [Sigma(X_i - A)/N] = 5 + (-20 / 10) = 5 - 2 = 3.

Test Item 2 of 10

If the geometric mean of two numbers is 8 and their harmonic mean is 6.4, what is their arithmetic mean?

Rationale: By the fundamental property relating Pythagorean means for two numbers, GM^2 = AM HM. Thus, 8^2 = AM 6.4 => 64 = AM * 6.4 => AM = 64 / 6.4 = 10.0.

Test Item 6 of 10

If the mean of 5 observations is 20 and four of the observations are 12, 16, 24, and 30, what is the fifth observation?

Rationale: Total sum = 5 * 20 = 100. Sum of 4 observations = 12 + 16 + 24 + 30 = 82. Fifth observation = 100 - 82 = 18.

Test Item 7 of 10

Why is the harmonic mean inappropriate when any dataset value is negative or zero?

Rationale: Harmonic mean relies on 1/X_i. A zero results in undefined division by zero, and negative values invert the direction of the average improperly.

Test Item 8 of 10

What is the relationship between the three quartiles (Q1, Median, Q3) in a positively skewed distribution?

Rationale: In positive skewness, the upper half of the data is stretched out, making the distance from the median to the third quartile (Q3 - Median) greater than the distance from the first quartile to the median (Median - Q1).

Test Item 9 of 10

If all observations in a dataset are identical (e.g., 5, 5, 5, 5), what is the relationship between AM, GM, and HM?

Rationale: When all observations in a dataset are identical, all Pythagorean means (Arithmetic, Geometric, and Harmonic) coincide and are equal to that constant value.

Test Item 10 of 10

Which of the following is a primary limitation of the mode as a measure of central tendency?

Rationale: The mode can be indeterminate (multiple modes or flat distributions with no clear peak) and is unstable in small samples, making it ill-defined.
