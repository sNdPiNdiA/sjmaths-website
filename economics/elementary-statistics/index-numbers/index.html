Elementary Statistics

Index Numbers

Index numbers are specialized statistical devices designed to measure the relative changes in a variable or a group of related variables over time, geographic space, or other characteristics. As a cornerstone of elementary statistics, they are vital for macroeconomic policy formulation, cost-of-living adjustments, and are heavily tested in UP PGT, UP TGT, UGC-NET, and Assistant Professor economics examinations.

🏛️ Key Economists & Landmark Publications

G. R. Carli
1764 - 'Del valore delle monete e del prezzo medesimo dei beni'

Constructed the very first recorded price index number to study the historical purchasing power of money and commodity price variations in Italy.

William Stanley Jevons
1865 - 'A Serious Fall in the Value of Gold Ascertained'

Introduced the geometric mean price index and advanced index number theory by analyzing the depreciation of gold values using index methodology.

Etienne Laspeyres
1871 - 'Die Berechnung einer mittleren Warenpreissteigerung'

Formulated the classic base-year weighted price index, which uses base period quantities as weights ($P_{01}^{L}$). Overstates inflation due to substitution bias.

Hermann Paasche
1874 - 'Ueber die Preisentwicklung der letzten Jahre'

Developed the current-year weighted price index, which uses given period quantities as weights ($P_{01}^{P}$). Understates inflation due to consumer response to price changes.

Irving Fisher
1922 - 'The Making of Index Numbers'

Propose the 'Ideal Index' (geometric mean of Laspeyres and Paasche), introduced time reversal and factor reversal tests for evaluating index number formulas.

⚖️ Fundamental Assumptions & Hypotheses

  • Homogeneity of Commodities: Items included in the index must be of uniform quality and standard across the base period and current period to isolate pure price changes.
  • Representative Basket: The selected basket of goods and services accurately reflects the consumption pattern or economic activity of the target population.
  • Fixed Weight Structure (for specific formulas): Implicit assumption that consumer preferences or production technologies remain stable within the immediate period under static weighting schemes.
  • Transitivity of Markets: Prices and quantities are assumed to be generated in competitive, clear markets without severe structural distortions or artificial rationing.
  • Additivity and Comparability: Economic aggregates are assumed to be measurable in monetary units that maintain cardinal comparability across temporal horizons.

1. Classification and Types of Index Numbers

Index numbers are broadly categorized by their economic objective: Price Index Numbers (measuring relative price shifts of commodities), Quantity Index Numbers (measuring physical volume changes in production or consumption), and Value Index Numbers (measuring aggregate monetary value changes). They can also be classified as Unweighted (Simple aggregative or simple average of relatives) and Weighted index numbers where explicit economic weights (quantities or values) are attached to commodities based on their relative importance.

Key Exam Takeaways:
  • Value index is equal to the product of price index and quantity index in value-weighted frameworks.
  • Simple aggregative index suffers from the 'unit test failure' because its value changes based on the unit of measurement.
  • Simple average of price relatives uses geometric or arithmetic means to neutralize unit bias.

2. Weighted Index Number Formulas (Laspeyres, Paasche, and Fisher)

Weighted indices resolve the unit test failure and account for varying economic importance of goods. Laspeyres Index uses base-period quantities ($q_0$) as weights, resulting in an upward bias because consumers substitute away from goods whose prices rise faster. Paasche Index uses current-period quantities ($q_1$) as weights, yielding a downward bias because it reflects current minimization behavior. Irving Fisher's Ideal Index takes the geometric mean of Laspeyres and Paasche, successfully satisfying both Time Reversal and Factor Reversal tests.

Key Exam Takeaways:
  • Laspeyres formula: $P_{01}^L = \frac{\sum P_1 Q_0}{\sum P_0 Q_0} \times 100$
  • Paasche formula: $P_{01}^P = \frac{\sum P_1 Q_1}{\sum P_0 Q_1} \times 100$
  • Fisher's Ideal Index: $P_{01}^F = \sqrt{P_{01}^L \times P_{01}^P}$ (Satisfies both major index tests)

3. Tests of Consistency for Index Numbers

To evaluate the mathematical validity and reliability of index formulas, mathematical tests formulated by Irving Fisher are applied. These include: (a) Unit Test (independence of units of measurement), (b) Time Reversal Test ($P_{01} \times P_{10} = 1$), which ensures consistency when base and current years are interchanged, (c) Factor Reversal Test ($P_{01} \times Q_{01} = \frac{\sum P_1 Q_1}{\sum P_0 Q_0}$), which ensures interchanging prices and quantities yields the true value ratio, and (d) Circular Test ($P_{01} \times P_{12} \times P_{20} = 1$), an extension of time reversal across multiple periods.

Key Exam Takeaways:
  • Fisher's Ideal Index satisfies both the Time Reversal Test and the Factor Reversal Test.
  • Neither Laspeyres nor Paasche satisfies the Time Reversal or Factor Reversal tests independently.
  • The Circular Test is satisfied by the geometric mean with fixed weights (e.g., Marshall-Edgeworth or Walsh fail circularity unless weights are constant chain-linked).

4. Deflation, Cost of Living, and Chain-Base Indices

Index numbers are essential policy instruments used to deflate nominal macroeconomic aggregates (such as Nominal GDP) into real aggregates (Real GDP) using a Price Deflator. Cost of Living Index (COLI) measures the change in minimum expenditure required to maintain a constant utility level, contrasting with fixed-basket indices. Chain-base index numbers use the immediately preceding period as the base, mitigating structural obsolescence of fixed base weights over long temporal horizons.

Key Exam Takeaways:
  • Real Income = $\frac{\text{Nominal Income}}{\text{Price Index}} \times 100$
  • Implicit GDP Deflator = $\frac{\text{Nominal GDP}}{\text{Real GDP}} \times 100$
  • Chain-base index link relatives eliminate the limitation of rigid fixed base weights during periods of rapid structural and technological shift.

📐 Mathematical Formulations & Equilibrium Conditions

Laspeyres Price Index
P01L = \sum (P1 Q0)\sum (P0 Q0) × 100

Measures percentage change in cost of purchasing the base-period basket of goods at current versus base prices. Tends to overstate inflation.

Paasche Price Index
P01P = \sum (P1 Q1)\sum (P0 Q1) × 100

Measures percentage change in cost of purchasing the current-period basket of goods at current versus base prices. Tends to understate inflation.

Fisher's Ideal Price Index
P01F = \sqrt{\sum P1 Q0\sum P0 Q0 × \sum P1 Q1\sum P0 Q1} × 100

Geometric mean of Laspeyres and Paasche indices; satisfies both Time Reversal and Factor Reversal Tests, making it mathematically optimal.

Marshall-Edgeworth Price Index
P01ME = \sum [P1 (Q0 + Q1)]\sum [P0 (Q0 + Q1)] × 100

Uses arithmetic mean of base and current quantities as weights. Satisfies the time reversal test and approximates Fisher's ideal index closely.

Time Reversal Test Mathematical Condition
P01 × P10 = 1

Ensures that the index calculated from period 0 to period 1, multiplied by the index from period 1 back to period 0, equals unity.

🧠 Exam Tips, Tricks & Mnemonics (10-Second Shortcuts)

💡 Mnemonic for Laspeyres vs Paasche Weights 0 (Zero) comes before 1 (One) -> L comes before P
Laspeyres uses Q0 (Base), Paasche uses Q1 (Current). Remember the alphabet: L (Laspeyres) has 0, P (Paasche) has 1 in numerator weights context.

Exam Application: Instantly eliminate incorrect options in matching questions by verifying whether Laspeyres uses Q0 and Paasche uses Q1.

💡 Fisher's Ideal Index Shortcut FLP Formula Rule
Fisher (F) is always the Square Root (Geometric Mean) of Laspeyres (L) times Paasche (P): F = sqrt(L x P).

Exam Application: If exam provides numerical values for Laspeyres (e.g., 110) and Paasche (e.g., 100), compute Fisher instantly as sqrt(110 x 100) = sqrt(11000).

💡 Time Reversal Test Trick Swap 0 and 1 = 1
To test time reversal, interchange base and current year subscripts (01 becomes 10). Their product must equal 1.

Exam Application: Quickly check test equations in UGC-NET assertions by verifying if $P_{01}(P) \times P_{10}(Q)$ or similar swaps result in exact cancellation.

💡 Unit Test Rule of Thumb Simple Aggregative Fails Unit Test
Simple aggregative index depends heavily on the unit of pricing (kg vs grams), whereas price relatives and weighted indices are unit-free.

Exam Application: Directly answer conceptual multiple-choice questions asking which basic index fails the unit test.

⚠️ Economic Paradoxes & Boundary Exceptions

Exception / Paradox Substitution Bias Paradox

Conditions: When relative prices change, consumers substitute away from relatively expensive goods toward cheaper alternatives.

Diagrammatic Behavior: Laspeyres index ignores substitution, placing excessive weight on items whose prices rose, creating an upward bias relative to true cost of living.

Exception / Paradox Index Number Inconsistency (Laspeyres vs Paasche Divergence)

Conditions: Occurs during periods of extreme price volatility and massive changes in consumer tastes or quantity structures.

Diagrammatic Behavior: Divergence between $P^L$ and $P^P$ widens significantly; $P^L > P^F > P^P$ under normal downward-sloping demand conditions.

Exception / Paradox Weight Obsolescence Paradox

Conditions: Fixed base-weight indices (like Laspeyres) used over extended decades with rapid technological innovation.

Diagrammatic Behavior: Index fails entirely to capture newly introduced goods (e.g., smartphones in a 1950 basket), rendering the index economically meaningless over long horizons.

⚡ Must-Remember Economic Identities & Laws

  • Laspeyres Price Index formula: P01L = \sum p1 q0\sum p0 q0 × 100 (uses base period quantities).
  • Paasche Price Index formula: P01P = \sum p1 q1\sum p0 q1 × 100 (uses current period quantities).
  • Fisher's Ideal Index formula: P01F = \sqrt{P01L × P01P} = \sqrt{\sum p1 q0\sum p0 q0 × \sum p1 q1\sum p0 q1} × 100.
  • Time Reversal Test Identity: P01 × P10 = 1, where P10 is obtained by interchanging base (0) and current (1) subscripts.
  • Factor Reversal Test Identity: Price Index × Quantity Index = Value Index (\sum p1 q1\sum p0 q0). Fisher satisfies this exactly.
  • Deflated Real Income / Wage formula: Real Income = Nominal IncomePrice Index × 100.
  • Chain-Base Link Relatives formula: Chain Index = Current Year PricePrevious Year Price × Previous Year Chain Index.
  • Duesenberry's / Cost of Living Index condition: True cost of living index bounds Laspeyres from above and Paasche from below when consumer preferences are homothetic.

🚫 Common Analytical Confusions (Contrast Table)

Laspeyres Index VS Paasche Index

Laspeyres uses base-period quantities ($q_0$) as weights and tends to overstate inflation (upward bias). Paasche uses current-period quantities ($q_1$) as weights and tends to understate inflation (downward bias) due to consumer substitution effects.

Time-Reversal Test VS Circular Test

Time-reversal tests consistency when comparing period 0 to 1 and back to 0 ($P_{01} \times P_{10} = 1$). The circular test extends this to three or more periods ($P_{01} \times P_{12} \times P_{20} = 1$), allowing multi-period cyclical comparisons without base shifts.

Nominal Wage VS Real Wage

Nominal wage is the monetary wage received by a worker unadjusted for inflation. Real wage is the purchasing power of the nominal wage obtained by deflating it with a Consumer Price Index (CPI).

Fixed-Base Index VS Chain-Base Index

Fixed-base index compares all periods to a single constant base year, making long-term structural comparisons difficult over time. Chain-base index compares each period to the immediate predecessor, accommodating structural changes and introduction of new goods seamlessly.

📚 Complete Concept-by-Concept Glossary

Every fundamental term, curve, and mechanism covered in this chapter.

Index Number: A statistical device measuring relative changes in a group of variables over a period of time or space, expressed as a percentage.
Price Index: A specialized index measuring the general change in prices of a basket of goods and services between two distinct time periods.
Quantity Index: An index number measuring changes in the physical volume or quantity of goods produced, consumed, or distributed over time.
Value Index: An index measuring changes in the total monetary value (price multiplied by quantity) of a basket of goods, combining both price and quantity changes.
Simple Index Number: An unweighted index number computed from a single variable or raw prices without incorporating quantity weights.
Weighted Index Number: An index number where each commodity is assigned an explicit weight reflecting its relative economic importance in consumption or production.
Laspeyres Price Index: A weighted price index that uses the quantities of the base period as weights ($P_{01} = \frac{\sum p_1 q_0}{\sum p_0 q_0} \times 100$), known to have an upward substitution bias.
Paasche Price Index: A weighted price index that uses the quantities of the current period as weights ($P_{01} = \frac{\sum p_1 q_1}{\sum p_0 q_1} \times 100$), known to have a downward substitution bias.
Fisher's Ideal Index: The geometric mean of the Laspeyres and Paasche price indices ($P_{01} = \sqrt{L \times P}$), satisfying both the time-reversal and factor-reversal tests.
Marshall-Edgeworth Price Index: A weighted index using the sum of base and current period quantities as weights ($q_0 + q_1$). Satisfies the unit test and time-reversal test.
Time-Reversal Test: A test formulated by Irving Fisher stating that the product of the index for period 0 to 1 and period 1 to 0 must equal unity ($P_{01} \times P_{10} = 1$).
Factor-Reversal Test: A test stating that the product of the price index and quantity index must equal the true value ratio ($P_{01} \times Q_{01} = V_{01}$). Satisfied only by Fisher's Ideal Index.
Circular Test: An extension of the time-reversal test for multiple time periods, requiring that $P_{01} \times P_{12} \times P_{20} = 1$. Satisfied by the chain-base method.
Deflation: The process of converting nominal values into real values by dividing the nominal series by a corresponding price index (deflator).
Cost of Living Index (COLI): An index measuring changes in the minimum cost of maintaining a given level of utility or standard of living across different price regimes.
Chain-Base Index: An index number where relatives for each period are computed with respect to the immediately preceding period, multiplying them successively to form a continuous series.

Question 1 of 18

Which of the following index number formulas uses base-period quantities as weights?

Explanation: Laspeyres' Price Index uses base-period quantities ($q_0$) as weights, represented as $P_{01} = \frac{\sum p_1 q_0}{\sum p_0 q_0} \times 100$. Paasche uses current-period quantities ($q_1$). Therefore, option B is correct.

Question 2 of 18

Fisher's Ideal Index is known as 'ideal' because it satisfies which of the following tests?

Explanation: Fisher's Ideal Index is unique because it satisfies both the Time-Reversal Test and the Factor-Reversal Test formulated by Irving Fisher, making it mathematically superior.

Question 3 of 18

If the Laspeyres index is 120 and the Paasche index is 108, what is Fisher's Ideal Index?

Explanation: Fisher's Ideal Index is the geometric mean of Laspeyres (L) and Paasche (P): $F = \sqrt{L \times P} = \sqrt{120 \times 108} = \sqrt{12960} \approx 113.84$. Thus, option B is correct.

Question 4 of 18

An upward substitution bias is typically associated with which index number?

Explanation: Laspeyres index uses fixed base-period quantities. When prices rise, consumers substitute cheaper goods for expensive ones. Since Laspeyres keeps quantities fixed at base levels, it assumes consumers buy the same expensive basket, creating an upward bias.

Question 5 of 18

The Circular Test is an extension of which of the following tests?

Explanation: The circular test is an extension of the time-reversal test. It requires that $P_{01} \times P_{12} \times P_{20} = 1$, ensuring consistency when shifting base years across multiple periods.

Question 6 of 18

Which of the following index numbers satisfies the Circular Test?

Explanation: Neither Laspeyres, Paasche, nor Fisher satisfies the circular test. However, the chain-base index using geometric averages of price relatives satisfies the circular test.

Question 7 of 18

If nominal wages increase by 15% over a year and the Consumer Price Index (CPI) increases by 10%, what is the approximate percentage change in real wages?

Explanation: Real wage growth $\approx$ Nominal wage growth $-$ Inflation rate = $15\% - 10\% = 5\%$. More precisely, $\frac{1.15}{1.10} - 1 = 1.0454 - 1 = +4.54%$. Option B is correct.

Question 8 of 18

Which index formula uses current-period quantities ($q_1$) as weights?

Explanation: Paasche's index uses current-period quantities as weights: $P_{01} = \frac{\sum p_1 q_1}{\sum p_0 q_1} \times 100$.

Question 9 of 18

The Unit Test requires that an index number formula:

Explanation: The unit test requires that the index number is independent of the units of measurement in which commodities are priced and weighed (e.g., kilograms vs. grams).

Question 10 of 18

What does the Value Index measure?

Explanation: The Value Index ($\frac{\sum p_1 q_1}{\sum p_0 q_0}$) measures total monetary expenditure changes, incorporating variations in both prices and quantities.

Question 11 of 18

In the chain-base method, the link relative for year $t$ is calculated with respect to:

Explanation: A chain-base index computes link relatives by taking the current year's price as a percentage of the immediately preceding year's price ($(p_t / p_{t-1}) \times 100$).

Question 12 of 18

Why is Paasche's Index said to have a downward substitution bias?

Explanation: Paasche uses current quantities ($q_1$). Because consumers buy fewer goods whose prices have risen rapidly, $q_1$ is higher for goods whose prices rose less, leading to an index that underestimates overall inflation (downward bias).

Question 13 of 18

If $\sum p_1 q_0 = 150$ and $\sum p_0 q_0 = 100$, the Laspeyres Price Index is:

Explanation: Laspeyres Index $= \frac{\sum p_1 q_0}{\sum p_0 q_0} \times 100 = \frac{150}{100} \times 100 = 150$. Option B is correct.

Question 14 of 18

Which of the following is NOT a test of consistency for index numbers?

Explanation: The standard index number consistency tests established by Irving Fisher and others are the Time-Reversal Test, Factor-Reversal Test, Unit Test, and Circular Test. Regression-reversal is not an index test.

Question 15 of 18

To convert a nominal national income series into real national income, one must:

Explanation: Deflation involves dividing nominal national income by the appropriate price index (like GDP deflator) and multiplying by 100 to yield real national income.

Question 16 of 18

If the time-reversal test $P_{01} \times P_{10} = 1$ is satisfied, it implies that:

Explanation: The time-reversal test ensures symmetry; moving from period 0 to 1 and then reversing from 1 to 0 should yield reciprocal index values whose product is unity.

Question 17 of 18

Which of the following index number formulas uses arithmetic means of price relatives with base period value weights?

Explanation: Laspeyres' index can be algebraically expressed as a weighted arithmetic mean of price relatives where weights are base period values ($p_0 q_0$). Thus, Laspeyres' index is equivalent to the weighted average of price relatives with base value weights.

Question 18 of 18

What is the primary advantage of the Chain-Base Index over the Fixed-Base Index?

Explanation: Chain-base index smoothly accommodates the entry of new goods and obsolescence of old goods over time, making it superior for long-term dynamic economic comparisons.

Previous Year Pattern • Q6

If the Consumer Price Index rises from 150 to 200, by what percentage must nominal income rise to keep real income constant?

A. 25.0%
B. 33.33%
C. 50.0%
D. 20.0%
