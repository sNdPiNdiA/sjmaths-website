Indian Economy

Poverty and Development

Poverty and Development examines the conceptualization, measurement, and multi-dimensional causal mechanisms of deprivation alongside macro-level growth frameworks. It forms a cornerstone of welfare economics and Indian economic planning, holding high weightage in competitive exams like UP PGT, TGT, and UGC-NET.

🏛️ Key Economists & Landmark Publications

Dadabhai Naoroji
1901, Poverty and Un-British Rule in India

Pioneered the estimation of 'poverty line' in India using a jail cost-of-living approach (Drain Theory).

Amartya Sen
1981, Poverty and Famines: An Essay on Entitlement and Deprivation

Introduced the 'Entitlement Approach' shifting the focus of famine and poverty study from food availability decline (FAD) to personal entitlements.

Mahbub ul Haq
1990, Human Development Report (UNDP)

Conceptualized the Human Development Index (HDI) shifting economic focus from pure income growth to human capabilities.

Amartya Sen & Anand Kumar
1976, Poverty Indices

Formulated the Sen Poverty Index which combines headcount ratio, poverty gap, and income distribution (Gini coefficient).

Ragnar Nurkse
1953, Problems of Capital Formation in Underdeveloped Countries

Formulated the 'Vicious Circle of Poverty' explaining how low productivity leads to low savings, investment, and growth.

⚖️ Fundamental Assumptions & Hypotheses

  • Additive and cardinal/ordinal comparability of welfare metrics depends on the chosen social welfare function.
  • Poverty lines assume a fixed basket of basic nutritional and non-food requirements priced at current market rates.
  • Economic development implies structural transformation, shifting labor share from low-productivity agriculture to modern industrial sectors.
  • Income distribution follows a skewed pattern in developing economies, necessitating separate treatment of mean income and median income.
  • Capabilities approach assumes that commodities are inputs into human capabilities, which are the ultimate objects of well-being.
  • Households act as consolidated units pooling resources to cross subsistence thresholds.

1. Concepts and Measurement of Poverty: Absolute vs. Relative

Absolute poverty defines deprivation in terms of an absolute minimum standard of living based on basic calorie requirements (e.g., 2100 kcal for urban and 2400 kcal for rural India as per the Alagh Committee). Relative poverty measures inequality, looking at the lowest deciles or quintiles of income distribution relative to the societal mean. The poverty gap index measures the depth of poverty by calculating the average shortfall of the poor from the poverty line.

Key Exam Takeaways:
  • Absolute poverty uses a fixed poverty line anchor; relative poverty moves with income distribution.
  • Headcount Ratio (HCR) = (Number of Poor / Total Population) * 100.
  • Sen Index ($P_S$) accounts for headcount, depth, and distribution among the poor.

2. Evolution of Poverty Estimation Committees in India

India has a rich history of poverty estimation committees starting with the Planning Commission's Expert Group (Lakdawala Committee, 1993) which used state-specific consumer price indices. The Tendulkar Committee (2009) shifted to mixed reference period (MRP) consumption and included health and education spending. The Rangarajan Committee (2014) raised calorie, protein, and fat norms along with a higher non-food expenditure allowance.

Key Exam Takeaways:
  • Lakdawala Committee used caloric norms based on NSSO data and State-level CPI-AL and CPI-IW.
  • Tendulkar Committee recommended a monthly per capita consumption expenditure (MPCE) of Rs 27 in rural and Rs 33 in urban areas (2004-05 prices).
  • Rangarajan Committee estimated higher poverty lines: Rs 32 in rural and Rs 47 in urban areas per day.

3. Multidimensional Poverty Index (MPI) and Capability Approach

Developed by Oxford Poverty and Human Development Initiative (OPHI) and UNDP, the MPI uses the Alkire-Foster methodology. It evaluates deprivations across three equally weighted dimensions: Health, Education, and Standard of Living, utilizing 10 indicators. A person is multi-dimensionally poor if their weighted deprivation score equals or exceeds 33.33% (one-third).

Key Exam Takeaways:
  • MPI dimensions: Health (nutrition, child mortality), Education (years of schooling, school attendance), Living Standards (cooking fuel, sanitation, drinking water, electricity, housing, assets).
  • It overcomes the limitation of single-metric income poverty.
  • NITI Aayog releases the National Multidimensional Poverty Index for India.

4. Theories of Underdevelopment and Vicious Circle of Poverty

Underdevelopment is perpetuated by self-reinforcing circular mechanisms on both demand and supply sides. On the supply side: low productivity $\rightarrow$ low real income $\rightarrow$ low savings $\rightarrow$ low investment $\rightarrow$ capital deficiency. On the demand side: low income $\rightarrow$ low purchasing power $\rightarrow$ low inducement to invest $\rightarrow$ low capital formation. Breaking this circle requires a 'Big Push' or balanced/unbalanced growth strategies.

Key Exam Takeaways:
  • Supply side core formula: Low Income $\rightarrow$ Low Savings $\rightarrow$ Low Investment.
  • Ragnar Nurkse coined the aphorism: 'A country is poor because it is poor.'
  • Addressed via balanced growth (Nurkse) or unbalanced growth (Albert Hirschman).

📐 Mathematical Formulations & Equilibrium Conditions

Headcount Ratio (HCR)
H = qN

Measures the proportion of the population ($N$) whose income or consumption falls below the poverty line ($z$), denoted by $q$.

Poverty Gap Index (PGI)
PG = 1N \sumi=1q z - yiz

Measures the depth of poverty by averaging the proportional shortfall ($z - y_i$) of the poor from the poverty line.

Squared Poverty Gap (Foster-Greer-Thorbecke Index, FGT where $\alpha=2$)
P2 = 1N \sumi=1q z - yiz 2

Measures poverty severity by giving greater weight to the poorest individuals (inequality among the poor).

Sen Poverty Index
PS = H · Pp + (1 - H) · Gp · qq+1

Integrates Headcount ratio ($H$), normalized poverty gap of the poor ($P^p$), and Gini coefficient of income inequality among the poor ($G_p$).

Remember chronological order: 1993 (Lakdawala) $\rightarrow$ 2009 (Tendulkar) $\rightarrow$ 2014 (Rangarajan).

Exam Application: Instantly solves questions asking to arrange Indian poverty committees in historical order.

💡 FGT Index Exponents Rule ALPHA: 0=Headcount, 1=Gap, 2=Severity
For FGT index $P_\alpha$: if $\alpha=0$ it is HCR; if $\alpha=1$ it is Poverty Gap; if $\alpha=2$ it is Squared Poverty Gap (Severity/Inequality).

Exam Application: Match numerical values of $\alpha$ directly to poverty properties in matching-type UGC-NET questions.

💡 Sen Index Components H-G-G (Headcount, Gap, Gini)
Sen Index = Headcount ($H$) $\times$ Gap component $\times$ Gini of poor ($G_p$).

Exam Application: Helps eliminate incorrect options in formula-based PGT/NET questions concerning poverty measurement axioms.

⚠️ Economic Paradoxes & Boundary Exceptions

Exception / Paradox Immiserizing Growth

Conditions: Jagdish Bhagwati's proposition where economic growth (via export expansion) can result in a deterioration of a country's terms of trade so severe that the country is worse off than before.

Diagrammatic Behavior: Outward shift of Production Possibility Frontier accompanied by a worsening barter terms of trade line that shifts utility inwards.

Exception / Paradox Growth-Poverty Disconnect (Jobless Growth)

Conditions: High GDP growth rates accompanied by stagnant or declining employment elasticity, failing to trickle down to poverty reduction.

Diagrammatic Behavior: Divergence between the macro-aggregate GDP growth trajectory and the flat bottom decile consumption curves.

Exception / Paradox Kuznets Inverted-U Hypothesis Exception

Conditions: Assumes income inequality worsens in early stages of development and improves later. Exceptions occur in economies with heavy welfare states or targeted asset distribution where inequality remains low throughout.

Diagrammatic Behavior: An inverted U-curve plotting per capita income on the X-axis and Gini coefficient/Inequality on the Y-axis.

⚡ Must-Remember Economic Identities & Laws

  • Headcount Ratio (HCR): H = Q / N, where Q is the number of poor individuals and N is the total population.
  • Foster-Greer-Thorbecke (FGT) General Index: Palpha = (1/N) * summation from i=1 to Q of [((z - yi) / z)alpha], where z is poverty line, yi is income of individual i, and alpha is poverty aversion parameter.
  • Nurkse's Supply-Side Vicious Circle: Low Productivity -> Low Income -> Low Savings -> Low Investment -> Low Productivity.
  • Nurkse's Demand-Side Vicious Circle: Low Income -> Low Purchasing Power -> Low Inducement to Invest -> Low Capital Formation -> Low Income.
  • Sen's Poverty Index (Ps): Ps = H [g + (1 - g) Gp], where H is headcount ratio, g is poverty gap ratio, and Gp is Gini coefficient of income distribution among the poor.
  • Dual-Gap Model (Chenery-Strout): Investment requirements are constrained by savings gap (I - S) and foreign exchange gap (M - X).
  • Per Capita Calorie Norms (Alagh Committee): Rural = 2,400 kcal/day/capita; Urban = 2,100 kcal/day/capita.

🚫 Common Analytical Confusions (Contrast Table)

Absolute Poverty VS Relative Poverty

Absolute poverty is a fixed threshold of deprivation required for basic survival regardless of economic growth, whereas relative poverty is tied to societal distribution and falls when income inequality narrows, even if absolute living standards remain unchanged.

undefined VS undefined

URP collects consumer expenditure data for all items based on a 30-day recall period. MRP uses a 365-day recall period for infrequently purchased items (clothing, footwear, education, institutional medical care) and a 7-day or 30-day recall period for food and other items.

undefined VS undefined

Balanced growth advocates simultaneous investment across all sectors to create synchronized market demand, while unbalanced growth advocates intentional disequilibrium by investing in leading sectors with high forward and backward linkages.

undefined VS undefined

Poverty Gap Index measures the depth of poverty as an average distance from the poverty line, satisfying monotonicity. The Squared Poverty Gap Index also satisfies the transfer axiom by weighting poorer individuals more heavily, thus capturing poverty severity.

📚 Complete Concept-by-Concept Glossary

Every fundamental term, curve, and mechanism covered in this chapter.

Absolute Poverty: A condition characterized by severe deprivation of basic human needs, including food, safe drinking water, sanitation facilities, health, shelter, and education, measured using a poverty line.
Relative Poverty: Poverty defined in terms of the society in which an individual lives and therefore differs across countries; typically measured as falling below a certain percentage (e.g., 50%) of median national income.
Poverty Line: The threshold level of income or consumption expenditure deemed necessary to maintain an adequate standard of living in a given country.
Headcount Ratio (HCR): The proportion of a population that exists, or lives, below the poverty line; calculated as the number of poor individuals divided by the total population.
Poverty Gap Index: The mean distance below the poverty line (expressed as a proportion of the poverty line) as a proportion of the population, capturing the depth of poverty.
Squared Poverty Gap Index (Foster-Greer-Thorbecke Index, alpha=2): A measure that assesses both the incidence and depth of poverty while giving greater weight to the poorest of the poor by squaring the poverty gaps.
Capability Approach: A normative framework advanced by Amartya Sen emphasizing that poverty should be measured not merely by low income, but by the deprivation of basic capabilities (what people can do and be).
Multidimensional Poverty Index (MPI): An international measure of acute poverty covering developing countries, utilizing three dimensions (Health, Education, Standard of Living) across 10 indicators developed by OPHI and UNDP.
Vicious Circle of Poverty: A circular constellation of forces tending to act and react in such a way as to keep a poor country, region, or family in a state of poverty (e.g., low income leads to low savings, low investment, low productivity, and low income).
Big Push Theory: A development economics theory associated with Paul Rosenstein-Rodan stating that a 'minimum quantum' of investment is necessary to jump-start economic development in stagnant economies.
Balanced Growth Theory: Nurkse's doctrine advocating simultaneous and harmonious investments in all sectors of an underdeveloped economy to break the vicious circle of poverty by creating mutual markets.
Unbalanced Growth Theory: Albert Hirschman's strategy arguing that deliberate imbalances should be created in strategic sectors to exploit linkages and stimulate entrepreneurial decision-making.
Alagh Committee (1979): The first official task force in India to define a poverty line based on minimum calorie consumption requirements (2100 kcal for urban, 2400 kcal for rural areas).
Lakdawala Committee (1993): Committee that recommended disaggregating national poverty lines to state-level consumer price indices (CPI-AL and CPI-IW) and maintained calorie norms.
Tendulkar Committee (2009): Expert group that shifted away from calorie-only norms to a wider consumption basket incorporating health and education expenditure, using Mixed Reference Period (MRP).
Rangarajan Committee (2014): Expert committee that recommended a higher poverty line based on revised normative levels of nourishment, clothing, housing, and other essentials using Uniform Reference Period (URP).

Question 1 of 18

Which poverty estimation committee in India was the first to transition from a calorie-centric poverty line to a comprehensive consumption basket including health and education?

Explanation: The Suresh Tendulkar Expert Group (2009) moved away from independent calorie-based poverty lines to a broader consumption basket based on MRP and included expenditures on health and education.

Question 2 of 18

In the context of the Multidimensional Poverty Index (MPI), which three dimensions are evaluated?

Explanation: The global MPI assesses acute multidimensional poverty across 10 indicators grouped into three equal dimensions: Health, Education, and Standard of Living.

Question 3 of 18

What does the Foster-Greer-Thorbecke (FGT) index measure when the parameter alpha is set to 2?

Explanation: When alpha = 0, the FGT index gives the Headcount Ratio. When alpha = 1, it gives the Poverty Gap Index. When alpha = 2, it gives the Squared Poverty Gap Index, which measures poverty severity by giving greater weight to the poorest.

Question 4 of 18

According to Ragnar Nurkse, the supply-side vicious circle of poverty begins and ends with which primary economic variable?

Explanation: Nurkse's supply-side circle posits that low productivity leads to low real income, low savings, low investment, and back to low productivity.

Question 5 of 18

Amartya Sen's Capability Approach defines poverty primarily as:

Explanation: Amartya Sen argued that poverty should be seen as the deprivation of basic capabilities (freedom to achieve valuable functionings) rather than just lack of income.

Question 6 of 18

Which of the following committees recommended a daily calorie norm of 2400 kcal for rural areas and 2100 kcal for urban areas in India?

Explanation: The Task Force on Projections of Minimum Needs and Effective Consumption Demand (1979), headed by Y.K. Alagh, officially established these calorie norms.

Question 7 of 18

What is the primary distinction between absolute and relative poverty?

Explanation: Absolute poverty uses a fixed basket of goods representing basic needs. Relative poverty is comparative, defining the poor as those whose incomes fall below a fraction of the national median.

Question 8 of 18

Which development theory advocates 'balanced growth' to break the vicious circle of poverty by simultaneously investing in diverse sectors?

Explanation: Ragnar Nurkse emphasized balanced growth to expand the domestic market size through simultaneous investments in multiple consumer goods industries.

Question 9 of 18

In poverty measurement, what does the 'Poverty Gap' indicate?

Explanation: The poverty gap measures the depth of poverty by calculating the mean percentage distance separating the population's income from the poverty line.

Question 10 of 18

The Rangarajan Committee (2014) estimated the monthly per capita consumption expenditure (MPCE) poverty line for urban areas at:

Explanation: The Rangarajan Committee estimated the poverty line at a monthly per capita consumption expenditure of Rs. 1407 for urban areas and Rs. 972 for rural areas.

Question 11 of 18

Which of the following conditions satisfies the 'Monotonicity Axiom' for a poverty index?

Explanation: The monotonicity axiom states that, other things equal, a reduction in the income of a poor person must increase the poverty index (and an increase in income must reduce it).

Question 12 of 18

The concept of 'Cumulative Causation' explaining regional disparities and underdevelopment was propounded by:

Explanation: Gunnar Myrdal formulated the theory of circular and cumulative causation to explain how economic changes reinforce regional inequalities (backwash effects outweighing spread effects).

Question 13 of 18

What is the main advantage of the Multidimensional Poverty Index (MPI) over income-based poverty measures?

Explanation: The MPI captures overlapping deprivations experienced by individuals simultaneously in health, education, and living standards, overcoming the limitations of monetary metrics alone.

Question 14 of 18

According to the Tendulkar Committee report, what percentage of India's population was estimated to be poor in 2011-12?

Explanation: Using the Tendulkar methodology, India's poverty ratio declined from 37.2% in 2004-05 to 21.9% in 2011-12.

Question 15 of 18

Which economic theorist introduced the 'Dual-Economy Model' of development where a traditional agricultural sector coexists with a modern capitalist sector?

Explanation: W. Arthur Lewis developed the classic two-sector surplus labor model of economic development in 1954.

Question 16 of 18

What is the primary critique of the Lakdawala Committee poverty estimates?

Explanation: The Lakdawala Committee relied on state-level consumer price indices for agricultural and industrial workers and kept consumption baskets anchored strictly to historical calorie norms, attracting criticism for ignoring changing consumption patterns.

Question 17 of 18

The 'Transfer Axiom' in poverty measurement requires that:

Explanation: The Sen-Shorrocks-Thon transfer axiom ensures that any regressive transfer of income from a poor person to someone even poorer decreases overall poverty, reinforcing sensitivity to the distribution among the poor.

Question 18 of 18

Which international organization computes and publishes the global Multidimensional Poverty Index (MPI) alongside the UNDP?

Explanation: The global MPI was developed by the Oxford Poverty and Human Development Initiative (OPHI) at the University of Oxford in collaboration with the Human Development Report Office of the UNDP.

Test Item 1 of 10

If a country's total population is 100 million, and 30 million people have consumption expenditures below the official poverty line, what is the Headcount Ratio (HCR)?

Rationale: Headcount Ratio = (Number of poor / Total population) 100 = (30 million / 100 million) 100 = 30%.

Test Item 2 of 10

Which of the following committees explicitly computed poverty lines separately for rural and urban areas for the first time in India?

Rationale: V.M. Dandekar and N. Rath (1971) provided the first systematic assessment of poverty in India based on a calorie intake norm of 2250 calories per day, separating rural and urban poverty estimates.

Test Item 3 of 10

Under the Global Multidimensional Poverty Index (MPI), what is the deprivation score threshold (weighted sum of deprivations) above which an individual is classified as multidimensionally poor?

Rationale: An individual is identified as multidimensionally poor if the weighted sum of their deprivations in health, education, and standard of living is equal to or greater than 33.3% (one-third of the weighted indicators).

Test Item 4 of 10

Which of the following is NOT a component of the supply-side vicious circle of poverty as formulated by Ragnar Nurkse?

Rationale: 'Low Inducement to Invest' belongs to Nurkse's demand-side vicious circle of poverty, whereas low productivity, low savings, and low capital formation belong to the supply-side circle.

Test Item 5 of 10

Consider two individuals A and B with poverty lines at Rs. 1000. Individual A earns Rs. 800, and Individual B earns Rs. 600. What are their individual poverty gaps?

Rationale: The normalized poverty gap is calculated as (z - y_i)/z. For A: (1000 - 800)/1000 = 200/1000 = 0.2. For B: (1000 - 600)/1000 = 400/1000 = 0.4.

Test Item 6 of 10

Which committee's methodology was criticized for setting the poverty line so low that it only ensured bare subsistence calorie intake without adequately accounting for actual living conditions?

Rationale: The traditional Alagh-Lakdawala calorie-anchored poverty lines were heavily criticized for treating poverty strictly as nutritional deficiency and ignoring wider non-food basic needs.

Test Item 7 of 10

What does the 'Subgroup Consistency Axiom' require of a satisfactory poverty measure?

Rationale: Subgroup consistency requires that if poverty increases in any sub-national region or demographic subgroup while remaining constant elsewhere, the aggregate national poverty index must register an increase.

Test Item 8 of 10

Which theoretical model argues that underdevelopment is caused by external dependency and unequal exchange between core capitalist nations and peripheral developing nations?

Rationale: Dependency theory, advanced by scholars like Raul Prebisch, Hans Singer, and André Gunder Frank, explains underdevelopment as a structural result of international capitalist exploitation and unequal trade relations.

Test Item 9 of 10

Which reference period was recommended by the Tendulkar Committee for measuring consumption expenditure on clothing, footwear, education, and institutional medical care?

Rationale: The Tendulkar Committee adopted the Mixed Reference Period (MRP), which uses a 365-day recall period for five infrequently purchased items (clothing, footwear, education, institutional medical care, and durable goods) and a 30-day recall period for other items.

Test Item 10 of 10

If a poverty index satisfies both the Monotonicity Axiom and the Transfer Axiom, which FGT index parameter configuration is active?

Rationale: The Headcount Ratio (alpha=0) satisfies neither. The Poverty Gap (alpha=1) satisfies monotonicity but not the transfer axiom. The Squared Poverty Gap (alpha=2) satisfies both monotonicity and the transfer axiom.
