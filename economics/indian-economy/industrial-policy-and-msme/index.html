Indian Economy

Industrial Development in India: Industrial Policies, Problems, and MSME Sector

This study note provides an exhaustive theoretical and institutional analysis of India's industrial development, covering landmark policy milestones, structural bottlenecks, and the evolving role of the Micro, Small, and Medium Enterprises (MSME) sector. Designed specifically for competitive examinations such as UP PGT Economics, UP TGT Social Science, UGC-NET, and State Assistant Professor tests, it bridges theoretical economic frameworks with empirical policy realities.

🏛️ Key Economists & Landmark Publications

P.C. Mahalanobis
1953, 'The Approach of Operational Research to Planning in India'

Formulated the Two-Sector and Four-Sector Growth Models emphasizing heavy capital goods industries as the cornerstone of India's Second Five-Year Plan.

Jawaharlal Nehru & S.K. Bose
1948, The Industrial Policy Resolution (IPR 1948)

Established the foundational mixed-economy framework, demarcating spheres for state-owned monopolies and private enterprises.

Government of India (Monopolies Inquiry Commission)
1965, Dutt Committee Report (Licensing Policy Inquiry Committee)

Highlighted the concentration of economic power and inefficiencies of the industrial licensing (Permit-License-Quota Raj) framework.

Manmohan Singh & P.V. Narasimha Rao
1991, New Industrial Policy (NIP 1991)

Championed structural economic reforms, abolishing industrial licensing for most sectors, opening FDI, and introducing liberalization, privatization, and globalization (LPG).

K.V. Kamath Committee
2020, Committee on Resolution of Stressed Assets

Recommended financial parameters for the restructuring of COVID-19-stressed industrial and MSME loans.

⚖️ Fundamental Assumptions & Hypotheses

  • State Primacy in Capital Accumulation: Early industrial policies assumed the state must directly undertake heavy manufacturing investment due to market failure and private capital scarcity.
  • Import-Substituting Industrialization (ISI): Assumed that shielding domestic infant industries through high tariff walls and quotas would foster indigenous self-reliance.
  • Employment-Output Trade-off: Traditional planning assumed a structural dichotomy where large industries maximize output growth while small/MSME units maximize employment.
  • Exogenous Technological Spillovers: Assumed that public sector undertakings (PSUs) would automatically generate positive technological externalities for downstream private industries.
  • Rigid Factor Proportions: Assumed limited substitutability between capital and labor in core industrial sectors during early plan periods.
  • Bounded Financial Markets: Assumed that directed credit and subsidized institutional financing via development banks (IDBI, IFCI) are necessary to overcome private capital market imperfections.

1. Evolution of Industrial Policies in India (1948 to Post-1991)

Traces the legislative and strategic trajectory of India's industrial policy framework. The IPR 1948 laid down the mixed economy model. The IPR 1956 (the 'Economic Constitution' of India) categorized industries into Schedule A (state monopoly), Schedule B (mixed state-private), and Schedule C (private sector). Subsequent policies (1977, 1980) emphasized decentralized rural/cottage industries and export promotion. The watershed New Industrial Policy (NIP) of July 1991 dismantled industrial licensing (except for 18, later pruned to 4 strategic sectors), abolished MRTP limits, liberalized Foreign Direct Investment (FDI), and initiated public sector disinvestment, shifting India from a command economy to a market-driven competitive regime.

Key Exam Takeaways:
  • IPR 1956 is known as the 'Economic Constitution' or 'Bible' of India's industrial policy.
  • NIP 1991 abolished industrial licensing for all except 18 industries, later reduced to 4 (defense electronics, atomic energy, railway transport, and specified hazardous chemicals).
  • MRTP Act 1969 was repealed and replaced by the Competition Act, 2002 on the recommendation of the S.V.S. Raghavan Committee.

2. Structural Problems and Bottlenecks of Indian Industry

Analyzes the chronic impediments hindering India's manufacturing sector. Major structural bottlenecks include infrastructural deficits (power, transport, logistics), stringent labor market regulations (such as Chapter VB of the Industrial Disputes Act requiring government permission for retrenchment in firms with 100+ workers, later modified in states), capital scarcity, inadequate technological upgrading, and delayed judicial dispute resolution. Furthermore, India faces the phenomenon of 'jobless growth' where services outpaced manufacturing in GDP contribution, failing to absorb surplus agricultural labor adequately.

Key Exam Takeaways:
  • Chapter VB of the Industrial Disputes Act, 1947, was historically cited as a major deterrent to firm scaling.
  • India's manufacturing share in GDP stagnated around 15-17% for decades, falling short of the National Manufacturing Policy (2011) target of 25% by 2022.
  • Logistics costs in India have historically hovered around 13-14% of GDP, reducing international trade competitiveness.

3. The MSME Sector: Definition, Classification, and Macroeconomic Significance

The Micro, Small, and Medium Enterprises (MSME) sector serves as the economic backbone of India, contributing nearly 30% to GDP, 45% to manufacturing exports, and employing over 110 million people. Following the landmark revision under the Atmanirbhar Bharat Abhiyan, the definition shifted from investment-only criteria to a composite criterion combining Investment in Plant & Machinery/Equipment AND Annual Turnover. This prevents the 'dwartism' syndrome where firms deliberately stay small to avoid losing policy sops. Institutional support is anchored by SIDBI, CGTMSE, and the CHAMPIONS portal.

Key Exam Takeaways:
  • Micro: Investment <= ₹1 crore and Turnover <= ₹5 crore.
  • Small: Investment <= ₹10 crore and Turnover <= ₹50 crore.
  • Medium: Investment <= ₹50 crore and Turnover <= ₹250 crore.

4. MSME Challenges, Policy Interventions, and Credit Support Mechanisms

Despite its immense socio-economic contribution, the MSME sector suffers from severe credit gaps, delayed payments from large corporate buyers, archaic compliance burdens, and limited formalization. To mitigate these, the government instituted the Udyam Registration portal for seamless digital registration, the Credit Guarantee Fund Trust for Micro and Small Enterprises (CGTMSE) for collateral-free loans, the TReDS (Trade Receivables Discounting System) platform to resolve liquidity choking from delayed receivables, and the Production Linked Incentive (PLI) schemes to foster global champions.

📐 Mathematical Formulations & Equilibrium Conditions

Mahalanobis Two-Sector Investment Allocation Model
λk + λc = 1   and   g = \frac{Yt - Yt-1}{Yt-1} = f(βk, λk, βc, λc)

Demonstrates how dividing total investment allocation between capital goods (\lambda_k) and consumer goods (\lambda_c) determines long-run aggregate growth and employment paths.

Effective Rate of Protection (ERP)
ERP = V' - VV = \frac{tf - aij ti}{1 - aij}

Measures the percentage increase in value added per unit in an industry resulting from tariff structures on inputs (t_i) and final outputs (t_f).

Incremental Capital-Output Ratio (ICOR)
ICOR = Δ KΔ Y = Ig

Measures the units of capital investment (I) required to generate an additional unit of output growth (g). Lower ICOR reflects higher capital efficiency in industrial production.

Total Factor Productivity (TFP) Growth (Solow Residual)
\frac{\dot{A}}{A} = \frac{\dot{Y}}{Y} - α \frac{\dot{K}}{K} - (1 - α) \frac{\dot{L}}{L}

Quantifies the portion of industrial output growth not accounted for by traditional inputs of capital and labor, capturing technological progress and efficiency gains.

⚠️ Economic Paradoxes & Boundary Exceptions

Exception / Paradox The Dwartism Paradox (Size Stagnation in Indian Manufacturing)

Conditions: Firms deliberately restrict their growth, employment scale, and capital expansion to remain within small-scale exemption brackets, fearing rigorous labor and tax compliances.

Diagrammatic Behavior: A bimodal firm-size distribution curve with a 'missing middle'—a massive base of micro enterprises and a few large firms, but very few medium-sized firms.

Exception / Paradox Jobless Industrial Growth Paradox

Conditions: Aggregate industrial output and capital intensity expand rapidly following trade liberalization, but formal employment growth remains sluggish due to premature capital-deepening.

Diagrammatic Behavior: Upward-sloping capital-labor ratio line (K/L) coupled with declining employment elasticity of output in the organized manufacturing sector.

Exception / Paradox Sick Industry Syndrome (BIFR Paradox)

Conditions: Loss-making public and private enterprises continue operations due to political pressure, legal hurdles in liquidation, and continuous subsidized credit injection.

Diagrammatic Behavior: Prolonged zombie firm survival where Return on Capital Employed (ROCE) remains permanently below the cost of capital.

⚡ Must-Remember Economic Identities & Laws

  • MSME Classification Rule (Atmanirbhar Bharat 2020): Micro (Inv <= 1 Cr, Turn <= 5 Cr); Small (Inv <= 10 Cr, Turn <= 50 Cr); Medium (Inv <= 50 Cr, Turn <= 250 Cr). Both criteria must be satisfied; if an enterprise crosses either ceiling, it transitions to the next higher bracket.
  • Licensing Exemption Rule (NIP 1991): Industrial licensing was abolished for all industries except 18 (later pruned down to 4, viz., defense aerospace, explosives, hazardous chemicals, and tobacco/cigars).
  • MRTP Threshold Limit: Initially applied to dominant undertakings with asset size exceeding Rs. 20 crores, which was progressively raised and finally dissolved with the Competition Act, 2002.
  • Public Sector Schedule Rule (IPR 1956): Schedule A reserved 17 industries exclusively for the state; Schedule B listed 12 industries where the state established new units while private units supplemented; Schedule C left the rest to the private sector.
  • Priority Sector Lending (PSL) Mandate for MSMEs: Commercial banks in India are mandated to allocate a specified percentage of Adjusted Net Bank Credit (ANBC) toward the priority sector, with explicit sub-targets for Micro Enterprises.
  • Disinvestment Proceeds Accounting: Capital receipts from PSU share sales do not form part of the government's revenue receipts; they are credited directly to the Capital Account reducing fiscal deficit.
  • Index of Industrial Production (IIP) Core Weightage: The eight core industries (Coal, Crude Oil, Natural Gas, Refinery Products, Fertilizers, Steel, Cement, Electricity) constitute nearly 40.27% of the total weight of the IIP basket.

🚫 Common Analytical Confusions (Contrast Table)

Industrial Licensing (Pre-1991) VS Industrial Deregulation (Post-1991)

Pre-1991 licensing required prior government permission (MRTP and FERA clearances) for capacity expansion, location, and production of goods, causing license-raj bottlenecks. Post-1991 abolished licensing for almost all sectors, substituting it with market-driven entrepreneurial freedom.

Investment Criterion Only (Old MSME Definition) VS Composite Criterion (Investment + Turnover, Post-2020 MSME Definition)

Older definitions relied solely on original investment in plant & machinery (distinguishing manufacturing and services), which created perverse incentives for firms to underreport size. The post-2020 composite definition incorporates annual turnover and merges manufacturing and services, removing artificial scale biases.

Privatisation VS Disinvestment

Disinvestment is the sale of government equity in a PSU, which may or may not involve transfer of management control. Privatisation specifically implies transfer of management control and majority ownership (above 50%) to private strategic investors.

MRTP Act 1969 VS Competition Act 2002

MRTP Act focused on 'monopoly control' and curtailing big business houses based on asset size thresholds (ex-ante control). The Competition Act 2002 focuses on 'prohibiting anti-competitive agreements, abuse of dominant position, and regulating combinations (mergers)' to promote consumer welfare and market freedom (ex-post behavioral regulation).

Strategic Disinvestment VS Minority Disinvestment

Minority disinvestment involves selling a small percentage of government shares while retaining majority control (usually 51% or more). Strategic disinvestment involves the sale of a substantial portion of government shareholding down to 50% or lower, along with transfer of management control.

📚 Complete Concept-by-Concept Glossary

Every fundamental term, curve, and mechanism covered in this chapter.

Industrial Policy Resolution (IPR) 1948: India's first post-independence industrial policy that laid down the framework of a mixed economy, demarcating spheres for the public and private sectors.
Industrial Policy Resolution (IPR) 1956: The 'Economic Constitution' of India that classified industries into three schedules (Schedule A, B, and C), prioritizing public sector dominance and heavy industries.
MRTP Act 1969: Monopolies and Restrictive Trade Practices Act enacted to curb economic power concentration and prevent monopolistic practices, later replaced by the Competition Act 2002.
FERA 1973: Foreign Exchange Regulation Act, which strictly regulated foreign equity participation in Indian companies, capping foreign shareholding generally at 40%.
New Industrial Policy (NIP) 1991: A watershed policy package introducing LPG (Liberalisation, Privatisation, and Globalisation), abolishing industrial licensing except for a short negative list, and dismantling MRTP limits.
Navratna and Maharatna Status: Autonomous performance-based operational and financial empowerment conferred by the government to select Central Public Sector Enterprises (CPSEs) to compete globally.
Public Sector Undertaking (PSU) Disinvestment: The dilution or sale of government equity in state-owned enterprises to private entities to enhance efficiency, reduce fiscal deficit, and raise capital.
Structural Rigidities in Industry: Bottlenecks such as archaic labor laws (Industrial Disputes Act), complex exit policies, power shortages, and infrastructural deficits impeding industrial growth.
Infant Industry Argument: Protectionist economic rationale suggesting nascent domestic industries require temporary tariff or non-tariff barriers to mature and achieve economies of scale.
MSME Revised Definition (2020): A composite criterion based on both Investment in Plant & Machinery/Equipment and Annual Turnover, eliminating distinctions between manufacturing and services.
Micro Enterprise (MSME): An enterprise where investment does not exceed Rs. 1 crore and turnover does not exceed Rs. 5 crores.
Small Enterprise (MSME): An enterprise where investment does not exceed Rs. 10 crores and turnover does not exceed Rs. 50 crores.
Medium Enterprise (MSME): An enterprise where investment does not exceed Rs. 50 crores and turnover does not exceed Rs. 250 crores.
Credit Guarantee Fund Trust for Micro and Small Enterprises (CGTMSE): A scheme providing collateral-free credit support to micro and small enterprises through institutional lenders backed by government guarantee.
Udyam Registration: A seamless, paperless, Aadhaar-based online registration system introduced by the Ministry of MSME for formalizing micro, small, and medium enterprises.
TReDS (Trade Receivables Discounting System): An electronic platform facilitating the financing of trade receivables of MSMEs from corporate buyers through multiple financiers.

Question 1 of 18

Which Industrial Policy Resolution is historically referred to as the 'Economic Constitution' of independent India?

Explanation: IPR 1956 formed the basis of the Second Five-Year Plan and classified industries into three schedules, assigning commanding heights to the public sector, hence earning the title 'Economic Constitution'.

Question 2 of 18

According to the revised MSME classification introduced in July 2020, what are the criteria for a 'Small Enterprise'?

Explanation: Under the 2020 revision, a Small Enterprise is defined as one where investment in plant and machinery or equipment does not exceed Rs. 10 crores and annual turnover does not exceed Rs. 50 crores.

Question 3 of 18

Which of the following was NOT a core component of the New Industrial Policy (NIP) of 1991?

Explanation: Nationalization of private commercial banks occurred earlier in 1969 and 1980. NIP 1991 focused on deregulation, liberalization, public sector reforms, and welcoming FDI, not nationalization.

Question 4 of 18

Under IPR 1956, industries listed in Schedule A were exclusively reserved for:

Explanation: Schedule A of IPR 1956 comprised 17 industries that were the exclusive responsibility of the state, giving public enterprises the commanding heights of the economy.

Question 5 of 18

What is the primary objective of the TReDS platform in the context of MSMEs?

Explanation: TReDS (Trade Receivables Discounting System) is an electronic platform that helps MSMEs convert their trade receivables (invoices) into cash quickly by facilitating competitive bidding by multiple financiers.

Question 6 of 18

Which of the following committees recommended the abolition of industrial licensing and MRTP asset limits in 1991?

Explanation: Policy changes leading to NIP 1991 were shaped by structural reform committees including the Narasimham Committee (financial) and structural shifts initiated by Montek Singh Ahluwalia and committee reports advising dismantling of license-permit raj.

Question 7 of 18

In the MSME composite criteria 2020, if an enterprise has an investment of Rs. 8 crores and an annual turnover of Rs. 60 crores, how will it be classified?

Explanation: Since the turnover exceeds Rs. 50 crores (reaching Rs. 60 crores, crossing the small enterprise threshold), the enterprise is upgraded to a Medium Enterprise, as both criteria must be met within limits.

Question 8 of 18

What does the term 'Navratna' signify in the context of Indian public sector enterprises?

Explanation: Navratna status is granted to high-performing CPSEs, giving them board-level autonomy to make investment decisions up to specified financial limits without government approval.

Question 9 of 18

Which of the following problems is characteristic of structural bottlenecks in Indian industry?

Explanation: Structural bottlenecks in Indian industry traditionally include power shortages, transport/logistical bottlenecks, rigid labor regulations (like the Industrial Disputes Act requirements), and bureaucratic red tape.

Question 10 of 18

The CGTMSE scheme primarily addresses which major hurdle faced by MSMEs?

Explanation: The Credit Guarantee Fund Trust for Micro and Small Enterprises (CGTMSE) provides guarantee cover to lenders, enabling them to extend collateral-free loans to micro and small enterprises.

Question 11 of 18

Assertion (A): The New Industrial Policy of 1991 abolished industrial licensing for almost all industrial sectors. Reason (R): Licensing was viewed as a major impediment to private investment, industrial growth, and market competition.

Explanation: NIP 1991 dismantled the license raj because bureaucratic licensing stifled private initiative and slowed economic growth, making (R) the exact theoretical justification for (A).

Question 12 of 18

Which of the following acts was enacted to replace the MRTP Act with the objective of preventing anti-competitive practices in markets?

Explanation: The Competition Act, 2002 repealed the MRTP Act, shifting focus from curbing mere monopoly size to preventing anti-competitive agreements and abuse of dominant position.

Question 13 of 18

What is the threshold limit of investment in plant and machinery for a 'Micro' enterprise under the current MSME definition?

Explanation: Under the revised 2020 MSME definition, a micro enterprise is defined as one with investment up to Rs. 1 crore and turnover up to Rs. 5 crores.

Question 14 of 18

Which committee recommended reservation of items for the Small Scale Industries (SSI) sector in India during the 1960s/1970s?

Explanation: The Village and Small Scale Industries Committee (Karve Committee, 1955) emphasized the role of SSIs in employment generation and rural development, paving the way for item reservations.

Question 15 of 18

The Index of Industrial Production (IIP) in India is compiled and published monthly by which organization?

Explanation: IIP is compiled and published monthly by the National Statistical Office (NSO) under the Ministry of Statistics and Programme Implementation (MoSPI).

Question 16 of 18

Strategic sale of Public Sector Undertakings (PSUs) implies:

Explanation: Strategic sale involves the sale of a substantial block of government equity along with transfer of management control to a strategic partner.

Question 17 of 18

What is the primary rationale behind the 'Priority Sector Lending' (PSL) norms for commercial banks regarding MSMEs?

Explanation: PSL targets ensure that vulnerable and employment-generating sectors like agriculture and MSMEs receive necessary bank credit without facing market discrimination.

Question 18 of 18

Which of the following sectors accounts for the largest share of employment generation in the Indian manufacturing landscape outside agriculture?

Explanation: The MSME sector is the second-largest employment generator in India after agriculture, providing tens of millions of jobs across urban and rural areas.
