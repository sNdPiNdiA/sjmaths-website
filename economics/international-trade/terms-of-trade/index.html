International Trade

Terms of Trade

Terms of trade measure the relative price of exports in terms of imports, serving as a vital indicator of international purchasing power and the distribution of gains from trade. Mastery of this concept is essential for conceptual clarity in international trade theory and holds high weightage in competitive examinations like UP PGT Economics and UGC-NET.

🏛️ Key Economists & Landmark Publications

Robert Torrens
1844, 'The Budget: On Commercial and Colonial Policy'

Pioneered the analysis of how changes in reciprocal demand affect international prices and introduced early versions of terms of trade.

John Stuart Mill
1848, 'Principles of Political Economy'

Formalized the theory of reciprocal demand, showing how the actual terms of trade settle within the limits set by comparative costs.

Alfred Marshall and Francis Y. Edgeworth
1888/1923, 'Pure Theory of Foreign Trade'

Developed offer curves (reciprocal demand curves) to determine equilibrium terms of trade graphically.

Jacob Viner
1937, 'Studies in the Theory of International Trade'

Expanded the taxonomy of terms of trade by introducing the Single Factoral and Double Factoral Terms of Trade to account for productivity changes.

Raul Prebisch and Hans Singer
1950, UN Economic Commission for Latin America Reports

Formulated the Prebisch-Singer thesis arguing for a secular deterioration of the commodity terms of trade for developing nations.

⚖️ Fundamental Assumptions & Hypotheses

  • Two-country, two-commodity framework: Standard trade models assume a simplified global economy to isolate price relationships.
  • Balanced trade: The value of total exports equals the value of total imports in equilibrium.
  • Perfect competition in international markets: Individual countries act as price takers unless specified as large open economies.
  • Constant returns to scale: Production functions exhibit CRS, ensuring unambiguous cost structures.
  • Absence of transport costs and trade barriers: Tariffs, quotas, and freight costs are initially assumed to be zero unless modified.
  • Full employment of resources: All available factors of production are fully utilized within each trading nation.

1. Commodity or Net Barter Terms of Trade

Introduced by Taussig, the Net Barter Terms of Trade (NBT) is the ratio of the price index of exports to the price index of imports ($NBT = (P_x / P_m) imes 100$). An increase indicates an improvement, meaning a unit of exports buys more units of imports.

Key Exam Takeaways:
  • Most widely used measure in official trade statistics.
  • Does not account for changes in export or import productivity.
  • An increase in NBT does not necessarily mean an increase in national welfare.

2. Gross Barter Terms of Trade and Income Terms of Trade

Gross Barter Terms of Trade (GBT) measures the ratio of physical quantity of imports to physical quantity of exports ($GBT = Q_m / Q_x$). The Income Terms of Trade (ITT), formulated by G.S. Dorrance, adjusts NBT by export volume ($ITT = (P_x / P_m) imes Q_x$), measuring the economy's total import capacity.

Key Exam Takeaways:
  • GBT reflects trade balance adjustments including unilateral transfers and capital flows.
  • ITT is superior for developing nations as it captures the purchasing power of total export earnings.
  • If export volume grows faster than NBT falls, ITT still improves.

3. Factoral Terms of Trade (Single and Double)

Single Factoral Terms of Trade (SFTT) corrects NBT by domestic export sector productivity changes ($SFTT = (P_x / P_m) imes Z_x$, where $Z_x$ is export sector productivity). Double Factoral Terms of Trade (DFTT) accounts for productivity changes in both exporting and importing nations ($DFTT = (P_x / P_m) imes (Z_x / Z_m)$).

Key Exam Takeaways:
  • SFTT measures the amount of foreign goods obtained per unit of domestic factor sacrifice.
  • DFTT incorporates technological progress abroad.
  • Vital for assessing immiserizing growth scenarios where productivity gains worsen real income.

4. Prebisch-Singer Hypothesis and Secular Deterioration

The Prebisch-Singer thesis posits that primary commodity exporters experience a secular decline in their commodity terms of trade against manufactured goods due to low income elasticity of demand for primary goods, asymmetrical market power of oligopolistic firms, and technological substitution.

Key Exam Takeaways:
  • Serves as the theoretical justification for Import Substitution Industrialization (ISI).
  • Contrasts sharply with neoclassical trade gains propositions.
  • Empirically debated due to historical commodity price booms.

📐 Mathematical Formulations & Equilibrium Conditions

Net Barter Terms of Trade (NBT)
NBT = PxPm × 100

Measures relative export-import price ratio. Higher value represents favorable movement.

Income Terms of Trade (ITT)
ITT = PxPm × Qx

Measures total import capacity funded by export revenues, combining price ratio and volume.

Single Factoral Terms of Trade (SFTT)
SFTT = PxPm × Zx

Adjusts NBT by domestic export sector productivity index $Z_x$.

Double Factoral Terms of Trade (DFTT)
DFTT = PxPm × ZxZm

Accounts for productivity changes in both domestic export sector ($Z_x$) and foreign export sector ($Z_m$).

Marshall-Lerner Condition for Trade Balance Improvement
Ex + Em > 1

Condition where currency devaluation improves the trade balance and affects terms of trade adjustments.

🧠 Exam Tips, Tricks & Mnemonics (10-Second Shortcuts)

💡 Mnemonic for Terms of Trade Indices N-G-I-S-D
Net (N), Gross (G), Income (I), Single Factoral (S), Double Factoral (D).

Exam Application: Instantly recall the five primary types of terms of trade when matched with their creators (Taussig, Viner, Dorrance) in matching questions.

💡 Quick Rule on NBT vs ITT Divergence Q_x Direction Rule
If NBT falls but export volume (Q_x) rises faster than the price drop, ITT rises.

Exam Application: Fast calculation in numerical questions where developing nations experience falling commodity prices but massive volume surges.

⚠️ Economic Paradoxes & Boundary Exceptions

Exception / Paradox Immiserizing Growth

Conditions: Bhagwati's theorem where economic growth (via export expansion) worsens a country's terms of trade so severely that real national welfare drops below pre-growth levels.

Diagrammatic Behavior: Offer curve shifts outward aggressively, driving equilibrium price line to a much flatter slope, lowering utility.

Exception / Paradox Transfer Problem Paradox (Keynes-Ohlin Debate)

Conditions: A capital transfer from Country A to Country B can paradoxically improve Country A's terms of trade if the marginal propensity to spend on export goods in B exceeds that in A.

Diagrammatic Behavior: Offer curves shift in directions contrary to standard assumptions, reversing expected price adjustments.

Exception / Paradox Dutch Disease

Conditions: Discovery of natural resources appreciates the real exchange rate, destroying the manufacturing export sector via adverse terms of trade effects.

Diagrammatic Behavior: Resource boom shifts production possibility frontier asymmetrically, appreciating currency and raising non-tradable prices.

⚡ Must-Remember Economic Identities & Laws

  • Commodity Terms of Trade Formula: Tc = (Px / Pm) × 100
  • Gross Barter Terms of Trade Formula: Tg = (Qm / Qx) × 100
  • Income Terms of Trade Formula: Ty = (Px / Pm) × Qx (or Tc × Qx / 100)
  • Single Factoral Terms of Trade Formula: Ts = Tc × Zx, where Zx is domestic export sector productivity
  • Double Factoral Terms of Trade Formula: Tdf = Tc × (Zx / Zm), incorporating foreign productivity Zm
  • Equilibrium Condition for Balanced Trade in Terms of Trade: Value of Exports = Value of Imports (Px · Qx = Pm · Qm)
  • Bhagwati Immiserizing Growth Condition: Growth must be ultra-export-biased, trade must be a large share of GNP, and the foreign offer curve must be inelastic.

🚫 Common Analytical Confusions (Contrast Table)

Commodity (Net Barter) Terms of Trade ($Tc$) VS Gross Barter Terms of Trade ($Tg$)

$Tc$ measures price ratios ($Px/Pm$), focusing on purchasing power per unit of export. $Tg$ measures physical quantity ratios ($Qm/Qx$), reflecting trade balance and commodity weights.

Income Terms of Trade ($Ty$) VS Commodity Terms of Trade ($Tc$)

$Tc$ only reflects price ratios. $Ty$ multiplies $Tc$ by the physical volume of exports ($Qx$), capturing the actual import capacity derived from export earnings.

Single Factoral Terms of Trade ($Ts$) VS Double Factoral Terms of Trade ($Tdf$)

$Ts$ adjusts $Tc$ by domestic export productivity changes only ($Zx$). $Tdf$ adjusts $Tc$ by both domestic and foreign productivity changes ($Zx / Zm$).

Prebisch-Singer Hypothesis VS Immiserizing Growth

Prebisch-Singer is a secular long-term trend of declining primary-to-manufactured terms of trade. Immiserizing growth is a specific welfare-reducing outcome caused by rapid export expansion under inelastic foreign demand.

📚 Complete Concept-by-Concept Glossary

Every fundamental term, curve, and mechanism covered in this chapter.

Commodity (Net Barter) Terms of Trade: Introduced by Taussig, it is the ratio of the price index of exports to the price index of imports, expressed as $Tc = (Px / Pm) \times 100$.
Gross Barter Terms of Trade: Formulated by Taussig, it is the ratio of the physical quantity index of imports to the physical quantity index of exports, expressed as $Tg = (Qm / Qx) \times 100$.
Income Terms of Trade: Suggested by Dorrance, it measures a country's capacity to import based on export earnings, expressed as $Ty = (Px / Pm) \times Qx$, representing export value relative to import prices.
Single Factoral Terms of Trade: Devised by Jacob Viner, it adjusts the commodity terms of trade by the productivity index in the domestic export sector: $Ts = (Px / Pm) \times Zx$, where $Zx$ is the export productivity index.
Double Factoral Terms of Trade: Also devised by Jacob Viner, it accounts for productivity changes in both the home country's export sector and the foreign country's export sector: $Tdf = (Px / Pm) \times (Zx / Zm)$.
Prebisch-Singer Hypothesis: States that there is a secular (long-term) deterioration in the terms of trade of developing countries (primary commodity exporters) vis-à-vis developed countries (manufactured goods exporters).
Engel's Law: As household income rises, the proportion of income spent on food and primary commodities decreases, contributing to slower demand growth for primary exports relative to manufactured goods.
Income Elasticity of Demand for Primary Goods: Generally less than unity (< 1), meaning that as global incomes grow, the demand for primary products grows at a slower pace than income.
Income Elasticity of Demand for Manufactured Goods: Generally greater than unity (> 1), meaning that global demand for manufactured goods expands rapidly as world income rises.
Technical Progress in International Trade: Classified by Hicks into neutral, export-biased, and import-biased, which alters factor supplies, output, and subsequently the terms of trade.
Immiserizing Growth: Bhagwati's concept where economic growth in an export-oriented developing country leads to such a severe deterioration in its commodity terms of trade that its real welfare falls below pre-growth levels.
Singer's Asymmetric Market Structures: Highlights that primary commodity markets are competitive with downward price flexibility, whereas manufactured goods markets feature oligopolistic price stickiness and upward wage-push inflation.
Utility Terms of Trade: Measures the changes in the amount of satisfaction a country obtains from a given volume of exports and imports, incorporating consumer preferences.

Question 1 of 18

Who formulated the Net Barter (Commodity) Terms of Trade concept?

Explanation: F.W. Taussig originally formulated the Net Barter Terms of Trade (also known as Commodity Terms of Trade) defined as the ratio of export prices to import prices.

Question 2 of 18

If a country's export price index rises from 100 to 120 and its import price index rises from 100 to 150, what is the new Commodity Terms of Trade (base 100)?

Explanation: Commodity Terms of Trade $Tc = (Px / Pm) \times 100 = (120 / 150) \times 100 = 0.8 \times 100 = 80$.

Question 3 of 18

Which of the following terms of trade measures the actual 'capacity to import' based on current export earnings?

Explanation: Income Terms of Trade ($Ty = (Px / Pm) \times Qx$) represents the volume of imports that can be purchased with the total earnings of exports, hence measuring import capacity.

Question 4 of 18

Jacob Viner introduced which of the following concepts to correct the deficiency of Commodity Terms of Trade regarding productivity changes?

Explanation: Jacob Viner introduced Single and Double Factoral Terms of Trade to incorporate productivity changes in export industries.

Question 5 of 18

The Prebisch-Singer hypothesis posits that over the long run, the terms of trade for developing nations tend to:

Explanation: Prebisch and Singer argued that developing countries exporting primary products experience a secular deterioration in their terms of trade against manufactured goods.

Question 6 of 18

According to Prebisch, what is the income elasticity of demand for primary commodities in developed nations?

Explanation: Primary commodities exhibit low income elasticity of demand (less than unity) according to Engel's Law, meaning demand grows slower than world income.

Question 7 of 18

What does the Gross Barter Terms of Trade ($Tg$) focus on?

Explanation: Gross Barter Terms of Trade is expressed as $Tg = (Qm / Qx) \times 100$, capturing the ratio of physical quantities of imports to exports.

Question 8 of 18

If domestic export sector productivity ($Zx$) increases while $Px$ and $Pm$ remain constant, what happens to the Single Factoral Terms of Trade ($Ts$)?

Explanation: $Ts = (Px / Pm) \times Zx$. An increase in $Zx$ directly raises $Ts$, reflecting higher factor productivity per unit of export.

Question 9 of 18

Which economist is associated with the concept of 'Immiserizing Growth' where terms of trade deterioration outweighs output growth?

Explanation: Jagdish Bhagwati (1958) demonstrated that growth can result in immiserization if adverse terms of trade effects dominate output gains.

Question 10 of 18

In the context of market structures, Singer argued that wages and prices in developed nations are flexible downwards because of strong trade unions. Is this statement true or false?

Explanation: False. Singer argued that developed countries have oligopolistic markets with upward wage-price stickiness, whereas primary producers face competitive markets with downward price flexibility during downturns.

Question 11 of 18

If $Tc$ increases, does it always mean an increase in the nation's total welfare?

Explanation: An improvement in $Tc$ means higher purchasing power per unit of export, but if export quantities drop drastically, total export revenue and welfare might decline.

Question 12 of 18

The Income Terms of Trade is also referred to in literature as:

Explanation: Dorrance's Income Terms of Trade ($Ty = (Px/Pm) \times Qx$) is widely recognized and used as an index of a country's capacity to import.

Question 13 of 18

Which factor contributes to the secular deterioration of terms of trade for primary producing nations according to Prebisch?

Explanation: Technical progress in manufacturing creates synthetic substitutes for primary goods, reducing demand and worsening primary exporters' terms of trade.

Question 14 of 18

Double Factoral Terms of Trade ($Tdf$) is expressed as:

Explanation: Double Factoral Terms of Trade is $Tdf = (Px / Pm) \times (Zx / Zm)$, adjusting for productivity in both domestic export and foreign export sectors.

Question 15 of 18

If the export price index of a country is 140 and its import price index is 120, what is the Commodity Terms of Trade?

Explanation: $Tc = (140 / 120) \times 100 = 1.1667 \times 100 = 116.67$.

Question 16 of 18

Which of the following describes 'neutral technical progress' in the exporting sector in terms of its effect on terms of trade?

Explanation: Hicksian neutral technical progress shifts production isoquants outward equiproportionately, leaving the relative output ratio constant, thus exerting neutral pressure on terms of trade.

Question 17 of 18

The concept that links terms of trade to the amount of consumer satisfaction derived from traded goods is known as:

Explanation: Utility terms of trade incorporates changes in consumer tastes and preferences, measuring the utility obtained from trading.

Question 18 of 18

Assertion (A): Developing nations experienced declining terms of trade during the post-WWII decades. Reason (R): The demand for primary products grows faster than world income.

Explanation: Assertion (A) aligns with the Prebisch-Singer hypothesis. Reason (R) is false because demand for primary products grows slower than world income due to low income elasticity (Engel's Law).

Previous Year Pattern • Q2

If the export price index ($Px$) increases by 10% and import price index ($Pm$) increases by 20%, what is the percentage change in the Commodity Terms of Trade?

A. A. Increase of 10%
B. B. Decrease of approximately 8.33%
C. C. Decrease of 10%
D. D. Increase of 5.55%
Solution: Let initial $Px=100$, $Pm=100$. New $Px=110$, New $Pm=120$. New $Tc = (110/120) \times 100 = 91.67$. Percentage change = $((91.67 - 100) / 100) \times 100 = -8.33\%$, representing an approximate 8.33% decrease.
Previous Year Pattern • Q3

Which of the following statements about the Prebisch-Singer thesis is/are correct? I. It predicts long-term improvement in terms of trade for developing countries. II. It is based on differences in income elasticities of demand between primary and manufactured goods. III. Technological progress in developed countries often creates synthetic substitutes for primary exports.

A. A. Only I and II
B. B. Only II and III
C. C. Only I and III
D. D. I, II and III
Solution: Statement I is incorrect because Prebisch-Singer predicts long-term deterioration, not improvement. Statements II and III are correct pillars of the hypothesis.
Previous Year Pattern • Q4

In Bhagwati's analysis of 'Immiserizing Growth', growth in export sectors leads to a deterioration of terms of trade that is so severe that:

A. A. Domestic employment drops to zero
B. B. Real national welfare falls below the pre-growth level
C. C. The foreign offer curve becomes perfectly elastic
D. D. Export volume declines absolutely
Solution: Immiserizing growth occurs when adverse terms of trade changes outweigh the positive primary gains of growth, causing overall real welfare to fall below pre-growth levels.
Previous Year Pattern • Q5

Given: Export price index $Px = 150$, Import price index $Pm = 125$, and Physical volume of exports $Qx = 200$. What is the Income Terms of Trade?

A. A. 120
B. B. 240
C. C. 300
D. D. 187.5
Solution: Income Terms of Trade $Ty = (Px / Pm) \times Qx = (150 / 125) \times 200 = 1.2 \times 200 = 240$.
