Micro Economics

Market Structures: Perfect Competition, Monopoly, Monopolistic Competition, and Oligopoly

Market structures define the organizational and competitive characteristics of a market, dictating how firms price and output their products. Mastery of these structures is essential for understanding resource allocation, pricing efficiency, and industrial organization in advanced microeconomics and competitive teacher recruitment examinations.

🏛️ Key Economists & Landmark Publications

Adam Smith
1776, 'The Wealth of Nations'

Introduced the foundational concepts of market competition, the 'invisible hand', and the natural price versus market price mechanism.

⚖️ Fundamental Assumptions & Hypotheses

  • Homogeneous Product: Under perfect competition, firms produce identical, standardized goods, ensuring zero consumer preference for any specific seller.
  • Large Number of Buyers and Sellers: Individual market participants are price takers with negligible market share, preventing strategic market manipulation.
  • Free Entry and Exit: Absence of legal, financial, or technological barriers allows firms to enter or leave the industry in the long run.
  • Perfect Information: All economic agents possess complete and costless knowledge regarding prices, product quality, and production techniques.
  • Profit Maximization: The behavioral objective of every firm is strictly to maximize economic profit where Marginal Revenue equals Marginal Cost.
  • Non-Interference / No Collusion: Firms make independent decisions without coordinating output, pricing, or marketing strategies.

1. Perfect Competition: Equilibrium, Price Taker Behavior, and Efficiency

Perfect competition represents an idealized market structure where the firm faces a perfectly elastic demand curve (horizontal at the market-determined price). Since Price equals Average Revenue and Marginal Revenue ($P = AR = MR$), the firm maximizes profit where $MC = MR$. In the short run, firms can earn supernormal profits, normal profits, or incur losses, continuing production as long as price covers average variable cost ($P \ge AVC$). In the long run, free entry and exit eliminate economic profits, driving price down to the minimum of Long-Run Average Cost ($LAC$), achieving both productive and allocative efficiency.

Key Exam Takeaways:
  • In perfect competition, the firm's short-run supply curve is its Marginal Cost curve lying above the minimum of Average Variable Cost.
  • Allocative efficiency occurs at $P = MC$, and productive efficiency occurs at minimum $LAC$.
  • The industry demand curve slopes downward, but the individual firm's demand curve is perfectly elastic (infinite price elasticity).

2. Pure Monopoly: Price Maker, Barriers to Entry, and Welfare Loss

Monopoly features a single seller dominating the entire market, facing a downward-sloping market demand curve and a Marginal Revenue curve that lies strictly below the Average Revenue curve. Monopolies arise due to structural barriers such as economies of scale (natural monopoly), legal patents, control over raw materials, or exclusive licensing. Equilibrium occurs where $MR = MC$, yielding a price higher than marginal cost ($P > MC$). This distortion generates a Deadweight Loss (DWL) to society, representing lost consumer and producer surplus, and fails to achieve either productive or allocative efficiency.

Key Exam Takeaways:
  • Monopolist's Marginal Revenue relationship with price is given by $MR = P(1 - \frac{1}{|e|})$.
  • A monopolist never operates in the inelastic portion of its demand curve ($|e| < 1$) because $MR$ would be negative.
  • Price discrimination (First, Second, and Third Degree) allows a monopolist to capture consumer surplus and increase profits.

3. Monopolistic Competition: Product Differentiation and Excess Capacity

Developed simultaneously by Chamberlin and Robinson, monopolistic competition combines elements of monopoly and competition. Many firms produce differentiated yet substitutable products through branding, packaging, or location. Because of product differentiation, each firm faces a negatively sloped, highly elastic demand curve. In the short run, firms earn economic profits, which attracts new entrants. In the long run, entry shifts the firm's demand curve inward until it is tangent to the Average Cost curve at a point of excess capacity (operating to the left of the minimum point of $LAC$).

Key Exam Takeaways:
  • Monopolistic competition is characterized by product differentiation and non-price competition (advertising/selling costs).
  • The long-run equilibrium condition requires $P = LAC > \text{minimum } LAC$, proving the existence of excess capacity.
  • Unlike perfect competition, price exceeds marginal cost ($P > MC$) in long-run equilibrium.

4. Oligopoly: Interdependence, Game Theory, and Kinked Demand Curve

Oligopoly is defined by few interdependent firms where the action of one firm directly influences the profits and strategies of rivals. This strategic interaction is analyzed using game theory (Nash equilibrium, prisoner's dilemma) or traditional models such as Cournot, Bertrand, and Stackelberg. To explain price rigidity under non-collusive oligopoly, Paul Sweezy introduced the 'Kinked Demand Curve' model, where rivals match price cuts but ignore price increases, creating a discontinuous, step-down marginal revenue curve that explains why prices remain stable despite cost fluctuations.

Key Exam Takeaways:
  • Oligopoly models focus on strategic interdependence and game-theoretic behavior.
  • Sweezy's Kinked Demand curve exhibits a kink at the prevailing market price, resulting in a vertical gap in the Marginal Revenue curve.
  • Collusive oligopolies often form cartels (e.g., OPEC) to joint-maximize profits, though prone to cheating.

⚠️ Economic Paradoxes & Boundary Exceptions

Exception / Paradox Bertrand Paradox

Conditions: Under oligopoly with homogeneous products and constant marginal costs, firms competing in prices simultaneously drive price down to marginal cost ($P = MC$), replicating perfect competition with only two firms.

Diagrammatic Behavior: Price collapses immediately to the competitive level, eliminating monopoly profits even in a duopoly setting.

Exception / Paradox Kinked Demand Curve Discontinuity

Conditions: When rival firms asymmetric-respond to price changes (matching cuts, ignoring hikes), the demand curve kinks at the prevailing price.

Diagrammatic Behavior: The resulting Marginal Revenue curve develops a vertical gap or discontinuity, making optimal output invariant to moderate marginal cost shifts.

Exception / Paradox Natural Monopoly Cost Structure

Conditions: When fixed setup costs are exceptionally high and marginal costs are low, Average Total Cost declines continuously over the entire range of market demand.

Diagrammatic Behavior: The LAC curve slopes downward throughout the relevant market output, making competitive market structures economically unviable.

⚡ Must-Remember Economic Identities & Laws

  • Profit Maximization Condition: Marginal Revenue equals Marginal Cost (MR = MC).
  • Perfect Competition Long-Run Equilibrium: P = MR = AR = ext{Minimum LRAC} = MC.
  • Monopoly Markup Pricing Rule: P = [Ed / (Ed - 1)] imes MC, where Ed is the absolute value of price elasticity of demand.
  • Lerner Index Identity: L = (P - MC) / P = 1 / |Ed|.
  • Cournot Reaction Function Derivation: partial pi1 / partial q1 = 0 implies q1 = R1(q2) = (a - c - b q2) / 2b (for linear demand P = a - b(q1 + q2) and zero MC).
  • Collusive Oligopoly Joint Profit Maximization: Aggregate MR = Sigma MCi distributed across firms such that MC1 = MC2 = dots = MRc.
  • Euler's Theorem Application in Long-Run Perfect Competition: Exhaustion of total product when returns to scale are constant (Y = MPL cdot L + MPK cdot K).
  • Kinked Demand Marginal Revenue Discontinuity: MR exhibits a vertical gap at the quantity corresponding to the kink price.

🚫 Common Analytical Confusions (Contrast Table)

Perfect Competition (Price Taker) VS Monopolistic Competition (Price Maker)

Perfect competition features homogeneous products and horizontal demand ($P = MR$), whereas monopolistic competition features differentiated products and downward-sloping demand ($P > MR$). Both have zero economic profit in the long run due to free entry.

Cournot Competition (Quantity Competition) VS Bertrand Competition (Price Competition)

In Cournot, firms choose quantities simultaneously and price settles above marginal cost. In Bertrand with homogeneous goods, firms choose prices simultaneously, driving price down to marginal cost even with just two firms.

Allocative Efficiency VS Productive Efficiency

Allocative efficiency requires $P = MC$ (producing what society wants). Productive efficiency requires production at the minimum of the average cost curve ($P = ext{min LRAC}$). Perfect competition achieves both; monopoly achieves neither.

Collusive Oligopoly (Cartel) VS Non-Collusive Oligopoly (Game Theory / Sweezy Model)

Collusive oligopolies explicitly or implicitly coordinate to act as a multi-plant monopolist. Non-collusive oligopolies feature strategic independence, rivalry, uncertainty about competitor reactions, and incentives to cheat.

First-Degree Price Discrimination VS Third-Degree Price Discrimination

First-degree (perfect) discrimination charges each consumer their exact maximum willingness to pay, extracting all consumer surplus and eliminating deadweight loss. Third-degree segments the market into groups and charges different prices based on group elasticity, equating marginal revenues across segments.

📚 Complete Concept-by-Concept Glossary

Every fundamental term, curve, and mechanism covered in this chapter.

Price Taker: A firm under perfect competition that has zero market power and must accept the market-determined equilibrium price.
Lerner Index of Monopoly Power: A measure of market power calculated as $(P - MC) / P$, which equals $1 / |E_d|$ at profit maximization.
Deadweight Loss (DWL): The net loss of total economic surplus (consumer plus producer surplus) resulting from monopolistic underproduction and suboptimal pricing.
Excess Capacity Theorem: The condition in monopolistic competition where firms produce on the downward-sloping portion of their LRAC curve, leading to output levels below the cost-minimizing scale.
Product Differentiation: The real or perceived differences in products (branding, features, location) that grant monopolistic competitors downward-sloping demand curves.
Kinked Demand Curve: Paul Sweezy's oligopoly model featuring a demand curve with a kink at the prevailing price, based on the assumption that rivals match price cuts but ignore price hikes.
Cournot Duopoly Model: An oligopoly model where firms simultaneously choose output quantities, treating rival output as given, leading to the Cournot-Nash equilibrium.
Bertrand Duopoly Model: An oligopoly model where firms simultaneously choose prices, leading to price competition that drives price down to marginal cost (Bertrand Paradox).
Stackelberg Leadership Model: A sequential-move oligopoly model where a leader firm chooses its output first, anticipating the follower's best-response reaction function.
Collusion and Cartel: An agreement among oligopolistic firms to coordinate prices and output levels to act collectively as a monopolist and maximize joint profits.
Dominant Strategy: In game theory, a strategy that yields the highest payoff for a player regardless of the strategies chosen by other players.
Nash Equilibrium: A set of strategies in a game where no player has an incentive to unilaterally deviate given the strategies chosen by opponents.
Natural Monopoly: An industry where economies of scale are so extensive throughout the relevant range of output that a single firm can supply the entire market at a lower cost than multiple firms.
Allocative Efficiency: A state of the economy where price equals marginal cost ($P = MC$), maximizing the sum of consumer and producer surplus.
Productive Efficiency: Production occurring at the minimum point of the long-run average cost curve ($P = ext{Minimum LRAC}$).

Question 1 of 18

In a perfectly competitive market, the short-run supply curve of an individual firm is represented by:

Explanation: A perfectly competitive firm's short-run supply curve is its marginal cost curve for all prices at or above the minimum average variable cost (AVC). Below min AVC, the firm shuts down and produces zero output.

Question 2 of 18

If a monopolist faces a linear inverse demand curve given by $P = 100 - 2Q$ and has a constant marginal cost of $20$ with zero fixed cost, what is the profit-maximizing output and price?

Explanation: Total Revenue $TR = P cdot Q = (100 - 2Q)Q = 100Q - 2Q^2$. Marginal Revenue $MR = 100 - 4Q$. Set $MR = MC implies 100 - 4Q = 20 implies 4Q = 80 implies Q = 20$. Substitute $Q = 20$ into demand: $P = 100 - 2(20) = 60$.

Question 3 of 18

The Lerner Index of Monopoly Power is defined as $(P - MC) / P$. If the price elasticity of demand for a firm's product is $2$, what is its Lerner Index and markup factor?

Explanation: The Lerner Index equals $1 / |E_d|$. Given $|E_d| = 2$, the Lerner Index is $1 / 2 = 0.5$. The pricing equation is $P = [E_d / (E_d - 1)] imes MC = [2 / (2 - 1)]MC = 2MC$, yielding a markup factor of 2.0.

Question 4 of 18

Which of the following conditions characterizes the long-run equilibrium of a firm in monopolistic competition?

Explanation: In monopolistic competition, free entry drives economic profit to zero ($P = LRAC$), but because demand is downward-sloping, this tangency occurs on the falling portion of the LRAC curve, implying $P > MC$ and positive excess capacity.

Question 5 of 18

In the Cournot duopoly model with linear demand $P = a - b(q_1 + q_2)$ and zero marginal cost, what is each firm's equilibrium output share of the total market output?

Explanation: In a symmetric Cournot duopoly with $n$ firms, each firm produces $1 / (n+1)$ of the competitive output. For $n = 2$, each firm produces $1/3$ of the competitive output $a/b$, making total output $2/3$ of the competitive level.

Question 6 of 18

The 'Bertrand Paradox' occurs when homogeneous-product oligopolists competing on price find that:

Explanation: Under Bertrand competition with identical goods and constant marginal costs, firms undercut each other until price equals marginal cost ($P = MC$), replicating the perfectly competitive outcome with just two firms.

Question 7 of 18

The kinked demand curve model of oligopoly (Sweezy model) explains:

Explanation: Sweezy's kinked demand curve explains why prices tend to remain rigid in oligopolistic markets: rivals match price cuts (inelastic lower segment) but ignore price increases (elastic upper segment), creating a discontinuity in MR.

Question 8 of 18

Which of the following efficiency types is lost under pure monopoly?

Explanation: A pure monopoly violates allocative efficiency because $P > MC$. It also often fails to achieve productive efficiency due to lack of competitive pressure ('X-inefficiency') and failure to operate at minimum LRAC.

Question 9 of 18

In a Nash equilibrium of a static simultaneous game:

Explanation: A Nash equilibrium is a profile of strategies such that each player's strategy is an optimal response to the other players' strategies. No player has a incentive for unilateral deviation.

Question 10 of 18

What is the primary barrier to entry that gives rise to a natural monopoly?

Explanation: A natural monopoly arises when long-run average costs decline continuously over the range of market demand, meaning one firm can supply the market cheaper than multiple firms.

Question 11 of 18

If a perfectly competitive industry experiences an increase in market demand, in the short run, existing firms will:

Explanation: An upward shift in market demand raises the equilibrium price in the short run. Since price-taking firms face this higher price, they expand output until their higher $MC$ equals the new price, earning short-run economic profits.

Question 12 of 18

In the Stackelberg leadership model, the leader's advantage stems from:

Explanation: The Stackelberg leader moves first and predicts how the follower will react (using the follower's reaction function), allowing the leader to capture a larger market share and higher profits than in Cournot equilibrium.

Question 13 of 18

Under third-degree price discrimination, a profit-maximizing monopolist selling in two separate markets will charge a higher price in the market with:

Explanation: Using the markup formula $P = [E / (E - 1)]MC$, the market with less elastic demand (lower absolute elasticity) bears a higher price, while the more elastic market bears a lower price.

Question 14 of 18

What does the 'Excess Capacity Theorem' imply about monopolistic competition?

Explanation: Excess capacity is defined as the difference between the cost-minimizing output level and the output level actually produced by a monopolistically competitive firm operating at the tangency point of its downward-sloping demand and LRAC curve.

Question 15 of 18

Which of the following is a characteristic feature of an oligopoly market?

Explanation: Oligopoly is defined by the presence of a few dominant firms whose decisions regarding price and output are mutually interdependent, meaning each firm must anticipate rivals' reactions.

Question 16 of 18

If a cartel breaks down due to individual members cheating on output quotas, the resulting market outcome tends to approach:

Explanation: Cartel instability arises because individual members have an incentive to secretly expand output. When all cheat, output expands toward the Cournot or competitive level, driving prices down toward marginal cost.

Question 17 of 18

In the context of market structures, 'X-inefficiency' (Leibenstein) refers to:

Explanation: X-inefficiency occurs when monopolies or sheltered firms lack competitive pressure, leading to organizational slack, bloated managerial perks, and higher costs than necessary.

Test Item 4 of 10

Test Item 5 of 10

In the context of game theory, what defines a 'Dominant Strategy Equilibrium'?

Rationale: A dominant strategy equilibrium occurs when every player in a game has a dominant strategy and plays it. Since a dominant strategy is optimal regardless of opponents' choices, this equilibrium is a strict subset of Nash equilibrium.

Test Item 6 of 10

What is the primary reason why firms in monopolistic competition engage in heavy non-price competition (advertising and branding)?

Rationale: Monopolistic competitors use advertising and product differentiation to make their demand curves steeper (more inelastic), granting them greater pricing power and shielding them from direct price competition.

Test Item 7 of 10

Which of the following describes the 'Bertrand Paradox' resolution when firms have capacity constraints (Edgeworth model)?

Rationale: Edgeworth resolved the Bertrand paradox by introducing capacity constraints. If a firm cannot serve the entire market at a low price, undercutting fails to capture all customers, leading to price fluctuations above marginal cost.

In long-run equilibrium under monopolistic competition, economic profit is zero because:
