Micro Economics

Theories of Interest and Profit: Classical, Loanable Funds, Liquidity Preference, and Knight's Theory

This comprehensive study guide explores the foundational and advanced theories of factor pricing, specifically interest and profit, bridging classical real theories with modern monetary and risk-bearing frameworks. Essential for UP PGT Economics, UGC-NET, and State Assistant Professor examinations, it systematically dissects classical real interest theory, neo-classical loanable funds framework, Keynesian monetary liquidity preference, and Knight's uncertainty theory of profit.

🏛️ Key Economists & Landmark Publications

Adam Smith
1776, An Inquiry into the Nature and Causes of the Wealth of Nations

Pioneered early real wage and profit concepts, viewing profit as a deduction from the produce of labour and interest as a derivative of profit.

Eugen von Böhm-Bawerk
1889, The Positive Theory of Capital

Developed the Austrian round-aboutness theory of production and time-preference theory of interest, linking interest to the productivity of waiting.

John Maynard Keynes
1936, The General Theory of Employment, Interest and Money

Revolutionized monetary economics by introducing the Liquidity Preference Theory of interest, defining interest as a purely monetary phenomenon.

Frank H. Knight
1921, Risk, Uncertainty and Profit

Established the definitive Chicago school theory of profit, distinguishing measurable risk from unmeasurable uncertainty as the true source of pure profit.

Dennis Robertson & Bertil Ohlin
1930s, Loanable Funds Theory (Neo-Classical Synthesis)

Synthesized monetary and real forces into the Loanable Funds framework, incorporating savings, investment, hoarding, and credit creation.

⚖️ Fundamental Assumptions & Hypotheses

  • Perfect competition in capital and money markets: Interest rates are flexible and adjust instantaneously to clear excess demand or supply.
  • Rational economic agents: Borrowers maximize discounted profit or utility, while lenders optimize inter-temporal consumption allocation.
  • Dichotomy between real and monetary sectors (Classical view): Money acts merely as a medium of exchange and does not affect real interest rates in the long run.
  • Uncertainty vs. Risk dichotomy (Knightian view): True entrepreneurial profit arises strictly under conditions of non-quantifiable Knightian uncertainty.
  • Liquidity preference motives: Individuals hold money for transactions, precautionary, and speculative motives, with the latter being interest-elastic.
  • Closed economy framework (baseline models): Net foreign capital flows are initially assumed to be zero unless explicitly integrated into open-economy loanable funds extensions.

1. Classical Theory of Interest (Real Theory)

The classical theory posits that the rate of interest is determined by the demand for and supply of capital (or savings and investment). Real savings represent the supply of capital, driven positively by the interest rate due to time preference. Real investment represents the demand for capital, driven negatively by the interest rate due to diminishing marginal productivity of capital. Equilibrium interest rate clears the capital market where Savings (S) equals Investment (I). It assumes full employment via wage-price flexibility and Say's Law.

Key Exam Takeaways:
  • Interest is a real phenomenon, not monetary.
  • Savings is an increasing function of the interest rate: dS/dr > 0.
  • Investment is a decreasing function of the interest rate: dI/dr < 0.

2. Loanable Funds Theory of Interest (Neo-Classical Synthesis)

Advanced primarily by Swedish and Cambridge economists (Wicksell, Robertson, Ohlin), the Loanable Funds theory rejects the pure classical view by introducing monetary factors, bank credit, and hoarding/dishoarding. The supply of loanable funds consists of savings (S), bank credit creation (ΔM), dishoarding (DH), and domestic disinvestment. The demand for loanable funds consists of investment (I), hoarding (H), and consumption demand for loans (CD). Equilibrium occurs where total supply equals total demand for loanable funds.

Key Exam Takeaways:
  • Supply of Loanable Funds (SLF) = S + ΔM + DH
  • Demand of Loanable Funds (DLF) = I + H + CD
  • Bank credit and hoarding act as dynamic monetary levers shifting the equilibrium rate away from the pure classical S-I intersection.

3. Keynesian Liquidity Preference Theory of Interest

Keynes discarded the classical and loanable funds real approaches, defining the rate of interest as a purely monetary phenomenon—specifically, the reward for parting with liquidity for a specified period. The demand for money (L) is divided into transactions and precautionary motives ($L_1$), which depend on income level ($Y$), and the speculative motive ($L_2$), which depends inversely on the rate of interest ($r$). The supply of money ($ar{M}$) is fixed exogenously by the central bank. Intersection of liquidity preference and money supply determines the equilibrium interest rate.

Key Exam Takeaways:
  • Keynesian Equilibrium condition: M_s = L_1(Y) + L_2(r).
  • Interest rate is determined by demand and supply of money, not capital.
  • At very low interest rates, liquidity trap occurs where money demand becomes perfectly elastic.

4. Knight's Uncertainty Theory of Profit

Frank Knight argued that in a static, perfectly competitive equilibrium with complete foresight, pure economic profit tends to zero. Profit emerges exclusively in a dynamic, uncertain world. Knight famously distinguished between 'Risk' (statistically calculable probabilities using objective actuarial data, insurable) and 'Uncertainty' (non-calculable, unique subjective events, uninsurable). True entrepreneurial profit is the residual reward earned by the entrepreneur for bearing unmeasurable uncertainty and making successful managerial decisions.

Key Exam Takeaways:
  • Risk is insurable; Uncertainty is uninsurable.
  • Pure economic profit is the reward for bearing unmeasurable uncertainty.
  • Entrepreneurs act as 'shock absorbers' in the economic system.

📐 Mathematical Formulations & Equilibrium Conditions

Classical Capital Market Equilibrium
S(r) = I(r)

Equates real aggregate savings with real aggregate investment, determining the natural rate of interest where time preference equals capital productivity.

Loanable Funds Equilibrium Condition
S(r) + Δ M + DH = I(r) + H + CD

Integrates monetary expansion (\Delta M) and hoarding behavior (H, DH) with real savings and investment.

Keynesian Liquidity Preference Function
M = L1(Y) + L2(r)

Total money demand equals active balances ($L_1$, function of income $Y$) plus idle speculative balances ($L_2$, inverse function of interest rate $r$).

Knightian Profit Residual Equation
π = TR - (Explicit Costs + Implicit Costs + Uncertainty Premium)

Defines pure economic profit (\pi) as total revenue minus all opportunity costs and the implicit reward for bearing non-insurable uncertainty.

🧠 Exam Tips, Tricks & Mnemonics (10-Second Shortcuts)

💡 Knightian Profit Distinction R-I-U (Risk = Insurable, Uncertainty = Uninsurable)
Remember: Risk has probabilities (Insured via insurance companies); Uncertainty has NO probabilities (Uninsured, yields Profit).

Exam Application: Instantly spot trick questions in UP PGT asserting that risk yields profit—Knight explicitly argued risk yields wages of management, only uncertainty yields profit.

💡 Loanable Funds Components Acronym SIDH - IC (Supply: Savings, $\Delta M$, Hoarding; Demand: Investment, Consumption loans)
SLF = S + M + D | DLF = I + H + C. Memorize that bank credit adds to supply, while hoarding adds to demand.

Exam Application: Helps solve equation-based numericals on loanable funds shifts in UGC-NET economics paper.

💡 Keynesian Motives Breakdown T-P-S (Transactions, Precautionary, Speculative)
T and P depend on Income (Y); S depends inversely on Interest Rate (r).

Exam Application: Quickly evaluate shifts in LM curve slope and position based on income elasticity vs. interest elasticity.

⚠️ Economic Paradoxes & Boundary Exceptions

Exception / Paradox Liquidity Trap

Conditions: When the rate of interest falls to an extremely low floor level, bond prices are exceptionally high, and agents expect interest rates to rise (bond prices to fall).

Diagrammatic Behavior: The Liquidity Preference curve becomes perfectly horizontal (infinite interest elasticity), rendering monetary policy completely ineffective.

Exception / Paradox Wicksellian Cumulative Process

Conditions: When the market (money) rate of interest diverges from the natural rate of interest due to bank credit creation.

Diagrammatic Behavior: Triggers cumulative inflation (if market rate < natural rate) or deflation (if market rate > natural rate) without self-correcting mechanisms.

Exception / Paradox Paradox of Thrift (Keynesian Exception)

Conditions: An autonomous attempt by society to increase its rate of saving during a recession.

Diagrammatic Behavior: Leads to a reduction in aggregate demand, lower equilibrium national income, and ultimately leaves aggregate savings unchanged or lower.

⚡ Must-Remember Economic Identities & Laws

  • Classical Equilibrium Condition: Savings (S) = Investment (I) determines the real rate of interest.
  • Loanable Funds Equilibrium: Total Supply of Loanable Funds (Sl = S + DH + BM + NFI) = Total Demand for Loanable Funds (Dl = I + Ho + Cl).
  • Keynesian Money Market Equilibrium: Total Money Supply (M) = Transactions/Precautionary Demand (L1) + Speculative Demand (L2).
  • Fisher Equation relating nominal and real rates: i = r + πe (where i is nominal, r is real, and πe is expected inflation).
  • Knightian Profit Formula: Pure Profit = Total Revenue - (Explicit Costs + Implicit Costs + Normal Return to Risk-bearing).
  • Speculative Demand Inverse Relationship: L2 = f(r), where dL2/dr < 0, becoming infinite at the lower bound of interest.
  • Marginal Efficiency of Capital (MEC) Rule: Investment continues until MEC equals the market rate of interest (r).

🚫 Common Analytical Confusions (Contrast Table)

Classical Theory of Interest VS Loanable Funds Theory of Interest

Classical theory is a real theory where interest is determined exclusively by real savings and real investment. Loanable funds theory is a neo-classical synthesis that includes monetary factors such as bank credit creation, hoarding, and dishoarding alongside savings and investment.

Knightian Risk VS Knightian Uncertainty

Risk refers to random events with mathematically determinable objective or subjective probabilities, which can be insured against. Uncertainty refers to unique, non-repetitive events where probabilities cannot be assigned, and the assumption of this uncertainty yields pure profit.

Transactions Demand for Money (Keynes) VS Speculative Demand for Money (Keynes)

Transactions demand depends primarily on the level of income and is interest-inelastic. Speculative demand depends inversely on the rate of interest and is driven by expectations of future bond price fluctuations.

Natural Rate of Interest VS Market Rate of Interest

The natural rate equates the demand for savings with the supply of savings at full employment (Wicksell/Classical). The market rate is the actual monetary interest rate charged by banks in the financial market.

Normal Profit VS Economic/Pure Profit (Knight)

Normal profit is the minimum payment necessary to keep an entrepreneur in their current line of production (treated as an explicit or implicit opportunity cost). Pure profit is the residual surplus earned specifically by bearing uninsurable uncertainty.

📚 Complete Concept-by-Concept Glossary

Every fundamental term, curve, and mechanism covered in this chapter.

Real Rate of Interest: The interest rate adjusted for inflation, representing the true purchasing power yield on savings and the real cost of borrowing in the Classical framework.
Nominal Rate of Interest: The monetary rate of interest unadjusted for changes in price levels, directly observed in financial markets.
Liquidity Trap: A situation described in Keynes's Liquidity Preference theory where the rate of interest is extremely low, and the demand for money becomes perfectly elastic (horizontal) as everyone expects bond prices to fall.
Loanable Funds: The total amount of funds supplied and demanded in the financial market at a given interest rate, incorporating savings, dishoarding, new bank credit, and net foreign lending.
Knightian Uncertainty: A situation where future outcomes are completely unknown and cannot be assigned mathematical probabilities, distinct from measurable risk; serves as the foundation for Knight's theory of profit.
Risk vs Uncertainty: Risk involves situations with known probability distributions of outcomes, whereas uncertainty involves unknown distributions where objective probabilities cannot be calculated.
Speculative Demand for Money: Money demanded for holding as an asset to take advantage of future changes in interest rates and bond prices, varying inversely with the rate of interest.
Transactions Demand for Money: Money held by households and firms to bridge the time gap between receipt of income and its expenditure, varying directly with the level of income.
Precautionary Demand for Money: Money held to meet unexpected emergencies, unforeseen expenses, or sudden business opportunities, depending directly on the level of income.
Neo-Classical Synthesis: The integration of monetary and real factors in the determination of the rate of interest, as seen in the Loanable Funds theory combining classical savings/investment with monetary supply.
Marginal Productivity of Capital (MPC): The net addition to total output resulting from the employment of an additional unit of capital, determining the investment demand curve in the Classical theory.
Dishoarding: The release of previously hoarded cash balances into active circulation, forming a component of the supply of loanable funds in the Wicksellian and Ohlin framework.
Monopsonistic Exploitation of Entrepreneurship: The extraction of pure entrepreneurial profit through superior forecasting under uncertainty, where the entrepreneur acts as a residual claimant.
Wicksellian Cumulative Process: The dynamic interaction between the natural rate of interest and the money rate of interest leading to cumulative inflation or deflation.

Question 1 of 18

According to the Classical theory of interest, the rate of interest is determined by the intersection of:

Explanation: In the Classical theory, interest is a real phenomenon determined by the supply of savings (reflecting abstinence or time preference) and the demand for investment (reflecting the marginal productivity of capital).

Question 2 of 18

Which of the following components is NOT included in the supply of loanable funds?

Explanation: Hoarding represents a withdrawal of funds from circulation and is a component of the demand for loanable funds (or a leakage), whereas dishoarding, savings, and bank credit expansion form the supply of loanable funds.

Question 3 of 18

In Keynes's Liquidity Preference Theory, the speculative demand for money is:

Explanation: Keynes posited that speculative demand for money (L2) is an inverse function of the rate of interest. When interest rates are high, bond prices are low, so people hold bonds; when interest rates are low, bond prices are high, so people hold cash.

Question 4 of 18

What happens to the speculative demand for money in a liquidity trap?

Explanation: At a very low floor rate of interest, everyone expects interest rates to rise and bond prices to fall, making the speculative demand for money infinite, rendering the demand curve horizontal (liquidity trap).

Question 5 of 18

Who propounded the Uncertainty Theory of Profit?

Explanation: Frank H. Knight introduced the distinction between risk and uncertainty in his 1921 work 'Risk, Uncertainty and Profit', arguing that pure economic profit is the reward for bearing uninsurable uncertainty.

Question 6 of 18

According to Frank Knight, which of the following can be insured against?

Explanation: Risk involves measurable probabilities based on the law of large numbers and can be actuarially insured. Uncertainty involves non-measurable, unique events which cannot be insured.

Question 7 of 18

The Loanable Funds theory of interest is often regarded as a synthesis of:

Explanation: The Loanable Funds theory (Robertson, Ohlin, Wicksell) combines the real factors of the Classical theory (savings and investment) with monetary factors (bank credit, hoarding, and dishoarding).

Question 8 of 18

In the Classical model, an increase in thriftiness (desire to save) leads to:

Explanation: An upward shift in the savings schedule, with investment remaining constant, increases the supply of savings relative to demand, lowering the equilibrium real rate of interest.

Question 9 of 18

Which economist criticized the Classical theory for assuming that savings and investment are sensitive to the rate of interest?

Explanation: Keynes argued that savings depend primarily on income rather than interest rates, and investment depends on the Marginal Efficiency of Capital and business expectations, rendering the Classical interest mechanism flawed.

Question 10 of 18

The Fisher Effect postulates a direct relationship between the nominal interest rate and:

Explanation: The Fisher equation states that i = r + πe. Holding the real interest rate constant, a rise in expected inflation (πe) results in a one-for-one rise in the nominal interest rate (i).

Question 11 of 18

Under Knight's theory, if perfect foresight and complete information prevail in a competitive market economy:

Explanation: Under conditions of perfect competition and complete foresight (absence of uncertainty), all revenues are exhausted by contractual payments to factors of production, leaving zero pure profit.

Question 12 of 18

In the loanable funds framework, the demand curve for loanable funds slopes downward from left to right because:

Explanation: The demand for loanable funds consists of Investment (I), Hoarding (Ho), and Consumer Loan Demand (Cl), all of which vary inversely with the rate of interest, making the aggregate demand curve downward-sloping.

Question 13 of 18

Keynes's total demand for money function can be written as:

Explanation: Keynes partitioned money demand into two parts: active/transactions-precautionary demand (L1) which depends on income (Y), and speculative demand (L2) which depends on the interest rate (r).

Question 14 of 18

According to Knight, the entrepreneur earns profit not because they manage the firm, but because they:

Explanation: Knight explicitly distinguishes between routine managerial functions (which can be hired out for a wage) and uncertainty-bearing/decision-making, which is the unique function of the entrepreneur yielding profit.

Question 15 of 18

In Wicksell's monetary theory, when the market rate of interest is below the natural rate of interest:

Explanation: When the market rate is below the natural rate, investment exceeds voluntary savings, credit expands, and a cumulative process of rising prices (inflation) is triggered.

Question 16 of 18

Which of the following is a monetary theory of interest?

Explanation: Keynesian Liquidity Preference theory treats the interest rate strictly as a monetary phenomenon determined by the demand and supply of money, unlike real or loanable funds theories.

Question 17 of 18

The Paradox of Thrift, closely associated with Keynes's critique of Classical savings, implies that:

Explanation: The paradox of thrift shows that attempts by society to save more during a downturn reduce aggregate demand, lowering income and output, which may ultimately depress total actual savings.

Question 18 of 18

In the context of Knightian uncertainty, entrepreneurial capacity is treated as:

Explanation: Entrepreneurial capacity and judgment under uncertainty are scarce attributes that cannot be standardized or freely hired in a market, allowing successful entrepreneurs to earn pure profits.

In Keynesian liquidity preference theory, if the marginal propensity to consume increases, what happens to the IS curve and the equilibrium interest rate in the IS-LM model?

Rationale: An increase in the marginal propensity to consume (MPC) increases the Keynesian multiplier, making the IS curve flatter (more interest-elastic). With a flatter IS curve, expansionary shifts or monetary interactions lead to a higher equilibrium interest rate and output.

Test Item 2 of 10

Consider the Loanable Funds equilibrium: Sl = S + DH + BM + NFI and Dl = I + Ho + Cl. If there is an autonomous increase in bank credit creation (BM), what is the immediate impact on the equilibrium rate of interest and investment?

Rationale: An increase in bank credit expansion (BM) shifts the supply curve of loanable funds to the right. This excess supply puts downward pressure on the rate of interest, which in turn stimulates investment demand (Dl).

Test Item 3 of 10

Which of the following scenarios best exemplifies Knightian uncertainty rather than risk?

Rationale: Launching a brand-new, unprecedented product involves unique market conditions and unknown consumer reactions with no historical data or probability distribution, representing true Knightian uncertainty.

Test Item 4 of 10

In the Classical theory, if savings is interest-inelastic (vertical savings curve) and investment is interest-elastic, an upward shift in the investment demand function will:

Rationale: If the savings curve is vertical (completely interest-inelastic), the quantity of savings supplied is fixed at full employment. An upward shift in investment demand will drive up the rate of interest, but the equilibrium quantity of savings remains constant.

Test Item 5 of 10

Why did Keynes reject the Classical doctrine that interest is the price paid for abstaining from consumption (waiting)?

Rationale: Keynes argued that the amount of savings is primarily governed by the level of a person's income via the psychological propensity to consume, rather than being significantly responsive to changes in the rate of interest.

Test Item 6 of 10

In the liquidity preference framework, if income (Y) increases while the money supply (M) remains constant, what is the effect on the rate of interest?

Rationale: An increase in income (Y) shifts the transactions and precautionary demand for money outward (L1 increases). Given a fixed money supply, individuals must liquidate bonds to get cash, driving bond prices down and raising the rate of interest.

Test Item 7 of 10

According to Frank Knight, which of the following factor payments is a contractual, fixed cost, whereas profit is a non-contractual residual?

Rationale: Contractual payments like wages, rent, and explicit interest must be paid regardless of firm profitability. Pure profit is the non-contractual residual left over after all these costs are met.

Test Item 8 of 10

What is the Wicksellian 'Cumulative Process' driven by?

Rationale: Wicksell demonstrated that whenever the bank rate diverges from the natural rate of interest, aggregate demand and supply diverge, initiating a cumulative upward price process (inflation) or downward price process (deflation).

Test Item 9 of 10

In the context of the Loanable Funds theory, hoarding of money (Ho) as a component of demand for loanable funds implies:

Rationale: Hoarding represents cash withheld from active circulation and lending, acting as a withdrawal or leakage from the pool of available loanable funds.

Test Item 10 of 10

If the speculative demand for money curve is represented by L2 = k - h(r), and the interest rate (r) approaches infinity, the speculative demand for money:

Rationale: As the interest rate (r) becomes very high, bond prices are extremely low and attractive to hold, driving speculative demand for money (L2) down toward zero.
